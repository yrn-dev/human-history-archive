# Netherlands under Luxemburg

The history of the Netherlands and its associated territories involves various military campaigns and administrative shifts, including the service of notable figures under the command of Luxemburg and the evolving status of the region of Luxemburg itself.

## Military Service under Luxemburg
During the military campaigns in the Netherlands, Claude Alexandre, Comte de Bonneval (1675–1747), served under Luxemburg. A descendant of an old Limousin family, Bonneval had joined the Royal Marine Corps at age thirteen and later rose to command a regiment in the army. While serving in the Netherlands under Luxemburg, Bonneval demonstrated great military ability and indomitable courage. 

Luxemburg himself is recognized as a marshal and a successor to Turenne and Conde. Along with Marshal Crequi, Luxemburg added to his reputation during the "Reunion War" of 1680–84, eventually achieving his greatest triumph ten years later.

## Territorial History of Luxemburg
The district of Luxemburg was originally included in the Roman province of *Belgica prima* and later became part of the empire of Charlemagne and the Frankish kingdom of Austrasia. Around 1060, it came under the rule of Conrad, who held the title of count of Luxemburg. The county was ruled by his descendants until 1437. In 1354, Luxemburg was established as a duchy.

Following the marriage of Elizabeth, daughter of Emperor Sigismund, to the German king Albert II, the duchy passed to the house of Habsburg. However, it was seized in 1443 by Philip III, the Good, duke of Burgundy. The Habsburgs regained the duchy in 1477 through the marriage of Maximilian I to Mary, the daughter and heiress of Charles the Bold. By 1555, the duchy passed to Philip II of Spain, remaining part of the empire.

After France was ceded a section of the territory in 1659, the remainder was given to Emperor Charles VI via the treaty of Utrecht in 1713. France conquered the region again in 1795, retaining it until the end of the Napoleonic wars.

## Political Transitions and the Belgian Revolt
In the 16th century, Alexander Farnese governed the Netherlands for Philip II. Farnese employed a policy of conciliation and diplomacy to bring southern provinces back to the allegiance of the King. This resulted in the Treaty of Arras (1579), concluded between the new governor and the representatives of Namur, Hainaut, French Flanders, Luxemburg, and Artois.

When the house of Austria took possession of Belgium, Luxemburg was one of ten provinces remaining out of the seventeen of the old Spanish Netherlands. Later, the Congress of Vienna (1814–1815) established Luxemburg as a grand-duchy, adding part of the duchy of Bouillon to it. It was assigned to William I, king of the Netherlands, in exchange for German territories of the house of Orange-Nassau that Napoleon had confiscated in 1806.

## The Independence of Belgium and Partition
During the collapse of the united kingdom of the Netherlands in 1830, the Belgian provinces separated from Holland. Efforts were made to include Luxemburg in the new kingdom of the Belgians. However, in November 1831, the powers decided that a portion of the grand-duchy should be retained by the king of Holland. Because the king of Holland refused this arrangement, the Belgians remained in possession of the whole of Luxemburg until 1838. The partition of 1831 was eventually enforced by the treaty of April 19, concluded at the conference of London.

The Conference of London had previously proposed that the Grand Duchy of Luxemburg become an appanage of the house of Orange. This proposal, along with others that robbed the Belgians of Dutch Flanders and the towns of Venloo and Maestricht, was rejected by the Belgians, as the inhabitants of those regions had participated in the Belgian revolt.

## Honors and Orders
The house of Nassau established the Order of the Golden Lion, founded jointly by Adolphus of Nassau and William III of the Netherlands. Upon the death of William in 1890, this family order passed to the grand duke of Luxemburg. Other orders associated with the region include the Order of the Oak Crown (founded 1841, modified 1858) and the Order of Adolphus of Nassau (founded 1858), the latter of which is awarded for military and civil merit.
