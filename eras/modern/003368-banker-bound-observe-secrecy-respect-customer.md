# Banker-Customer Relations and Obligations

The relationship between a banker and a customer is characterized by a combination of debtor-creditor dynamics and specific legal obligations regarding the management of accounts and the processing of instruments.

## Secrecy and Account Management
A banker is legally bound to observe secrecy regarding a customer's account. This obligation persists even if the account becomes overdrawn, although the banker may disclose information if a good cause for disclosure exists (_Hardy_ v. _Veasey_). 

Regarding the documentation of accounts, entries in a pass-book serve as *prima facie* evidence against the banker. If a customer returns a pass-book without comment, the entries may also be considered *prima facie* evidence against the customer, though the idea that such a return constitutes a final settlement of the account is a subject of dispute. In cases where a cheque is repudiated on the grounds of forgery, the customer's dealings or omissions concerning the pass-book do not appear to prevent them from objecting to the debit and holding the banker responsible for the loss (_Kepitigalla Rubber Co._ v. _National Bank of India_). However, credit entries in a pass-book cannot be disputed by the customer if they have altered their position based on those entries, and the banker must honor cheques drawn against an apparent balance (_Holland_ v. _Manchester & Liverpool District Bank_).

## Obligations Regarding Cheques
The banker's primary obligation is to honor a customer's cheques, provided there is a sufficient and available balance (_Foley_ v. _Hill_). If a banker dishonors a cheque despite having sufficient funds, they are liable to the customer for substantial damages, regardless of whether actual injury is proven (_Rolin_ v. _Steward_). If multiple cheques are presented simultaneously and funds are insufficient for all, the banker should pay as many as the funds allow without discriminating between specific cheques.

The banker's authority to pay cheques is terminated by the customer's bankruptcy, insanity, or death, or by notice of an available act of bankruptcy. Any revocation of authority to pay must be unequivocal in method and terms and must come to the banker's conscious knowledge; for example, a banker is not required to act on an unconfirmed telegram (_Curtice_ v. _London City & Midland Bank_).

## Legal Treatment of Cheques and Payments
Under the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, a cheque is not considered an assignment of funds in the banker's hands (sec. 53). Consequently, if payment is refused, the holder of the cheque has no claim against the banker and must seek remedy from the endorser or drawer. This section supports the English banking custom of refusing to pay a portion of a cheque if funds are available but insufficient to cover the full amount. This custom does not apply in Scotland, where banks are seemingly bound to pay whatever funds are available toward the cheque.

Regarding the timing of cheques:
* **Post-dating:** A banker is bound to refuse payment if a cheque is presented before its apparent date of issue (_Morley_ v. _Culverwell_). However, they cannot refuse payment after the apparent date simply because they know the cheque was post-dated, as the Bills of Exchange Act (sec. 13, subs. 1) states a bill is not invalid because it is post-dated, ante-dated, or dated on a Sunday.
* **Stale Cheques:** There is a common practice of refusing "stale" cheques presented an unreasonable time after their date, though the specific timeframe varies between banks (some use six months, others twelve).

## Collection and Dishonour
When acting as an agent for a customer in the collection of crossed cheques, a banker is granted qualified immunity under sec. 82, provided they act in good faith, without negligence, and strictly as a "conduit pipe" for the customer. Negligence includes failing to ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct or taking a cheque marked "account payee" for a different account.

If a cheque paid in for collection is dishonoured, the banker must give notice of the dishonour to either the parties liable on the bill or to the customer. The standard practice is to return the cheque to the customer, a custom validated by sec. 49, subs. 6 of the Bills of Exchange Act.

## Trust Accounts and Fiduciary Relations
While bankers are theoretically supposed to refuse trust accounts, in practice, many are opened with headings indicating fiduciary capacity. In such cases, the obligation to honor the cheque remains predominant. A banker is not entitled or bound to question the object or propriety of a cheque unless there is very clear evidence of impending fraud (_Gray_ v. _Johnston_). When dealing with known trust moneys, the banker may insist on the authority of the entire body of trustees.

Conversely, a standard deposit account (whether at call or on fixed notice) does not create a fiduciary relation but is merely a debt due from the banker to the depositor.

## Banker's Lien and Overdrafts
Bankers possess an exceptional right known as the banker's lien, which allows them to retain and apply any of the customer's securities coming into their possession as a banker to discharge the customer's liability. This includes cheques and bills paid in for collection (_Currie_ v. _Misa_). However, the lien does not extend to moneys paid in for specific bills or goods deposited for safe custody.

Regarding overdrafts, there is no general obligation for a banker to permit a customer to overdraw unless there is an express agreement or an implied agreement based on the course of business. Interest is chargeable on overdrafts based on universal banking custom.
