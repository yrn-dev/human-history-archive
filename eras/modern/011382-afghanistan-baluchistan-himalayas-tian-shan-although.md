# Central Asian Geography and Regional Territories

## Geological Structure and Mountain Ranges
The geography of central Asia is characterized by a broad belt of folding that extends in a series of arcs from west to east. This zone of "crumpling" includes the Himalayas, the Tian-shan, Afghanistan, Baluchistan, Persia, and Asia Minor, as well as the Burmese ranges, despite their different direction. Within this same belt are the Nan-shan, the Kuen-lun, and the mountain ranges of southern China, though these may be of an earlier date.

The Kuen-lun Mountains serve as the backbone of the continent, stretching in a wavy line for nearly 2,500 miles from east to west. In the west, lateral compression has squeezed these ranges into a breadth of 150–200 miles, forcing summits to higher altitudes. In the east, they spread to a breadth of 600 miles and are flatter and lower. While the Kuen-lun crests reach altitudes of 21,500 to 22,000 feet, they generally lack the towering individual peaks found in the Himalayas. The Kuen-lun is significantly older than both the Tian-shan to the north and the Himalayas to the south. A distinctive feature of the northern edge of the Tibetan plateau is the "twinning" of its outermost border-ranges, such as the Astin-tagh and Western Kuen-lun, as well as its intermont lake-basins.

## Hydrography and Continental Divides
Asia is divided laterally along the 40th parallel north by a depression extending from the east of the Gobi desert through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. This depression, which may be below sea-level near Lop Nor and generally does not exceed 2,000 feet, was once a vast inland sea. 

A great central water-parting or divide stretches from the Black Sea through the Elburz and the Hindu Kush to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, continuing to the Bering Strait. While parts of this divide, including the Altai, Khangai, Hindu Kush, and Tian-shan, reach great heights, other portions remain below 5,000 feet. To the south of this divide, the land drops to the waterless interior of the Gobi. Further south lies a second mighty hydrographic barrier that separates the interior from the low plains of India, China, Siam, and the Amur.

## Regional Territories: Afghanistan and Baluchistan
Afghanistan is recognized as an undoubtedly distinct entity among Asiatic nations. Its north-eastern territory extends to just short of 75 degrees east, following the water-divide to the head of the Taghdumbash Pamir and the water-parting of the Hindu Kush. The boundary of Afghanistan is demarcated to maintain a width of independent or neutral territory between it and British India. This independent belt includes Bajour, Swat, and portions of Mohmand territory north of the Kabul river.

Baluchistan, by contrast, is not regarded as a distinct entity in the same manner. While it exists in various degrees of independence, it is subject to British control. "British Baluchistan" officially comprises the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts were originally Afghan but were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, although they had nominally been handed to Kalat forty years prior. The Baluchistan Agency extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. In this region, the agent to the governor-general of India exercises supreme control, and the political status of the province resembles that of the native states of the Indian peninsula. From the Gomal river, Baluchistan serves as an intervening state between Afghanistan and British India.

## Ethnography and Population
The populations west of the great Mongolian region include the Tajiks, who are recognized as the ancient Sacae or Scyths. Found in the central highlands' inaccessible glens and mountains, the Tajiks form the underlying population of Afghanistan, Baluchistan, Persia, and Badakshan. Their language contains Aryan or Sanskrit derivations not found in Persian. Although largely dispossessed of their land by Turkish conquests and immigration, they are noted for retaining an intellectual superiority over the surrounding mixed tribes and Turks.

The Kirghiz are divided by Russian authorities into the Kassak Kirghiz, who are closely allied to the Mongol type, and the Kara Kirghiz, who are unmistakably Turkish and reside primarily in the valleys of the Altai and Tian-shan mountains. The Kirghiz are a nomadic people who follow the religion of Mahomet and speak Turki; they hold the Sarts (town dwellers) in great contempt. Other groups include the Turkomans, who represent the purest form of the Turk element and tongue, and the Kalmucks, a Buddhist Mongolian people who migrated from Dzungaria to Siberia and the Lower Volga before returning to Kulja under Chinese rule in the late 18th century.

## Climate and Flora
The rainfall patterns of south Asia are influenced by mountain barriers. In the west, the projection of the south-east coast of Arabia limits the south-west monsoon, causing rains to cease on the southern border of Baluchistan. Consequently, vapour-bearing winds carry uncondensed rain further inland to Afghanistan and the outer ranges of the western Himalaya.

The flora of the less elevated parts of Baluchistan and Afghanistan, as well as southern Persia, resembles that of the hotter Mediterranean region and the mountainous south-western Arabia (Arabia Felix). These regions are characterized by abundant aromatic shrubs and rare trees, specifically *Celtis*, *Pistacia*, *Dodonaea*, poplars, and the date palm. Dry hills are covered by *Astragalus* and *Statice*. Spring brings herbaceous vegetation and bulbous plants, some of which extend into the Himalaya.
