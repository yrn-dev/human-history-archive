# Timber and Botanical Varieties

The archive's records contain various entries regarding forest growth, specific tree species, and the commercial application of different types of wood across several global regions.

## White Oak and Other Oak Varieties
White oak is a significant timber resource in certain regions. In one state, white oak accounted for 392,804 M. ft., B.M. of the total cut in 1900. By 1905, oak remained a primary product of the sawed lumber industry, with 295,776 M. ft., B.M. produced. In Kentucky, oak is among the most numerous trees in the eastern forests, while it is also found in the western forests.

Other species are referred to as "oaks" due to a fancied resemblance of their wood to that of the oak. These include members of the *Casuarina* genus, which are described as having very hard wood and are valuable as timber trees. Depending on the region, these are known by various names, including "she-oak," "swamp-oak," "shingle-oak," "river-oak," "iron-wood," and "beef-wood." Additionally, the western white oak (*Quercus garryana*) is listed as one of the important trees in a Canadian province.

## Red and White Wood Species
Various species of trees are categorized by red and white characteristics:

*   **Ash:** In America, species include the white ash (*Fraxinus americana*) and the red ash (*Fraxinus pubescens*).
*   **Spruce:** The white spruce (*Picea alba*) is found in Canada.
*   **Alder:** The red alder (*Alnus rubra*) is noted as an important tree in Canadian forest growth.
*   **Maple:** The North American sugar, rock, or bird's-eye maple (*A. saccharinum*) is characterized by the whiteness of its bark and white wood, though the wood acquires a rosy tinge after exposure to light. The curled maple (*A. rubrum*) is also mentioned.
*   **Dogwood:** The common dogwood (*C. sanguinea*) has branches that are dark red and leaves that turn red in autumn; its flowers are dull white and its wood is white and very hard.

## Commercial and Industrial Timber
In certain Canadian provinces, the Douglas fir is the principal timber of commerce, often reaching 300 ft. in height and 8 to 10 ft. in diameter. Its tough and strong wood is highly valued for building and ships' spars. Red or giant cedar is also plentiful and used for interior work and shingles.

The sugar maple is used for axle-trees, spokes, and Windsor chairs, often serving as a substitute for oak when oak is scarce. It is also prized for writing-desks, shoe-lasts, and bedsteads. The wood of the dogwood is utilized for gunpowder charcoal, forks, skewers, wheel-work, and ladder-spokes.
