# Cellular Structures in Biological Tissues

The study of biological organisms reveals a diverse array of cellular arrangements, ranging from the specialized tissues of mosses to the complex epithelial membranes found in animal anatomy. These structures are often categorized by their shape, layering, and specific physiological functions.

## Cellular Structure in Sphagnales
The genus *Sphagnum*, known as bog-mosses, possesses a peculiar stem and leaf structure. In a cross-section of the stem, a thin-walled central tissue is surrounded by a zone of thick-walled cells. Beyond this zone are one to five layers of large clear cells. When these clear cells reach maturity, they become empty and dead. Their walls are perforated with round pores and strengthened by spiral thickening, allowing them to conduct and absorb water through capillarity. 

A similar arrangement is found in the leaves of *Sphagnum*, which lack a midrib. In these leaves, empty cells occur regularly among narrow cells that contain chlorophyll, creating the appearance of a green network.

## Epithelial Tissues and General Characteristics
In anatomy, epithelial cells form continuous sheets that cover every body surface potentially in contact with foreign substances. These cells are characterized by a clearly marked outline and a large nucleus that is either ellipsoidal or spherical. The protoplasm is usually abundant and frequently contains granules.

From these surface epithelia, further structures can originate through ingrowths or outgrowths, including the glands, hairs, nails, the central nervous system, and the sensitive surfaces of special sense organs. Gland cells are situated upon a basement membrane, which may be a homogeneous membrane or composed of very thin flattened cells. In smaller ducts and the secretory portion of the gland, the epithelial layer is only one cell thick, while larger ducts possess two layers.

## Classification of Epithelial Cells by Shape
Individual cells within an epithelial membrane are classified based on their morphology:
*   **Squamous (Flattened):** Very flat cells, such as those found in the surface of the skin or mesothelial cells.
*   **Cubical:** Cube-shaped cells, found in the lowest layer of stratified epithelium or surrounding stomata in mesothelial layers.
*   **Columnar:** Tall cells found lining the intestinal tract (from the end of the oesophagus to the start of the rectum) and the ducts of many glands.
*   **Irregular:** Cells with non-uniform outlines, such as endothelial cells.
*   **Ciliated or Flagellated:** Cells possessing fine protoplasmic processes. Cilia are found in the trachea, bronchi, uterus, and parts of the nasal cavities, where they move to drive fluid in one direction. A single, larger process is known as a flagellum.

## Specialized Epithelial Varieties
Epithelial membranes vary in their layering and specialization:

**Stratified Epithelium**
Found in the skin and various mucous membranes (such as the vagina, rectum, oesophagus, and mouth), this type covers surfaces exposed to friction. It consists of multiple layers: the lowest are cubical or columnar, the middle are polyhedral, and the surface cells are flattened (squamous). In the skin, surface cells are rubbed off and replaced by new cells from the formative deepest layer.

**Stratified Ciliated Epithelium**
This variety lines the epididymis, vas deferens, and parts of the respiratory passages. It features columnar ciliated superficial cells, fusiform cells in the middle, and cubical or pyramidal cells at the base.

**Transitional Epithelium**
Located in the lining of the bladder, this epithelium changes appearance based on whether the bladder is distended or contracted. In a contracted state, the superficial cells are cubical and fit over pear-shaped cells. When distended, these cells, particularly the surface cells, stretch out transversely and become very flattened.

**Mesothelial and Endothelial Cells**
Mesothelial cells are irregularly polyhedral and flattened, resembling squamous epithelial cells. They are united by a scanty intercellular cement substance and form a layer pierced by stomata, which allow materials from the body cavity to enter lymph vessels or spaces. Endothelial membranes are similar in structure but typically consist of elongated cells with serrated borders.

## Other Cellular Systems
In certain organisms, the nervous system is essentially epidermal. A layer of nerve-fibres exists immediately outside the basement membrane that separates the musculature from the epidermis. In the *Ptychoderidae* family, the collar nerve-tube is connected to the epidermis via unpaired outgrowths from the dorsal wall, which contain an axial lumen.

In the *Anthocerotales* group of Bryophyta, the thallus is composed of parenchymatous cells, typically with one large chloroplast per assimilating cell. The capsule wall of the sporogonium is several layers thick; its epidermis contains functional stomata and the underlying cells contain chlorophyll, enabling assimilation.
