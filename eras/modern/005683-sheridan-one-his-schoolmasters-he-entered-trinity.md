# Henry Brooke

## Early Life and Education
Henry Brooke was an Irish author born around 1703 at Rantavan in County Cavan. He was the son of William Brooke, who served as the rector of Killinkere in the same county, and the daughter of Simon Digby, the bishop of Elphin. During his early education, Dr. Thomas Sheridan served as one of his schoolmasters. In 1720, Brooke was entered at Trinity College, Dublin. Following his time there, he was sent to London in 1724 to pursue the study of law.

## Literary Works and Early Career
Brooke married his ward and cousin, Catherine Meares, before she had reached the age of fourteen. After returning to London, he published a philosophical poem consisting of six books titled *Universal Beauty* in 1735. 

In 1738, Brooke translated the first and second books of Tasso's *Gerusalemme liberata*. The following year, he wrote a tragedy titled *Gustavas Vasa, the Deliverer of his Country*. Although the play underwent five weeks of rehearsals at Drury Lane, the performance was forbidden at the last moment. This prohibition was attributed to a suspected portrait of Sir Robert Walpole in the role of Trollio, as well as a general dislike by the government for the fervent patriotism present in the play. Despite the ban, the work was printed and sold extensively, and it was later performed on the Irish stage under the title *The Patriot*. This event led Samuel Johnson to write a satirical pamphlet in 1739 titled "A Complete Vindication of the Licensers of the Stage from the malicious and scandalous Aspersions of Mr Brooke."

## Political Affiliations and Return to Ireland
Brooke aligned himself with the party of the prince of Wales and resided in a small house at Twickenham, located near Alexander Pope. However, his wife grew concerned that his associations with the opposition were imprudent, which prompted his return to Ireland.

While in Ireland, Brooke took an interest in the nation's literature and history. He projected a history of Ireland from the earliest times and a collection of Irish stories, but both projects were abandoned due to disputes regarding the ownership of the materials.

## Later Life and Public Service
During the Jacobite rebellion of 1745, Brooke published *Farmer's Six Letters to the Protestants of Ireland* (collected in 1746), a work inspired by the form of Swift's *Drapier's Letters*. As a result of this service, the government appointed him as the barrack-master at Mullingar, a position he held until his death in 1783. Additionally, he wrote other pamphlets supporting the Protestant side and served as the secretary to an association.
