# British Columbia

British Columbia is the western province of the Dominion of Canada. Its geography is defined by the Pacific Ocean to the west, where it fronts for approximately 600 miles, and the coast strip of Alaska for an additional 400 miles. To the east, the province is bounded by the continental watershed in the Rocky Mountains, though its boundary follows 120° W. north to 60° N., which incorporates a portion of the Peace river country east of the mountains. The northern boundary is 60° N., and the southern boundary is formed by 49° N. and the strait that separates the state of Washington from Vancouver Island. The province includes the Queen Charlotte Islands, Vancouver Island, and various smaller islands off the western coast of Canada.

## History
The discovery of British Columbia occurred in 1774 by the Spaniard Perez, followed by the beginning of geographical coastal exploration in 1778 during a visit by Cook. Between 1792 and 1794, Vancouver surveyed almost the entire coast of the province, as well as much of the coast to the north and south, on behalf of the British government. During this same period, Mackenzie and traders from the N.W. Company entered the interior. In 1821, the N.W. Company amalgamated with the Hudson's Bay Company, which then ruled the territory for twenty-eight years with "beneficent despotism."

Vancouver Island was proclaimed a British colony in 1849. In 1858, the mainland territory was established as a colony named British Columbia following the discovery of gold and a large influx of miners. These two colonies were united under the name British Columbia in 1866. The province entered the confederation and became part of the Dominion of Canada in 1871, sending six (later seven) members to the House of Commons and three senators to the federal parliament.

A condition of entering the dominion was the prompt construction of the Canadian Pacific railway. When this promise was not fulfilled by 1876, and the government in Ottawa appeared indifferent to representations from the province, relations became strained. These tensions were only ameliorated once the construction of a transcontinental road began. Other significant historical events include the founding of the city of Vancouver by the C.P.R., the establishment of Canadian steamship lines to Australia and to China and Japan, the discovery of the Boundary and Kootenay mining districts, and disputes with the United States regarding pelagic sealing.

## Cities and Settlements
The capital of the province is Victoria, located on Vancouver Island. Other chief cities include Vancouver on the mainland, Nanaimo on Vancouver Island, and New Westminster on the Fraser. Additionally, several other locations are noted for their importance: Grand Forks in the Boundary district, Fernie in East Kootenay, and Nelson and Rossland in West Kootenay.

## Infrastructure and Railways
The province's railway system is heavily dominated by the Canadian Pacific railway, which owned or controlled 1,187 meters of the province's total 1,627 meters of railway in 1905. The Pacific division of the Canadian Pacific railway enters the province through the Rocky Mountains in the east and runs for about 500 miles before reaching its terminus at Vancouver. Another branch of this railway leaves the main line at Medicine Hat, crosses the Rocky Mountains via the Crow's Nest Pass, and enters British Columbia just north of the United States boundary. This line runs approximately parallel to the boundary as far as Midway in the Boundary district, crossing a productive mining district and opening extensive coal fields.

On Vancouver Island, there are two railways: the Victoria & Sydney railway (approximately 16 miles) and the Esquimalt & Nanaimo railway (78 miles), the latter of which connects southern ports with coal fields. Additionally, the Great Northern operates several short lines in the southern part of the province that connect to its system in the United States.
