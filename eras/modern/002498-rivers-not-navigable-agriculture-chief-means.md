# Regional Geography: Navigation and Agriculture

The relationship between river navigability and agricultural subsistence varies significantly across different global regions, with some areas relying on navigable waterways for transport and others depending almost entirely on land-based agriculture due to the nature of their river systems.

## Regions with Non-Navigable Rivers

In certain territories, rivers do not serve as means of transport, making agriculture the primary means of subsistence. On the island of Bali, the rivers are not navigable. In this region, agriculture is the chief means of survival, with rice being a crop of particular importance. Other agricultural products grown for export include indigo, cocoa, tobacco, and coffee. Beyond farming, the region engages in gold-working, wood-carving, the production of musical instruments and arms, and the weaving of silk, cotton, and gold thread.

Similarly, in the vassal state of Darvaz (a small state of Bokhara), the geography is emphatically mountainous. In this region, agriculture is only possible in the lower parts of the valleys.

## Navigable River Systems and Improvements

In contrast, many regions have developed extensive river navigation, often through significant engineering efforts.

### Europe
In the maritime department of Ille-et-Vilaine in north-western France, the Vilaine river is navigable for barges as far as Rennes, and is tidal up to the port of Redon. Other navigable streams in the department include the Rance, the Airon, and the Meu and Seiche. Additionally, the Ille-et-Rance canal connects St Malo and Dinan with Rennes. In the government of Kovno in north-western Russia, navigation on the rivers is described as brisk. In Ireland, the town of Carrick-on-Suir benefits from a navigable channel on the river Suir, which has been improved through dredging and is tidal up to the town.

The Danube has undergone extensive improvements during the 19th century to increase its availability as a communication route. Between Vienna and Ulm, rectification works have improved navigability over a distance of 629 m. In Vienna, an artificial channel 10 m in length and 330 yds in width was constructed between 1866 and 1902 to protect the city from inundations and improve navigation. Other connections include the Ludwigs Canal, which links the Danube at Kelheim to the Rhine at Mainz.

### Asia
In Bokhara, the Oxus (or Amu-darya) is navigated from the mouth of the Surkhan, with steamboats operating up to Karki near the Afghan frontier. In Iraq, the Tigris allows for steam communication between Bagdad and its port, Basra. However, navigation between Bagdad and Korna is often slow and uncertain because the river runs through flat alluvial soil and is prone to changing its channel. During flood times, the area can become a huge lake, while the dry season brings the risk of grounding on shoals. Furthermore, the digging of irrigation canals by inhabitants along the eastern bank reduces water levels and contributes to the shifting of the channel. Above Bagdad, sailing vessels of 30 tons or more navigate to Samarra and beyond, while local service is provided by the *kufa*, a circular bitumen-covered basket-work boat.

In China, the Yangtsze is described as a huge navigable river. However, the Yellow River (Hoang ho) is characterized by a current so swift that ordinary navigation is impossible, making boat crossings dangerous.

## Agriculture and Land Use

Agriculture remains a dominant economic activity across various regions, often integrated with river systems for irrigation or drainage.

In the government of Kovno, agriculture is the principal occupation, with 63% of the surface under crops, including potatoes and grains such as barley, oats, rye, and wheat. In Ille-et-Vilaine, the Marsh of Dol provides valuable agricultural land protected by dykes. The primary crops there include wheat, barley, oats, and buckwheat, as well as hemp, flax, early vegetables, potatoes, and tobacco.

In Bokhara, the Zarafshan river's water is largely utilized for irrigation. In other regions, such as those inhabited by the inland Dyaks, agriculture involves taking two crops—one of vegetables or maize and another of rice—before allowing the ground to lie fallow for eight to ten years. These populations also grow their own indigo for dyeing cotton cloth.
