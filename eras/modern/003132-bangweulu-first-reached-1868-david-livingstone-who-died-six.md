# Bangweulu

Bangweulu is a shallow lake located in British Central Africa, situated between 10° 38' and 11° 31' S. and cut by 30° E. It is formed by the head streams of the Congo and occupies the north-west portion of a central basin within an extensive plateau, reaching an altitude of approximately 3,700 to 3,760 feet above sea level.

## Geography and Hydrology
The lake is characterized by a surrounding land that slopes gently into a depression from the north, east, and south. A considerable number of streams drain into this area, transforming much of it into a morass of papyrus and reeds. While the term "Bangweulu" is sometimes used to describe the entire depression, it properly refers only to the area of clear water. The banks of the lake are clearly defined only on its western and south-west sides.

The greatest extent of open water measures approximately 60 miles from north to south and 40 miles from east to west. The water surface is diminished by various islands and long, narrow sandbanks. These sandbanks nearly separate the main body of water from Chifunawuli, the western pan of the lake. Notable islands include Kirui (also known as Chiru) on the east side near the swamp, a small central island called Kisi (or Chishi) located just south of 11° S., and Mbawali, which measures 20 miles long by 3 miles broad and lies south of Kisi.

The hydrological system involves several key water bodies:
* **The Luapula:** This river leaves Bangweulu at its southernmost point. It is about a mile wide at the outflow but narrows to approximately 300 yards. It flows south through the swamp for 50 miles before turning west and then north. The Luapula is believed to be slowly draining the lake.
* **The Chambezi:** The most remote head stream of the Congo, the Chambezi flows into the east side of the swamp south of Bangweulu. It mingles its waters with the Luapula in the swamp without ever entering the lake itself.
* **Lake Kampolombo:** Located west of the Luapula near its outflow, this lake is 20 miles long and 8 miles broad at its southern end. It is separated from Bangweulu by a sandy track and from the Luapula by a narrow, forest-clad tongue of land called Kapata, though various channels connect the river to the lake.

The swamp, which extends south of Bangweulu to 12° 10' S., is gradually being solidified by alluvium deposited by the Chambezi and other streams.

## Exploration and History
The region was heard of as early as 1798 by the Portuguese traveller Francisco de Lacerda. However, the first European explorer of education to penetrate the country was the Portuguese official Dr. F.J.M. de Lacerda e Almeida, who travelled from Tete on the Zambezi to the vicinity of Lake Mweru.

The modern history of the area is closely linked to David Livingstone. After discovering Lake Nyasa in 1859 and journeying to Lake Mweru and the south end of Tanganyika, Livingstone first reached Bangweulu in 1868. His explorations opened this part of South Central Africa and established British interests in the region. Livingstone died in 1873 among the swamps to the south of the lake.

Following Livingstone's death, the area saw the arrival of Scottish settlers and various missionary societies who began evangelizing the country. These groups opposed Portuguese attempts to extend their influence from the Zambezi and Moçambique provinces. This period also saw the emergence of the African Lakes Trading Corporation, which engaged in a struggle for possession of the country against Arabs established at the north end of Lake Nyasa between 1885 and 1896.

Further geographical documentation of Bangweulu occurred in 1883, when the French traveller Victor Giraud partially surveyed the lake. In 1896, Poulett Weatherley became the first person to circumnavigate it.
