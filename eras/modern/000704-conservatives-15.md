# Conservatives

Across various national histories, conservative movements and parties have emerged as political forces often defined by their opposition to progressive or liberal reforms, their adherence to traditional duties, and internal divisions over economic policies such as trade.

## British Conservative and Unionist Politics
In British parliamentary history, the Conservative Party experienced significant periods of power and decline. After nearly twenty years of holding power, the party suffered a major defeat in the January 1906 polls, which resulted in a Liberal landslide. In Manchester, a key battleground, eight Conservative colleagues and Mr. Balfour were replaced by nine Labour or Liberal men. Following this, the Conservatives returned to the House of Commons with barely 150 members, while the Liberals held a majority of over one hundred over all other combined parties.

Internal fractures have historically plagued the party, particularly regarding trade. The issue of "Protection," also known as Tariff Reform, split the Conservatives, leading to the resignation of six Ministers and the withdrawal of support from fifty Conservative or Unionist members. This loss included many younger men necessary for the party's driving power during opposition. This movement was actively sustained by figures such as the Duke of Devonshire and Sir Michael Hicks-Beach, and was indirectly endorsed by Lord Salisbury from retirement. This event was noted as the most formidable loss to the party since the expulsion of the Peelites.

Earlier, in 1846, a similar breach occurred when Sir Robert Peel accepted a policy of free trade. This led to an irreparable split with Lord Stanley, who became the leader of the Protectionist party, supported by Disraeli and Lord George Bentinck in the Commons.

In later conflicts, the Conservative party provided full support and encouragement to the Protestant counties of Ulster in their preparations for armed resistance against the Home Rule Bill, which was set to become law under the Parliament Act.

## Continental European Conservatism
In Denmark, the Conservative *Landsting* (Upper House) was involved in a political crisis in May 1897 when it rejected a budget that had been voted for by the Radical *Folketing*. This led to the resignation of the ministry in favor of the moderate Conservative Hörring cabinet. However, the Conservatives faced further defeat in the 1898 elections. By the 1900-1901 session, the Conservative party in office—specifically the Sehested cabinet, which had superseded the Hörring cabinet in April 1900—underwent further disintegration.

In Austria, conservative elements were present across various ethnic groups. In the general election of May 14, 1907, conservative elements across all races were defeated by "extremer" elements. To establish the Christian Socialist (Lueger) party as the strongest group in parliament, an amalgamation was formed between them and the conservative Catholic party. According to archival data on German and Polish representation, German Conservatives included 96 Christian Socialists and 19 German Agrarians, while Polish Conservatives numbered 15.

## Conservatism in Asia
In Japan during the 1860s, conservatives were driven by the goal of expelling foreigners. Following a marriage between the shogun and the Emperor's sister in 1861, Bakufu officials pledged to expel foreigners within ten years. This inspired conservatives, including several samurai, to visit Yokohama and threaten death to any Japanese merchant trading with "aliens." These conservatives argued that the shogun's title of *sei-i* (barbarian-expelling) explicitly mandated the expulsion of foreigners as a duty, criticizing the shogun's officials as "craven" for suggesting an apology instead of fulfilling this duty.

In China's National Assembly, there are no distinct parties; instead, Radicals, Liberals, and Conservatives are mixed. The assembly is structured with representatives of the nobility, officials, scholars, and leading merchants on the first four benches, followed by delegates from provincial assemblies. Those in the front benches—the nobility and officials—generally lean toward conservative tendencies, while those in the back tend toward progressive ideas, though some members of the "Commons" hold reactionary or antiquated views.
