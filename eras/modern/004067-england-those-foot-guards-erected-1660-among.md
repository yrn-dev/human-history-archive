# England in the Late 17th Century and Military Infrastructure

## The Restoration of Charles II
Following the death of Cromwell on September 3, 1658, England moved away from the attempt to establish a commonweal of free men. In 1660, Charles II, the son of Charles the "Martyr," returned to England. His return was marked by manifestations of personal loyalty, and the country experienced a period of relaxation regarding its naval and military efficiency. During this time, the Puritans were finished, and the era of "Merrie England" returned.

Charles II assumed control of the state's foreign affairs from 1660. In 1670, he entered into a secret treaty with Louis XIV of France, agreeing to subordinate English foreign policy to that of France in exchange for an annual pension of £100,000. Additionally, Dunkirk, which had been captured by Cromwell, was sold back to France. A noted sportsman, Charles II's love for horse racing is reflected in the racing centre at Newmarket.

## Political and Diplomatic Figures
During the Restoration, figures such as Downing played significant roles. Downing, an able diplomat, made his peace with Charles II in April 1660, communicating Thurloe's despatches and renouncing principles he had adopted in New England. In May 1660, he was knighted and maintained his embassy in Holland and his tellership of the exchequer. He was also granted land for building purposes near St James's Park, which became known as Downing Street.

Downing was a supporter of the Navigation Act of 1660 and remained hostile toward the Dutch, whom he viewed as commercial rivals. He was returned for Morpeth in the convention parliament of April 1660 and served in every subsequent parliament until his death, speaking on commercial and financial matters. Later, in 1671, he was appointed a commissioner of the customs and returned to Holland to incite war between England and Holland to further French policy, though he eventually fled back to England due to extreme unpopularity and fear of the mob.

## Evolution of Barrack Construction
Historically, Great Britain did not require barracks beyond fortifications as long as troops were levied specifically for particular wars. The royal bodyguard was an exception. For a time, the government utilized billeting—quartering soldiers among town populations—but this was found to be burdensome to the public, detrimental to military efficiency, and subversive of discipline.

The earliest recorded barracks in England were those erected in 1660 for the foot guards. Later, in 1700, the Royal Barracks were established at Dublin, with further barracks built across Ireland during the 18th century. In England, the majority of early barracks were constructed between the end of the 18th and the beginning of the 19th century.

Initially, the King's Engineers (later the Corps of Engineers), under the master-general of the ordnance, handled construction for barracks connected to fortresses. In 1796, a special civil department was created under commissioners for the affairs of barracks to manage those separate from fortifications. By 1816, a civilian comptroller was appointed to oversee the upkeep and erection of barrack hospitals and barracks located outside fortified areas.

## Military Organization and the Foot Guards
The British army utilized a system of "crack" companies known as grenadiers, who were the most powerful men in a regiment. These companies took the right of the line on parade and wore distinctive headdress. While many such elite companies were often detached for special service—a practice that lasted until approximately 1858—some remained. The Grenadier Guards are the only remaining grenadiers in the British service. This unit was originally the 1st regiment of Foot Guards, formed in 1660 from a nucleus of English royalists who had followed Charles II during his exile.

## Literature and Cultural Influence (1660–1700)
Between 1660 and 1700, English literature entered a phase heavily influenced by France. During the reign of Charles II, the works of Bossuet, La Fontaine, Moliere, and Racine were at their peak. Cavalier exiles, including Hobbes, Cowley, and Waller, returned from Paris, and the critic Saint-Evremond settled in England.

This French influence was most evident in the forms of prose and verse, specifically in satire, comedy, epigrams, maxims, and criticism. It also impacted thought and theology. While Greek literature and the dramatists became less prominent, secondary forms of Latin poetry—such as the works of the satirists and Juvenal, as well as the critical and epistolary style of Horace—came to the fore, often introduced through the modernizing influence of Boileau and the translations of Dryden.
