# Francisco Gordillo and the Expedition of 1521

## Overview of the Expedition
In 1521, Francisco Gordillo was dispatched on an exploring expedition to the North American mainland. This voyage was organized and funded by Lucas Vasquez de Ayllon, a Spanish adventurer and colonizer who had previously served as a member of the superior court of Hispaniola and a magistrate of various towns, including La Concepcion. Ayllon, who had achieved significant profit through various commercial enterprises, sought to extend Spanish settlements into North America.

## Route and Findings
During the expedition, Gordillo touched upon the coast of the Florida peninsula. From that point, he continued to coast northward for some distance. Upon his return, Gordillo provided a report of the region that was described as favorable.

## Impact on Subsequent Colonization
The positive nature of Gordillo's report served as the catalyst for further Spanish efforts in the region. Based on these findings, Lucas Vasquez de Ayllon obtained a charter from Charles V in 1523, which granted him the right to plant colonies, although the charter was considered rather indefinite. 

Following this, Ayllon continued his efforts to settle the area:
* **1525:** Ayllon sent another reconnoitring expedition.
* **1526:** Ayllon personally set out with a group consisting of approximately 100 African slaves and 500 colonists.

## The Settlement of San Miguel
During his 1526 voyage, Ayllon stopped at several coastal locations. At one of these stops, the expedition replaced a wrecked ship with a new one, an event regarded as the first instance of shipbuilding on the North American continent. 

Sailing further north to approximately latitude 33deg 40', Ayllon began constructing a town named San Miguel. The precise location of San Miguel is a subject of dispute among writers; some suggest it was located where Jamestown, Virginia, was later established, while others, such as Lowery, contend it was situated near the mouth of the Pedee river.

## Failure of the Colony
The settlement of San Miguel was short-lived due to several factors:
* **Health and Mortality:** The location was unhealthy, and a fever killed many of the colonists, including Lucas Vasquez de Ayllon, who died on October 18, 1526.
* **Social Unrest:** Following Ayllon's death, dissensions arose among the colonists. Additionally, some of the African slaves—whose employment there was the first instance of such labor in what later became the United States—rebelled and escaped into the forest.

By December 1526, the town was abandoned. The remaining colonists embarked for Hispaniola, though fewer than 150 of them arrived safely.
