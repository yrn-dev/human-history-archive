# Administration and Tenure of Public Office

The history of public administration across various civilizations demonstrates a wide range of structures regarding the appointment, duration, and conduct of officials holding office. These roles have spanned judicial, executive, and diplomatic functions, with varying rules governing their tenure and the ethical expectations attached to them.

## Judicial and Legal Offices
In England, the office of the Lord Keeper of the Great Seal served as a significant state position. Originally, the Great Seal was entrusted to a chancellor, but because chancellors were often ecclesiastics with diocesan duties or absences from England, the seal was frequently placed in the hands of a vice-chancellor or keeper. This evolved into a permanent appointment. The Lord Keeper held office during the king's pleasure and was appointed via the delivery of the seal rather than by patent. An act of Elizabeth established that the Lord Keeper, specifically Sir Nicholas Bacon, was entitled to the same jurisdiction, pre-eminence, and execution of laws as the lord chancellor. The final individual to hold this title was Sir Robert Henley, who became chancellor upon the accession of George III.

Other English legal figures held multiple high offices over extended periods. Sir Julius Caesar, for example, served during the reigns of James I and Elizabeth. His career included a mastership in chancery (1588), a judgeship of the admiralty court (1584), and a mastership of the court of requests (1595). He later served as under treasurer and chancellor of the exchequer in 1606, and from 1614 until his death in 1636, he held the office of master of the rolls.

In ancient Rome, the Prætor Urbanus was the primary official responsible for the administration of justice during his year of office. By B.C. 47, Cæsar increased the number of prætors from eight to ten, possibly to provide rank and office to his dependents. Brutus served as Prætor Urbanus in B.C. 44, the year Cæsar was assassinated.

## Executive and Provincial Governance
Administrative tenure has often been subject to strict legal limits or reform. In ancient Greece, Bœotarchs were required by law to lay down their office during the first month of the year, known as Boukation. Pelopidas and a colleague were once tried for their lives because they held their office for four additional months while settling affairs in Arcadia, Laconia, and Messenia, though both were eventually acquitted.

In Japan, Emperor Konin addressed abuses in provincial administration that had arisen from religious mania during the reigns of Koken and Shomu. During that time, individuals of substance were granted official posts in exchange for contributions of land, cereals, or money to build images and temples. This led to the presence of thirty-one supernumerary provincial governors who used their offices to recoup nomination costs, harming taxpayers and those liable to the corvée. To remedy this, Koken issued an edict in 774 dismissing governors who had held office for five years or more, while those with shorter terms were removed after completing five years.

## Diplomatic and National Leadership
In Bolivia, the presidency was held by several individuals during a ten-year period of recovery following a war. These included Dr. Pacheco, who succeeded Campero and served a full term; Dr. Aniceto Arce, who held office until 1892; and his successor, Dr. Mariano Baptista. In 1896, Dr. Severe Alonso became president. During his tenure, diplomatic relations with Great Britain were resumed, leading to the appointment of Senor Aramayo as minister plenipotentiary in July 1897 and the conclusion of an extradition treaty in March 1898.

## Ethics and Conduct in Office
The conduct of officials during their tenure has frequently been a subject of scrutiny. In the case of Bacon, a weighty charge against his character is connected to the years he held office. Buckingham frequently sent Bacon letters recommending the causes of suitors. While many appeared to be courtesy letters, the case of Dr. Steward raised suspicions. It appears that Bacon may have had a first judgment on the case set aside and the matter reopened to obey a request from Buckingham, accommodating the strongly recommended Dr. Steward.
