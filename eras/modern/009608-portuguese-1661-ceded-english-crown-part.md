# Portuguese Colonial and Territorial History

The Portuguese presence in various global regions was characterized by the establishment of trading posts, forts, and administrative settlements, often involving conflict and diplomatic negotiation with other European powers and local rulers.

## Presence in India

Portuguese activity in India led to significant friction with the English, particularly as the English East India Company expanded its trade and factories in the early 17th century. The English efforts to open traffic with Persia were especially contentious, as the Portuguese had long held a monopoly on that trade through their possession of Hormuz. This rivalry escalated into maritime war by December 1621. In 1622, five English vessels, allied with the governor of Fars (Imam Kuli Khan), attacked Kishm and besieged Hormuz. After a ten-week defense, the Portuguese surrendered Hormuz on May 1, 1622. At this time, Portugal was subject to the crown of Spain.

In southern India, the Portuguese established a presence in Kodungalur. In 1502, Syrian Christians sought Portuguese protection, and the Portuguese built a fort there in 1523, enlarging it in 1565. However, Portuguese cruelty in the region drove most of the local population of Jews to Cochin. The Portuguese eventually lost the Kodungalur fort to the Dutch in 1661.

In the region of the Gulf of Cambay, the Portuguese established a permanent settlement at Damaun. Although the town was sacked and burnt by the Portuguese in 1531, they took it again in 1558, converting a mosque into a Christian church. Damaun proper was conquered by the Portuguese in 1559, and the territory of Nagar Havili was later ceded to them by the Mahrattas in 1780 as indemnification for piracy.

## The Cession of Bombay

The island of Bombay did not prosper under Portuguese rule. Land was distributed via a system called *aforamento*, where fiefs were granted to individuals or religious corporations—such as the Jesuits and Franciscans—in exchange for quit-rents or military service. The Jesuits and Franciscans built several churches in the northern districts, but the intolerance of their rule hindered the growth of the settlement. By 1661, the population was only 10,000.

The English recognized Bombay's value as a naval base due to its safety from land attacks and the excellence of its harbor. After an unsuccessful attempt to gain the island in 1626 and suggestions for its purchase in 1653, the island was ceded to the English crown in 1661. This transfer occurred as part of the dower of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. Despite the legal cession, the British did not actually occupy the island until 1665, facing significant opposition from the Portuguese and religious orders. In 1668, the English crown transferred Bombay to the East India Company for an annual payment of £10.

## Activities in the Americas

In the Americas, the coast of Maranhão was discovered by Pinzon in 1500 and included in Portuguese captaincy grants in 1534. The region faced competition from other European powers; for instance, the Portuguese expelled a French colony from the island of Maranhão in 1615. In 1621, the "Estado do Maranhão" was created, uniting Pará, Maranhão, and Ceará, and operating independently of southern captaincies. This independence lasted until 1774, when the region returned to the colonial administration of Brazil. Following the declaration of independence in 1822, the Portuguese were driven out of the province in 1823 by Admiral Lord Cochrane.

## Other Global Interactions

Portuguese influence extended to East Asia, where Jesuits were active in Japan. However, these missions faced political challenges; the Spanish Franciscans entered Japan as ambassadors and intrigued against the Portuguese Jesuits. While the Jesuits were punished by Hideyoshi, the Franciscans were occasionally favored to offset Jesuit influence. Luis Froez was a notable Portuguese Jesuit who died in 1597.
