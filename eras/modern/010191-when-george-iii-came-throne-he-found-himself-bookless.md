# George III

George III ascended to the English throne in 1760. Unlike his predecessors, George I and George II, who were entirely German and could not speak English, George III had been born in England and possessed a fair command of the English language.

## Political System and Governance
Upon his accession, the political landscape was marked by the dismissal of Newcastle, the disgrace of Pitt, and the rise of Bute. These shifts indicated a desire by the court to alter the political system established by the Revolution of 1688. Under the previous system, government power was held by a territorial oligarchy consisting of a few families with shrewd political sense and large possessions. The first two Hanoverian kings had maintained their positions by accepting Revolution principles and conciliating these families.

By 1760, the peril to the dynasty had ended, and George III, or his advisors, sought to concentrate substantial political power in the hands of the sovereign rather than the aristocracy. Under this proposed shift, ministers were no longer intended to be members of a united great party following a common policy. Instead, they were to be nominees of the court, answerable individually and by department to the king. George III looked to the government of his cousin, the great Frederick, as a model. However, this attempt to treat a commercial and territorial aristocracy with the spirit of the drill-ground and barrack met with resistance, which characterized the first twenty-five years of the political life of Burke.

Despite these attempts at concentration of power, the practice of independent deliberations by the cabinet—which had become well established by the time George III mounted the throne—was never departed from. In the system of government, the sovereign retains the prerogative to select the person most fit to form a new cabinet upon the dissolution or resignation of a ministry. The prime minister acts as the constructor and author of the cabinet, holding it together and distributing political offices subject to the sovereign's ultimate approval.

## The Royal Library
When George III came to the throne, he found himself without books. He subsequently accumulated a magnificent library consisting of over 80,000 pamphlets and books, as well as 440 manuscripts. This collection reflected the literary and catholic spirit of book-lovers of the era. The library was rich in the classics of Rome and Greece, as well as English classics. It featured works from the first presses of Oxford, London, and Westminster, balanced by typographical first-fruits from Venice, Rome, and Mainz.

This library was passed to the British Museum in 1823. The museum had previously acquired a smaller, carefully chosen collection from the Rev. C.M. Cracherode (who died in 1799) and was later enriched in 1846 by the library of Thomas Grenville. Other contributions to the British Museum during George III's reign included natural history books from Sir Joseph Banks and a collection of English plays bequeathed by Garrick.

## Royal Residences and Estates
George III purchased Dutch House in 1781 to serve as a nursery for the royal children; this plain brick structure is now known as Kew Palace. Additionally, he purchased the estate of Kew House around 1789, dedicating his leisure time to its improvement. In 1802, the old house was demolished and a new mansion was started based on designs by James Wyatt. However, the king's death prevented the completion of this project, and the portion that had been built was removed in 1827.
