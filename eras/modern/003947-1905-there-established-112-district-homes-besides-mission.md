# Dr Barnardo's Homes

## Origins and Founder
Thomas John Barnardo (1845–1905) was an English philanthropist who founded and directed homes for destitute children. Born in Dublin, Ireland, to an English mother and a father of Spanish origin, Barnardo initially pursued a career in medicine. He studied at the London hospital, as well as in Paris and Edinburgh, eventually becoming a fellow of the Royal College of Surgeons. His original ambition was to perform medical missionary work in China.

However, during the cholera epidemic of 1865, Barnardo's medical work in the east end of London brought him face-to-face with the vast numbers of destitute and homeless children living in England's cities. With the support of the first Earl Cairns and the seventh earl of Shaftesbury, he abandoned his plans for foreign missionary labor to dedicate his life to these children.

## Establishment and Growth
The first of the "Dr Barnardo's Homes" opened in 1867 in Stepney Causeway, London, which continued to serve as the headquarters for the institution. The organization experienced steady growth throughout the remainder of the century. By the time of Barnardo's death in 1905, the institution had established 112 district "Homes" across the United Kingdom, in addition to various mission branches.

## Objectives and Admissions
The primary objective of the institutions was to search for and receive "waifs and strays." Once admitted, the children were provided with food, clothing, and education. Where possible, the homes provided industrial training tailored to the specific needs of each child.

The institution operated on a principle of immediate and free admission. There were no restrictions regarding nationality, religion, sex, or age. The only requirement for admission was destitution; consequently, the homes received both the incurably diseased and the physically robust.

## Operational System and Training
The institution utilized a structured system to manage children based on their age and gender:

*   **Infants and Young Children:** Younger boys and girls were primarily "boarded out" in rural districts.
*   **Girls:** Those above fourteen years of age were sent to industrial training homes to learn useful domestic occupations.
*   **Boys (13–17 years):** Boys in this age bracket were trained for various trades based on their physical or mental fitness.
*   **Boys (17+ years):** Boys over seventeen were first tested in labor homes. Following this, they were either emigrated, sent to sea, or placed in employment within the country.
