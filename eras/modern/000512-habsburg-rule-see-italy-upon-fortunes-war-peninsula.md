# Habsburg Rule in Italy and the Peninsula

The influence and supremacy of the House of Habsburg in the Italian peninsula were characterized by long-standing traditions of dominance that faced significant challenges and eventual breaches during the revolutionary periods of the late 18th and 19th centuries.

## The Breach of Habsburg Supremacy
The tradition of Habsburg supremacy in Italy was fundamentally altered by the rise of Napoleon and the subsequent French Revolutionary Wars. A critical landmark in this decline was the treaty of Campo Formio, signed on October 17, 1797. While this treaty resulted in Austria gaining Venice, it simultaneously confirmed the loss of the Belgian provinces and established French preponderance throughout the remainder of Italy. This shift created a breach in the established tradition of Habsburg authority in the peninsula, the full effects of which became apparent during the political struggles of the following century.

## Territorial Losses and Shifts
The instability of the Napoleonic era continued to erode Habsburg holdings in the region. Following the defeat at Austerlitz, the treaty of Pressburg was signed on January 1, 1806. Under the terms of this agreement, Austria suffered further territorial losses, specifically losing both Tirol and Venice.

Earlier in the 18th century, the house of Habsburg had also faced challenges to its Italian holdings. Following the war of the Austrian succession, the treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748 resulted in cessions made at the expense of the house of Habsburg within Italy.

## The Crisis of the 19th Century
By the mid-19th century, the stability of Habsburg rule was once again threatened by widespread unrest. The fall of Metternich served as a signal for a "storm" of revolutions that swept through central Europe and Italy. In the Italian peninsula, the news of Metternich's collapse triggered a concerted rising against Habsburg rule.

This uprising had profound implications for the broader Austrian Empire. The Austrian government found itself in a position of impotence during this crisis because it was forced to keep the bulk of its military forces in Italy to combat the rebellion. Consequently, the ultimate outcome of the revolutions within the Austrian dominions—including the movements for new constitutions in Hungary and Bohemia—depended heavily upon the fortunes of the war in the Italian peninsula.
