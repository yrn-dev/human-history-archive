# Voluntary and Non-Denominational Colleges

The history of higher education includes a variety of institutions established outside of direct denominational control or as voluntary organizations. These range from theological colleges and non-sectarian universities to specialized vocational and regional schools.

## Baptist Voluntary Colleges in Great Britain
In Great Britain, the Baptists recognized an early need to provide an educated ministry for their congregations. While some pastors were educated at English universities and others, such as Dr. John Gill—noted for his knowledge of Hebrew and his nine-volume *Exposition of the Holy Scriptures* (1746-1766)—attained learning through their own efforts, formal institutions were eventually established.

Early efforts were supported by bequests, including one from Edward Terrill (who died in 1685) for the training of young men under the superintendence of the pastor of the Broadmead Church in Bristol. By 1720, the Baptist Academy began receiving students for the ministry. This was expanded by the Bristol Education Society in 1770, leading to the erection of the Bristol Baptist College around 1811. Other similar institutions emerged, including a society formed in 1804 at Bradford, Yorkshire (later moved to Rawdon), a college formed in London at Stepney in 1810 (moved to Regent's Park in 1856), and the Pastors' College connected to the Metropolitan Tabernacle in 1856. In 1866, a Baptist College was instituted at Bury for "Strict" Baptist views, later moving to Manchester.

These institutions are characterized as voluntary colleges not under denominational control. A similar status applies to the Midland Baptist College in Nottingham. Originally maintained by the General Baptists since 1797 and supported by the General Baptist Association, it became a voluntary institution following the amalgamation of the General and Particular Baptist bodies. Other Baptist theological colleges exist in Glasgow, Ireland (one college), and Wales (two colleges), with a combined total of approximately 210 students.

## Non-Sectarian and Private Institutions in the United States
In the United States, several institutions were established with a strict mandate for non-sectarianism or as private entities. Cornell University is an example of an absolutely non-sectarian institution. Its charter mandates that persons of no religious denomination, or any religious denomination, are equally eligible for all appointments. To prevent religious dominance, the charter stipulates that a majority of the board of trustees cannot belong to one religious sect or to no religious sect. Despite this, the university hosts an active Christian Association and religious services in Sage chapel, provided for by the Dean Sage Preachership Endowment.

In Kentucky, Berea College is listed as a non-sectarian institution among the state's private and denominational colleges. Other private colleges in the region include Transylvania University in Lexington, as well as denominational schools such as Georgetown College (Baptist), Central University (Presbyterian), and Kentucky Wesleyan College (M.E. South).

## Regional Voluntary and Specialized Schools
In Scotland, the archives note the presence of voluntary schools within specific counties. In Dumbartonshire, several voluntary schools operate alongside the school-board jurisdiction, including St Peter's Roman Catholic College in New Kilpatrick. Additionally, the region utilizes the Glasgow Technical College for county students attending agricultural lectures. Similarly, in Ayrshire, there are a considerable number of voluntary schools. The Kilmarnock Dairy School, established in 1899, serves as part of the West of Scotland Agricultural College and receives contributions from the Glasgow and West of Scotland Technical College.

## Baptist Educational Expansion in America
American Baptists saw a significant increase in educational infrastructure between 1812 and 1906. In 1812, the denomination possessed only one degree-conferring college with a small faculty and student body. By 1906, this grew to more than 100 universities and colleges, with an annual income of about $3,000,000 and endowments and equipment valued at $30,000,000. Theological education also expanded from no seminaries in 1812 to 11 seminaries by 1906, supporting 1,300 students and over 100 instructors.

One of the earliest efforts to create a denomination-wide institution began around 1762 via the Philadelphia Association. This led to the 1764 charter of an institution in Rhode Island, chosen for its religious liberty and wealthy Baptist constituency. After preliminary work at Warren, where the first Baptist degrees were conferred in 1769, the college moved to Providence in 1770. Known since 1804 as Brown University, it served for many years as the only degree-conferring institution controlled by Baptists.
