# Photography

Photography has emerged as a versatile tool across various fields, serving as a critical instrument in scientific discovery, a strategic asset in modern warfare, and a "dangerous weapon" in the realm of financial forgery.

## Applications in Astronomy and Astrophysics
Since the middle of the 19th century, photography has been introduced step by step into the observation of heavenly bodies. Previously, observations were limited to the human eye; however, photographic systems now threaten to supersede eye observations in many astronomical determinations. 

In the field of astrophysics—the branch of astronomical science dealing with the physical constitution of heavenly bodies—photography has been a primary driver of development. The recognition of astrophysics as a distinct science resulted from the combination of photography, photometry, and spectroscopy. This combination allowed scientists to move beyond viewing heavenly bodies merely as disks or points of light.

Specific advancements include "prominence photography," which was fully realized in 1891 by Henri Deslandres in Paris and Professor George E. Hale in Chicago. These researchers captured images using only the violet ray of calcium, eliminating other beams via a double slit. Professor Hale further developed the "spectroheliograph," an instrument that allows the sun's disk to be photographed in calcium-light by moving the image rapidly relative to the sensitive plate.

## Role in Military Warfare
In the context of modern scientific warfare, photography became a highly developed art during the First World War. By 1916, aerial photography had been developed into a "wonderful art," which, along with observation balloons and aeroplanes, enormously enhanced the aerial side of artillery work. This progress occurred alongside the evolution of aeroplanes, which had transitioned from being used chiefly for scouting and dropping marks for artillery to carrying machine guns for aerial fighting and performing bombing work.

## Impact on Counterfeiting and Bank-Note Security
In the history of currency, photography is described as the most dangerous weapon available to the counterfeiter. Before its discovery, forgers were held in check by the use of special water-marks in the paper and the inclusion of emblematical figures, views, or engine-turned ornaments in the engraving. Photography changed this dynamic by allowing the perfect reproduction of vignettes and complicated patterns.

To combat this, Henry Bradbury introduced anti-photographic bank-note printing in 1856. This method involved printing essential portions of a note in one colour and overlaying it with a protective second colour, which created a confused mingling of colours when photographed. As photographic knowledge advanced, counterfeiters found ways to separate these colours. This necessitated the introduction of a third colour and a specific photographic relation between the three to prevent separation.

It is noted, however, that photography is not the only means of imitation. A note secure against photographic reproduction may still be easily copied by an engraver of ordinary skill if other necessary features are absent. There are two primary engraving systems used for bank-notes:
1. **Line-engraving:** Lines are cut into copper or steel plates (plate-printing), producing a finer, sharper impression. Because a perceptible body of ink is transferred to the paper, some believed these were easier to photograph.
2. **Relief-engraving:** Lines stand above the plate (surface-printing), where ink is spread on the face of the lines. While these produce less sharp impressions, they are more likely to escape detection when imperfect copies are made.

Historically, early bank-notes were printed forms on water-marked paper with amounts written by hand, and forgeries were rare. Forgery became rife at the end of the 18th century when small notes for L1 and L2 were circulated, leading to a constant trial of skill between bank-note makers and counterfeiters.
