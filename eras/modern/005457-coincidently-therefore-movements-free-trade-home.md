# Imperial Trade and the Movement Toward Tariff Reform

The evolution of British commercial policy from the mid-19th century to the early 20th century was marked by a tension between the established doctrines of free trade and a growing movement toward imperial preference and tariff reform. This transition reflected changing perceptions of the relationship between the United Kingdom and its colonies, as well as reactions to international economic competition.

## The Era of Free Trade and Early Agitation
During the Victorian age, British business philosophy was heavily influenced by an individualistic free-trading philosophy, rooted in the ideas of economist Adam Smith, which posited that world-wide prosperity required unencumbered trade across the earth. This period saw significant domestic agitation against restrictive trade laws. In 1838, figures such as Richard Cobden and John Bright began speaking against the Corn Laws. By 1839, they had founded the Anti-Corn Law League, a movement dedicated to the principles of free trade.

## The Shift Toward Imperial Preference
By the late 19th century, a new "imperial sentiment" began to emerge in commercial matters. At a Colonial Conference in London in 1887, a South African delegate proposed a preferential system that would impose a 2% duty on all foreign goods to fund the maintenance of the imperial navy. This proposal included the request to denounce treaties with foreign nations that restricted trade between different parts of the empire. Consequently, in 1897, treaties with Belgium and Germany were denounced.

A growing perception developed that Great Britain's foreign trade in manufactured goods was linked to the enlargement of home markets for colonial raw materials. This view was formally expressed at the Ottawa Colonial Conference in 1894. Canada led the way in implementing these ideas; in 1897, it introduced an imperial tariff that granted a 25% deduction on customs duties for British goods without requiring reciprocal advantages. This preference was also extended to other British colonies. In subsequent years, New Zealand, Australia, and the South African states adopted similar preferential treatments for British goods.

## Tariff Reform and the Chamberlain Programme
In 1903, Mr. Chamberlain initiated a movement for tariff reform. This programme had two primary objectives: protecting imperial trade through retaliative duties on foreign goods and granting preference to colonial goods. This movement was seen as a natural evolution of the imperialist idea, supported by trade statistics showing that colonial trade constituted an increasingly large proportion of the United Kingdom's total trade.

The proposal created significant political friction within the Unionist party. While Mr. Balfour admitted the "onesidedness" of the English free-trade system and supported retaliation against "dumping" and unfair foreign competition, he was hesitant to adopt Chamberlain's full programme. The plan involved a radical alteration of the fiscal system, including new taxes on food to facilitate colonial preference. This was strongly opposed by "out-and-out Unionist free-traders" such as Lord George Hamilton and Mr. Ritchie.

The conflict led to a period of government instability. Mr. Balfour, professing "no settled convictions" and anxious to avoid a party rupture, requested full figures from the Board of Trade to investigate alleged diminutions in British trade. In September, the tension culminated in the resignations of Mr. Chamberlain and the "strait free-traders," including Mr. Ritchie and Lord George Hamilton. Balfour subsequently published a pamphlet on "Insular Free Trade," arguing for the freedom to negotiate to increase exchange, while noting that England remained the only free-importing country.

## International Context and Outcomes
The movement for colonial preference continued to gain momentum despite the 1906 defeat of the Unionist party and the rise of a Liberal government opposed to measures inconsistent with free trade. By the Imperial Conference of 1907, all colonial premiers advocated for tariff reform, citing the progress their own states had made in granting preferences to British and mutual colonial goods.

Outside the British Empire, other nations experienced similar shifts. In Austria-Hungary, the Customs Union established in 1867 initially relied on commercial treaties with nations like Great Britain and France, creating a system nearly devoid of customs duties. However, after 1873, Austrian manufacturers—facing competition from English, Belgian, and German goods—pushed for protective duties. By 1877, a new treaty was established; while raw materials remained duty-free, low duties were placed on corn and textile goods, and excises on brandy and sugar were increased.
