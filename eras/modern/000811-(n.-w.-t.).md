## Mathematical Analysis of Power Series
In the study of differentiable functions, a power series is defined as an infinite series where the general term is $a_nz^n$, with $a_n$ representing a constant and $z$ acting as the variable for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$. For such a series to be classified as a power series, there must exist a real and positive number $M$ such that $|a_n z_0^n| < M$ for $z = z_0$ and every $n$. If a series converges for $z = z_0$, it is proven to converge absolutely for every $z$ where $|z| < |z_0|$, and it converges uniformly over every range $|z| < r'$ where $r' < |z_0|$.

Every power series possesses a circle of convergence. Within this circle, the series converges absolutely and uniformly, rendering the function of $z$ represented by the series continuous. If the series converges for a point $z_0$ on the circumference, the sum for an interior point $z$ is continuous with the sum for $z_0$ as $z$ approaches $z_0$. Furthermore, two power series, $\sum a_n z^n$ and $\sum b_n z^n$, can be multiplied together using ordinary rules within a common circle of convergence.

## Differentiable Functions and Coefficients
If the region of definition of a differentiable function $f(z)$ includes a region bounded by two concentric circles with radii $r$ and $R$ centered at the origin, and $z_0$ is an interior point, the function can be expressed as:
$f(z_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_R \frac{f(t)}{t - z_0} dt - \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_r \frac{f(t)}{t - z_0} dt$
where the integrals are counter-clockwise around the two circumferences. By substituting $(t - z_0)^{-1}$ with specific summations, it is found that $f(z_0) = \sum_{-\infty}^{\infty} A_n z_0^n$. The coefficients $A_n$ are determined by:
$A_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int \frac{f(t)}{t^{n+1}} dt$
This integral is taken around any circle centered at the origin with a radius between $r$ and $R$. In cases where the region of definition includes the entire interior of the outer circle, $r$ may be taken as 0. In this instance, coefficients $A_n$ for $n < 0$ vanish, and $f(z_0)$ is expressed for the interior $|z_0| < R$ by the power series $\sum_{0}^{\infty} A_n z_0^n$. Consequently, a differentiable function of $z$ is expressible by a power series in $z - c$ about every interior point $c$ of its region of definition.

## Monogenic Functions and Polynomials
A monogenic function $f(x)$ can be represented by a series of polynomials that converge uniformly within an arbitrary finite region, provided barriers joining the singular points of $f(x)$ to $x = \infty$ are excluded. For any arbitrary real positive number $\sigma$, it is possible to determine a polynomial $Q(x)$ of order $m$ such that $|f(x) - Q(x)| < \sigma$ for all interior points of the region. If $f(x)$ is not a single-valued function, the series represents one branch of that function.

If a monogenic function of $t$, $\phi(t)$, can be expressed as a power series in $t-x$ about a point $x$ for $|t - x| \le \rho$, and $|\phi(t)| < g$ for all points of this circle, then $|\phi^{(n)}(x)| < g\rho^{-n}(n!)$.

## Periodic Functions
For a single-valued monogenic function of $z$ that is periodic, one of two conditions applies:
1. All periods are real multiples of one period $\Omega$, taking the form $M\Omega$, where $M$ is an integer.
2. The function has two periods whose ratio is not real, in which case all periods are expressible as $N\Omega + N'\Omega'$, where $N$ and $N'$ are integers.

In the first case, by setting $\zeta = 2\pi iz/\Omega$ and $f(z) = \phi(\zeta)$, the function $\phi(\zeta)$ has the period $2\pi i$. If $f(z)$ is an integral function regarded as a function of $t$, it possesses singularities only at $t = 0$ and $t = \infty$, allowing it to be expanded as $\sum_{-\infty}^{\infty} a_n t^n$.
