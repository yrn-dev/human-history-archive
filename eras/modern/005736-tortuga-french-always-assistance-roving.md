# Tortuga and the Buccaneers

## Origins of the Buccaneer
The emergence of the buccaneers was rooted in the colonial conditions of the West Indies during the early 16th century. The island of Santo Domingo had been largely depopulated due to the oppressive colonial policies of Spain. Spanish officials imposed monopolies on the produce of the soil, which deprived local Spanish establishments of convenient markets. Consequently, French, Dutch, and English vessels were welcomed by these establishments to trade their cargoes.

The island became home to vast herds of wild cattle, and smugglers frequently used Santo Domingo for provisioning. The remaining native inhabitants possessed skills in preserving meat at small establishments known as *boucans*. Adventurers adopted this practice of "boucanning" from the natives, leading to the development of an extensive and illicit trade in butchery on Hispaniola.

The combination of Spanish monopolies and the allure of a roving life—marked by occasional skirmishes with Spanish forces—created a class of individuals known as buccaneers. These men functioned alternately as hunters, sailors, and occasionally planters. They were characterized as bold, unscrupulous, and often savage, possessing an intense detestation of Spain.

## Settlement and Conflict in the West Indies
Because Spain refused to recognize the right of other nations to trade or establish settlements in the West Indies, the governments of Holland, England, and France did not control their subjects who invaded the islands, leaving them to settle at their own risk. This led to the simultaneous planting of English and French colonists on the island of St Kitts (also known as St Christopher).

Tensions existed between the English and French settlers. In 1629, after some English colonists moved to an adjoining island, a Spanish fleet of thirty-nine sail surprised and partially dispersed the remaining colonists. Once the fleet departed, the scattered bands returned and found encouragement from their countrymen in Santo Domingo.

## The French Possession of Tortuga
France expanded its influence in the region by appointing the governor of St Kitts as the "Governor-General for the French West India Islands." In 1641, this governor took possession of Tortuga, expelling all English residents from the island. He also attempted to do the same in Santo Domingo, though with less success.

The Spanish eventually responded to the presence of the buccaneers. Eight years after a period of inaction, the Spaniards attacked Tortuga while many buccaneers were away on the larger island, massacring every settler they could capture. However, the survivors returned, and the buccaneers entered a state of open hostility toward Spanish arms. For the next seventy-five years, they became a scourge to the dominions and trade of Spanish-America, recruiting members from every European trading nation.

## The Role of the Roving Community
The buccaneers operated as a mercenary navy, offering their services to any nation willing to employ them against Spanish power in exchange for a share of the plunder. They were recognized for their extraordinary skill in seamanship, as well as their daring and cruelty.

The French reliance on this roving community was absolute during their efforts to control Tortuga; the taking and re-taking of the island by the French was always achieved with the assistance of these rovers. A similar influence was exerted by the buccaneers in favor of the English navy during the conquest of Jamaica in 1655.

The stability of these holdings remained precarious. In 1654, the Spaniards regained Tortuga from the French, though the French recovered the island six years later. The insecurity of these positions meant that the rovers would have succumbed if the English Commonwealth navy had not secured a refuge for them through the conquest of Jamaica.
