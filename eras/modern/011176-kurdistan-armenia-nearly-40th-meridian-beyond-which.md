# Kurdistan and Armenia

## Geography and Topography
The regions of Kurdistan and Armenia are situated upon an extensive high plateau in Asia, west of British India. This plateau covers an area comparable in size to Tibet, though it exists at a lower altitude. The high land of this region follows nearly the 36th parallel North to the southern shore of the Caspian Sea and extends to the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmora.

The southern and southwestern face of this table-land is defined by a mountain scarp that rises in many locations to 10,000 feet. This scarp flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris, extending along Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian. Beyond this point, the scarp turns along the north-eastern angle of the Mediterranean and the Taurus range. In its wider sense, Kurdistan is described as the "country of the Kurds," encompassing the wild mountain districts watered by the Little and Great Zab—which mark the western end of the great Iranian plateau—as well as the portion of Mount Taurus that buttresses the Armenian table-land. This area is further intersected by the Bohtan Su, the Batman Su, and other tributaries of the Tigris.

## Regional Boundaries and Industry
Historical accounts of the boundaries of Kurdistan vary. According to Hamd Allah, the boundaries included Azerbaijan, Diarbekr, Persian ’Irák, Khuzistán, and Arabian ’Irák. Persian Kurdistan extends south beyond Kermanshah.

The region has been noted for specific manufactures. Buckram (or Bucherame) was principally manufactured in Mardin (Kurdistan), Mush and Erzinjan (Armenia), and Ispahan (Persia), before being shipped west to Famagusta, Acre, Satalia, and Constantinople.

## Ethnic Groups and Social Structure
The Guran are identified as a dominant tribe in southern Kurdistan during the 14th century, occupying the area from the Hamadan frontier to Shahrizor. Their name is likely derived from *gur* or *giri* (mountain), similar to the term Zagros (*za-giri*), meaning "beyond the mountain." Described as a hardy, brave, and active race of excellent soldiers, the Guran differ in appearance from the tribal Kurds of the northern districts; while northern Kurds prefer gay colors, the Guran wear homely costumes consisting of felt caps, coats, and coarse blue cotton vests. In much of Kurdistan, the term Guran has become synonymous with an agricultural peasantry, distinguishing them from migratory shepherds.

## Political History and Administration
In 1514, Sultan Selim I annexed Kurdistan and Armenia after defeating Shah Ismail. He appointed Idris, a Kurd historian from Bitlis, to organize the territories. Idris found the region filled with castles held by practically independent hereditary tribal chiefs of Armenian, Arab, and Kurd descent. To manage this, Idris divided the territory into districts known as sanjaks and installed the local chiefs as governors, maintaining the principle of heredity. Additionally, he resettled Kurds from the Bohtan and Hakkiari districts into the pastoral country between Erivan and Erzerum, which had been waste since the passage of Timur.

This administrative system remained in place until the end of the Russo-Turkish War of 1828-29. Over time, the Kurds increased their power due to the decline of Turkey and the remoteness of their lands, spreading as far west as Angora. Following the 1828-29 war, the Kurds attempted to seek freedom from Turkish control, leading Reshid Pasha to reduce them to subjection in 1834 by garrisoning principal towns and replacing Kurd beys with Turkish governors. A subsequent rising led by Bedr Khan Bey in 1843 was repressed, and Turkish control was further strengthened after the Crimean War.

Between 1880 and 1881, Sheikh Obaidullah attempted to establish an independent Kurd principality under Turkish protection. This effort was initially encouraged by the Porte as a response to plans for a Russian-suzerain Armenian state. However, the attempt collapsed after Obaidullah raided Persia, prompting the central government to reassert authority.

## Inter-Ethnic Relations
Historically, there was little hostile feeling between the Armenians and Kurds until the Russo-Turkish War of 1828-29. As late as 1877-1878, the mountaineers of both races generally coexisted well, as both groups suffered under Turkish rule.
