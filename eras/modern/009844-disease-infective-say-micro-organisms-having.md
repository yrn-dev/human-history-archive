# Infective Diseases and Bacterial Infection

Infective diseases are characterized by the action of micro-organisms that invade the tissues of a host. In animals, a bacterial infection is analyzed as a form of intoxication. This process involves two essential factors: the production of toxins and the multiplication of living organisms within the tissues. The multiplication of these organisms is necessary to regulate the supply of toxins. Notably, there is no fixed relationship between the two; some organisms are highly active in producing toxins despite having a limited ability to invade tissues.

## Mechanisms of Infection and Spread
In certain conditions, such as septic osteomyelitis, the disease is described as infective because micro-organisms germinate in damaged bone and utilize the blood-stream to reach other tissues. This process can lead to blood-poisoning. In infants, these germs may move into neighboring joints, resulting in acute arthritis of infants. The disease often begins in the delicate growing tissue located between the cartilaginous end and the main shaft of the bone (diaphysis), frequently manifesting as pain above or below the wrist, elbow, ankle, or knee.

In other medical contexts, such as embolism in the kidney, a plug may be infective due to the presence of septic micro-organisms, which can lead to the formation of small pyaemic abscesses. Similarly, lymphadenitis involves the inflammatory infection of lymphatic glands caused by the absorption of infective or toxic material from the periphery. This occurs in poisoned wounds or acute infectious diseases such as German measles, mumps, diphtheria, and scarlet fever. If pyogenic organisms are absorbed, the inflammation may progress to suppuration.

## Factors Influencing Susceptibility and Virulence
The result of a virulent bacterium entering animal tissues varies based on circumstances rather than following hard and fast characters. Two primary factors determine the outcome: the virulence of the bacterium and the susceptibility of the host.

Susceptibility varies across different species, races, and individuals. It also changes with the period of life; for example, adults are less susceptible to diphtheria than young subjects. Acquired susceptibility can be increased by factors that lower general resisting powers, such as exposure to cold, starvation, and fatigue. Local resistance may also be lowered by depressed vitality or injury, predisposing the subject to the action of the bacterium.

Virulence, the potency of the bacterium, generally increases when the organism sojourns in the tissues of an animal. This increase is particularly marked through the method of "passage," where the organism is inoculated from animal to animal in series, acting as an adaptation to the surroundings. Conversely, virulence tends to diminish when the organism is cultivated on artificial media outside the body.

## Bacterial Action in Plants
Bacterial diseases also occur in higher plants, though the primary agency of bacteria in some cases is debated. Some diseases, such as the slime fluxes of oaks or the Sereh disease of sugar-cane, are considered doubtful cases where bacteria may simply be saprophytic forms entering tissues already injured by other agents, such as fungi or insects.

However, bacteria are certainly the primary agents in other plant diseases, such as those affecting cucumbers, potatoes, and cabbages. These are characterized by the stoppage of vessels and the subsequent wilting of shoots. Transverse sections of these shoots typically reveal vessels that are brown and choked with a dark yellowish slime containing bacteria.

## Classification and Treatment
In the respiratory system, bronchitis is categorized as either non-specific or specific. Specific bronchitis is that which occurs in infectious disorders—such as pneumonia, measles, influenza, and diphtheria—and is caused by the micro-organisms observed in those diseases.

Defense against these diseases depends on the ability to either kill the organisms producing toxins or neutralize the toxins themselves. Research into immunity has explored the use of vaccines (attenuated cultures or dead organisms). While once thought to be only preventive, work by Sir Almroth Wright indicated that vaccines may be useful after infection has occurred, particularly in localized diseases, by stimulating a general reaction to help destroy invading organisms. This administration is regulated by observing the "opsonic index."
