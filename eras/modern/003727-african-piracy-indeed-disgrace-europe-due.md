# Barbary Pirates

The Barbary pirates were coastal populations of northern Africa who engaged in piratical attacks on the opposite shores of Europe. While piracy existed in the region throughout the middle ages, the barbarian invasions, the Mahommedan conquest, and the decline of the Roman empire, it became particularly flagrant during the 14th century as native Berber dynasties entered a period of decadence. During this time, the town of Bougie served as a notorious haunt for these "skimmers of the sea."

## Rise and Peak of Activity
The organized force known as the Barbary pirates arose in the 16th century. They reached their greatest height during the 17th century, a period described as the "flowering time" of their activities. Their operations extended across the Mediterranean and, for a time, into the ocean. The primary victims of these attacks were the inhabitants of the coasts of Spain, Naples, and Sicily. Additionally, any traders from nations that did not pay blackmail to secure immunity were liable to be captured at sea.

The pirates operated out of several key states, including Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Salli. In Algiers alone, it was said that more than 20,000 captives were imprisoned. The fate of these captives depended on their wealth: the rich were permitted to redeem themselves, while the poor were condemned to slavery. In many instances, masters refused to allow captives to secure their freedom even by professing Mahommedanism. Captives included people of high social position, including English and German travelers in the south, as well as Italians and Spaniards.

## European Relations and Complicity
The continued existence of African piracy was considered a disgrace to Europe, as it was fueled by the jealousies of European powers. Various nations used the pirates as tools in their political rivalries; France encouraged them during her rivalry with Spain, and later, Great Britain and Holland supported the pirates against France. In the 18th century, British public men viewed Barbary piracy as a useful check on the carrying trade competition of weaker Mediterranean nations.

To avoid attacks, the most powerful European states condescended to tolerate insults and make payments to the pirates, disguised as ransoms or presents. However, these payments did not always guarantee safety. Because of the prevalence of captivity, religious orders such as the Lazarites and the Redemptionists worked to redeem captives, often funded by large legacies left for this purpose.

## Efforts Toward Suppression
European military responses varied over the centuries. In 1541, an expedition to Algiers took place involving Charles V and Cortes; it is noted that had Cortes's advice been followed, Europe might have been delivered from organized piracy nearly three centuries earlier. In 1655, British Admiral Robert Blake dealt a "severe beating" to the Tunisians. During the reign of Charles II, the British fleet undertook a long series of expeditions.

By 1815, following a general pacification, the suppression of African piracy was viewed as a necessity. This urgency was heightened after a Tunisian squadron sacked Palma in the island of Sardinia, carrying off 158 inhabitants. Great Britain, having acquired the Ionian Islands and Malta, sought to end the large-scale slave trade practiced by the Barbary states at Europe's expense—an objective also shared by the Congress of Vienna.

## Final Extinction
In 1816, Lord Exmouth was sent to obtain treaties from Algiers and Tunis. After British subjects were brutally ill-treated at Bona, Exmouth returned and, combined with a Dutch squadron under Admiral Van de Capellen, conducted a "smashing bombardment" of Algiers on August 27. This action forced the pirates of Tunis and Algiers to release over 3,000 prisoners.

Despite these losses, the pirates were not reformed. Algiers continued slave-taking and piracy on a smaller scale, leading to further discussions at the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818 and another British bombardment of Algiers in 1824 under Admiral Sir Harry Neal. The pirate communities, which lived solely by plunder, were not thoroughly tamed until the French conquest of Algiers in 1830, which effectively extinguished the Barbary pirates. Isolated cases of piracy continued to occur on the Rif coast of Morocco in later times.
