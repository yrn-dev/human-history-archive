# The Capture of Porto Bello and the War on Spain (1739–1748)

## Outbreak of War and Strategic Planning
Great Britain declared war on Spain on October 23, 1739. At the onset of the conflict, there was a universal belief that Spanish colonies would fall immediately upon being attacked. To achieve this, a plan for combined operations was developed to assault the colonies from both the east and the west. 

The western operation, consisting of both naval and military forces, was placed under the command of Admiral Edward Vernon. Simultaneously, a second force commanded by Commodore George Anson (later Lord Anson) was tasked with rounding Cape Horn to attack the Pacific coast. However, the overall success of this hopeful scheme was undermined by several factors, including bad preparations, delays, dockyard corruption, and unpatriotic squabbles between the involved military and naval officers.

## The Capture of Porto Bello
On November 21, 1739, Admiral Vernon succeeded in capturing the Spanish harbour of Porto Bello, located in what is now the republic of Panama. The harbour was ill-defended, and the archive characterizes this victory as a trifling success to boast of. Despite this capture, Vernon failed to prevent Spanish convoys from reaching Europe. During this period, Spanish privateers operated with destructive effect against British trade in both European waters and the West Indies.

## The Siege of Cartagena
Following the action at Porto Bello, Admiral Vernon was joined by a strong body of troops and naval reinforcements under Sir Chaloner Ogle. This combined force launched an attack on Cartagena, located in present-day Colombia, from March 9 to April 24, 1741. 

The Spanish admiral, Don Bias de Leso, had used the preceding delays to prepare the city's defenses. Consequently, the siege failed, resulting in a dreadful loss of life for the assailants. The failure of the operation was attributed largely to the brutal insolence of Admiral Vernon and the incompetence of the military officers. Following this defeat and two other unsuccessful attacks on Spanish territory, the war in the West Indies declined and did not revive until 1748.

## The Expedition of Commodore Anson
The expedition led by Commodore Anson departed England on September 18, 1740. The mission was hampered from the start, as the fleet sailed late, was less strong than intended, and was very ill provided. The force consisted of six ships.

By the time Anson returned to England on June 15, 1744, he was alone with his flagship, the *Centurion*; the other vessels had either been lost or failed to round Cape Horn. Despite these losses, Anson's cruise is regarded as a great feat of endurance and resolution. During his voyage, he harried the coasts of Peru and Chile and captured a Spanish galleon of immense value near the Philippines.

## Admiral Vernon and the Marine Force
Beyond his combat operations, Admiral Vernon expressed views on the organization of British military forces. During a period of fifty-four years where the marine force—a military body applied to naval purposes—appeared and disappeared with war, Vernon urged the necessity of converting most marching regiments into marines. He suggested that if these men were admitted to be discharged as seamen once they became such, it would create a "good nursery" for breeding marines. The marine force's primary functions included fighting in ships, maintaining ship discipline, and seizing land positions advantageous to naval operations.
