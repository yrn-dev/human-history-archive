# Kwaka Dua I

## Accession and Governance
Kwaka Dua I ascended to the throne in 1838, succeeding Osai Okoto. He is described as a peace-loving monarch who provided encouragement for trade during his reign.

## Conflicts with the British and Fanti
Despite his peace-loving nature, tensions persisted regarding the authority of the Ashanti over the Fanti within the Gold Coast protectorate. In 1852, the Ashanti attempted to reassert this authority. Later, in 1863, a war broke out after the British refused a demand made by Kwaka Dua I for the surrender of a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief.

The Ashanti emerged victorious in two battles during this conflict and retired unmolested. Although the governor, Mr. Richard Pine, suggested that the British should advance on Kumasi, the British government denied this request. While no further fighting occurred following these events, the prestige of the Ashanti was greatly increased. Reflecting on the conflict, Kwaka Dua I remarked that while "the white men" bring many cannon to the bush, "the bush is stronger than the cannon."

## Death and Succession
Kwaka Dua I died in April 1867. His death was followed by a period of civil war before he was succeeded by Kofi Karikari. Upon being enstooled, Kofi Karikari signaled a departure from the peace-loving approach of his predecessor, swearing, "My business shall be war." This shift in leadership led to preparations for an attack on the Fanti tribes, resulting in the war of 1873-74.

## Historical Context of Ashanti Sovereignty
The tensions during the reign of Kwaka Dua I were rooted in a long history of conflict between the Ashanti and the coast tribes. Earlier, under Osai Tutu Quamina (who became king in 1800), the Ashanti had invaded the country of the Fanti after they refused to deliver fugitive chiefs of the Assin. This resulted in the destruction of a town and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants.

In 1820, a treaty was drawn up by the consul Mr. Joseph Dupuis in Kumasi, which acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory. However, this treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and the British government in London. Subsequent conflicts included a battle on January 21, 1824, at Essamako, where the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy. Following a signal defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826, Ashanti power over the coast tribes waned, eventually forcing the king in 1831 to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, for 600 oz. of gold and the delivery of his son as a hostage.
