# War of the Austrian Succession (1740-1748)

The War of the Austrian Succession was a major European conflict that took place between 1740 and 1748. The war was initiated by the invasion of Silesia by Frederick II of Prussia in 1740 and concluded with the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen) in 1748. While the struggle involved nearly all European powers after 1741, the central enduring interest of the conflict was the contest between Prussia and Austria for control of Silesia.

## Origins and the Prussian Invasion
The conflict was precipitated by the death of Emperor Charles VI in 1740 and the subsequent accession of Maria Theresa to the Habsburg throne. The stability of the monarchy was threatened by the perceived fragility of the Pragmatic Sanction; Charles VI had ignored the advice of Prince Eugene to maintain an army of 200,000 men as a guarantee of the sanction, relying instead on the signatures of various powers. Consequently, the Austrian forces were disorganized due to the long confusion of the Turkish wars and were unable to withstand the Prussian military.

Prussia in 1740 was a small, highly organized power possessing a standing army of 100,000 men. Although regarded as a minor army, the Prussian infantry was drilled to a level of perfection that allowed soldiers to fire five shots for every three fired by the Austrians. On December 16, 1740, without a declaration of war, Frederick II crossed the frontier into Silesia. The Austrian generals were only able to garrison a few fortresses before falling back to the mountain frontiers of Moravia and Bohemia. Frederick quickly overran Silesia, investing the strongholds of Neisse, Brieg, and Glogau.

## Belligerents and Alliances
The war evolved into a broad European struggle. The primary combatants were divided as follows:
*   **The Austrian Cause:** Supported primarily by England and Holland, the traditional enemies of France. Sardinia and Saxony also served as important allies to Austria at various intervals.
*   **The Opposing Powers:** France, Prussia, Spain, and Bavaria were constant allies. Following the Prussian victory at Mollwitz on April 10, 1741, other powers ambitious for Austrian expansion—including Saxony and Sardinia—also entered the field against Austria.

French involvement was driven in part by the diplomacy of the Comte de Belle-Isle, who was sent to Germany in 1741 as a plenipotentiary. Belle-Isle sought to reorganize the empire and secure the election of Charles, the elector of Bavaria, as emperor.

## Internal Austrian Challenges and Recovery
The Habsburg dynasty faced significant internal peril during the conflict. The administration was characterized by corruption and confusion, and the finances were ruined. Public opinion in Vienna was loudly in favor of the claims of Charles of Bavaria, while Magyars and Slavs remained perennially discontent.

However, the monarchy found unexpected strength in the character of Maria Theresa. Through her personal appeals and timely concessions, the Hungarians rallied to her support. This marked a historical turning point where the Hungarians felt an enthusiastic loyalty to a Habsburg monarch and recognized that their interests were bound to those of Austria.

## Major Campaigns and Military Leadership
The war's battle-grounds were primarily located in Italy, the Low Countries, and Southwest Germany. In the Austrian military, Count von Leopold Josef Daun distinguished himself through careful leadership. A lieutenant field marshal during the early years of the war, Daun served at Prague and Chotusitz and later led the advanced guard of Khevenhuller's army during the victorious Danube campaign of 1743. He also held important commands at Soor and Hohenfriedberg in 1744.

The year 1744 marked the beginning of the Second Silesian War. Frederick II concluded a new secret alliance with Louis XV, leading France to declare direct war on Sardinia and Austria in April 1744. Louis XV personally led 90,000 men to invade the Austrian Netherlands, capturing Ypres and Menin. However, these plans were disrupted by Prince Charles and the veteran Traun, who maneuvered over the Rhine near Philipsburg in July, capturing the lines of Weissenburg.

## Conclusion and Aftermath
The war ended in 1748 with the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle. The results of the conflict included:
*   **Silesia:** Transferred from Austria to Prussia via the treaty of Dresden.
*   **Italy:** Cessions were made at the expense of the house of Habsburg.
*   **France:** Despite victories at Raucoux and Fontenoy, France gained nothing from the peace, leading to diplomatic criticism of the king.

The conclusion of the war led to a period of shifting alliances known as the "Diplomatic Revolution" before the Seven Years' War (1756). In this reversal, France repudiated its alliance with Frederick the Great and allied with its former enemy, Austria.
