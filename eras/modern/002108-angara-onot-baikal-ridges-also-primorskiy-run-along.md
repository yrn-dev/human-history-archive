# Angara

The Angara is a river of East Siberia associated with Lake Baikal. It serves as the lake's only outflow, issuing from the west shore through a rocky cleft. The river flows toward the city of Irkutsk, where it is joined by the Irkut. While the Irkut once reached Lake Baikal, it no longer does so; instead, it abandons a broad valley leading to the lake and travels northwards through a narrow mountain gap to meet the Angara. Further downstream, at the confluence of the Angara and the Oka, the town of Bratskiyostrog was established around a block-house built in 1631.

## Geography and Lake Baikal
The Angara is inextricably linked to Lake Baikal, which is the largest fresh-water basin in Eurasia and the sixth largest lake in the world. Known as *Bai-kul* to Turkish tribes and *Dalai-nor* to the Mongols, the lake stretches from southwest to northeast. It has an area of 13,200 square miles—nearly the size of Switzerland—and a length of 386 miles. Its width varies between 20 and 50 miles.

The lake is fed by over 300 streams, primarily short mountain torrents. Major tributaries include the Selenga to the south-east, the Barguzin to the east, and the Upper Angara, which enters at the north-east extremity. The lake's altitude is estimated between 1,500 and 1,679 feet, placing it 118 feet above the level of the Angara at Irkutsk. The water level is subject to slight oscillations; for instance, heavy rains in 1869 caused the lake to rise 7 feet, submerging a large territory at the mouth of the Selenga.

## Surrounding Mountains and Ridges
Lake Baikal is surrounded by lofty mountains, with the exception of the Selenga delta. The geography of the shores is characterized by several distinct ranges:
* **North-west Shore:** The Onot and Baikal ridges (also known as Primorskiy) run along this shore, striking it diagonally.
* **South Shore:** The Khamar-daban border-ridge fringes the south, featuring steep cliffs and a summit reaching 5,300 feet above the lake.
* **East Shore:** The Barguzin range impinges upon the lake obliquely.
* **North-east Shore:** An Alpine complex of mountains, some of which remain unexplored, rises here.
* **South-east:** The Ulanburgasu mountains intrude into the delta of the Selenga.

Additionally, a massive highland that is deeply ravined occupies the space between the Angara and the Irkut.

## Geology
Geological evidence indicates that Lake Baikal was much larger in previous ages, extending up the lower valleys of the Barguzin and Upper Angara, as well as westwards into the Irkut valley. The region has experienced volcanic activity at the end of the Tertiary or during the Quaternary Age, resulting in great streams of lava covering the valley of Irkut and the Khamar-daban and Sayan mountains. Earthquakes continue to occur frequently along the shores of the lake.

## Regional Populations and Culture
The Buriats, a Mongolian race, inhabit the vicinity of Lake Baikal, primarily within the Trans-Baikal Territory and the government of Irkutsk. They are organized into clans and tribes, often named after their localities. Physically, they are described as broad-shouldered and inclined to stoutness, with high cheekbones, thick lips, small slanting eyes, and broad, flat noses. Buriat men wear pigtails and shave their heads, similar to the Chinese. Their clothing consists of furs and sheepskins in winter and cotton and silk gowns in summer.

The Buriats are skilled in trapping, fishing, and the rearing of horses and cattle. The Buriat horse is noted for its endurance. In some areas, the Buriats have adopted agriculture from the Russians and are considered better farmers than the Russians in Irkutsk due to their industry in irrigation and manuring.

The religious landscape of the Buriats is diverse:
* **Buddhists:** The majority are Buddhists, led by the Khambo Lama who resides at the Goose Lake (Guisinoe Ozero).
* **Shamanists:** For those practicing Shamanism, the most sacred site is the Shamanic stone located at the mouth of the river Angara.
* **Christians:** Several thousand Buriats around the lake are Christians.

The Buriats speak Mongolian in three distinct dialects. Literacy is common, particularly among those in Trans-Baikalia, who use books largely translated from Tibetan.
