# Fractures

Fractures are the breaking of bones, which may occur through direct violence where the bone is struck, indirect violence resulting from a strain, or through muscular action, such as a rib breaking due to a violent cough. Generally, fractures from direct violence are transverse, while those from indirect violence are more or less oblique. In fully developed bones, the break is fairly across; however, the soft bones of young people may simply bend, a condition referred to as willow fracture or green stick fracture.

## Classification and Characteristics
Fractures are categorized as either simple or compound. A simple fracture is analogous to subcutaneous laceration in soft parts. A compound fracture is analogous to an open wound, where communication exists between the external air and the injured bone. This may happen if the force that caused the fracture also wounded the soft parts or if a sharp point of the fractured bone pierced the skin. Compound fractures carry higher risks, as germs of suppuration can enter the site, potentially leading to blood-poisoning, osteomyelitis, necrosis (death of large pieces of bone), or delayed union.

In most fracture cases, *crepitus*—the feeling elicited when two rough osseous surfaces rub together—can be detected. Crepitus is absent when a bone is merely bent or in impacted fractures, where the broken extremities are driven into one another.

## General Treatment and Recovery
To achieve firm bony union, accurate apposition of the fragments is necessary. The process of putting broken ends together is called "setting the fracture," and splints are used to provide the necessary rest. It is generally advisable to fix the joint above or below the fracture with the splint.

Modern improvements in treatment involve not keeping simple fractures at rest in splints for as long. Instead, they are frequently "taken down" to allow for gentle movements and massage, which helps swelling and pain disappear more quickly and ensures a more complete recovery, reducing the frequency of stiff hands and feet. The French surgeon Just Lucas-Championniere is credited with demonstrating the advantages of these exercises, massage, and discreet movements.

Surgeons now use X-rays to verify that fractured surfaces are in close apposition. If ordinary methods cannot achieve satisfactory adjustment, the surgeon may cut down to the broken ends and fix them with a strong wire suture that remains permanently in the tissues. In compound fractures, immediate wiring of fragments is considered a necessary part of primary treatment if easy adjustment is not possible.

## Specific Fracture Types and Complications
### Fractures Near Joints
When bone splintering occurs into a joint, particularly if tendons are injured, effusion may occur in the tendon sheaths and joint. This can organize into fibrous tissue and cause permanent stiffness, especially in old people. Recovery requires passive movement and gentle exercises to keep tendons and joints free. For example, in a fracture close to the wrist joint, the splint must be arranged to allow the patient to move the thumb and fingers; the splint is removed daily so the fingers and wrist can be straightened, bent, and exercised.

### Fractures of the Patella
Treatment of the knee-cap (patella) has seen various approaches. Some surgeons use a back-splint or plaster of Paris splints, which can be successful if the fibrous bond of union is protected from being stretched by premature bending of the leg. However, this can sometimes leave the patient with an enfeebled limb if fragments remain wide apart. 

In 1883, Lister demonstrated that the knee-joint could be opened and fragments secured in close apposition with a stout wire suture, leading to solid bony union. While some surgeons feared the risk of septic micro-organisms invading the joint, Lister's method is considered to give perfect results with comparatively small risk when performed by skillful surgeons. Other alternatives include Professor A.E.J. Barker's method of passing a strong silver wire vertically around the fragments with a large needle without opening the joint, or using silk or metal sutures circumferentially.

### Fractures in Young People
In children and growing persons, long bones have cartilaginous ends. An injury may cause the end of the bone to detach, known as *diastasis*. This can result in joint stiffness or the arrest of the bone's growth. 

Other complications include:
*   **Delayed union:** Consolidation occurs very slowly or not at all. If bones are well-positioned, treatment involves patience, general health measures, and massage.
*   **Ununited fracture:** No attempt at consolidation is made by nature after many weeks or months. This may be caused by constitutional defects, the interposition of tendon or muscle, constant disturbance of the fracture site, or fragments not being close enough. Treatment involves cutting down to the ends, sawing off a thin slice to freshen them, and fixing them with a screw or wire. This condition is particularly rebellious in the leg-bones of children.
