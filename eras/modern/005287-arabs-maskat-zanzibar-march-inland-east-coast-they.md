# Arab Settlements and Inland Expansion in East Africa

## Coastal Foundations and the Zenj Empire
From the 8th century to the 11th century, Persians and Arabs established settlements along the East African coast. These groups gained political supremacy in various locations, leading to the creation of the "Zenj empire." A prominent center of this influence was the sultanate of Kilwa, which is reputed to have been founded around A.D. 975 by Ali ibn Hasan, a Persian prince from Shiraz. 

Kilwa was established on the site of an ancient Greek colony known as Rhapta. Initially confined to the town itself, the state expanded its influence along the coastline from Zanzibar to Sofala, with Kilwa serving as the capital of the Zenj empire. An Arab chronicle records that over forty sovereigns reigned at Kilwa over a period of five hundred years. By the time the Portuguese navigator Pedro Alvares Cabral visited in 1500, Kilwa was a wealthy, large city featuring three hundred mosques.

## Portuguese Conflict and the Imams of Muscat
The Portuguese arrived in the region in 1502, when Kilwa submitted to Vasco da Gama. However, due to the sultan's failure to pay imposed tributes, the Portuguese occupied the city in 1505 and constructed a fort, the first they built on the east coast of Africa. Following a period of fighting between the Portuguese and the Arabs, the city was destroyed. Although the Portuguese temporarily abandoned the site in 1512 due to fever, Kilwa later became a primary center for the slave trade.

Toward the end of the 17th century, Kilwa fell under the dominion of the imams of Muscat. This arrangement lasted until 1856, when the imams separated their African and Arabian possessions, at which point Kilwa became subject to the sultan of Zanzibar. In 1890, Germany acquired Kilwa along with other southern continental dominions of the sultan.

## Inland Expansion and Trade
Arab influence extended beyond the coastal cities into the interior of the continent. During the second and third quarters of the 19th century, Arab slave-dealers penetrated from Zanzibar toward the Congo and the great lakes. While their power was described as formidable, it eventually disappeared without leaving permanent traces.

The town of Bagamoyo, located 26 miles from Zanzibar, served as a critical entrepot for trade with the interior and the starting point for caravans heading toward the great lakes. It was used as a base for the journeys of several explorers, including J. H. Speke, Sir Richard Burton, Sir H. M. Stanley, and J. A. Grant. In the interior of British Central Africa, Arabs from Zanzibar settled in the country, though records indicate this did not occur earlier than the beginning of the 19th century.

## Language and Cultural Influence
The interaction between Arabic and indigenous populations resulted in the development of Swahili, the language of Zanzibar and the opposite coast. Swahili is an archaic Bantu dialect, likely indigenous to the East African coast south of the Pangani (Ruvu) river. Through intermixture with Arabic, it became the *lingua franca* of eastern Africa between the Zambezi and the White Nile, spreading widely as a commercial language across Central and Eastern Africa.

Swahili-speaking colonies were established at Malindi, Mombasa, and Lamu, and extended as far north as the Shebeli river in Somaliland and as far south as Angoche along the coast of Portuguese and German East Africa.

## Later Political Transitions
In the 19th century, the sultanate of Zanzibar maintained significant mainland dominions. In 1862, Sultan Said Majid attempted to build a town and palace at Dar-es-Salaam, though the project was abandoned after his death in 1871. Later, in 1887, Dr. Carl Peters occupied the bay for the German East Africa Company, leading to fighting with the Arabs. By 1889, the settlement was handed over to the German imperial government.

British influence also grew through connections with the sultanate of Zanzibar. In the last quarter of the 19th century, Sultan Bargash relied heavily on the advice of the British representative, Sir John Kirk. This relationship led to various concessions, including the 1887 grant to an association formed by William Mackinnon, which later became the Imperial British East Africa Company in 1888. This company was chartered to open the hinterland beyond the sultan's mainland territories, which were defined by international agreement as extending 10 miles inland from the coast.
