# The Great Depression: The Global Economic Collapse of the 1930s

## Overview
The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world. While often associated with the U.S. stock market crash of October 1929, the Depression was a complex global phenomenon resulting from a convergence of systemic weaknesses. It was characterized by a catastrophic collapse in GDP, hyperinflation in some regions, and unprecedented levels of unemployment—reaching nearly 25% in the United States.

The crisis fundamentally altered the relationship between governments and their economies. In the U.S., Franklin D. Roosevelt’s "New Deal" introduced sweeping social welfare programs and financial regulations, signaling a shift toward Keynesian economics—the belief that government spending is necessary to stimulate demand during a recession.

## Key Facts
*   **The Banking Crisis:** Between 1929 and 1933, thousands of banks failed as panicked depositors attempted to withdraw their money simultaneously ("bank runs"), wiping out the life savings of millions.
*   **The Dust Bowl:** Environmental catastrophe compounded economic misery. Severe droughts and poor farming practices in the American Great Plains led to massive soil erosion, forcing "Okies" and other migrants to flee toward California.
*   **Global Contagion:** The crisis spread via the Gold Standard. As nations tried to protect their gold reserves, they raised interest rates and restricted trade, which strangled international commerce.
*   **Historical Debate:** Historians and economists continue to debate the primary cause of the Depression. **Monetarists**, such as Milton Friedman, argue that the Federal Reserve caused the crisis by failing to provide liquidity to the banking system. Conversely, **Keynesians** argue that a collapse in aggregate demand and under-consumption were the primary drivers.

## Did You Know?
1. **The "Hoovervilles":** Shanty towns built by homeless people during the early 1930s were named "Hoovervilles" after President Herbert Hoover, whom many blamed for the crisis.
2. **Deflationary Spiral:** Prices didn't just stop rising; they plummeted. This created a "deflationary spiral" where consumers stopped spending because they expected prices to drop further, which in turn forced businesses to cut wages and jobs.
3. **The 21st Hour:** In some U.S. cities, "bread lines" became so long that people waited for hours for a single bowl of soup or a piece of bread provided by charities.
