# Afghanistan

Afghanistan is recognized as a distinct entity among the Asiatic nations. Its political status and geographical limits have been shaped by a series of boundary demarcations and treaties with neighboring powers, including Russia and British India.

## Political Status and Governance
Afghanistan is a country where Islam is paramount. The influence of Islam in the region is characterized by a fusion of religious and secular power, resulting in a state where the Moslem Church is a Moslem state. This system is characterized by the autocracy of the ruler, the theoretical equality of Moslems, polygamy, and slavery. In this political structure, Moslems hold a superior status to non-Moslems.

Historically, the nation has been governed by various dynasties, including the Barakzai. Dost Mahommed Khan, the founder of the Barakzai dynasty, was born in 1793. During his reign, he controlled Ghazni and Kabul, the latter being the richest of the Afghan provinces. His tenure was marked by conflicts with Ranjit Singh, the Sikh ruler of the Punjab, and complex diplomatic relations with England and Russia. Between 1839 and 1840, British forces advanced through the Bolan Pass to Kandahar and Kabul, leading to the proclamation of Shah Shuja as amir and the eventual capture of Dost Mahommed.

## Territorial Boundaries
The political entity of Afghanistan has been defined through the demarcation of its boundaries during a period of boundary-making in Asia starting around 1880.

### Northern and Western Borders
The northern limits were established by the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886 and a subsequent commission in 1895. The boundary between Afghanistan and Russia is defined as follows:
* **Western Section:** The line begins at Zulfikar on the Hari Rud (the river of Herat), located approximately 70 miles south of Sarakhs. The most southerly point of this boundary crosses the Kushk about 60 miles north of Herat.
* **Northern Section:** From the junction at Khamiab—roughly 150 miles above the Russian Trans-Caspian railway crossing at Charjui—the main channel of the Oxus river serves as the northern boundary, separating Afghanistan from Russia. This continues to the river's source in Victoria Lake of the Great Pamir.
* **Eastern Section:** The 1895 Anglo-Russian Commission extended the line to the glaciers and snowfields overlooking the Chinese border.

### Southern and Eastern Borders
The southern and eastern boundaries were defined by several minor commissions based on the Kabul agreement of 1893. This process continued for nearly four years, concluding with the Mohmand settlement following an expedition in 1897.

## Regional Geography and Influence
Afghanistan's geography is defined by significant mountain systems. A definite but irregular mountain system connects the water-divide north of Herat with the southern abutment of the Hindu Kush near Bamian. The value of the Pamirs and Herat has been assessed as bases of aggression.

The country shares borders with Persia, Russia, and Baluchistan. In the north-east, the Deggán tribe was once spread across the region but is now found only in Laghman and Kunar. To the south, the border with Baluchistan is marked by the Afghan-Baluch frontier, where a railway reaches New Chaman.

## Relations with British India
Afghanistan has had extensive political interactions with British India. Under the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, Afghanistan ceded certain tracts of land to the British government. These areas, which had originally been Afghan, include the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts were formally declared part of British India in 1887 and now form an official province of British Baluchistan. Additionally, there exists a sphere of British influence along the border of Afghanistan.
