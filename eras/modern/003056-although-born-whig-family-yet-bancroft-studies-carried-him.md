# George Bancroft

George Bancroft (1800–1891) was a prominent American statesman and historian. Born on October 3, 1800, in Worcester, Massachusetts, he was the son of Aaron Bancroft, a clergyman, author, and distinguished revolutionary soldier. His family had a presence in America dating back to 1632.

## Education and Early Career
Bancroft received an extensive education, attending Phillips Academy in Exeter and Harvard University. He furthered his studies in Europe at the universities of Berlin, Heidelberg, and Goettingen. While at Goettingen, he studied natural science with Blumenbach, New Testament Greek with Eichhorn, and Plato with Heeren. Bancroft was particularly influenced by Heeren, whom he regarded as the greatest living historical critic and a forerunner of the modern school; from Heeren, Bancroft adopted an enthusiasm for meticulous and painstaking erudition.

Following his studies, Bancroft took a European tour where he met numerous distinguished figures in art, science, and letters, including Hegel, Goethe, Humboldt, Byron, Schleiermacher, Niebuhr, Bunsen, Savigny, Cousin, Constant, and Manzoni. 

Upon returning to the United States in 1822, Bancroft attempted to enter the ministry, as his father was a Unitarian. However, his early experiments at preaching were unsatisfactory, as he felt the theological teaching of the era had replaced faith with literature and criticism. He subsequently became a tutor at Harvard. During this time, he was often ridiculed for his "knight-errant" manner and his dissatisfaction with Harvard's pedantic spirit and narrow curriculum. In 1823, he published a volume of original pieces and translations of poetry, though it did not bring him fame.

## Political Career
Despite being born into a Whig family, Bancroft's studies led him to join the Democratic party. While operating his own school, he was elected to the state legislature as a Democrat, though he refused to serve due to pressure from the ardent Whig family of his first wife. He similarly declined a nomination for secretary of state from the Massachusetts Democrats in 1831.

Bancroft eventually became an influential figure within the Democratic party. President Van Buren appointed him collector of the port of Boston, a role he performed successfully; his appointees in this position included Nathaniel Hawthorne and Orestes Brownson. Although he ran as the Democratic candidate for governor in 1844, he was defeated.

In 1845, Bancroft joined the cabinet of President Polk as secretary of the navy, a post he held until 1846, during which time he also served as acting secretary of war for one month. His achievements during this cabinet tenure included:
* Establishing the naval academy at Annapolis.
* Issuing the orders that resulted in the occupation of California.
* Sending Zachary Taylor into the debatable land between Mexico and Texas.
* Advocating for the annexation of Texas as a means of extending "the area of freedom."

Additionally, Bancroft established himself as an authority on the North-Western Boundary question and maintained a high moral position regarding slavery despite his party affiliation.

In 1846, he served as minister to London, where he maintained a constant companionship with Hallam and Macaulay. After returning in 1849, he withdrew from public life to reside in New York. He later returned to public service when Congress chose him in 1866 to deliver a special eulogy for Lincoln. From 1867 until his resignation in 1874, he served as minister to Berlin. In his final years, he lived in Newport and Washington, where he died on January 17, 1891. His later official achievements included winning a case with skill and versatility in the San Juan arbitration and negotiating naturalization treaties with the north German states and Prussia. These treaties provided the first international recognition of the right of expatriation.

## Historical Works and Legacy
Bancroft is ranked as the foremost historian of the United States. His work is characterized by vivid generalizations, acute insight, and a philosophic spirit. He was known for his rigorous approach to style, often rewriting chapters and testing descriptions in up to eight different forms. While some critics noted that he occasionally lacked representative power or used artificial tones, he was regarded as a man of common-sense and sound maxims who valued the past without being a dreamer.

His literary contributions include:
* A translation of Heeren's work, *The Politics of Ancient Greece*.
* Frequent contributions to Walsh's *American Quarterly* and the *North American Review*.
* An 1835 article in the *North American Review* titled "The Documentary History of the Revolution," which introduced the reading public to a new method and the wealth of available material.
* The *History of the United States*, the first volume of which appeared in 1834 and the second in 1837.

His historical volumes were highly successful, seeing large sales in the U.S., reprints in England, and translations into French, German, Italian, and Danish. His academic standing was recognized internationally; the universities of Munich, Heidelberg, and Berlin provided a testimonial of regard, and the Royal Academy honored him as a guest at a public dinner. Until the end of his life, he remained the unofficial dean of the best society and a confidential advisor to statesmen in Washington.
