# Jacques Pierre Brissot

Jacques Pierre Brissot (1754–1793), who also assumed the name de Warville, was a celebrated French Girondist. Born in January 1754 in Chartres to a father who was an inn-keeper, Brissot received a good education and later entered the office of a lawyer in Paris.

## Early Works and Journalism
Brissot first gained recognition as an able and facile writer. His initial publications focused on the philosophy of law, specifically the *Théorie des lois criminelles* (1781) and the *Bibliothèque philosophique du législateur* (1782). These works demonstrated that he was thoroughly imbued with the ethical precepts of Rousseau. Brissot dedicated his first work to Voltaire, who received it with much favor.

Following these publications, Brissot was engaged as a writer for several papers, including the *Mercure* and the *Courrier de l'Europe*.

## Humanitarian Efforts and Political Struggles
Ardently devoted to the service of humanity, Brissot projected a scheme to create a general concourse of all the savants in Europe. To serve as the organ for their views, he started a paper in London titled *Journal du Lycée de Londres*. This plan was unsuccessful.

Upon returning to Paris, Brissot was imprisoned in the Bastille on charges of publishing a work against the government. He was released after four months and returned to pamphleteering, though he was soon forced to retire to London for a period of time.

## Abolitionism and Later Activities
During his second visit to London, Brissot became acquainted with leading Abolitionists. This influence led him to found the Société des Amis des Noirs in Paris, serving as its president during 1790 and 1791. In his capacity as an agent for this society, Brissot visited the United States in 1788. He later published a three-volume work regarding this journey in 1791 titled *Nouveau Voyage dans les États-Unis de l'Amerique Septentrionale*.
