# Cheques and Banking Law

In the United Kingdom, the cheque serves as the primary mechanism for settling large transactions, functioning through a system of ledger transfers. A cheque is defined under Section 73 as a bill of exchange drawn on a banker and payable on demand. While most laws applicable to bills payable on demand apply to cheques, specific rules exist due to the unique legal relationship between the banker (the drawee) and the customer (the drawer).

## The Banker-Customer Relationship
The relationship between a banker and a customer is fundamentally one of debtor and creditor. Superadded to this is the banker's obligation to honor a customer's cheques, provided there is a sufficient and available balance. If a banker dishonors a cheque despite having sufficient funds, they are liable to the customer for substantial damages, even if no actual injury is proven.

A customer is legally entitled to draw on their account via cheques; however, the right to overdraw the account can only arise from a specific agreement. Furthermore, a banker's authority to pay these cheques is terminated by the customer's death, insanity, bankruptcy, or notice of an available act of bankruptcy. Authority may also be terminated by a countermand of payment. For such a revocation to be valid, it must be unequivocal in its method and terms and come to the banker's conscious knowledge; for example, a banker is not bound to act upon an unconfirmed telegram.

## Crossing and Protection of Bankers
To facilitate safe transmission by post and guard against fraud, a system of "crossing" is used, which ensures cheques are paid only through specific channels. Under the Bills of Exchange Act 1883 (Sections 76 to 82), there are two primary types of crossing:
*   **General Crossing:** Indicated by two parallel lines with the words "& Co." written between them. Such cheques cannot be paid over the counter and must be presented by a banker.
*   **Special Crossing:** Indicated by adding the name of a specific banker, meaning the cheque can only be presented through that particular banker.

Both types may further be marked "not negotiable." If a banker pays a crossed cheque in accordance with its ostensible tenor, they obtain protection under Section 80 and the proviso to Section 79. Conversely, if a banker pays a crossed cheque contrary to the crossing, they are liable to the true owner for any resulting loss and may be unable to charge the customer for the amount.

## Qualified Immunity and Negligence
Under Section 82, a banker is accorded qualified immunity when collecting crossed cheques for a customer. To qualify for this protection, the banker must meet several conditions:
1.  The cheque and the money must be received from a customer with whom the banker has existing and definite business relations.
2.  The cheque must be already crossed (generally or specially) to the banker; a crossing made by the banker themselves under Section 77 is inefficacious.
3.  The banker must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care in the interest of the true owner. To avoid a finding of negligence, the banker must ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct and verify authority for endorsements made *per procuration*. Furthermore, as established in *Bevan v. National Bank* and *Hannan's Lake View Central Ld. v. Armstrong & Co.*, a banker must not take a cheque for a private account if the face of the document indicates the holder possesses it in an official capacity, as an agent, or for partnership purposes. Additionally, *Bevan v. National Bank* stipulates that a banker must not take a cheque marked "account payee" for any account other than the one indicated.

The House of Lords in *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903) clarified that this protection only applies while the banker acts as an agent or "conduit pipe" for the customer. If a banker treats the cheque as their own—such as by crediting cheques as cash in the bank's books before the money is actually received—they are viewed as the owner or transferee and lose the protection of Section 82.

## Other Legal Aspects of Cheques
Under the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, a cheque is not an assignment of funds. Consequently, if payment is refused, the holder of the cheque has no claim against the banker, but must seek remedy from the drawer or endorser. This legal standing supports the English banking custom of refusing to pay a partial amount of a cheque if funds are insufficient for the full amount, though this does not apply in Scotland.

Regarding the timing of payments, the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 (Section 13, subs. 1) states that a bill is not invalid because it is post-dated. A banker cannot refuse payment simply because they know a cheque was post-dated if it is presented after its apparent date. However, they are bound to refuse payment if it is presented before the apparent date.
