# The Politics of Count Taaffe and the Czech National Movement

The political landscape of Austria during the late 19th century was defined by the struggle for national rights among Slav races, particularly the Czechs and Poles, and the governing strategies of Count Taaffe. Serving as first minister, Taaffe navigated a fractured parliament by employing a policy of "unpolitical politics," which sought to maintain stability by balancing competing interests and limiting contentious legislation.

## The Rise of the "Iron Ring"
Taaffe's ascent to power was predicated on the failure of the constitutionalists and Liberals. After the Liberals voted against the government on a military bill, Taaffe turned to the Right for support. He formed a coalition known as the "iron ring," consisting of Clericals, Poles, and Czechs. This combination allowed him to govern against the German Liberals, who found themselves in a minority within a parliament they had originally created.

To secure this position, Taaffe engaged in private negotiations to bring previously excluded groups into the fold. He persuaded Bohemian feudal proprietors to grant seats to the Feudalists and reached an agreement with the Czechs. Consequently, the Czechs abandoned their policy of "passive resistance," taking their seats in the diet at Prague in 1878 and subsequently in the parliament at Vienna. While they took the required oath without reservation, Rieger read a formal reservation of right on the first day of the session, and the emperor noted that they had entered without prejudice to their convictions.

## Czech National Aspirations and Internal Divisions
Despite the political concessions of the Taaffe era, the Czechs remained focused on their primary objective: the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom. Over time, a divide grew between the "Old Czechs," whose leaders were aging and potentially satisfied with their attained influence, and the "Young Czechs," who were more impatient for results.

Hopes for the recognition of the kingdom rose when Count Thun was appointed governor of Bohemia, as he was believed to favor the coronation of the emperor at Prague. However, in 1890, Taaffe attempted to facilitate a reconciliation between opposing parties rather than proceeding with the coronation. Taaffe's broader strategy was to diminish the influence of parliament by playing different parties against one another to clear a field for the government.

In January 1890, conferences were held in Vienna with representatives of the German party, the Czechs, and the Feudal party. An agreement was reached regarding a revision of the electoral law and a separation between German and Czech districts. However, the resulting bills failed in the diet because the Young Czechs had been excluded from the conference. They opposed the measures on the grounds that they threatened the unity of the Bohemian country. By the 1891 elections, the Young Czechs achieved a complete victory over the Old Czechs.

## Language and Education
A central point of contention in the empire was the use of language in public life and education. The constitution of 1867 established that all races had an inviolable right to preserve their nationality and language, recognizing the equality of "customary" (*landesueblich*) languages.

Under Taaffe, the minister of education, Prazak, granted the demand that Slavs be provided with their own schools even when they were a small minority in a town, rather than being compelled to attend German schools. This was particularly significant as Czech populations increased in towns and manufacturing districts due to economic migration. While Germans viewed German as the *Kultur-sprache* and hoped education would lead to Germanization, the Czechs used political liberty and education to reassert their national individuality. This tension extended to the "external service," where the Czechs claimed their language should be recognized as customary even in almost completely German districts, such as Reichenberg.

## The End of the Taaffe Era
Taaffe maintained his position for fourteen years by standing between the Right and the possibility of a Liberal government. However, his tenure ended in October 1893 following a bold attempt at electoral reform. Taaffe proposed a bill to grant the franchise to everyone who could read and write and had resided in one place for six months, while maintaining the *curiae* for chambers of commerce and feudal proprietors.

This move was opposed by the Liberals, who feared the growth of socialism and anti-Semitism. Crucially, Taaffe failed to consult his key ally, Hohenwart, and the Conservatives refused to support a measure that transferred power from the well-to-do to the indigent. Additionally, the Poles opposed the bill because it would increase the influence of the Ruthenes. Facing a hopeless position, Taaffe resigned.

Following the Taaffe era, parliamentary life in Austria became paralyzed by a feud between Germans and Czechs, exacerbated by the Badeni language ordinances of 1897. By the close of the 19th century, the government was largely carried on by cabinets of officials using the emergency clause (paragraph 14) of the constitution, leaving political life at a low ebb.
