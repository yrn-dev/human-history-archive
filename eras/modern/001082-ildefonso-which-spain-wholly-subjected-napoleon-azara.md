# The Treaty of San Ildefonso and Don Jose Nicholas de Azara

## Don Jose Nicholas de Azara
Don Jose Nicholas de Azara (1731–1804) was a Spanish diplomatist born in Barbunales, Aragon. His career included several high-level appointments, serving as the procurator-general and Spanish agent in 1765, and later as the ambassador at Rome in 1785. During his tenure in Rome, Azara was recognized as a patron of art and a collector of Italian antiquities. He was an active diplomat who played a leading role in the hazardous task of expelling the Jesuits from Spain and helped secure the election of Pius VI.

When the French took possession of Rome in 1798, Azara withdrew to Florence, though he continued to act on behalf of the pope during his exile and following the pope's death at Valence in 1799. Azara eventually served as the Spanish ambassador in Paris.

## The Treaty of San Ildefonso
While serving as ambassador in Paris, Azara was compelled by his government to lead the negotiations for the treaty of San Ildefonso. Signed on October 1, 1800, this treaty resulted in Spain being wholly subjected to Napoleon. Although Azara was personally friendly toward a French alliance, his experiences led him to believe that Spain was being sacrificed to Napoleon. Napoleon, serving as First Consul at the time, found Azara easy to influence and liked him personally.

The treaty of San Ildefonso was not ratified by Charles IV of Spain. However, the treaty of Madrid, signed on March 21, 1801, confirmed the terms of San Ildefonso and was signed by Charles IV on October 15, 1802. One significant outcome of these diplomatic shifts was the retrocession of Louisiana to France in 1800, a territory that France had previously ceded to Spain in 1762.

Azara died in Paris in 1804, worn out and embittered by the realization of the ills the French alliance would bring to Spain.

## Napoleon's Influence over Spain
Following the treaty and the subsequent years of French dominance, Napoleon continued to manipulate the destiny of Spain. In May 1808, Napoleon appointed his brother, Joseph, as the King of Spain. This appointment followed the treatment of the de facto king, Ferdinand VII. 

Joseph's reign was largely titular and fraught with difficulty. Despite a proclamation of benevolent intentions issued from Bayonne on June 23, 1808, reconciliation between the French and the Spaniards proved impossible. Joseph was forced to leave Madrid in August 1808 due to Spanish success at Baylen, though Napoleon reinstated him by the end of the year. Joseph remained in a subordinate position, offering to abdicate on four separate occasions, though Napoleon refused these offers and demanded more energetic governance.

By 1810, Napoleon further diminished Joseph's authority by placing the north-eastern and northern provinces under the command of French generals as military districts. In late 1811, Napoleon united Catalonia with France. Joseph's position remained precarious until July 22, 1812, when Wellington's victory at Salamanca forced him to leave the capital.

## Resistance and the Peninsular War
The French occupation of Spain led to widespread conflict. Spain rose against Napoleon in 1808, initiating the Peninsular War. A British army led by Wellington gradually pushed the French forces northward out of the peninsula. During this period, the French attempted to impose a new constitution on Spain, drafted with the assistance of the duc de Bassano (Maret), but the Spaniards rejected it.

## Related Figures
Don Jose Nicholas de Azara had a younger brother, Don Felix de Azara (1746–1811). Felix spent twenty years as a commissioner in South America, where he worked to delimit the boundary between Portuguese and Spanish territories. He conducted observations on the natural history of the region and wrote an account of the history and discovery of Rio de la Plata and Paraguay. These findings were published in Paris in 1809 in the work *Voyage dans l'Amerique meridionale depuis 1781 jusqu'en 1801*.
