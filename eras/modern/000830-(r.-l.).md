## Mathematical Computation of Easter
In the context of computing the date of Easter Sunday for any year following the Reformation, specific variables are used to determine the number of days from March 21st to the holiday. In this system, $L$ represents the dominical letter. 

To find the value of $p$ (the number of days), the formula $p = P + (L - l)$ is employed. The value of $l$ is determined based on the epact ($E$):
*   If $E < 24$, then $l = 27 - E$, or $((27 - E) / 7)_r$.
*   If $E > 23$, then $l = 57 - E$, or $((57 - E) / 7)_r$.

Because the value of $L - l$ cannot be zero or negative, 7 must be added to $L$ as often as necessary to ensure a positive whole number. For example, in the year 1840, the dominical letter $L$ is calculated as 4, which corresponds to the letter D. With an epact ($E$) of 26, the value of $l$ is calculated as 3. Using the formula $p = 28 + (4 - 3)$, the result is $p = 29$.

## Structural Engineering and Deflection

The method involves drawing a curve using arcs of length $l_1, l_2, l_3$, etc., with corresponding radii $r_1, r_2, r_3$. The actual deflection of the bridge ($V$) is then determined by the measured deflection of the curve ($v$) using the formula $V = av/b$, where $b$ is a constant chosen to allow the arcs to be drawn with available tools.

## Linguistic Evolution in Eastern Languages
In the study of Middle Prakrit (Mg. Pr.), the letters $r$ and $l$ are central to the phonetic shifts between western and eastern dialects. A typical characteristic of Mg. Pr. is that the western Prakrit $r$ becomes $l$. 

Historically, in Sanskrit, western Prakrits, and modern western languages, $r$ is a cerebral letter with a cerebral sound. In contrast, $l$ has always been a dental letter. In modern eastern languages, the $r$ is a dental letter with a dental sound. This indicates that the shift from western Pr. $r$ to Mg. Pr. $l$ involved the western $r$ losing its cerebral sound to become a dental letter.

In the Bihari language, $r$ and $l$ are frequently confounded with each other or with $n$ (another dental letter). Examples of this phonetic overlap include:
*   The words *kali* or *kari* (meaning black).
*   The words *phar* or *phal* (meaning fruit).
*   The Sanskrit *rajju-* becoming the Bengali *leju-ri* (meaning a string).
*   The town name *Lakhnaur* being commonly pronounced as *Nakhlaul*.
