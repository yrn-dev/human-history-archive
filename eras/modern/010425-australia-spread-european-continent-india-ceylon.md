# Global Expansion and Regional Characteristics: Australia, Europe, India, and Ceylon

## Colonial and Religious Expansion
During the third quarter of the eighteenth century, Europe experienced a period of instability and division, lacking a unifying religious or political idea. However, the availability of printed maps, books, and new ocean-going shipping allowed Western Europeans to dominate global coasts. This led to the peopling of America and the designation of New Zealand, Australia, and South Africa as prospective homes for European populations.

In the realm of religious organization, the Salvation Army—founded by William Booth (born April 10, 1829)—expanded its operations globally. After establishing itself in London and evolving from the East London Revival Society and the East London Christian Mission, the organization expanded to the United States in 1880 and to Australia in 1881. From there, it spread to India, Ceylon, and the European continent.

## Trade and Political Influence in India and Ceylon
European powers struggled for political and commercial footholds in India and the East following Vasco da Gama's voyage to Calicut. Initially, the Portuguese seized control of the sea trade from Red Sea Arabs, making Lisbon a primary mart for oriental spices. In the seventeenth century, the Dutch challenged this monopoly, establishing twelve settlements in India, six in Ceylon, and stations throughout the East Indies, as well as at the Cape of Good Hope and Mauritius.

By the eighteenth century, the English and French entered vigorous competition with the Dutch. The English established headquarters at Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras, while the French focused on Chandernagore and Pondicherry.

## Geography and Natural History
### Flora and Fauna
The vegetation of the Malayan Islands largely mirrors the hotter, wetter regions of India. However, the proximity of the Australian continent has allowed for the diffusion of Australian plant types. For example, the casuarina, a characteristic Australian form, is abundant on the eastern mountains of Java, and some temperate Australian forms appear on the higher mountains of Borneo. Australian genera also extend to southern China and the Philippines. Additionally, the plant Hop (_Humulus Lupulus_) has been introduced into the Himalayas, Australia, and Brazil.

In terms of avian species, the kite _M. isurus_ is peculiar to Australia. Other related species include the common pariah kite of India (_M. govinda_) and _M. affinis_, which is found in Burma, Ceylon, and some Malay countries. Australia is also home to the black-winged kite congeners _E. axillaris_ and _E. scriptus_.

### Cartography and Surveying
A trigonometrical survey of British India began in 1800, resulting in a survey comparable to those of most European states. Since 1862, these surveys have extended beyond the borders of India proper. An _Indian Atlas_ (begun in 1827) includes the Malay Peninsula and Ceylon. While India has an official map (1903) and provincial maps including Burma, Ceylon and the Straits Settlements maintain their own surveyors-general.

Historically, early geographical knowledge was limited; the map of Eratosthenes, adopted by Strabo (c. 30 B.C.), extended in the extreme east only to the Sacae (Kirghiz), India, and Taprobane (Ceylon).

## Cultural and Religious History
### Buddhism
The spread of Buddhism was significantly influenced by Káshmir, which served as a headquarters for a particular school of the doctrine. From Káshmir, Buddhism penetrated Bactria, Kabul, and Kandahar. Tibetan Buddhism also originated from Káshmir. The importance of this region is highlighted by the account of King Dutthagamini of Ceylon (B.C. 157), who consecrated the great Tope at Ruanwelli with the assistance of 280,000 priests from Káshmir. Consequently, Buddhists in Burma look to Ceylon with a similar reverence that Mongols and Lamas once held for Káshmir.

### Science and Intellectual Exchange
In ancient times, India studied grammar and prosody with great minuteness several centuries before Christ. Mathematics were cultivated by Indians, Arabs, and the Chinese. While the influence of India was mainly directed toward the East, early Greek philosophy and Alexandrine ideas suggest some Indian affinities, though no clear connection exists. In contrast, Europe's primary influence was felt from western Asia.
