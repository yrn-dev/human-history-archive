# Spanish Colonial and Global Activity

## Conquest of the Americas
In the 16th century, Spanish forces expanded into the Americas, notably in Mexico and Peru. In Mexico, Cortes arrived at the capital city, built on an immense lake, accompanied by a handful of Spaniards and 6,000 natives. Initially received with pomp by Montezuma, Cortes fortified himself in a palace. After a Mexican general attacked the garrison at Vera Cruz and sent the head of a Spaniard to the capital, Cortes seized Montezuma, demanding the delivery of the general and his officers, whom he burned alive. Cortes forced Montezuma to acknowledge himself a vassal of Charles V, receiving 600,000 marks of pure gold and many precious stones in return.

In Peru, the conqueror Pizarro moved inland from San Miguel in September 1532 toward Caxamarca. He encountered Atahuallpa, the Inca who had previously defeated his brother Huascar in a civil war. On November 16, Atahuallpa entered the square of Caxamarca with five or six thousand men, most of whom were unarmed or carried only slings and short clubs. Pizarro’s soldiers and artillery were positioned in the streets. A priest, Vicente de Valverde, demanded that Atahuallpa become a Christian, obey the pope, and pay tribute to Charles V.

Following the conquest of Peru, the native populations faced significant tyranny and oppression. While the Spanish home government and the council of the Indies attempted to implement protective regulations to prevent ill-usage and serfdom, these were largely ignored by mine-owners and settlers. Although some claims suggest eight million Indians died from forced labor in mines, evidence suggests the population decline was primarily caused by alcohol and European epidemics.

## Caribbean and Atlantic Settlements
Spanish activity extended to the islands of the Caribbean. In 1641, the Spaniards expelled the British from a settlement in New Providence. Later, in July 1703, Spanish and French forces launched a descent on New Providence, where they spiked the guns, blew up the fort, burnt the church, and transported the governor and principal inhabitants to Havana. The Spaniards returned in October for a second descent to complete the destruction of the island.

## European and Mediterranean Engagements
Spanish involvement in European and Mediterranean affairs spanned several centuries. In ancient history, a Spaniard named Abelox in Saguntum betrayed Hannibal by delivering the children of distinguished Spanish families—who had been taken as hostages—to the Romans.

In the Netherlands, Philip II, King of Spain and sovereign of the Netherlands, was characterized as an autocratic and rigid bigot who believed in the superiority of Catholicism. His inability to understand the Belgian love of liberty and his slow decision-making process led to various disasters in the theater of war. Additionally, records indicate that during a war at Acre, approximately 20,000 men were slain, the majority of whom were Spaniards and Genoese.

## Relations with Japan
In the early 17th century, the Tokugawa chief, Ieyasu, sought to develop oversea trade and a mercantile marine. He offered the Spaniards, along with the English, Dutch, and Portuguese, sites for settlements in a suburb of Yedo. Ieyasu sent envoys to Manila to request expert naval architects and to urge the opening of trade near Yedo. 

However, tensions existed; Xavier had previously warned Charles V that the Japanese were as warlike as they were covetous and could capture any hostile fleet. By 1609, while Ieyasu hoped both the Dutch and Spaniards would open trade, his expectations were only met by the Dutch. A Spanish embassy from Manila arrived around the same time as the Dutch vessel *Brack* to settle matters regarding the Hollanders.

## Liberation of South America
By the early 19th century, Spanish possession in South America was challenged by Simon Bolivar. After the Spaniards were expelled from Colombia, Bolivar sought to liberate Ecuador and Peru. In June 1822, the republicans entered Quito after the prowess of Sucre routed the Spaniards at the battle of Pichincha. Bolivar then entered Lima in triumph and was invested as dictator with absolute power.

Following a temporary withdrawal to Truxillo due to republican intrigues in Peru, Bolivar reorganized his army. On August 6, 1824, he defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin. Sucre subsequently pursued the royalists into Upper Peru, achieving a decisive victory at Ayacucho. The remaining Spanish forces were confined to the castles of Callao, which were maintained by Rodil for over a year. In 1825, Upper Peru became the separate state of Bolivia, named in honor of Bolivar, who was declared its perpetual protector.
