# The Treaty of San Stefano and the Berlin Treaty

## Overview of the Treaty of San Stefano
The Treaty of San Stefano was signed on March 3, 1878, following the victorious advance of the Russian army to Constantinople. The treaty was the result of Russian intervention in 1877, which occurred after the Christian races in the Balkans, specifically the Bulgarians, became restless and insurgent, leading to violent repressive measures and large-scale massacres of Bulgarians by the Turks. 

The treaty is described as having been a sensible agreement that aimed to establish a "natural map" by breaking up the artificial Turkish Empire. It largely realized the national aspirations of the Bulgarian race by including all provinces of European Turkey where the Bulgarian element predominated into an autonomous principality.

## Territorial Extent of "Big Bulgaria"
Under the terms of the Treaty of San Stefano, the newly created autonomous Bulgarian principality was extensive, covering three-fifths of the Balkan Peninsula with a population of 4,000,000 inhabitants. Its borders extended:
*   **North to South:** From the Danube to the Aegean.
*   **East to West:** From the Black Sea to the Albanian mountains.

This territory enclosed the districts of Pirot and Vranya, as well as Kastoria, Dibra, and Ochrida (the ancient capital of the Shishmans). The state also possessed a Mediterranean port at Kavala. However, certain areas were excluded: Adrianople, Salonica, and the Chalcidian peninsula remained with Turkey, and the Dobrudja was reserved for Rumania as compensation for the Russian annexation of Bessarabia, despite the Dobrudja's Bulgarian population.

## The Treaty of Berlin and European Intervention
The Great Powers intervened because they anticipated that such an extensive territory would become a dependency of Russia. British policy, under the premiership of Lord Beaconsfield, specifically sought to thwart Russian designs. This led to the assembly of a conference in Berlin in 1878 to revise the Treaty of San Stefano, primarily to benefit the Austrian and Turkish monarchies.

On July 13, 1878, the Treaty of Berlin was signed, which effectively divided the "Big Bulgaria" of San Stefano into three portions:
1.  **The Principality of Bulgaria:** An autonomous and tributary principality under the suzerainty of the sultan of Turkey from 1878 until October 5, 1908.
2.  **Eastern Rumelia:** An autonomous province lying south of the Balkans.
3.  **Turkish Administration:** A remaining portion including part of the vilayet of Adrianople and almost the entire region of Macedonia.

Additionally, the Treaty of Berlin sanctioned the transfer of the Dobrudja to Rumania and gave the districts of Nish, Pirot, and Vranya to Servia.

## Frontiers and Ethnological Limits
The boundaries established by the Treaty of Berlin were considered artificial and did not correspond with the ethnological limits of the Bulgarian race. According to impartial authorities, Bulgarian populations mainly inhabited large tracts of the Dobrudja, the northern half of the vilayet of Adrianople, a considerable portion of Macedonia, and the Servian districts of Vranya and Pirot.

The resulting frontier of the Bulgarian kingdom (covering 37,240 sq. m.) was defined as follows:
*   **North:** The Danube, from the Timok confluence to the eastern suburbs of Silistria, then a line to the Black Sea coast 10 m. south of Mangalia.
*   **East:** The Black Sea.
*   **South:** The Turkish frontier began 12 m. south of Sozopolis, running south-west across the river Maritza at Mustafa Pasha to the Arda at Adakali. While the Berlin Treaty originally followed the Arda to Ishiklar and the Rhodope crest, the cantons of Rupchus and Krjali were restored to Turkey in 1886. The subsequent frontier passed north of these districts to the Rhodope watershed and followed the Rilska Planina crest to the summit of Tchrni Vrkh.
*   **West:** From Tchrni Vrkh (where Bulgarian, Turkish, and Servian territories meet), the frontier passed northwards, leaving Pirot to the west and Trn to the east, reaching the Timok near Kula and following it to the Danube.

## Historical Context of the Balkan Peninsula
The ethnographic composition of the region was shaped by the great Slavonic immigration beginning in the 3rd century A.D. By the 7th century, Serbo-Croats invaded north-western regions, including Montenegro, Servia, Bosnia, Herzegovina, northern Albania, and Croatia. In the late 7th century, the Bulgars (a Turanian race) crossed the Danube and subjected the Slavonic inhabitants of Thrace and Moesia. Under Tsar Krum (802-815), they invaded central Macedonia and Adrianople; under Simeon (893-927), the empire extended from the Black Sea to the Adriatic.
