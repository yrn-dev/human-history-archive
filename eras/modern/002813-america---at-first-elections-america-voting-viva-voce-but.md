# America

## Early Colonial Governance and Settlement
The early political foundations of America were shaped by liberal ideas of the middle eighteenth century, which were crystallized in the United States constitution. This system dispensed with the crown and state-church, rejected titles, and provided strong protections for property as a means of ensuring freedom. Among the early settlers was John Carver, born probably in Nottinghamshire, England, around 1575. After seeking refuge from religious persecution in Holland around 1607, where he served as a deacon in the church at Leiden, Carver emigrated to America in 1620 aboard the "Mayflower." He founded the Plymouth colony and served as its first governor, having been elected before leaving England and confirmed after the signing of the "Compact." He was re-elected on March 23, 1620 (1621 N.S.), but died in early April from sunstroke.

## Evolution of Voting Methods
At the start of American elections, voting was conducted *viva voce* (by voice). However, several colonies transitioned early to the use of printed or written ballots. By 1775, ballots were utilized in Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, South Carolina, and the New England states. New Jersey adopted ballots in 1776, followed by New York in 1778. Consequently, when the United States constitution was adopted, *viva voce* voting remained the standard for public elections only in Georgia, Virginia, and Maryland.

Among the states that later entered the Union, only Arkansas, Missouri, Kentucky, and Illinois lacked a ballot system upon their entry. The transition to ballots continued through the 19th century: Maryland, Georgia, and Arkansas (1846) and Illinois (1848) adopted the system during the first half of the century. Kentucky continued *viva voce* voting until 1819, while Virginia did not provide for ballots until 1869. In Missouri, ballots were introduced in some localities in 1845, though general adoption did not occur until 1863.

## Electoral Challenges and Party Machines
Early voting methods were characterized as barbarically crude. In primitive town meetings, voters wrote their own ballots or used those provided by candidates. As elections developed, political committees for various parties took over the preparation and distribution of ballot-tickets. Because these were prepared as party-lists, voting a mixed ticket was difficult. Furthermore, because voters received ballots within a few feet of the polls, secrecy was nearly impossible, which facilitated frequent bribery and intimidation.

The makers of America lacked knowledge regarding the complexities of vote manipulation and the science of elections. They were unaware of the need for the transferable vote to prevent specialized organizations from "working" the elections. These rigid and crude methods left the political system vulnerable to great party machines.

## Franchise and Education
The creators of political America established a wide franchise for the time. While conditions varied by state—Pennsylvania offered the widest franchise, allowing every adult male taxpayer to vote—the United States was well within sight of manhood suffrage by the end of the eighteenth century, giving a vote to nearly every adult male citizen.

Regarding education, the founders believed that granting land for the maintenance of schools and colleges would be sufficient to let them grow on their own. However, this approach is viewed by some as failing to recognize that education is a delicate crop that requires careful adjustment rather than being a weed that grows in any soil. By the standards of other existing states, the level of common education in America is high, yet it is characterized as an uneducated country when compared to what it might be.

## Press and Public Information
The founders of America believed that leaving the press free would ensure the public lived "in the light." They relied on the privately owned printing press and public meetings to inform citizens of domestic and foreign events. This lack of foresight failed to account for the possibility that a free press could develop a constitutional venality through its relationship with advertisers, allowing large newspaper proprietors to act as "buccaneers of opinion."

## Political Terminology
The term "carpet-bagger" emerged as political slang in America. It originally referred to speculative bankers in the western states who arrived with no property other than what they could carry in a carpet-bag and absconded upon failure. During the reconstruction period following the Civil War, the term became a expression of contempt for northern political adventurers in the South who gained control of the administration with the assistance of the negro vote.
