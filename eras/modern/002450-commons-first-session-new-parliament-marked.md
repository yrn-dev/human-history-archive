# The House of Commons in Early Parliamentary Sessions

The history of the House of Commons is characterized by varying levels of cooperation between the legislature and the crown, often dictated by the prevailing political climate, the influence of royal ministers, and the specific grievances of the members.

## The First Parliament of Charles I
The first session of the new parliament under Charles I was marked by significant tension and a lack of confidence in the king's leadership and his ministers. Charles faced difficulty gaining credit during this session due to engagements entered into under the guidance of Buckingham. These engagements involved an enormous expenditure and a war on the continent, a venture that had never been popular within the House of Commons.

Furthermore, the Commons suspected that the marriage treaty contained engagements they disapproved of. In response to these concerns, the Commons demanded the full execution of laws against Roman Catholics and voted very little money for the crown. The atmosphere worsened before the parliament reassembled at Oxford on August 1st, as English ships had fallen into French hands and were being used against La Rochelle. Consequently, the Commons met in an "ill-humour." They expressed a total lack of confidence in Buckingham and refused to vote a single penny until persons they could trust were admitted to the king's council. Because Charles continued to support his minister, he dissolved this first parliament on August 12th.

## The Second Parliament and the Gunpowder Plot
In contrast to the first session of Charles I, the second parliament experienced a shift in sentiment. The Gunpowder Plot had aroused warmer feelings toward the king among the members of the Commons. This shift in mood led the Commons to grant a triple subsidy and pass severe laws against recusants. Despite this increased warmth toward the monarch, the Commons continued to collect grievances they intended to move.

## Parliamentary Management and Minority Governments
The House of Commons has also been the site of complex management during periods of minority government. In February 1852, Lord Derby formed his first administration while in a minority, eventually dissolving parliament to appeal to the country. Later, after the defeat of Lord Palmerston on the Conspiracy Bill in February 1858, Lord Derby assumed government for a second time. Although he still lacked a working majority, he was able to carry on affairs for a full session. This was achieved primarily through the dexterous management of Mr. Disraeli in the Commons. During this 1858 session, the government successfully passed two significant measures, including a bill to remove disabilities for the Israelites (referred to in the sources as "Jewish disabilities").

## Leadership and Transition in the Commons
The leadership of the Commons has often evolved during sessions of transition. In 1907, during the premiership of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, much of the leadership in the Commons devolved upon the chancellor of the exchequer, Mr. Asquith, due to the prime minister's ill-health. While the party initially viewed Asquith coldly, his sobriety of criticism and common-sense approach became vital. By the opening session of 1908, Asquith acted as the prime minister's deputy. His leadership was emphasized by his introduction of a system of old-age pensions in the budget of May 1908 and his successful exposition of the Licensing Bill in the Commons, though the latter was later rejected by the Lords.

## The Role of the Chancellor of the Exchequer
The House of Commons serves as the primary venue for the management of state finances. The chancellor of the exchequer typically lays a statement before the House in April detailing the actual results of revenue and expenditure from the previous finance year (ending March 31). This includes an estimate of produce for the current year and projected expenditures. If revenue is estimated to be less than expenditure, the chancellor may propose new or increased taxes; conversely, a large surplus may allow for the reduction of taxes. These estimates are divided into "consolidated fund services" (covering the civil list, courts of justice, pensions, and debt charge) and "supply services" (covering the navy, army, revenue departments, civil service, and telegraph and post-office services).
