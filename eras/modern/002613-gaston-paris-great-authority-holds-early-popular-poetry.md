# Gaston Paris and Early Popular Poetry

Gaston Paris, recognized as a great authority in his field, has provided significant insights into the nature and development of early popular poetry. In his work *Histoire poetique de Charlemagne*, Paris posits that early popular poetry is "improvised and contemporary with its facts."

## Analysis of the Dictum in Border Ballads
The application of Paris's dictum to specific historical ballads—such as "Jock o' the Side," "Jamie Telfer," "Kinmont Willie," and "The Bonny Earl o' Murray"—reveals a discrepancy between the theory of contemporary improvisation and the actual content of the poems. When these ballads are compared with contemporary letters, despatches, and specifically Bain's *Border Papers* (1894-1896), it becomes evident that the poets often possessed little knowledge of the events they described, or recorded those facts incorrectly.

In many instances, the facts within these ballads are so distorted and confused that they appear to have been composed in later generations based on erroneous oral traditions. In other cases, such as *The Queen's Marie*, the texts seem to have been defaced over time by the fantastic interpolations of reciters. Evidence suggests that the existing form of most border ballads likely dates no earlier than the generation of 1603-1633, following the union of the crowns. Furthermore, even when ballads were taken from oral recitation, the reciters may have been influenced by printed broadsheets or "stall copies." Even as late as 1750, ballads concerning the sons of Rob Roy remained more or less mythopoeic.

## National Songs and the Epic Stage
In *Histoire poetique de Charlemagne*, Gaston Paris also examines the progression of national songs across different cultures. He notes that in Russia, Lithuania, and Scandinavia, national songs have been arrested in a form that is intermediate between the epic and contemporary poetry. 

Paris distinguishes these from "true epics," which are found in France, Britain, Germany, Greece, Persia, and India. While many of these true epics lack useful points of comparison due to their extreme difference from Homer or a lack of evidence regarding anterior popular songs, the French "chansons de geste" serve as an instructive parallel. The *Chanson de Roland* is cited as the earliest and best example of these poems, which are traced back with probability to the 10th century. These epic works were recited by professional *jongleurs*.

## Academic Influence and Legacy
Gaston Paris's influence extended to the training of other distinguished scholars. Arsene Darmesteter (1846-1888), a noted man of letters and philologist who became a professor of Old French language and literature at the Sorbonne, studied under Paris at the Ecole des Hautes Etudes. Paris also wrote a piece titled "James Darmesteter," published in *Penseurs et poetes* (1896), spanning pages 1-61.
