# Ostend and Regional Sea Level Measurements

## Ostend: Maritime and Strategic Importance
Ostend is one of the two Belgian ports that carry on ocean commerce, the other being Antwerp. In 1904, the total number of vessels entering these two ports was 7,650, with a combined tonnage of 10,330,127. Steamers from Ostend primarily maintain lines with London and Dover; the passenger route to Dover is specifically served by a number of state-owned steamers.

Due to its coastal position, Ostend has served as a strategic point for military operations. In August 1914, the British Admiralty planned to send a brigade of 3,000 Marines to Ostend to delay a southward German advance and create a diversion for the Belgian Army. This operation involved the use of battleships, cruisers, and an aeroplane squadron to cover the disembarkation. The Marines were tasked with occupying the town, establishing an entrenched picket line to cover the potential debarkation of an Army Division, and pushing out reconnaissances toward Thourout, Bruges, and Dixmude.

Despite its utility, some military assessments indicated that it was impossible to supply an army at Antwerp via Ostend and Ghent. Efforts were made by the Belgians to keep open the line connecting Ostend, Bruges, Ghent, St. Nicholas, and Antwerp. Historical records also note the evacuation of Ostend and the subsequent blocking of Ostend Harbor.

## Baltic and North Sea Level Measurements
Hydrographic measurements have been conducted to determine the relative mean levels of various bodies of water. Levellings from Swinemuende indicate that the mean level of the Baltic surface at that location is 0.066 metres (0.216 ft.) below the level of the North Sea at Ostend. Additionally, the surface at Swinemuende is 0.093 metres (0.305 ft.) below the surface of the North Sea at Amsterdam.

The Baltic Sea exhibits a mean level that rises approximately 0.5 metres (1.6 ft.) from Memel to the coast of Holstein, a phenomenon likely caused by prevailing westerly winds. This difference can be reversed or disappear during easterly winds and is exceeded during strong westerly winds.

## Levels from Swinemuende to the Adriatic
A specific line of levels extending from Swinemuende through Eger to the Adriatic Sea was used to compare the Baltic and the Adriatic. These measurements showed that the mean level of the surface of the Baltic is 0.499 metres (1.6 ft.) above that of the Adriatic Sea.

## Regional Geography of the Adriatic and Baltic
The Adriatic coast includes the Italian territorial division known as The Marches (Le Marche), which comprises the provinces of Ascoli Piceno, Macerata, Ancona, and Pesaro and Urbino. Except for the narrow coast strip and river valleys, the general level of this region is more than 500 ft. above the sea. The region is bounded by the Adriatic to the east, the Abruzzi to the south, the Emilia to the north, and Tuscany and Umbria to the west.

In the Baltic region, the drainage area is relatively large, extending to 461,450 sq. sea m. (611,700 sq. English m.). The largest river-basin in this area is the Neva in the east, followed by the Oder and the Vistula in the south. The drainage system of western Finland and Sweden is characterized by narrow parallel troughs at right angles to the coast.

## Tidal and Wave Characteristics
Tides within the Baltic proper are scarcely perceptible, with the range at Copenhagen being about one foot. However, meteorological influences cause a marked annual rise and fall, with a mean range of 13.9 cm. (0.46 ft.) at Swinemuende and 11.4 cm. (0.37 ft.) at Travemuende, peaking in August. The waves of the Baltic are typically irregular and short, which often poses a danger to navigation. Records also mention *Seebaren* (bores), which are destructive waves likely caused by distant earthquakes.
