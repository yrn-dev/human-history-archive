# Kolhapur, Akalkot, Sawantwari, and the Southern Mahratta

Within the Bombay Presidency of British India, the administration of native states was organized into several chief groups. Among these were the southern Mahratta states, which included Kolhapur and other states, the Deccan Satara Jagirs—comprising Akalkot, Bhor, Aundh, Phaltan, Jath, and Daphlapur—and the South Konkan and Dharwar group, which included Sawantwari and Janjira.

## Kolhapur
Kolhapur is a native state located within the Deccan division of Bombay. It is the principal state under the political control of the government of Bombay and is ranked as the fourth most important of the Mahratta principalities, following Baroda, Gwalior, and Indore. Together with its feudatories, or *jagirs*, the state covers an area of 3,165 sq. m. As of 1901, its population was 910,011, and its estimated revenue was £300,000.

### Geography and Economy
The state's territory extends from the heart of the Western Ghats eastward into the Deccan plain. The western region is characterized by picturesque hill slopes and valleys that were, until recently, covered in rich forests and primarily produce timber. This area also contains ancient Mahratta strongholds, specifically Rungna, Bavda, Vishalgarh, and Panhala. The center of the state features low hills running at right angles to the main range, while the east consists of a treeless, well-cultivated plain.

The rivers in the region are fordable during hot months, though boats of 2 tons burthen can navigate them during the rains. While stone quarries are plentiful and iron ore is found in the hills, the local iron smelting industry has been unable to compete with imports from Europe. Principal agricultural products include vegetables, safflower, cotton, tobacco, sugar-cane, millets, and rice.

### Governance and Infrastructure
The rajas of Kolhapur trace their lineage to Raja Ram, a younger son of Sivaji the Great, the founder of Mahratta power. The state's history includes British military expeditions in 1765 and 1792 due to the prevalence of piracy. In the early 19th century, misgovernment by the chief led to further British military operations and the appointment of an officer to manage the state. More recently, the state has been well governed according to British administrative patterns. Raja Shahu Chhatrapati, G.C.S.I., who is entitled to a 21-gun salute, succeeded to the throne by adoption in 1884.

Educational institutions in the state include a high school, Rajaram college, a technical school, an agricultural school, and training schools for mistresses and masters. The town of Kolhapur (also known as Karvir) serves as the terminus for a branch of the Southern Mahratta railway, located 30 miles from the main line. The town had a population of 54,373 in 1901 and contains modern public buildings alongside evidence of antiquity, including Buddhist remains that indicate its history as an important religious center.

## Regional Context and History
The southern Mahratta country and the broader Deccan region are the home of the Mahrattas, who constituted 30% of the Bombay Presidency's population in 1901. The Mahrattas are the dominating race in the province after Europeans, numbering 3,650,000 (including 1,900,000 Kunbis, 350,000 Konkanis, and 1,400,000 not otherwise specified). The primary language of the southern division is a mix of Mahratti and Kanarese.

Historically, the region saw the rise of Mahratta power under Sivaji, which began to make inroads into the kingdom of Bijapur under Mahommed Adil Shah. Later, the Bombay Presidency expanded significantly between 1803 and 1827. Following the defeat and capture of Baji Rao, the last of the peshwas, in 1817-1818, large portions of his dominions—including Sholapur, Belgaum, Dharwar, Poona, Nasik, and Ahmednagar—were incorporated into the presidency.

During the period of the Mutiny, the southern Mahratta country experienced local and isolated rebellions, and outbreaks among troops in Kolhapur were quickly suppressed.
