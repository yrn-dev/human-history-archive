# Herat and the Pamirs

The strategic geography of the region encompassing Herat and the Pamirs has been a subject of significant political and cartographic examination. In the context of practical politics, these areas have been assessed specifically as bases of aggression.

## The Strategic Position of Herat
Herat is situated within a complex mountain system that connects the water-divide north of the city with the southern abutment of the Hindu Kush near Bamian. This irregular mountain system, along with the barrier located between Merv and Herat—which connects to the Elburz of Persia—represents a critical factor in regional politics. 

The political status of the area was further defined by the demarcation of a boundary that gave Afghanistan a new political entity. A boundary was established between the Oxus and the Hari Rud (the river of Herat). This boundary is largely artificial in construction; it leaves the Hari Rud at Zulfikar, approximately 70 miles south of Sarakhs, and reaches its most southerly point about 60 miles north of Herat where it crosses the Kushk.

## The Pamirs: Geography and Terminology
The Pamirs are described not as a single steppe, but as a variety of "Pamirs"—lofty valleys between hill ranges with relatively flat floors, typically no more than five or six miles in width, providing luxuriant summer pasture. The Persian name for the Pamir is *Bam-i-dunya*, meaning "Roof of the World." This description is particularly applicable to the line of water parting between the Aksu and the Sarhadd.

The term "Pamir" is believed to be a Khokandi-Turki word meaning "wilderness," referring to a place that is waste or depopulated yet capable of habitation. According to local guides, when an area was inhabited by Kirghiz, it was known by village names (such as Bas Robát or Chárling); it only became "Pamir" if it were deserted. Some sources, such as Severtsof, suggest that "Pamir" is a generic term for all high plateaux in the Thian Shan.

The region is divided into several distinct areas, including:
* Pamir Kalan (Great Pamir)
* Pamir Khurd (Little Pamir)
* Pamir Alichur
* Pamir Khargosi ("of the hare")
* Pamir Sares
* Pamir Rang-kul

## Political Boundaries and the Sarikol Range
The boundary between Russia and China on the western edge of the Kashgar plains is defined by the meridional range of Sarikol (known in medieval times as Bolor and to the ancients as Taurus). This range consists of two parallel chains:
1. **The Western Chain:** Serves as the water-divide of the Pamirs.
2. **The Eastern Chain:** Also known as the Kandar or Kashgar range, which contains lateral gorges that allow drainage from the eastern Pamir slopes.

The boundary between the Russian trans-Caspian conquests and Afghanistan was determined by the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886. From Khamiab, the main channel of the Oxus river serves as the northern boundary of Afghanistan, separating it from Russia up to the river's source in Victoria Lake of the Great Pamir. Beyond this point, the Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895 demarcated a line extending to the glaciers and snowfields overlooking the Chinese border.

## Historical Exploration and Routes
The Plateau of Pamir was traversed by three Venetians starting around November 1271. After traveling through Hormuz, Kerman, Khorasan, Balkh, and Badakhshan, they ascended the upper Oxus (or Panja) to reach the Pamir highlands before descending toward Kashgar. This specific route was not known to have been followed by another European traveler until Lieutenant John Wood of the Indian Navy in 1838.

Later explorations, such as those by Captain Younghusband in 1891 and Colonel Lockhart's mission, mapped the connections between Little Pamir (at Bozai-Gumbaz) and the Pamir-i-Wakhán via the Wakhijrui Pass. Routes from Wakhán to Little Pamir via the Sarhadd include a winter route over the frozen river and spring/summer routes along the snowy chain of the right bank of the Sarhadd.
