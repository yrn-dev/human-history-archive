# GERMANY: History

## Ancient and Early Period
In the prehistoric era, southern Germany was inhabited by lions, which were larger than the existing form and remained in the region until the Neolithic period. During the era of the Roman Empire, the region was characterized by the presence of various tribes. The Heruli and the Burgundi (or Uregundi) are specifically mentioned in historical accounts, and the Marcomannic war was partially caused by the pressure of barbarous tribes fleeing from more northern barbarians.

The ethnic composition of the region was complex. While Pliny referred to the Bastarnæ as Germans, other sources such as Strabo and Tacitus expressed doubt. Ptolemy and Dion categorized them as Scythians, while Livy, Plutarch, and Diodorus Siculus identified them as Gauls. It is considered probable that the Bastarnæ descended from Gauls who entered Germany under Signoesus, as they were consistently associated with Gaulish tribes like the Taurisci and the Boll rather than German tribes. Additionally, the Carpi are identified as a Suevian tribe that made an irruption into Mæsia in 237 before likely blending with the Ostrogoths.

## The Reformation and Early Modern Era
The history of the Reformation in Germany was significantly influenced by the Colloquy of Marburg (Marburger Religionsgespräch). This conference of divines took place in 1529 and was convened to promote the unity of Protestant Germany.

## The 19th Century and the Habsburg Influence
During the first half of the 19th century, Germany was heavily impacted by the policies of the Austrian statesman Metternich. For thirty years, Metternich imposed a system of "stability" across Europe to protect the Habsburg monarchy, which he viewed as a fragile structure that could be shattered by national aspirations or Liberal principles. To keep Germany quiet and ensure the stability of Austria, Metternich utilized the Vienna Final Act and the Carlsbad Decrees. He also persuaded Frederick William III that granting a popular constitution would be fatal to the Prussian monarchy.

In 1848, the Austrian government agreed to send delegates from its German provinces to the parliament of united Germany, which convened in Frankfort on May 18. This led to a dilemma regarding the place of the Austrian monarchy in a united Germany. The Austrian government refused any settlement that would split the monarchy in half by including only its German provinces. Conversely, German patriots were unwilling to include the vast non-German population of the whole monarchy. Although the crown of Germany was eventually offered to Frederick William of Prussia after the withdrawal of Austrian delegates, the tradition of Austrian hegemony and the shadow of the Holy Empire persisted. Austria eventually re-established the system devised at the Congress of Vienna, aided by the prestige of imperial tradition and Radetzky's victory of Novara, which allowed Austria to back its policy with military force.

## International Relations and the Early 20th Century
By the early 20th century, Germany maintained intimate relations with Austria-Hungary. This alliance was evident during a dispute between France and Germany regarding Morocco. At the Algeciras Conference on March 3, 1906, Austria-Hungary was the only power to side with Germany, and a proposal from the Austro-Hungarian plenipotentiary served as the basis for the final settlement between Germany and France.

This cordial relationship provided the necessary support for Baron von Aerenthal, the successor to Count Goluchowski in the Austrian government. In January 1908, Aerenthal pursued a bold policy in the Balkan Peninsula, undermining an Austro-Russian agreement by obtaining a railway concession from the sultan. This railway was intended to run from the Bosnian frontier through the sanjak of Novibazar to Mitrovitza. This action caused resentment in Servia and offended several great powers, but Aerenthal was able to proceed because he could rely on the support of Germany.

## Geography and Culture
The town of Honnef, located on the right bank of the Rhine at the foot of the Siebengebirge, serves as a climatic health resort. As of 1905, it had a population of 6,183 and featured both a Roman Catholic and an Evangelical church. The town is known for its vineyards, orchards, and a considerable trade in wine. It also possesses a mineral spring known as the Drachenquelle, used for bathing and drinking, and operates a sanatorium for consumptives.

In the field of academia and theology, Berlin and Gottingen were centers of study in the late 19th century. Notable figures such as August (born 1846), a professor of theology and co-director of the theological seminary at Wittenberg, contributed to the intellectual landscape through works on the theological system of Augustinus.
