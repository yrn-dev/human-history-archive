# The Influence of Bacon on the French Encyclopédie

## Relationship to the French Encyclopédie
The classification of the sciences developed by Bacon served as the foundation for the division of the sciences adopted in the great French *Encyclopédie*. This influence is documented in d'Alembert's *Discours* and Diderot's *Prospectus*. In the preliminary discourse of the *Encyclopédie*, d'Alembert describes Bacon as the most eloquent, universal, and greatest of philosophers. Furthermore, the *Encyclopédie* includes a eulogistic article titled "Baconisme."

Beyond the structural classification, Voltaire and the encyclopaedists hailed Bacon as "le pere de la philosophie experimentale" (the father of experimental philosophy). This sentiment was echoed by Condillac, who stated that no one knew the cause of human errors better than Bacon.

## Philosophical Contributions and the New Method
Bacon is closely associated with the theory of induction, the classification of the sciences, and the doctrine of Idols (*Idola*). In the first book of the *Organum*, Bacon examined the obstacles to progress in considerable detail. While this discussion is strictly extraneous to his overall scheme, it is regarded as a most important part of his philosophy.

The term *Idola* was borrowed from Plato, where it refers to the fleeting, transient image of a real thing, contrasting with Platonic Ideas as a "false appearance." Bacon's exploration of these concepts is further explained in book i of the *Advancement*.

## Scientific Impact and Legacy
Bacon's importance in natural science is evidenced by references to his work among principal French, German, and Italian scientists. Figures such as Descartes, Gassendi, S. Sorbiere, and Jean Baptiste du Hamel held him in high regard. Sorbiere specifically credited Bacon with powerfully soliciting the interests of physics and exciting the world to conduct experiments. Other notable thinkers, including Leibnitz and Huygens, provided critical testimony; Leibnitz described him as "divini ingenii vir" (a man of divine genius).

In England, Bacon's *New Atlantis* provided significant impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society. Dr. Thomas Sprat, the society's first historian, asserted that Bacon possessed the "true imagination of the whole extent" of the enterprise and provided the best arguments for the experimental method of natural philosophy.

## Comparison with Other Thinkers
The scheme of classification established by Bacon, and later adopted by the *Encyclopédie*, has been compared with subsequent attempts by Herbert Spencer, Comte, Cournot, and Ampere. 

Additionally, the archive notes a distinction between the *Idola* of Francis Bacon and the *offendicula* of a different Bacon (the 13th-century author of the *Opus Majus*). The *offendicula*—consisting of custom, authority, the opinion of the unskilled many, and the concealment of ignorance with a pretence of knowledge—have sometimes been viewed as an anticipation of the *Idola*, though the two classifications have little in common.
