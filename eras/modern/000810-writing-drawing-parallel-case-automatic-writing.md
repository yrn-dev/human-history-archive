# Writing and Drawing

Writing and drawing represent fundamental instruments for the human mind, providing a means of continuity and an enlargement of the range of mental action. While the development of articulate speech initially overshadowed the earlier achievement of drawing, drawing eventually reappeared for the purposes of signs, records, and pleasure.

## Evolution of Writing Systems
Before the emergence of formal writing, picture-writing was utilized—a practice still seen among the Bushmen, Amerindians, and other barbaric and savage peoples globally. This method involves drawing things and acts, supplemented by dots and strokes to represent quantitative ideas such as distances and days, as well as heraldic indications for proper names.

A significant advancement occurred when pictures began to indicate similar things rather than the objects they directly represented, a process mirrored in the modern rebus. The Sumerian language, composed of accumulated syllables, readily adopted this syllabic method to express ideas that could not be conveyed through direct pictures. Egyptian writing underwent similar developments. Eventually, foreign peoples with different speech methods modified and simplified these picture scripts, leading to the creation of alphabetical writing. All true alphabets of the later world are derived from a combination of Egyptian hieroglyphic (priest writing) and Sumerian cuneiform. In China, a conventionalized picture writing developed, though it never progressed to an alphabetical stage.

## Physical Formats and Binding
Writing was historically applied to vellum, paper, or papyrus rolls in three primary arrangements:
1. Short lines written at right angles to the length of the roll, which were stored in cylinders called *scrinium*.
2. Long lines spanning the entire length of the roll, as seen in Buddhist prayer-wheels.
3. Short lines parallel to the length of the roll with spaces left on either side. This method is still used in the scrolls of the law in Jewish synagogues.

The third method led to the development of the *orihon* (a Chinese and Japanese form), where the roll is folded backwards and forwards in the vacant spaces between columns. This created a link between the roll and the book. Early binding for such books involved "stabbing" the substance of the roll with cord or fibre through holes near the edge, a method found in an 8th-century Coptic papyrus from Thebes.

## Historiography
The motivations for writing history vary. Some authors write to demonstrate skill in composition and acquire a reputation for fine speaking, while others write to gratify those involved in the events. Others are driven by necessity because they were personally concerned in the facts and wish to preserve them for posterity or the public benefit, particularly when they perceive that others have perverted the truth of those actions in their own writings.

## Motor Automatism and Automatic Writing
In the field of psychical research, "motor automatism" refers to non-reflex movements of voluntary muscles executed while awake but not controlled by ordinary waking consciousness. This ranges from vague movements used in "bier-divination" (to infer a murderer or residence) and witch seeking, to more specialized actions like the use of a divining rod to trace criminals or find water.

Automatic writing and drawing represent a higher stage of this phenomenon. Automatic writing is defined as writing performed without the volition of the agent. The agent may be in a state of induced or spontaneous trance, a morbid state such as hystero-epilepsy, or a condition indistinguishable from normal wakefulness. While some automatists are aware of the specific words they are writing, they rarely perceive the meaning of the whole text.

### Characteristics and Applications
Automatic writing often resembles the agent's ordinary handwriting, but may involve multiple distinct handwritings, imitations of other people (living or dead), or reversed writing (*Spiegelschrift*) that requires a mirror to read. In some cases, the hand and arm are insensible, or the agent guides the hand via a special extension of muscular sense rather than sight.

Historically, automatic writing played a role in modern spiritualism, appearing on a large scale in America between 1850 and 1860. Notable works from this period include Andrew J. Davis's *Great Harmonia*, J. Murray Spear's *Messages from the Spirit Life*, and Charles Linton's *The Healing of the Nations*. In England, the Rev. W. Stainton Moses produced the *Spirit Teachings*, and early spiritualist newspapers featured "inspirational" writing, such as the *Pages of Ike Paraclete*. A heart-shaped board on wheels called a "planchette" was once used to facilitate this process.

In more recent years, the phenomenon has been used to investigate subconscious mental processes. It can reveal lapsed knowledge, such as foreign languages or forgotten poetry. Edmund Gurney demonstrated that information given to a subject in a hypnotic trance could later be reproduced through handwriting while the subject was occupied with other tasks. Pierre Janet and others have used automatic writing to treat cases of dissociation of consciousness or hystero-epilepsy, allowing patients in hysterical convulsions to answer questions in writing. Some cases, such as those of the American medium Mrs. Piper, are thought by some to indicate telepathy or communication with the dead.

A parallel to automatic writing is speech automatism, which can range from unintelligible babbling to trance speeches. An example is Glossolalia; in the case of Helene Smith, Th. Flournoy showed that these utterances could form a real language based on one already known to the speaker. Similarly, Th. Flournoy recorded a case where an automatist produced writing in an unknown character claiming to be the Martian language.
