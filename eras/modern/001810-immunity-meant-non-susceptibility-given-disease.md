# Immunity

Immunity is defined as the non-susceptibility of an organism to a specific disease or to experimental inoculation using a particular toxin or bacterium. This state is relative and depends on existing conditions; for instance, an animal might be susceptible during experimental inoculation but rarely suffer from the disease naturally if the necessary conditions for infection are absent in nature. Natural immunity is typically demonstrated through direct experiment by exposing the subject to such conditions.

## Factors of Susceptibility and Virulence
The development of a disease following the entrance of a virulent bacterium into an animal's tissues is not a fixed process but varies based on circumstances. The primary factors are the susceptibility of the host and the virulence of the bacterium. Susceptibility varies naturally across different species, races, and individuals, as well as by life stage; for example, adults are less susceptible to diphtheria than young subjects.

Acquired susceptibility can be increased by factors that lower general resisting powers, such as exposure to cold, starvation, and fatigue. Local resistance may also be diminished by depressed vitality or injury. Conversely, the virulence of a bacterium generally increases when it resides within the tissues of an animal, a process intensified by the "method of passage," where the organism is inoculated from animal to animal in series. Virulence typically diminishes when the organism is cultivated on artificial media outside the body.

## Types of Immunity
Animals that are naturally susceptible may acquire immunity either by successfully recovering from an attack of a disease or through various inoculation methods. Artificial immunity is divided into two chief varieties:

*   **Active Immunity:** This is produced by a series of reactions in the body, usually triggered by injections of bacteria or their products. It is the basis for preventive inoculation.
*   **Passive Immunity:** This occurs when serum from an actively immunized animal is transferred to a fresh animal. It is termed "passive" because it involves no active change in the tissues of the recipient animal and is the basis for serum therapeutics.

## Mechanisms of Active Immunity
Active immunity is based on the principle that recovery from certain infective diseases provides protection against future attacks. This can be replicated by producing a modified attack through the introduction of substances into the system. 

One primary method involves inoculation with an "attenuated virus"—bacteria whose virulence has been diminished. Attenuation can be achieved by growing organisms outside the body under unsuitable conditions, such as in the presence of weak antiseptics or at temperatures higher than the optimum. Some organisms lose virulence when grown on ordinary artificial media, a process that can be accelerated by passing air over the growth or by passing the bacterium through a different species. Immunity can be increased gradually using vaccines of increasing virulence, potentially reaching a high degree through "supervirulent" cultures created via the method of passage. Another method involves injecting the bacterium in a dead condition.

When high degrees of immunity are achieved using large doses of toxin or highly virulent bacteria, the immunity is generally specific to that bacterium or toxin. In contrast, non-specific immunity or increased tissue resistance can be produced locally (such as in the peritoneum) using peptone, nucleic acid, or non-pathogenic organisms; this type of immunity cannot be transferred to another animal.

## Biological Processes in Immunity
Several biological mechanisms contribute to the body's ability to resist or destroy bacteria:

**Phagocytosis and Opsonic Action**
Phagocytosis involves the ingestion of bacteria by white corpuscles (leucocytes) of the blood. This process is aided by "opsonic action," which is the effect a serum has on bacteria to make them more susceptible to phagocytosis. This is mediated by opsonins. Normal serum contains a labile opsonin that is destroyed at 55°C. However, animals immunized against a specific bacterium develop thermostable immune opsonins that retain their property even after being heated to 55°C. The "phagocytic index" is used to measure this power by comparing the average number of bacteria within leucocytes in a tested serum against a normal serum control.

**Bactericidal and Lysogenic Action**
Some blood serums possess a direct bactericidal action, though this varies by animal. Lysogenic (or bacteriolytic) action involves a change in the bacterium causing it to swell, become granular, and eventually undergo dissolution. This process requires two substances: a stable "immune body" (also called *substance sensibilisatrice* or *Immunkoerper*) that resists 60°C and provides specificity, and a more labile, ferment-like substance present in normal serum that is destroyed at 60°C.

**Natural Immunity against Toxins**
Natural immunity to toxins may result from a lack of chemical affinity between the toxic molecule and the living molecules of the tissues, or an insensitiveness to the toxophorous group. For example, the nervous system of the fowl shows little combining affinity for tetanus toxin.
