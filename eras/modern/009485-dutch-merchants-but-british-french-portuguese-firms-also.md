# European Commercial Expansion and Competition

The expansion of Western European powers across the world's oceans was driven by the innovation of the ocean-going sailing ship, which extended the range of European experience to the furthest limits of salt water. Among the nations extending their struggles across the seas were the Dutch, the British, the French, the Portuguese, the Spanish, and the Scandinavians.

## Trade in Africa and the Congo
In the 16th and 17th centuries, European traders established stations on the west coast of Africa. One such location was Boma (properly Mboma), a port on the north bank of the river Congo located approximately 60 miles from its mouth. Boma served as a well-known mart for slaves and an entrepot for the commerce of the lower Congo. While the trade in this region was chiefly managed by Dutch merchants, factories were also maintained there by British, French, and Portuguese firms. During this period, no European power exercised actual sovereignty over the area, despite occasional shadowy claims made by Portugal.

## Competition in Asia and the East Indies
The struggle for commercial and political footing in India and the East began following Vasco da Gama's voyage to Calicut. Initially, the Portuguese won the Indian sea trade from the Red Sea Arabs using larger ships with heavier armament, allowing Lisbon to surpass Venice as a center for oriental spices.

By the 17th century, the Dutch sought to grasp this monopoly. At the height of their power, the Dutch established a vast network of fortified stations, including:
* Twelve establishments in India
* Six in Ceylon
* Two in Persia
* Settlements at Mauritius and the Cape of Good Hope

The Dutch resolution to exclude traders of other European nationalities led to hostile competition with the English, French, Danes, and Swedes. In the Banda Islands, the Dutch expelled the Portuguese in the early 17th century. The British attempted to establish a presence there, building a factory on Wai in 1608 (which the Dutch demolished) and later gaining Banda Neira, Lontor, Run, and Wai. However, the Dutch attacked and expelled their British rivals. Although Cromwell forced the restoration of Run in 1654, the Dutch retook the island in 1664. The British eventually captured the Banda Islands in 1796, though they were restored to the Dutch via the treaty of Amiens in 1800, captured again, and finally restored to the Dutch by the treaty of Paris in 1814.

In India, the English and French entered vigorous competition with the Dutch at the start of the 18th century. The English established headquarters at Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras, while the French maintained chief settlements at Chandernagore and Pondicherry. Eventually, the British East India Company achieved complete dominance over the French, Portuguese, and Dutch in the Indian peninsula, practically capturing the decaying Mongol Empire of Akbar and Baber.

## Commercial Interests in the Americas
In the Americas, the Dutch sought profits through colonization. A Dutch company founded in 1621 attempted to establish itself in Brazil, but its position became untenable due to the religious hostility of the Roman Catholic Portuguese, the monopolist tendencies of Dutch colonization, and the support England and France provided to Portugal. Consequently, the company resigned all claims to Brazil by the treaty of 1661.

The Dutch found compensation in Essequibo and Surinam (Dutch Guiana), where there was no Spanish or Portuguese population to resist them. The company of 1621 was dissolved in 1674 due to financial embarrassment and reconstructed in 1675. This new entity exploited Dutch possessions in America until 1794, when the French invasion of Holland led to a general reorganization. A subsequent West India Company founded in 1828 to develop trade was not successful.

By the end of the third quarter of the 18th century, the British crown controlled the northern two-thirds of North America. While France had abandoned the continent, other areas remained under various European controls: Brazil was Portuguese, and small islands or areas were held by the Dutch, British, French, and Danish. Most of the remaining land to the south, including California, Louisiana, and Florida, was Spanish.
