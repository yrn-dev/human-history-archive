# Boracite and Related Mineralogical Properties

## Boracite Characteristics
Boracite is a mineral of particular interest due to its optical anomalies. It is found in considerable numbers as small crystals with sharply defined faces, embedded in anhydrite and gypsum within the salt deposits at Luneburg in Hanover, where it was first observed in 1787. These crystals are colorless or white, though they are often tinged with green, yellow, or grey, and possess a vitreous lustre ranging from translucent to transparent.

The external form of boracite crystals is cubic with inclined hemihedrism, sharing the same symmetry as tetrahedrite and blende. Depending on which form predominates, the habit varies between the cube, the tetrahedron, or the rhombic dodecahedron. In some instances, penetration twins are observed with a tetrahedron face serving as the twin-plane.

## Physical Properties and Pyroelectricity
Boracite possesses a specific gravity of 3.0 and a hardness that reaches as high as 7 on Mohs' scale. In 1791, R.J. Hauy first observed that these crystals are markedly pyroelectric. When a cube of boracite is heated, it becomes negatively electrified on four of its corners and positively electrified on the four opposite corners. In crystals where the rhombic dodecahedron predominates, the larger and bright tetrahedral faces (s') are located at the antilogous poles, while the smaller and dull tetrahedral faces (s) are situated at the analogous poles; the latter become positively electrified upon heating.

## Comparative Mineralogy
Other minerals documented in the archive share similar physical metrics or were studied by R.J. Hauy. Datolite, a basic calcium and boron orthosilicate first observed by J. Esmark in 1806, also possesses a specific gravity of 3.0 and a hardness of 5 to 5½. 

R.J. Hauy was also responsible for naming the mineral epidote (derived from the Greek *epidosis*, meaning "increase") and axinite (from the Greek *axine*, meaning "axe"). Axinite, named by Hauy in 1799, is a complex calcium and aluminium boro-silicate with a specific gravity of 3.28 and a hardness of 6½-7. Epidote, a basic calcium, aluminium, and iron orthosilicate, has a hardness of 6½ and a specific gravity that varies between 3.3 and 3.5 depending on the amount of iron present.

## Measurement of Specific Gravity
The archive describes various methods and applications for determining specific gravity. In a laboratory setting, a specific gravity bottle can determine the relative density of an insoluble solid available in small fragments. This is achieved by weighing the solid in air (W), weighing the bottle full of liquid (W₁), and weighing the bottle containing the solid filled with liquid (W₂). The relative density is calculated as W/(W + W₁ - W₂).

In other fields, the term "gravity" is used differently. In the brewing industry, the "gravity" of a brewer is 1000 times the specific gravity of a physicist. For example, a wort with a specific gravity of 1.045 is referred to by brewers as having a gravity of 1045, or forty-five "degrees of gravity."
