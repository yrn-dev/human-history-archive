# Germany, France, Italy, and the Low Countries

The regions of Germany, France, Italy, and the Low Countries have shared complex intersections across history, ranging from artistic and cultural developments to geopolitical conflicts and economic treaties.

## Cultural and Artistic Development
During the Renaissance, distinct regional styles emerged in architecture and printing. In the realm of interior design, Italian doors remained simple, as architects focused on the effect of the doorways. Conversely, in Germany and France, doors were elaborately carved—particularly during the Louis XV and Louis XIV periods—and occasionally featured niches, pediments, and columns with entablatures. While Italian design tended to increase scale by adding panels, French design often did the opposite; for example, a great door at Fontainebleau consists of two leaves designed to appear as a single large panel.

The evolution of printing also varied by region. While the development of the title-page was slow, other aspects of book completion progressed rapidly. In 1461, Albrecht Pfister produced small books with rude woodcut illustrations in Bamberg. By the 1480s, illustrations became common in France, Germany, and the Low Countries. In Italy, the full development of these illustrations occurred later, dating from approximately 1490. Both France and Italy experimented with copper engravings during the 15th century, though these efforts were unsuccessful.

## Geopolitical Conflicts and War
These regions have frequently served as theaters for European power struggles. During the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748), which began with Frederick II of Prussia's invasion of Silesia, Italy, the Low Countries, and Southwest Germany served as the primary battle-grounds for Austria and France. In this conflict, Spain and Bavaria were constant allies of France and Prussia, while England and Holland—traditional enemies of France—supported Austria.

In the early 20th century, tensions rose as Germany adopted a "swaggering imperialism." By 1914, the German upper-class youth and leadership were prepared for conflict, viewing the French as degenerate and the Russians as decayed. Prior to this, diplomatic friction occurred in 1906 when Germany compelled France to accept a conference at Algeciras under the threat of war, leading to the resignation of the French Foreign Minister, Monsieur Delcassé.

Following later conflicts, international commissions were established to manage river navigation. The Rhine was placed under a Central Commission meeting at Strassbourg, composed of four representatives from France (who also selected the President), four from Germany, and two each from Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, Great Britain, and Switzerland. Germany was required to provide France with all drafts and designs for the Rhine course between its frontiers and grant rights for water to feed canals.

## Economic and Demographic Data
Economic relations between these powers were often governed by commercial treaties. Austria-Hungary initially utilized a system of treaties with Italy, France, and Germany that specified maximum duties on articles and included "most favoured nation" clauses. However, after 1873, Austrian manufacturers pushed for protective duties to counter competition from Belgian, English, and German goods. By 1877, while raw materials remained duty-free, duties were placed on corn and textiles. Attempts to reach agreements with Germany eventually failed due to the policy changes adopted by Bismarck.

Demographic estimates for these regions have varied across historical records. One source computes the population of France at 20 million, Germany at 22 million, Italy (including islands) at 10 million, and the Low Countries at 4 million. Another authority provides more specific figures: France at 32,897,521; Germany (including Austrian and Prussian Poland and Hungary) at 56,136,213; Italy at 20,548,616; Belgium at 3,533,538; and Holland at 2,444,550.

## Religious and Social Attitudes
The influence of the papacy also saw regional shifts. In one historical instance, the French king acted with the consent of the Three Estates of France (the commons, church, and lords) in the rough treatment of the head of Christendom. This action did not meet with any general manifestation of disapproval in Germany, Italy, or England, suggesting that the power of the idea of Christendom over the minds of men had decayed.
