# Hungary

## Political Revolutions and the Role of Lajos Kossuth
In March 1848, following news of the revolution in Paris, Lajos Kossuth demanded constitutional government for Austria and a parliamentary government for Hungary. His influence was significant; his speech was read to the mob in Vienna that overthrew Metternich on March 13. Following these events, the government was forced to consent to a new constitution on March 31, which effectively established Hungary as an independent state.

Kossuth served as the minister of finance in the first responsible ministry formed by Batthyany. During his tenure, he worked to increase national self-consciousness by establishing a separate Hungarian coinage; the notes issued bore his name and became known as "Kossuth Notes." He also started a newspaper called *Kossuth Hirlapia*. As tensions rose with the reaction in Vienna and threats from the Serbs and Croats, Kossuth called for the nation to arm in self-defence on July 11, requesting 200,000 men. He is credited with the creation of the popular force of the *Honved*. After Batthyany resigned in September, Kossuth and Szemere were appointed to carry on the government provisionally.

Lajos Kossuth died in Turin on March 20, 1894, and was buried in Pesth. He is commemorated in the Kerepes cemetery by a bronze statue.

## The Independence Party and Parliamentary Obstruction
In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Hungarian politics were characterized by a struggle between the crown and the "Independence Party" (also known as the obstructionists). By the autumn of 1898, this party was led by Francis Kossuth, the eldest son of Lajos Kossuth. Francis Kossuth had previously spent thirty-three years in Italy working as a civil engineer and writing pamphlets on Hungarian independence before settling in Hungary in 1895 and entering the diet.

A significant point of contention arose in the autumn of 1902 regarding Army bills that proposed increasing annual recruit contingents by 21,000 men (6,500 from Hungary and 14,500 from Austria). The Hungarian Chamber resented the military authorities' decision to detain several thousand supernumerary recruits in barracks while awaiting parliamentary sanction. Francis Kossuth and the Independence Party used this grievance to obstruct ministerial measures, demanding the withdrawal of the bills or concessions to promote the use of the Magyar language and increase the Magyar character of Hungarian regiments.

## The Deadlock of 1905–1906
A political deadlock occurred between January 1905 and April 1906 involving the crown, Hungary, and Austria. The Coalition, holding the majority in the Chamber, refused to take office unless the crown granted demands including customs separation from Austria and the use of Magyar words of command. The crown refused, viewing these demands as threats to the dual system established in 1867.

In June 1905, the Tisza cabinet was replaced by a non-parliamentary administration led by General Baron Fejervary. To resolve the dispute, Fejervary proposed a scheme drafted by the minister of the interior, Kristoffy, to subject the conflict to the test of universal suffrage through radical franchise reform. This alarmed the Coalition, as they feared universal suffrage would destroy the political hegemony of the Magyar nobility and gentry and undermine the supremacy of the Magyar race by granting political equality to non-Magyars.

The deadlock ended only after the Chamber was dissolved by military force on February 19, 1906. Facing a potential breach of the constitution, the Coalition came to terms with the crown.

## The Pact of 1906 and Economic Relations
On April 8, 1906, a pact was concluded between the crown and the Coalition. The conditions included:
* The suspension of all military questions until after the introduction of universal suffrage.
* The voting of normal recruit contingents and estimates for 1905 and 1906.
* The ratification of commercial treaties concluded by Tisza.
* The introduction of a suffrage reform at least as extensive as the Kristoffy scheme.

Despite these "capitulations," the majority of the Coalition's adherents—members of the Kossuth or Independence party—remained pledged to the political and economic separation of Hungary from Austria, except regarding the person of the ruler. 

Efforts to assert independence continued. On June 2, 1905, during the deadlock, Kossuth obtained a motion to authorize the compilation of an autonomous Hungarian tariff. Later, on May 28, 1906, the Coalition cabinet was authorized to present the Szell-Koerber tariff as a Hungarian autonomous tariff. Although it was identical to the Austrian tariff, this concession of form led to the resignation of the Austrian premier, Prince Konrad Hohenlohe. His successor, Baron Beck, subsequently withdrew the Szell-Koerber compact on July 6, stating that the only remaining economic ties were the joint state bank, management of excise, commercial treaties with foreign countries, and freedom of trade. Beck asserted that any new agreement to regulate the relationship between the two countries must be concluded before the end of 1907.
