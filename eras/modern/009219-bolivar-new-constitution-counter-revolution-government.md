# Bolivar's Constitution and the Government of Bolivia

The political landscape of South America in the 1820s was marked by the transition from military liberation to the establishment of permanent political institutions. Simon Bolivar, the hero of South American independence, played a central role in this transition, most notably through the drafting of a constitution for the newly formed state of Bolivia.

## The Formation of Bolivia
Following the dispersion of Spanish forces in Peru, Bolivar visited Upper Peru in June 1825. This region had detached itself from the government of Buenos Aires to form a separate state named Bolivia in honor of the liberator. In August 1825, the first congress of the new republic assembled and declared Bolivar to be its perpetual protector. In this capacity, he was requested to prepare a constitution of government for the new republic.

## The Proposed Constitution
Bolivar presented his project for a constitution to the Bolivian congress on May 25, 1826. This document was accompanied by an address in which Bolivar detailed his opinions on the form of government he believed was most expedient for the newly established republics. 

The most extraordinary feature of this code was the provision for the executive authority. It proposed lodging the executive power in the hands of a president for life. Under this arrangement, the president would be without responsibility and would possess the power to nominate his own successor.

## Reception and Implementation
The constitution was reviewed by a committee appointed by the congress, which issued a favorable report. Consequently, the congress approved the document, declaring it the constitution of the republic, and it was subsequently sworn to by the people. 

In accordance with this constitution, General Sucre was chosen as president for life. However, Sucre accepted the appointment for only two years and did so on the express condition that 2,000 Colombian troops be permitted to remain with him.

## Reaction and Controversy
Despite its formal adoption, the constitution did not provide general satisfaction and sparked significant alarm. The provision for a lifelong, irresponsible president excited lively apprehensions among republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires, who viewed it as a threat to liberty. In Peru, the constitution contributed to accusations that Bolivar intended to unite Bolivia, Peru, and Colombia into a single state, rendering himself the perpetual dictator of such a confederacy.

## Subsequent Political Instability
The stability provided by Bolivar's constitution was short-lived. Repeated risings occurred, culminating in the end of 1827 when Colombian troops and General Sucre were driven from La Paz. In April 1828, a new congress formed at Chuquisaca, which modified the constitution Bolivar had provided and elected Marshal Santa Cruz as president. 

The government continued to face volatility; a revolution led by General Blanco overturned the government a year later. Order was not restored until 1831, when Santa Cruz promulgated a code of laws bearing his own name and organized the country's financial affairs.

## Other Constitutional Efforts
Bolivar's involvement in constitutional governance extended beyond Bolivia. Earlier, on December 17, 1819, he obtained the enactment of a fundamental law that united the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar as president. Later, on August 30, 1821, a constitution for Colombia was adopted with general approbation, maintaining Bolivar as president and Santander as vice-president. 

In later years, during periods of rebellion in Colombia, Bolivar exercised extraordinary powers authorized by the constitution for the president in cases of rebellion. In February 1827, Bolivar formally resigned the presidency to retire to his patrimonial estate and refute claims of ambition, though he eventually resumed his functions following a resolution by congress and the urging of Santander.
