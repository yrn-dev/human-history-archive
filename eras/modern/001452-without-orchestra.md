# B. Without Orchestra

In the context of historical performance and architectural design, the term "orchestra" refers to two distinct concepts: the physical space in ancient Greek theatres and the ensemble of musicians in musical compositions.

## The Greek Theatre Orchestra
In ancient Greek architecture, the orchestra was a circular area central to the theatre's design. At the theatre of Epidaurus, the orchestra is marked by a sill of white limestone, which enhances the overall effect of the structure. The theatre's plan is based on three centres, with the outer curves consisting of arcs of a larger circle than those used for the central portion. The acoustic properties of this space are described as extraordinarily good, allowing a speaker positioned in the orchestra to be heard throughout the auditorium without needing to raise their voice.

The relationship between the orchestra and the stage has evolved over time. At the Dionysiac theatre beneath the Acropolis, the representation of plays may have moved from an early orchestra in the Agora at the start of the 5th century B.C. Traces of the original circular Greek orchestra were identified by Dorpfeld in 1886. Over subsequent eras, the orchestra space was encroached upon by various stage buildings. During the time of Lycurgus (337-323 B.C.), the oldest stage-building was a rectangular hall with square projections known as *paraskenia*. In late Greek or early Roman times, a stage with a row of columns was built that intruded into the orchestra. This was followed by a larger stage during the time of Nero that advanced even further into the space, and finally, in the 3rd century A.D., it was faced by the "bema" of Phaedrus, a decorated platform-wall.

## Unaccompanied Musical Works
In music, certain compositions are written to be performed without an orchestra. A notable example is the E major partita for unaccompanied violin. The prelude of this work is described as one of the most complete and natural violin solos, utilizing arpeggios to produce full harmony. This avoids the constant attempt to play on all four strings simultaneously, a technique that often makes polyphonic movements a *tour de force* where steady rhythm is nearly impossible.

Some of these unaccompanied works were later transformed into orchestral pieces. The aforementioned prelude for unaccompanied violin was adapted into a sinfonia for organ obligato accompanied by a full orchestra, including a pair of drums and three trumpets, for the church cantata *Wir danken dir, Gott*. In this orchestral version, the proportions of the original are revealed, and the drums thunder out the rhythm at the climax while the trumpets provide holding-notes.

## Evolution of Orchestral Instrumentation
The composition of the orchestra has changed over time with the introduction and substitution of various instruments.

### The Horn
The natural horn was introduced to orchestras at different times across Europe. The Italians were the first to introduce it, though it was initially used only for calls to arms or hunting scenes. In Germany, Reinhard Keiser introduced two horns in C into the opening chorus of his 1705 opera *Octavia*, adding them to oboes and a string quartette. In Vienna, the horn was added to the opera in 1712, though solitary instances occurred earlier, such as in Cesti's *Il Pomo d'Oro* (printed 1667 and 1668). In Dresden, two Bohemian horn-players were added to the court orchestra in 1711. 

In France, the introduction of the natural horn into the French opera orchestra occurred much later, appearing in a fanfare in Andre Campra's *Achille et Deidamie* in 1735. Handel also introduced the horn into the London orchestra in his *Water-music*, composed for George I.

### Other Brass and Woodwinds
The symphonic orchestra has also seen the intrusion of the cornet, particularly in France, where it has tended to supersede the trumpet. While the cornet is valued for its technical facility—including the ability to perform rapid diatonic and chromatic passages that are difficult for horns or trombones—some argue its use is a detriment to tone colour.

The bassoon has been utilized by many masters, including Beethoven in his symphonies and Bach in his mass in B min., though it was not a favourite of Handel. Mozart wrote a concerto for bassoon with orchestra (Kochel, No. 191), and Weber wrote a concerto for bassoon in F scored for full orchestra.

Regarding the bass of the orchestra, the bombardon (or bass tuba) evolved from the *bombardone* (contrabass pommer), which served as the bass of medieval orchestras before the invention of the fagotto.
