# The Deccan

The Deccan is a geographical region and division located in India, specifically within the Bombay division. It is characterized by its distinct geological formations, diverse political history, and various native states.

## Geography and Geology
The Deccan is one of five well-demarcated tracts of the Bombay plains, alongside Sind, Gujarat, the Konkan, and the Carnatic. The plains of the Deccan and Khandesh are watered by large rivers; however, because rainfall is uncertain, these areas are generally bleak and devoid of vegetation for most of the year.

Geologically, the region is known for the Deccan Trap, which consists of extensive flows of basic lava. These flows are part of a history of great volcanic eruptions that occurred during or since the Cretaceous period. Within these great sheets of Deccan traps, a clay-like substance called bole—a hydrous aluminium silicate of red, brown, or yellow color—occurs as red partings.

## Historical Political Power
The history of the region has been marked by the struggle for mastery between various dynasties. In the southern Deccan, the Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas fought for dominance. By the 7th century, the Chalukyas had overcome the Rashtrakutas and other rivals, absorbing smaller kingdoms into their empire. However, the Chalukyas eventually succumbed to the Rashtrakutas, who succeeded to the bulk of their dominions. This included Gujarat, where the Rashtrakutas established a branch line. 

Between approximately 750 and 950, a balance of power existed between the Rashtrakutas and the Gurjaras, with neither kingdom possessing enough strength to significantly encroach upon the other. The Rashtrakutas' ambitions for larger conquests were further hindered by dissensions with their branch line.

## Native States and Administration
The Deccan division of Bombay includes several native states and principalities:

*   **Kolhapur:** This is the principal state under the political control of the government of Bombay and the fourth most important of the Mahratta principalities (following Baroda, Gwalior, and Indore). It covers an area of 3,165 sq. m. and had a population of 910,011 in 1901. The state stretches from the Western Ghats eastwards into the Deccan plain. Its landscape varies from wild hill slopes and valleys in the west—containing ancient Mahratta strongholds such as Bavda, Panhala, Rungna, and Vishalgarh—to a well-cultivated, treeless plain in the east. The region produces rice, tobacco, cotton, sugar-cane, vegetables, millets, and safflower. While iron ore is found in the hills, local smelting has declined due to competition from European imports.
*   **Kuruntwad (or Kurandvad):** A native state created in 1772 via a grant from the peshwa. It is part of the Southern Mahratta jagirs. The state was divided in 1855 between a junior and senior branch, and further divided in 1811, with a portion called Shedbal lapsing to the British government in 1857. The chiefs are of the Patwardhan family and are Brahmans by caste. The town of Kuruntwad is situated on the right bank of the Panchganga river near the Kistna. As of 1901, the senior branch had a population of 42,474 and the junior branch 34,003.

## British Influence and Development
The annexation of the Deccan and the British victory over the Mahrattas initiated a period of unrestricted development for Bombay. This era saw significant growth fostered by Mountstuart Elphinstone starting in 1819. The population of Bombay grew from 236,000 in 1838 to 821,000 by 1891, a leap attributed to the foundation of cotton factories, the opening of the Suez Canal, and the completion of the first railway line in 1853.
