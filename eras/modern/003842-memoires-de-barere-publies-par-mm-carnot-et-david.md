# Memoires de B. Barere

The *Memoires de B. Barere ... publies par MM. H. Carnot ... et David (d'Angers) ... precedes d'une notice historique* is a published work released in Paris between 1824 and 1844. The text is characterized as being false, although it is noted to contain valuable information.

## Editorial Components and Reception
The publication includes a historical notice (*notice historique*) written by H. Carnot. This notice is described as being very good and was subsequently published as a separate work in 1842. 

Critical reception of the material surrounding Barere varies among historians and essayists. F. A. Aulard addressed the subject in *Les Orateurs de la Constituante* (Paris, 1882) and *Les Orateurs de la Convention* (2nd ed., Paris, 1905). Additionally, an essay on Barere written by Macaulay and published in volume 79 of the *Edinburgh Review* is described as eloquent, but incorrect.

## Subject of the Memoirs
The memoirs concern Bertrand Barere de Vieuzac (1755–1841), a notorious member of the French National Convention. Born on September 10, 1755, in Tarbes, Gascony, Barere took his name from a small fief belonging to his father, who was a lawyer at Vieuzac. Barere began his professional life as an advocate at the parlement of Toulouse in 1770, where he gained a reputation as an orator. His skill as an essay writer led to his election to the Academy of Floral Games of Toulouse in 1788.

In 1789, the estates of Bigorre elected him as a deputy to the states-general. During the early stages of the Revolution, he was associated with the constitutional party and was known primarily as a journalist. He authored a paper called the *Point du Jour*; however, the reputation of this publication is attributed less to its own quality—as its reports of the National Assembly were dry and incomplete—and more to the fact that the painter David depicted Barere writing a report of the proceedings in the famous painting "Oath in the Tennis Court."

## Political Career and Character
Following the flight of the king to Varennes, Barere shifted his allegiance to the republican party. Despite this, he maintained a connection with the duke of Orleans, serving as a tutor to the duke's natural daughter, Pamela. 

Barere's political conduct is described as being devoid of guiding principles or conscience, regulated instead by a determination to align himself with the strongest party. His career included a tenure as one of the judges of the newly instituted court of cassation from October 1791.

## Contributors to the Publication
The memoirs were published by M. David (d'Angers) and H. Carnot. Lazare Hippolyte Carnot (1801–1888), the second son of L.N.M. Carnot, was a French statesman and man of letters. Born in Saint-Omer on October 6, 1801, he lived in exile with his father until returning to France in 1823. Before entering politics, he published translations of *Chants helleniques* (1828) and an *Expose de la doctrine Saint-Simonienne* (1830). 

Carnot later served as a deputy for Paris, starting in March 1839, and was a leader of the party hostile to Louis Philippe. In 1848, he served as the minister of education in the provisional government, where he proposed laws for free and obligatory primary instruction and secondary education for girls. He resigned on July 5, 1848, after alienating political factions by opposing purely secular schools. He eventually became a senator for life in December 1875.
