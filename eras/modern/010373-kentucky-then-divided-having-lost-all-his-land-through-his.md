# Kentucky

## Early Exploration and Settlement
The settlement of the region west of the Alleghany Mountains was a notable feature of American history following the close of the Seven Years' War in 1763. Kentucky was the first settlement in this movement and the first state west of the Alleghanies admitted into the Union. In 1763, the territory was claimed by Virginia via a 1609 charter, the Six Nations (Iroquois) as western conquests, and the Cherokees as hunting grounds. At that time, the area was inhabited only by a small tribe of Shawnees in the north and a few Chickasaws near the Mississippi river.

Early exploration began in 1750 when Dr. Thomas Walker, acting as a surveyor and agent for the Loyal Land Company, explored from the Cumberland Gap but remained within the mountain region. In 1751, Christopher Gist explored westward from the mouth of the Scioto river for the Ohio Company. In 1752, an Indian trader named John Finley descended the Ohio river in a canoe to the site of Louisville. Finley's descriptions later attracted Daniel Boone, who first visited the region in 1767. Between 1769 and 1771, Boone and companions, including Finley, roamed the area collecting deer, beaver, and bear skins while encountering roving bands of Indians.

The first permanent English settlement was established at Harrodsburg in 1774 by James Harrod. In 1775, Daniel Boone led a party of settlers to found Boonesborough. In October 1774, following the battle of Point Pleasant, Ohio Indians signed a treaty surrendering claims south of the Ohio river. Additionally, in March 1775, Richard Henderson and North Carolina land speculators met with approximately 1,200 Cherokee Indians on the Watauga river to conclude a purchase treaty.

## Political and Legal Development
Kentucky was initially part of Virginia. During this period, it was divided into three counties, one of which was Fayette county. Daniel Boone served as county lieutenant, sheriff, and deputy surveyor of Fayette county, and represented settlers in the Virginia legislature.

The state's legal framework evolved through several constitutions. The constitution of 1799 introduced the popular vote for senators and the governor and removed the original jurisdiction of the supreme court in land cases. Later, a new constitution in 1850 limited the legislature's power to misapply sinking funds or contract further obligations.

The state faced various political challenges, including the Burr conspiracy between 1804 and 1806. While some residents were interested in a filibustering attack on the Spanish in the South-West, there was little support for separating Kentucky from the Federal Union. During the War of 1812, no battles occurred within Kentucky, but the state's troops formed the majority of the forces under General William Henry Harrison, participating in operations at the Thames, the river Raisin, Fort Meigs, and Fort Wayne.

## Economy and Finance
Kentucky's financial history was marked by over-speculation in railways and land between 1830 and 1850. The state's funded debt reached four and one-half million dollars by 1850. Banking began in 1802 with a co-operative insurance company. The Bank of Kentucky, established in 1806 at Frankfort, held a monopoly for several years. In 1818-1819, the legislature chartered 46 banks, most of which failed during the panic of 1819. The Bank of the Commonwealth was chartered in 1820 but failed in 1829 following a court decision regarding the legal tender quality of its notes. By 1834, the Northern Bank of Kentucky, the Bank of Louisville, and the Bank of Kentucky were chartered; these survived the panic of 1837.

The state's manufactures are primarily based on raw materials from forests and farms. Distilled liquors are the most distinctive product. In 1787, Roman Catholic immigrants from Maryland settled along the Salt river and produced whisky from Indian corn. After the 1794 suppression of the Whisky Insurrection, distillers from Pennsylvania settled in what was then Bourbon county (now Mason county), creating the product known as "Bourbon" whisky. The quality of this whisky is attributed to Kentucky yeast germs and lime water. Between 1880 and 1900, the average annual product was 20,000,000 gallons. By 1900 and 1905, Kentucky ranked fourth in the Union for the value of distilled liquors.

## Mineral Resources
Kentucky possesses valuable mineral resources, primarily bituminous or semi-bituminous coal. In 1907, the state ranked eighth among coal-producing states, with an output of 10,753,124 short tons. Production is split between the western coalfield (5,800 sq. m.) and the eastern coalfield (11,180 sq. m.). In 1907, the western district produced 6,295,397 tons and the eastern produced 4,457,727 tons. The highest producing counties in 1907 were Hopkins, Muhlenberg, Bell, and Whitley. Kentucky is also the largest producer of cannel coal in the Union, with 1907 production reaching 77,733 tons (including semi-cannel coal).
