# Wells

In the field of construction and engineering, wells are utilized as a specific device for obtaining foundations in certain types of soil. This method is particularly applicable when dealing with running sands.

## Construction Process
The process of creating a foundation via a well involves building a masonry casing, similar to the structure of a standard well. Once the casing is in place, the soil inside the structure is excavated. As the interior soil is removed, the masonry casing gradually sinks into the ground. To maintain the structure, the masonry is continued at the surface as the sinking progresses.

## Completion and Finishing
Once the required depth for the foundation has been reached, the interior of the well is generally filled. The materials used for this filling are typically brick or concrete.

## Related Foundation Methods in Sand
The use of wells is one of several methods for establishing foundations in challenging soils. In sandy environments, disk piles may also be employed. These piles feature a flat flange at the bottom and are weighted to prevent them from rising. Water is pumped into the top of the pile to blow or pump sand from beneath it, allowing the pile to be lowered. This is especially useful in ground that resists piles driven by blows or in quicksand, where piles often slowly rise back to their original position after each blow.

## General Soil Considerations for Foundations
The choice of foundation method depends heavily on the nature of the soil. While wells are used for running sands, other soils require different approaches:
* **Rock:** If a building is erected on rock as hard as the wall materials, expanded footings are theoretically unnecessary.
* **Chalk, Gravel, Sand, or Clay:** Footings must be expanded based on the resistance of the structure used.
* **Loose or Imperfect Soil:** A solid platform, often a layer of concrete, is necessary under the constructed footing to prevent sinking.
* **Expanding/Contracting Soils:** Clays that swell when wet and shrink when dry may require concrete foundations to be interposed to protect the building from derangement. In such cases, concrete walls may be brought up from a level sufficiently below the ordinary ground surface.
