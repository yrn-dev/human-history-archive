# The War Between Chile, Peru, and Bolivia

The conflict between Chile, Peru, and Bolivia was a military struggle characterized by naval engagements, territorial seizures, and internal political upheavals within the allied nations of Peru and Bolivia.

## Origins and Outbreak of War
The conflict was preceded by tensions regarding a secret treaty and economic disputes. Chile was aware of a secret treaty and viewed a Peruvian offer to mediate as questionable. Furthermore, a law passed by Peru in 1875 had created a monopoly over the nitrate beds of Tarapaca, which Chile claimed seriously prejudiced Chilean enterprise. 

Chile demanded two conditions: the annulment of the secret treaty and an assurance of Peruvian neutrality. When Peru refused both demands, Chile declared war.

## Early Campaigns and Territorial Losses
Chilean forces initially established superiority at sea, despite the "heroic gallantry" of the Peruvians. This naval advantage allowed Chile to land troops to engage allied forces concentrated at other points in the south and at Arica. The Bolivian ports were captured early in the conflict.

A sea attack on Pisagua routed troops under the Peruvian General Buendia, opening a path into southern Peruvian territory. Upon hearing of this defeat, General Daza led Bolivian troops in a hasty and disorderly retreat to Tacna. Following this, San Francisco fell, and Iquique was occupied by Chileans after being evacuated by the allies without a struggle. After severe fighting, the province of Tarapaca surrendered, and by the end of 1879, Chile was in complete possession of the province.

## Internal Revolutions and Political Shifts
As the war progressed, both Peru and Bolivia experienced internal political instability. In Peru, General Prado was deposed and Colonel Pierola was proclaimed dictator. Similarly, in Bolivia, troops at Tacna—indignant at the incompetence or cowardice of their president—deprived him of command and elected Colonel Camacho to lead them. Simultaneously, a revolution in La Paz proclaimed General Narciso Campero president, a post he was officially elected to in June.

## The Final Campaigns and the Fall of Lima
During 1880, the war was primarily a naval struggle between Peru and Chile, with Bolivia playing little to no part. In January 1881, the battles of Miraflores and Chorillos took place, resulting in heavy slaughter and "savage excesses" by Chilean troops. These battles led almost immediately to the surrender of Callao and Lima, leaving Chileans as the practical masters of Peru.

During this period, Chilean naval officer Patricio Lynch led a destructive naval raid and later commanded a division under Baquedano during the campaigns of Miraflores and Chorrillos. Following his services at Chorillos, Lynch was appointed to command the Army of Occupation in Peru. During his tenure, he put the Peruvian president, Garcia Calderon, under arrest and eventually invested Iglesias with supreme power in Peru before returning to Chile in 1883.

## The Provisional Government of Admiral Montero
While Chile controlled the major cities, the war lingered in the interior of Peru, where Admiral Montero formed a provisional government. In September 1882, Admiral Montero and President Campero of Bolivia held a conference and decided to continue holding out for better terms.

However, the Peruvians eventually grew wearied of the struggle. On October 20, 1883, Peru concluded a peace treaty with Chile. Under the command of Admiral Montero, the troops at Arequipa surrendered the town. Montero subsequently fled to Bolivia for refuge, but was received coldly and eventually withdrew from the country to Europe.

## Peace Treaties and Aftermath
Following the Peruvian surrender, the Chilean army of occupation concentrated at Arequipa on November 9, while the remaining Bolivian army was located at Oruro. Negotiations led to a peace treaty signed between Chile and Bolivia on December 11. Under the terms of this treaty, Bolivia ceded its entire sea-coast to Chile, including the port of Cobija.
