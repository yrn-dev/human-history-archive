# British Colonial Administration and the Use of Ordinances

In various British colonial territories, the governor served as the central administrative authority, often exercising the power to issue ordinances to regulate the legal, financial, and social structures of the governed populations. The application of these ordinances varied by region, reflecting the specific political status of the territory and the relationship between the British administration and the local inhabitants.

## Administration in Ashanti
Ashanti was formally annexed to the British dominions on September 26, 1901, via an order in council. This order functioned as the first constitution granted to Ashanti by its British owners. Under this framework, the territory was placed under the control of the governor of the Gold Coast, who was represented in his absence by a chief commissioner. The chief commissioner was further supported by a staff of four commissioners and four assistant commissioners.

The constitution specifically outlined the governor's authority regarding the issuance of ordinances. When issuing ordinances related to the raising of revenue, the administration of justice, or any other matter, the governor was required to respect native laws regulating the civil relations of populations, tribes, or chiefs. The only exceptions to this requirement were instances where such laws were clearly injurious to the welfare of the natives or incompatible with British sovereignty. Following the 1901 annexation, relations between the governed and the governing power improved. By 1906, Chief Commissioner F.C. Fuller reported that suspicion of the "white man's" motives was decreasing, evidenced by the natives' preference for the British-established criminal and civil courts.

## Governance in the East Africa Protectorate
The government system in the East Africa Protectorate resembled that of a British crown colony. The administration was headed by a governor, who was assisted by a lieutenant-governor (serving as deputy) and provincial commissioners who presided over each province. The structure included legislative and executive councils, with the legislative council containing nominated unofficial members.

In this protectorate, legislation was conducted through ordinances made by the governor with the assent of the legislative council. While the judicial system was based on Indian models, native customs were considered in cases involving Africans. In the "ten-mile strip," where the sultan of Zanzibar remained the territorial sovereign, the laws of Islam applied to the Arab and native populations. 

Financial records for the protectorate show a steady increase in revenue, which was derived from excise, licences, customs, posts and telegraphs, and railway earnings, as well as a hut tax paid by natives. Between 1900 and 1907, revenue grew from £64,275 to £461,362, while expenditure rose from £193,438 to £616,088. These deficiencies were covered by grants-in-aid from the imperial exchequer.

## British Honduras
British Honduras transitioned from a period of complete independence (1638–1786), during which colonists elected their own magistrates, to a formal colony in 1862. Early governance was guided by "Burnaby's Laws," a codification of precedents published after a 1756 visit by Admiral Sir W. Burnaby.

The administrative structure evolved from a superintendent (appointed in 1786) to a lieutenant governor subject to the governor of Jamaica. In 1884, the lieutenant governor became the independent governor and commander-in-chief. He was assisted by an executive council consisting of three unofficial and three official members. The colony was divided into six districts: Toledo, Stann Creek, the Cayo, Orange Walk, Corosal, and Belize. Legal authority was based on English common law, modified by local enactments and the *Consolidated Laws of British Honduras*, a collection of customs and ordinances revised between 1884 and 1888.

## Executive Authority in Canada
In 1838, Lord Durham accepted the post of governor-general and lord high commissioner of Canada. He was granted almost dictatorial powers by an act passed in February 1838, which suspended the constitution of Lower Canada for two years. 

During his tenure, Durham used ordinances and proclamations to manage the aftermath of Papineau's rebellion. While he issued a general proclamation of amnesty on June 28, 1838, he simultaneously issued an ordinance providing for the imprisonment of eight excepted persons in Bermuda without trial. This specific ordinance became a point of contention in the English House of Lords, where Brougham challenged the legality of transporting prisoners to a location where Durham lacked jurisdiction. Despite initial approval from the prime minister and colonial secretary, the ordinance was eventually disallowed, leading to Durham's resignation.
