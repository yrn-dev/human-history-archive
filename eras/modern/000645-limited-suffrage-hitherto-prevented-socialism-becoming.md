# Socialism and the Influence of Suffrage

The development of socialism as a political force in Europe was closely tied to the struggle for suffrage and the shifting dynamics of class consciousness. In several nations, the limitation of voting rights served as a primary barrier to the political mobilization of the working classes, while the eventual move toward universal suffrage often signaled a transition in the governing regime.

## Socialism in Austria
In Austria, limited suffrage had previously prevented socialism from becoming a political force, a situation mirrored in Germany. Additionally, the creation of a centralized socialist party was consistently impeded by national divisions. Consequently, the working classes prioritized the attainment of political power, leading to mass petitions and demonstrations for universal suffrage in 1867.

A genuine socialist movement began to emerge in Styria and Vienna, areas characterized by significant industrial populations. However, the growth of this party was interrupted after 1879 by the introduction of anarchical doctrines. Anarchists, led by Peukert, gained considerable numbers, partly through the circulation of Most's paper, *Freiheit*, which entered the country via Switzerland. This period was marked by serious strikes in 1883-1884 and violent collisions between workmen and police. Some Austrian anarchists combined murder with robbery, prompting the government to implement severe repressive measures and expel leading anarchists. By 1887, under the leadership of Dr. Adler, the socialist party revived as the "party of violence" faded. The party steadily increased its membership, placing the demand for universal suffrage at the forefront of its political program.

## Socialist Movements in Belgium
In Belgium, socialism emerged as a political force by 1886, manifesting in two distinct forms: a German-type socialism among working men in Flemish towns, particularly Brussels and Ghent, and a French revolutionary-type socialism among factory hands and miners in Wallonia. 

On March 18, 1886, a socialist rising occurred in Liege to mark the anniversary of the Paris Commune, quickly spreading to other industrial centers in the Walloon districts. Thousands of workers went on strike to demand suffrage and better wages. Although the military suppressed the outbreak, the government conducted a comprehensive enquiry into the causes of the dissatisfaction. This process led to the creation of various social laws and eventually resulted in the establishment of universal suffrage, which replaced the middle-class regime in Belgium with a democratic one. This transition followed years of political strikes, demonstrations by franchise revision supporters, and long parliamentary discussions, culminating in a constitutional revision in May 1892.

## Theoretical Foundations and Class Consciousness
The broader rise of socialism was influenced by the "mechanical revolution," which brought different races, languages, and political ideas into close contact. This era saw the emergence of class-conscious socialism as an inspiring force for great masses of people, particularly as traditional authorities, such as official and orthodox Christianity, ceased to inspire or provide a common basis for cooperation.

The harsh and elementary form of communism associated with Marx disseminated globally during a period of stress between reluctant workers and selfish employers. Marx theorized that economic necessities limit human minds and create a necessary conflict between the employing, prosperous classes and the employed mass. He prophesied that as education advanced—necessitated by the mechanical revolution—the employed majority would become increasingly solid in their antagonism toward the ruling minority, eventually seizing power to inaugurate a new social state.

## Political and Economic Pressures in Germany
In the German Empire, the social and political unity of the state was strained by overflowing expenditure, particularly regarding naval expansion. This financial pressure created a deep cleavage between industrials and agrarians due to heavy duties on food-stuffs. While the propertied classes—including State-right men and Imperialists—shared an apprehension regarding the prospective inroad of a universal suffrage Parliament upon the field of direct taxation, the parties of the Left were strengthened. These Left-wing parties opposed expenditure on armaments and other costs, finding support in the new or increased taxation on popular indulgences.
