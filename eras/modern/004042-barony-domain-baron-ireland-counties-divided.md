# BARONY

A barony refers to the domain of a baron. The term also denotes the rank or dignity of a baron and the specific feudal tenure known as "by barony."

## Regional Applications and Definitions
The application of the term varies by region. In Scotland, a barony can refer to any large freehold estate, regardless of whether it is held by a commoner. In Ireland, counties are divided into "baronies." These divisions are equivalent to the "hundreds" found in England. According to the General Report of the Census of England (1873), Irish baronies appear to have been formed from the territories of Irish chiefs as they submitted to English rule. For example, the county of Donegal contains seven baronies.

## Feudal Evolution and Peerage
Historically, those who held land directly from the crown via military service—from earls downwards—were all considered "barons." Because of the significant differences in their importance and position, they were eventually divided into "greater" and "lesser" barons. This distinction was already present under Henry II, as noted in the *Dialogus de Scaccario*, which distinguished between "greater" and "lesser" baronies.

By 1164, a practice emerged where greater barons received a special summons to the council. In contrast, the Magna Carta (1215) stipulated that lesser barons were to be summoned only through sheriffs. This distinction eventually restricted the privileges and rights of peerage to the greater barons.

## Examples of Irish Baronies and Titles
Various baronies have been established in Ireland through royal creation or submission to English rule:

*   **Barony of Inchiquin:** Murrough O'Brien (d. 1551) was created baron of Inchiquin and earl of Thomond in 1543 after submitting to Henry VIII. Upon his death in 1551, his earldom passed to his nephew Donogh, while the barony was inherited by his son Dermod.
*   **Barony of Caher:** Created in 1543 for Sir Thomas Butler of Chaier (or Caher-down-Eske), this barony fell into abeyance in 1560 following the death of the 2nd baron. It was recreated in 1583 for Sir Theobald Butler but became extinct in 1858 upon the death of Richard Butler, the 13th baron.
*   **Barony of Dufferin:** Located in county Down, this barony became part of the family holdings of the Blackwoods after Sir John Blackwood married the heiress of the Hamiltons. The title of Baroness Dufferin was conferred in 1800 upon Sir John's widow.
*   **Bourke Baronies:** The baronies of Bourke of Connell (1580) and Bourke of Brittas (1618) were both forfeited in 1691 and later bestowed on branches of the family.

## Succession and Extinction of Baronies
Baronies may become extinct, dormant, or fall into abeyance based on the laws of inheritance:

*   **Extinction:** A barony becomes extinct when there are no eligible heirs. For instance, the barony held by Thomas (d. 1614), the 15th baron, became extinct because he left no children. Similarly, the barony of Baron Grey de Wilton became extinct in September 1814 upon the death of Sir Thomas Egerton without sons.
*   **Abeyance and Transfer:** The barony of Dudley fell into abeyance in October 1757 upon the death of Ferdinando Dudley Lea. In other cases, titles may pass to distant relatives; the barony of Wentworth passed to the heirs of Ada (d. 1852), eventually passing to her daughter and only child following the death of the 2nd earl of Lovelace.
*   **Separation of Titles:** Titles can be separated through inheritance. In the case of the baronies of Dudley and Ward, both were held by Edward (d. 1701), but they were separated after the death of his grandson William in May 1740.
