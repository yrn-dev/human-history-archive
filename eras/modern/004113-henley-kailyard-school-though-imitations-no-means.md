# W. E. Henley and Associated Literary Works

W. E. Henley was a prominent figure in late 19th-century literature, contributing as an editor, poet, and scholar. He served as the editor of the *Scots Observer* (which later became the *National Observer*), a publication to which the novelist and dramatist James Matthew Barrie contributed.

## Literary Collaborations and Influence
Henley maintained a close professional and artistic relationship with Robert Louis Stevenson. Together with R.A.M. Stevenson, Henley formed a coterie with Robert Louis Stevenson to study words with the precision of an art student studying paints. This group sought to create a new romantic school of writing that could be grafted onto the picturesque historic fiction associated with Dumas and Scott. 

The collaborative efforts of Henley and Robert Louis Stevenson extended to the theater, where the two authors used the "modern villain" type—originally seen in the melodrama *Auberge des Adrets* and the sequel *Robert Macaire*—for their own play titled *Macaire*.

## Poetic Contributions and the Ballade
In the latter part of the 19th century, Henley was among a group of writers who re-introduced the original ballade to English literature. This group included Edmund Gosse, Andrew Lang, Austin Dobson, and Algernon Charles Swinburne. The modern English ballade, which Henley and his contemporaries cultivated, was generally modeled on the 15th-century lines established by Henri de Croi. These artificial forms of verse are noted for their ability to convey both mockery and pathos, producing a melodic effect without appearing artificial.

## Scholarship on Robert Burns
Henley contributed significantly to the study of Scottish poetry. Along with T.F. Henderson, he prepared a definitive critical edition of the *Poetry of Robert Burns*, published in four volumes between 1896 and 1897 (and reprinted in 1901), commonly referred to as the "Centenary Burns." In addition to the critical notes and variant readings provided in the edition, Henley authored an essay in the fourth volume titled "Robert Burns. Life, Genius, Achievement."

## Related Regional Writing
While Henley edited the *Scots Observer*, other writers of the period were exploring regional themes. James Matthew Barrie, for example, published *Auld Licht Idylls* in 1888, a collection of sketches based on his native village of Kirriemuir, which he idealized as "Thrums." These works were influenced by his mother's tales and early observations. Similarly, the regional type of writing championed by figures like Rudyard Kipling—who utilized a technical vocabulary and "Oriental colouring"—became widely imitated.
