# The Foundation of the Bank of England (1694)

The Bank of England was established in 1694, emerging from a financial arrangement involving a loan to the government. This loan, totaling £1,200,000, was subscribed over a period of slightly more than ten days, beginning on Thursday, 21st June, and concluding at noon on Monday, 2nd July 1694.

## Early Governance and Administration
Following the subscription period, the bank's leadership was appointed on Tuesday, 10th July 1694. The subscribers selected Sir John Houblon to serve as governor and Michael Godfrey as deputy-governor. On the following day, Wednesday, 11th July 1694, the first twenty-four directors were elected. All of these directors were individuals of high mercantile standing. Among them were two brothers of Sir John Houblon; the Houblon family were descendants of James Houblon, a Flemish refugee who had escaped persecution by Alva.

Michael Godfrey, the deputy-governor, authored a pamphlet to explain the bank's establishment and its intended utility to the country. This document addressed contemporary anxieties regarding the bank's connection to William III, specifically refuting the fear that the institution might eventually join with the prince to make him absolute and thereby render parliaments useless. Michael Godfrey later died on 17th July 1695 during the siege of Namur.

## Facilities and Locations
The Bank of England's operations began in the Mercers' chapel. The institution remained at this location until 28th September, at which point it moved to Grocers' Hall. The bank continued as a tenant of Grocers' Hall until 1732.

On 1st August 1732, the first stone was laid for the building that the bank occupies to this day. This structure was built on the site of the house and gardens of the first governor, Sir John Houblon, which had been purchased for that purpose. The building was further expanded between 1764 and 1788 with the addition of wings. In 1780, the directors acquired the fabric of the adjacent church of St Christopher le Stocks through parliamentary powers, as they were concerned that the church might provide dangerous facilities for a mob. The building was developed into its present form around the start of the 19th century.

## Context of English Banking
The establishment of the Bank of England and the subsequent acts of parliament associated with it heavily influenced the trajectory of banking in the United Kingdom. While the roots of English banking can be traced back to the 16th century through the money dealings of London goldsmiths, the foundation of the Bank of England represents a pivotal development in the nation's financial history.
