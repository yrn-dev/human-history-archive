# Bohemia

Bohemia is a region whose history has been marked by struggles over autonomy, national identity, and the use of its native language within the framework of the Habsburg empire.

## Early Intellectual and Political History
The national language of Bohemia reached a high intellectual level by the 14th century, producing writers on philosophical and religious subjects. Notable figures included Thomas of Stitny (c. 1331-1401), whose works *O obecnych vecech Krestanskych* and *Besedni reci* are highly valued. Huss, another significant contributor to the development of the Bohemian language, wrote the *Vyklad viry, desatera Boziho prikazani, a patere* in 1412, as well as the *Postilla* and treatises on simony and the road to salvation.

Politically, the region faced significant upheavals in the 17th century. Following the deposition of Ferdinand and the election of Frederick, elector palatine, as king, warfare ensued between Bohemia and Austria. This period culminated in the Battle of the White Hill on November 8, 1620, where Bohemian forces were defeated. This event marked a turning point; the leaders of the national movement were executed on June 21, 1621, and their lands were confiscated and distributed among imperial army adventurers.

## Habsburg Rule and Centralization
Under Ferdinand, the ancient free constitution of Bohemia was formally suppressed by a decree in 1627. The new constitution established the heredity of the Bohemian crown within the house of Habsburg and stripped the diet of legislative power, leaving it only with the right to vote on taxes. During this time, the German language was granted equal right with Bohemian in law courts and government offices, a necessity caused by the influx of foreigners who did not know the national language.

Throughout the 18th century, the autonomy of Bohemia was further limited. In 1720, the estates sanctioned the female succession to the throne and recognized the Pragmatic Sanction. Later, under Maria Theresa and Joseph II, separate law-courts were abolished by a 1749 decree, and an Austro-Bohemian chancellor was appointed to control the administration.

## The Language Question and the Badeni Ordinances
By the late 19th century, the struggle over language became a central political issue in Austrian public life. Since the time of Joseph II, German had been the ordinary language of government, the army, and the internal service of public offices and law courts. This created a network of German-speaking officialism where no one could share in the government without proficiency in German.

In 1871, Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the empire by recognizing the rights of the kingdom, but this failed. Later, Count Eduard Taaffe succeeded in bringing Bohemian representatives back into the parliament of Vienna by removing anomalies in the electoral system and consenting to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.

In 1897, Count Badeni attempted to conciliate Bohemia further. He issued a decree stating that government officials wishing to be employed in Bohemia must prove knowledge of both German and Bohemian. In May 1897, Badeni published further "celebrated ordinances" which determined that:
1. All correspondence and documents brought before government officials should be conducted in the language in which they were first introduced, effectively introducing Czech into government offices throughout the kingdom.
2. After 1903, no one could be appointed to a government post in Bohemia without passing an examination in Czech.

## Political Crisis and Aftermath
The Badeni ordinances caused violent opposition among German Nationalists and Radicals, who feared the loss of German influence. They resorted to obstruction in parliament, bringing repeated motions to impeach ministers, which led to the prorogation of parliament in June 1897. Agitation spread across the country, with serious disturbances occurring at meetings in Aussig and Eger.

Because Badeni was a Pole, he had little experience in the western part of the empire and had not anticipated the severity of the reaction. The resulting political crisis led to the fall of Badeni's cabinet at the end of 1897. He was succeeded by Count Thun and then Count Clary, whose government repealed the decrees that had granted equal rights to the Bohemian language. This repeal sparked troubles in Prague, which were severely repressed by Austrian authorities.

In the subsequent ministries of Gautsch and Korber, Bohemians continued to assert their national rights and oppose the central government in Vienna. The feud between Germans and Czechs paralyzed parliamentary life in Austria at the close of the 19th century, forcing the government to operate via the emergency clause (paragraph 14) of the constitution, making ministers responsible to the emperor rather than to parliament.
