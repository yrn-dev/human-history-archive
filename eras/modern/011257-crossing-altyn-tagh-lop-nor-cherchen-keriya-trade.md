# Crossing the Altyn Tagh to Lop Nor and the Cherchen-Keriya Trade Route

The geography of the region between the Tibetan plateau and the Gobi desert is characterized by a complex system of mountain ranges and depressions. Central to this area are the Altyn Tagh and the trade routes connecting the Chinese borderlands to the Tarim basin.

## The Altyn Tagh and Regional Geography
The Altyn Tagh is a mountain range that forms part of a minor system branching north-east from the Kuen-lun mountains at approximately 83° E. longitude. It extends eastwards as a continuous water-divide reaching the Nan Shan mountains, which are located north of the Koko Nor basin. This system creates a geographical barrier between the high Tibetan plateau and the low-lying sands of the Gobi. Between these two extremes lies Tsaidam, a system of elevated valleys situated 8,000 to 9,000 feet above sea level, which serves as an intermediate steppe east of Lop Nor.

The terminology regarding these mountains varies across different sources. Etymologically, the correct form is Astin-tagh or Astun-tagh, meaning the "Lower or Nearer Mountains." This name appears in the *Atlas* of the Russian General Staff and *Stieler's Hand-atlas*. An alternative name, Ustun-tagh, meaning "Higher or Farther Mountains," is sometimes used, though it is not used locally for any specific range; it would more appropriately describe the higher, more southerly border-ranges of the Tibetan plateau.

## Exploration and Transit
The region was explored extensively by the Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky. In 1876, Prjevalsky visited Lop Nor and discovered the Altyn Tagh range. During his fourth journey between 1883 and 1885, he traveled from Sining, a major trade center of the Chinese borderland, through northern Tibet. During this expedition, he crossed the Altyn Tagh to reach Lop Nor and subsequently utilized the Cherchen-Keriya trade route to reach Khotan. From Khotan, he followed the Tarim river to Aksu.

Other explorers have also traversed this area. Bonvalot and Prince Henri d’Orléans followed an itinerary that took them from Lob-nor and Charkalyk to the Altyn Tagh, continuing in a nearly straight line to Tengri Nor.

## The Cherchen-Keriya Trade Route and Kuen-lun Ranges
The trade routes in this region are influenced by the rugged terrain of the Kuen-lun system. The Kuen-lun consists of a succession of ranges extending from the Pamirs in the west. In its narrower definition, it refers to the ranges separating the Takla-makan desert in the north from the Tibetan plateau in the south, extending nearly to the longitude of Cherchen (about 85½° E.).

The route involving Cherchen and Keriya passes through a region of parallel border-ranges. Between the transverse breach of the Keriya-darya (approximately 81½° E.) and that of the Kara-muren in the longitude of Cherchen (approximately 85½° E.), the ranges trend to the east-north-east. The lower or outer range in this section includes passes such as Choka-davan (9,530 ft.) and Dalai-kurghan-art (14,290 ft.). The upper range features higher passes, including the At-to-davan at 16,600 ft. and the Sarshu-davan at 15,680 ft.

## Adjacent Mountain Systems
To the south and east of the Altyn Tagh/Astin-tagh are other significant ranges:
*   **Akato-tagh:** Also known as the Altun Range of Carey or the Chimen-tagh on Russian General Staff maps. It is an arid range with a crest that is heavily denuded. It is crossed by the Chopur-alik pass at 16,160 ft.
*   **Chimen-tagh:** This range is identical in its western parts to the Piazlik-tagh and is equated in the east with the Tsaidam chain of Prjevalsky. Unlike the Akato-tagh, the Chimen-tagh acts as a "breakwater" for clouds, resulting in more abundant water, snow throughout the year, and the growth of grass and animal life.
*   **Keriya Mountains:** These are part of the more northerly range of the western Kuen-lun proper.

The geography of these elevated valleys and ranges explains why the primary trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed significantly to the north.
