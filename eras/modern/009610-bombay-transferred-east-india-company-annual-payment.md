# Transfer of Bombay to the East India Company

## Early English Interests and Portuguese Rule
The English first established a settlement in the Bombay presidency in 1618 with a factory at Surat, which operated under a charter from Emperor Jahangir. The English recognized the value of the island of Bombay as a naval base, specifically noting its safety from land attacks and the excellence of its harbour. This strategic value led to several early attempts to acquire the island, including an unsuccessful joint effort with the Dutch in 1626. By 1653, proposals were suggested for the purchase of the island from the Portuguese, and in 1654, the directors of the East India Company brought these suggestions to the attention of Cromwell.

Under Portuguese rule, the island did not prosper. Land was distributed through a system called *aforamento*, where fiefs were granted to individuals or religious corporations—such as the Jesuits and Franciscans in the northern districts—in exchange for quit-rents or military service. The intolerance of this rule hindered growth; by 1661, the population was only 10,000.

## Cession to the British Crown
Bombay became British property in 1661. It was ceded to the English crown as part of the dowry of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. Despite the legal cession, the British did not actually occupy the island until 1665, facing significant opposition from the Portuguese and religious orders.

## Transfer to the East India Company
The acquisition was not highly esteemed in England, and the administration under crown officers was unsuccessful. Consequently, in 1668, the crown transferred Bombay to the East India Company for an annual payment of £10. Following this transfer, the Company placed Bombay under the factory of Surat.

Along with the transfer, the East India Company was conferred powers for the administration of justice and the island's defence. To secure the territory, a European regiment was enrolled and fortifications were constructed. These defenses proved effective in 1673 when they deterred an intended attack by the Dutch.

## Development and Administration
The modern city's foundation is attributed to Gerald Aungier, who succeeded Sir George Oxenden as president of Surat in 1669 and moved his headquarters to Bombay in 1672. Aungier reorganized the settlement, replacing the oppressive Portuguese fiscal and religious policies. With the consent of a general assembly of the people's chief representatives, he replaced the burdensome land tax with a fixed money payment. He also protected the religious ceremonies of all castes and forbade the forced carrying of burdens by natives.

These reforms led to rapid population growth, increasing from 10,000 to 60,000 within eight years. The city became a haven for Armenians, Parsees, and the Hindu capitalist class (banya), who were granted freedom of trade and religion.

## Regional Integration and Later History
In 1687, Bombay was placed at the head of all the East India Company's possessions in India, and the presidency was removed from Surat. However, in 1753, the government of Bombay became subordinate to that of Calcutta.

The British encountered the Mahratta power in 1774. This conflict resulted in the 1782 treaty of Salbai, where Salsette was ceded to the British and Broach was handed to Sindhia. Following the second Mahratta war, which ended in 1803, the East India Company received the districts of Kaira and Broach (the latter having been annexed in 1800).

The broader Indian section of the empire, acquired via the royal charter granted to the East India Company by Queen Elizabeth in 1600, was eventually transferred to the imperial government in 1858.
