# The Badeni Language Ordinances of 1897

The Badeni language ordinances were a series of decrees published in May 1897 by Count Badeni, a Polish statesman serving in the Austrian government. These ordinances were designed to address grievances in Bohemia and conciliate the region by granting further concessions regarding the use of the Czech language in official administration.

## Political Context and Origins
Following an election, Count Badeni submitted his resignation, but the emperor refused to accept it. Consequently, Badeni sought political support from other nationalities within the empire, specifically the sixty Young Czechs and fifty-nine Poles. While the Poles were consistently ready to support the government, the moderate wing of the Young Czechs was open to negotiations to increase their influence over the administration. Their primary requirement for this support was further concession regarding language in Bohemia.

Badeni, being a Pole, had limited experience in the western part of the empire and did not anticipate the severe reaction his policies would provoke. While he did not believe it was feasible to reopen the question of Bohemian autonomy, he aimed to remedy the most serious grievances of the country through these language decrees.

## Provisions of the Ordinances
The celebrated ordinances of 1897 established two primary mandates:

1. **Official Correspondence:** It was determined that all documents and correspondence regarding any matter brought before government officials would be conducted in the language in which the matter was first introduced. This applied to the entirety of Bohemia, effectively introducing the Czech language into government offices throughout the kingdom.
2. **Employment Requirements:** The ordinances decreed that after 1903, no individual could be appointed to a government post in Bohemia without first passing an examination in the Czech language.

## German Opposition and Parliamentary Paralysis
The ordinances fulfilled the worst fears of the German inhabitants of Austria. German Radicals and Nationalists reacted with violent opposition, declaring that no government business should be conducted until the ordinances were repealed and Badeni was dismissed. 

The opposition manifested in several ways:
* **Obstruction:** German representatives resorted to obstruction in parliament, bringing repeated motions to impeach ministers.
* **Prorogation:** Due to the fact that no business of any kind was being transacted, parliament had to be prorogued in June.
* **Public Unrest:** Agitation spread across the country, leading to serious disturbances and great meetings at Aussig and Eger, which were attended by Germans from across the frontier.

During the parliamentary recess, Badeni attempted to open negotiations, but the Germans refused to enter any discussion until the ordinances were withdrawn.

## Aftermath and Political Consequences
The feud between the Germans and Czechs resulting from the 1897 ordinances—and the broader development of Polish and Czech influence during the Taaffe era (1879-1893)—paralyzed parliamentary life in Austria. This crisis contributed to a period where political life was at a low ebb and the constitution was observed in letter rather than in spirit. During this time, government was carried on by cabinets of officials utilizing paragraph 14 (the emergency clause) of the constitution. Under this system, ministers were nominally responsible to parliament but in practice were responsible only to the emperor.

The violent opposition eventually led to the fall of Count Badeni's cabinet at the end of 1897. He was succeeded briefly by Count Thun and then by Count Clary. The government of Count Clary repealed the decrees that had granted equal rights to the Bohemian language, which subsequently triggered troubles in Prague that were severely repressed by Austrian authorities. In the following ministries of Gautsch and Korber, Bohemians continued to assert their national rights and oppose the central government in Vienna.
