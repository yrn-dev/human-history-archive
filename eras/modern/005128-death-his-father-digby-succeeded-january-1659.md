# George Digby, 2nd Earl of Bristol

George Digby, the 2nd Earl of Bristol (1612–1677), was an English nobleman and politician known for his academic accomplishments, his shifting political allegiances, and his volatile relationship with the monarchy.

## Early Life and Education
Born in October 1612, George Digby was the eldest son of the 1st Earl of Bristol. He first gained public attention at the age of twelve when he appeared before the House of Commons to plead on behalf of his father, who was then imprisoned in the Tower. This appearance left a strong impression due to Digby's graceful person, youth, and delivery of speech.

Digby was admitted to Magdalen College, Oxford, on August 15, 1626, where he studied under Peter Heylin and earned his M.A. in 1636. Following his studies, he spent several years traveling. Upon his return, he was described by Clarendon as a remarkably handsome and accomplished individual. In 1638 and 1639, he wrote *Letters between Lord George Digby and Sir Kenelm Digby, Knt. concerning Religion* (published in 1651), a work in which he attacked Roman Catholicism.

## Parliamentary Career and the English Civil War
Digby's early relationship with the court was strained; in June 1634, he was committed to the Fleet until July for striking a court gentleman named Crofts in Spring Gardens. This incident, along with the disfavour shown to his father, contributed to his hostility toward the court.

In 1640, Digby was elected as the member for Dorsetshire in both the Short and Long parliaments. Initially, he aligned with Pym and Hampden in opposing Charles, moving for a committee to address the "deplorable state" of the kingdom on November 9 and joining the committee for the impeachment of Strafford. However, his zeal for the prosecution waned; he opposed the attainder and delivered an eloquent speech on April 21, 1641, arguing against *ex post facto* legislation and highlighting the weakness of the evidence provided by Vane. This shift led to hostility from the parliamentary party, and his speech was ordered to be burnt by the hangman on July 15. Additionally, on February 8, he spoke in the Commons to advocate for reformation while opposing the abolition of episcopacy.

## Influence and Exile
Digby eventually became an influential advisor to Charles, often preferred over figures such as Falkland and Hyde. In November, he was noted for his service in the Lords against instructions regarding evil counsellors. He urged the king to impeach five members and pushed for their arrest on January 4, 1642. However, his imprudence reportedly betrayed the king's plan, as he told Lord Mandeville that the king was "mischievously advised."

Following a charge of high treason related to a supposed armed attempt at Kingston, Digby fled to Holland to join the queen, and was impeached on February 26. He later visited Charles at York while disguised as a Frenchman. After being captured and taken to Hull, he managed to cajoled Sir John Hotham into permitting his escape. Digby distinguished himself at Lichfield, where he was wounded leading an assault, but later resigned his commission after a quarrel with Prince Rupert. He was appointed a privy councillor and secretary of state on September 28, 1643, and high steward of Oxford University on October 31. During this time, he supported the queen's policies regarding foreign alliances and help from Ireland, though his negotiations were often ill-conducted and his disputes with Rupert's party caused further embarrassment to the king's affairs.

## Later Life and the Restoration
In January 1659, following the death of his father, Digby succeeded to the peerage as the 2nd Earl of Bristol and was made a Knight of the Garter (K.G.) that same month. Upon returning to England during the Restoration, he found himself relegated to secondary importance and excluded from office due to his religion.

Driven by ambition and a desire for prominence, Digby became violently hostile toward Clarendon in parliament. In foreign affairs, he favored Spain and opposed the king's marriage to Catherine of Portugal. Although he persuaded Charles to send him to Italy to evaluate the Medici princesses, the royal marriage and treaty with Portugal were finalized while he was away.

In June 1663, Digby attempted to undermine Clarendon's management of the House of Commons. When Charles exposed the intrigue, Bristol was forced to appear before the House to exonerate himself; his "comedian-like speech," in which he admitted to "enlarging" the facts, became a source of general amusement. In early July, following a violent scene with the king, Digby issued fierce reproaches and threatened that if his requests were not granted within twenty-four hours, he would do something to "awaken" the king. On July 10, he impeached Clarendon for high treason in the Lords. After the charge was dismissed, he renewed the accusation and was expelled from court, spending two years in concealment to avoid a warrant for his apprehension.

In January 1664, Digby publicly renounced Roman Catholicism and declared himself a Protestant at his house in Wimbledon, likely to avoid charges of recusancy. He was eventually welcomed back to court when the fall of Clarendon became desired, taking his seat in the Lords on July 29, 1667.
