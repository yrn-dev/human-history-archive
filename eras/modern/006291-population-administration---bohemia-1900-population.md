# Population and Administration of Bohemia

## Population Statistics and Density
In 1900, Bohemia had a population of 6,318,280. This figure represents a significant increase from 1800, when the population was slightly over 3,000,000. In terms of total numbers among the Austrian provinces, Bohemia occupies the second place, following Galicia. Regarding population density, it ranks third, behind Lower Austria (which contains Vienna) and Silesia; the population of Bohemia in 1900 corresponded to 315 inhabitants per square mile.

## Nationality and Distribution
The population of Bohemia is divided by nationality into approximately 65% Czechs and 35% Germans. The distribution of these groups varies by region:
* **Czechs:** This group occupies the south, south-east, and the middle of the country.
* **Germans:** This group is concentrated in the large towns throughout the country and near the borders, particularly in the west and north.

The archive notes a dynamic shift in settlement patterns. There are numerous German-speaking enclaves located within purely Czech districts. Conversely, Czechs have shown a tendency to move into manufacturing and mining districts that were purely German. An example of this shift is seen in the town of Budweis; while it serves as the center of a German enclave in Czech Bohemia, the Czech element has steadily increased, reaching 60% of the town's population by 1908.

## Migration and Industry
Despite possessing great industrial development and rich natural resources, Bohemia experiences a steady flow of emigrants. These individuals settle in Russia, Germany, or other provinces of the monarchy, while others migrate across the Atlantic to America. 

Industrial activity is a hallmark of the region, placing Bohemia in the front rank of Austrian provinces alongside Moravia and Lower Austria. In the town of Budweis, for instance, there is a large and growing industry that includes the manufacture of machinery, paper, chemicals, bricks, tiles, matches, black-lead pencils, bell-founding, and corn and saw mills. Budweis also serves as the principal commercial center of South Bohemia, trading in industrial products, salt, lignite, timber, corn, and beer, with the latter being mostly exported to America.

## Religious and Ecclesiastical Administration
The vast majority of the population, 96%, belongs to the Roman Catholic Church. Administratively, the church divides Bohemia into the archbishopric of Prague and three bishoprics: Leitmeritz, Koniggratz, and Budweis. The see of the bishop in Budweis was established in 1783.

## Political and Constitutional Administration
The administration of Bohemia has been marked by tensions regarding its constitutional status within the Habsburg empire. In 1871, Count Karl Hohenwart, the Austrian prime minister, attempted to reach an agreement to re-establish Bohemia's ancient constitution in a modernized form. On September 14, 1871, an imperial message to the diet of Prague recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia, with the sovereign expressing willingness to confirm this via the coronation oath.

However, these efforts failed when a government with a pronounced German tendency took office in Vienna in 1872, leading Bohemians to temporarily refuse to attend the parliamentary assemblies of Prague and Vienna. In 1879, Count Eduard Taaffe became prime minister and persuaded Bohemian representatives to return to the parliament in Vienna. The Bohemians agreed, provided it was understood that they viewed Bohemia, along with Silesia and Moravia, as a separate state under the same sovereign as Hungary and Austria.

In recognition of this, the government of Count Taaffe made two significant administrative concessions:
1. The removal of some of the most gross anomalies in the electoral system, which had previously been heavily biased toward the German minority.
2. The foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague to aid the intellectual development of the country.
