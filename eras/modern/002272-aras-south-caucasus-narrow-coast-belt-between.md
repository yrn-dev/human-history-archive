# The Geography of the Baku Government and the Caucasus Region

The region encompassing the government of Baku in Russian Transcaucasia is characterized by a diverse landscape that stretches along the west coast of the Caspian Sea, from 38deg 30' to 41deg 50' N. lat. This administrative area covers 15,172 square miles and is bounded by Persia to the south, the province of Daghestan and the government of Elisavetpol to the west.

## Physical Terrain and Hydrography
The geography of the region is defined by the Caucasus mountain system, which lies between the Caspian and the Euxine. The eastern extremity of this range extends to the Caspian Sea, where it terminates in the Apsheron peninsula. Notable peaks in this range include the Bazardyuz, reaching 14,727 feet, and the Shad-dagh, which rises to 13,960 feet.

The region's hydrography is marked by the presence of the Kura and Aras (also known as the Araxes) rivers. The Cyrus (now the Cur) flows from the higher regions of the Caucasus through Albania and Iberia before being joined by the Aras. This united stream eventually enters the Caspian on its west coast through twelve mouths. 

The terrain includes the Kuba plain, located on the north-east slope of the Caucasus, and the steppes of the lower Kura and Aras situated to the south of the Caucasus. Additionally, there is a narrow coast-belt located between the Caspian and the Anti-Caucasus, a region that partially surrounds the south-opening Kizil-agach Bay.

## Agriculture and Land Use
The land varies significantly in fertility and vegetation. Both slopes of the Caucasus are well irrigated and very fertile, featuring flourishing gardens, fields of rice and other cereals, and fine forests. While the steppes of the Kura are also fertile, they require artificial irrigation, particularly for the cultivation of cotton. Beyond these crops, cattle-breeding is practiced, and the mulberry and vine are grown extensively.

In contrast, the Apsheron peninsula is described as dry and bare of vegetation.

## Petroleum Industry of Baku
The Apsheron peninsula is the site of the famous petroleum wells of Baku. These wells reach depths between 700 and 1,700 feet to yield crude naphtha. Through distillation, this naphtha produces kerosene and petroleum; the heavier residue, known as *mazut*, is utilized as fuel—such as for the locomotives of the Transcaspian railway—and as lubricating oil.

The industry saw massive growth between 1863 and 1904, with output increasing from 5,500 tons of crude naphtha to 9,833,600 tons, although a serious fire in 1905 caused significant business injury. The oil fields are concentrated around the town of Baku:
* **Balakhany-Sabunchi-Romany**: The largest field (6 sq. m.), located 8-1/2 miles north of the town.
* **Bibi-Eybat**: Located 3-1/2 miles south.
* **The "black town" (Nobel's)**: Located 2 miles south-east.
* **The "white town" (Rothschild's)**: Located beyond the "black town."

For export, lighter oil is transported via pipes to Batum on the Black Sea. Heavier oils are moved by tank railway-cars to Poti, Novorossiysk, and Batum.

## Cultural and Historical Landmarks
The region contains historical sites of religious significance. Located 13 miles east of the town of Baku at Surakhani is a temple of the Parsee fire-worshippers, which is now disused.
