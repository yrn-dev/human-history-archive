# European Waters and Naval Conflict (1739–1748)

## Declaration of War and Strategic Planning
On October 23, 1739, Great Britain declared war on Spain. At the onset of the conflict, there was a universal belief that Spanish colonies would fall quickly if attacked. To achieve this, a plan for combined naval and military operations was developed to assault these colonies from both the east and the west.

The strategic plan involved two primary forces. One force, consisting of both naval and military elements, was placed under the command of Admiral Edward Vernon and tasked with attacking from the West Indies. The second force was commanded by Commodore George Anson (later Lord Anson), who was ordered to sail around Cape Horn to strike the Pacific coast.

## Early Operations and Failures
The initial hopeful scheme suffered from several setbacks, including bad preparations, delays, corruption within the dockyards, and unpatriotic squabbles between the military and naval officers involved. Despite these issues, Admiral Vernon succeeded on November 21, 1739, in capturing the Spanish harbour of Porto Bello, located in the present-day republic of Panama. This was considered a trifling success, as Vernon failed to prevent Spanish convoys from reaching Europe.

During this period, Spanish privateers operated with destructive effect against British trade, conducting cruises in both European waters and the West Indies.

## The Siege of Cartagena
Admiral Vernon was eventually joined by Sir Chaloner Ogle, who brought a strong body of troops and naval reinforcements. Together, they launched an attack on Cartagena, in what is now Colombia, which lasted from March 9 to April 24, 1741.

The attack failed, resulting in a dreadful loss of life for the assailants. The failure is attributed largely to the brutal insolence of the admiral and the incompetence of the military officers. Additionally, the delays in the British operation provided the Spanish admiral, Don Bias de Leso, sufficient time to prepare the defenses. Following this and two other unsuccessful attacks on Spanish territory, the war in the West Indies died down and did not revive until 1748.

## The Expedition of Lord Anson
The expedition led by George Anson departed England on September 18, 1740. The mission was plagued by poor provisions, a late start, and a force that was less strong than originally intended, consisting of only six ships.

Anson's cruise was characterized as a great feat of endurance and resolution. He harried the coasts of Peru and Chile and captured a Spanish galleon of immense value near the Philippines. However, the expedition suffered heavy losses; most of the vessels were either lost or failed to round Cape Horn. Anson returned to England on June 15, 1744, alone with his flagship, the "Centurion."
