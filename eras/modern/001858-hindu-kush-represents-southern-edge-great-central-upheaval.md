# The Hindu Kush

The Hindu Kush is a major mountain system in Asia that serves as a critical geographical and hydrographic barrier. It represents the southern edge of a great central upheaval or plateau, characterized by a topography that breaks into long spurs extending southwards. These spurs contain deep, rugged, and snow-capped altitudes that isolate the valleys of Kafiristan from one another.

## Continental Geography and Topography
The Hindu Kush is part of a great central water-parting or divide that stretches across the continent from the Black Sea, through the Elburz and the Hindu Kush, to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, eventually reaching the Bering Strait in the extreme north-east. While much of this continental divide remains below 5,000 feet in altitude, the Hindu Kush is among the portions that rise to great heights.

To the north, the plateau associated with the Hindu Kush slopes gradually toward the Oxus. This descent begins at an average altitude of 15,000 feet, falling to 4,000 feet near Faizabad in central Badakshan, and continuing down to 1,100 feet at Kunduz in Kataghan, where the landscape merges into flat plains bordering the Oxus.

## Regional Boundaries and Badakshan
The Hindu Kush defines several political and regional boundaries, most notably in the province of Badakshan on the north-east frontier of Afghanistan. The southern boundary of Badakshan follows the crest of the Hindu Kush as far as the Khawak pass, which leads into the Panjshir valley. East of the Khawak pass, the crest is occupied by the Kafirs. The boundary becomes definite again at the Dorah pass, which connects the Lutku valley leading to Chitral with Ishkashim and Zebak. From the Dorah eastwards, the crest of the Hindu Kush serves as the boundary until it joins the Sarikol and Muztagh ranges.

In the north, the slopes of the Hindu Kush are relatively short—some 8 to 10 miles—separating the southern boundary at the crest from the northern boundary along the Oxus stream. This creates a "bottle-shaped" extension of Badakshan into the Pamirs, narrow at the neck and swelling to the east.

## Hydrography and Transit
The drainage of the northern slopes of the Hindu Kush west of the Dorah pass is managed by the Kunduz and Kokcha rivers. The Kokcha river traverses Badakshan from the south-east to the north-west, with some sources located near Zebak. It drains the northern slopes through deep lateral valleys that run more or less parallel to the mountain crest toward the Khawak pass.

The region has historically served as a vital corridor for movement. A significant continental high-road exists from the Oxus (1,000 ft.) through Faizabad (4,000 ft.) and Zebak (8,500 ft.). From Ishkashim, the route continues via the Panja through the Pamirs, forming a trade route connecting China and Kashgar with Afghan Turkestan. Additionally, the Dorah or Nuksán Pass provides a crossing over the Hindu Kush watershed into Chitral.

## Historical and Migratory Significance
The Hindu Kush has functioned as a primary gateway for immigration into the Indian peninsula. The group of passes extending from the Khawak to the Kaoshan, as part of the Kabul valley route, allowed various central Asian groups—including the Huns, Goths, Jats, Yue-chi, and Sacae—to enter the Punjab. Later historic invasions from central Asia typically followed the route leading from Kabul to Delhi and Peshawar.

Regarding early populations, the Aryans are believed to have been settled north of the Hindu Kush before migrating south-eastwards around 1500 B.C. The tableland extending to the Hindu Kush is described as the cradle of all warring races.

## Exploration and Mapping
Knowledge of the Hindu Kush and its surrounding highlands was expanded through several 19th-century missions. The Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886 mapped the Oxus regions and Afghan Turkestan. During this period, a mission led by Captain (later Sir William) Lockhart crossed the Hindu Kush into Wakhan and returned to India via the Bashgol valley of Kafiristan. This expedition allowed Colonel Woodthorpe to determine the conformation of the Hindu Kush and verify previous explorations conducted by W.W. M'Nair.
