# Great Book-Collectors

*Great Book-Collectors* is a work authored by Charles and Mary Elton and published in 1893. It belongs to a category of literature produced to document the history and pedigrees of book-loving owners, alongside other similar works such as W.Y. Fletcher's *English Book-Collectors* (1902) and Guigard's *Nouvel armorial du bibliophile* (1890).

## The Psychology of Book Collecting
The desire to collect books is driven by appeals to the eye, the mind, or the imagination. A book may attract a collector through its physical beauty, which can be derived from the harmony of its print and paper, its illustrations, or its binding. In France, high prices for French first editions and Elzevirs are frequently attributed more to their 18th and 17th century jackets than to the texts themselves. In England, the appreciation for old bindings increased following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club; this led to a higher demand for embroidered bindings, English blind stamped bindings, and those attributable to Samuel Mearne during the time of Charles II.

Beyond aesthetics, books often appeal to the imagination through their associations with famous events or persons. Books that once belonged to poets, soldiers, great statesmen, or royalty—such as Mary queen of Scots—possess an interest that defies analysis, particularly if the volume bears marks of being valued by the owner or is linked to a striking incident in their life. Collectors also value the "pedigree" of a work, tracing its ownership through a long series of previous book-lovers.

## Types of Valued Editions
The collecting instinct often drives a desire for first editions. This is partly due to the associations created by the original printing and partly because first editions may be positively superior to reprints for the purposes of study. However, the value placed on some editions can be fanciful. For example, during the days of George III, collectors paid high prices for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics despite these having little value as texts and no possible association with the authors' personalities. Currently, such books are primarily collected as specimens of printing. Conversely, the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces has grown significantly, as evidenced by the sale of a first folio Shakespeare (1623) in 1906.

Other books hold imaginative interest because they caused the persecution of their authors or were condemned to be burnt, though this specific interest has appealed more to writers than to collectors.

## Intellectual and Historical Collecting
Some collectors focus on the intellectual appeal of books, seeking works of historical value that have not been reprinted or whose original editions are more authentic and convincing than modern versions. In this capacity, the collector acts as both a rival and a servant to historical students. 

Notable examples of this practice include:
* **Lord Crawford**: Collected papal bulls and proclamations from Ireland, Scotland, and England.
* **Prince Lucien Bonaparte**: Assembled a philological library (catalogued by V. Collins in 1894).
* **The Foxwell Collection**: A collection of early books on political economy presented to the university of London by the Goldsmiths' Company.

While libraries of societies and institutes now carry out much of this work, private collectors continue to operate in this field.

## Evolution of Book Collecting
Modern book-collecting is a relatively recent growth, despite early precursors under the Roman Empire and in the late middle ages. Medieval texts, such as the *Philobiblon* (attributed to Richard de Bury, 1345), focused on the delights of literature rather than collecting. Similarly, Sebastian Brant later argued that possessing books was a poor substitute for actual learning.

The emergence of the modern collector required a surplus of books to be available, similar to how the collection of old china required the proliferation of cups and saucers. Even after the invention of printing reduced book costs by approximately 80%, collectors did not appear immediately. Early prominent book owners were not necessarily collectors in the modern sense. For instance, Jean Grolier (1479-1565) bought books to encourage contemporary printers and provide friends with Renaissance scholarship. Other figures who assembled the best books of their era and bound them in leather with heraldic badges or coats of arms include the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou (1553-1617) in France, as well as Henry, prince of Wales (1594-1612), Lords Lumley and Arundel, and Archbishop Cranmer in England.

## Bibliographical Literature
The literature surrounding book collecting has evolved to reflect changing ideals. Early "omnivorous" collectors relied on general manuals such as J.G.T. Graesse's *Tresor de livres rares et precieux* (1859-1867), W.T. Lowndes' *Bibliographer's Manual of English Literature* (1834), and Brunet's *Manuel du libraire* (1810). In the modern era, these general manuals have been largely replaced by more detailed handbooks and bibliographies focusing on special books.
