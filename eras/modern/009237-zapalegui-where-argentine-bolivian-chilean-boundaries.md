# Boundary Disputes and Settlements: Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile

The boundaries between Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile have been shaped by territorial disputes, military conflict, and subsequent diplomatic treaties, particularly regarding access to the sea and the control of mineral-rich regions.

## The War of the Pacific and Bolivian Territorial Loss
The boundary between Bolivia and Chile was fundamentally altered by a conflict that began in 1879. Tensions rose when General Hilarion Daza, the Bolivian president, demanded a tax of 10 cents per quintal on nitrates exported from the country, threatening to seize nitrates if the levy was not paid. In response, the Chilean fleet blockaded the ports of Tocapilla, Cobija, and Antofagasta. On February 14, 1879, Colonel Sotomayor occupied Antofagasta, leading Bolivia to declare war on March 1.

Bolivia allied with Peru, but the conflict resulted in a Chilean victory. By the end of 1879, Chile possessed the province of Tarapaca. Following the surrender of Lima and Callao in 1881, the Chileans became the masters of Peru. A peace treaty signed on December 11, 1881, between Chile and Bolivia required Bolivia to cede its entire sea-coast, including the port of Cobija.

## Post-War Disputes and the 1905 Treaty
Following the war, disputes continued regarding the occupation of the former Bolivian provinces of Arica and Tacna under the Parto de Tregna of April 4, 1884. In 1895, there was a possibility that Chile would concede a sea outlet to Bolivia in exchange for recognition of Chilean ownership of Tacna and Arica. However, this conciliatory attitude ended when Chile discovered secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina.

The situation for Bolivia became more precarious when the boundary dispute between Argentina and Chile was finally settled, removing the possibility of Argentine support. Consequently, President Pando and his successor, Ismail Montes (who took office in 1904), sought the best possible terms for a settlement. A treaty ratified in 1905 compelled Bolivia to cede the maritime provinces occupied by Chile since the war of 1881, leaving the republic without a seaport. In exchange, Chile agreed to:
* Construct a railway from Arica to the Bolivian capital, La Paz, at its own expense.
* Grant Bolivians free transit through Chilean territory to specific coastal towns.
* Pay a cash indemnity to Bolivia and provide pecuniary assistance for the construction of other necessary railways.

## Regional Geography and the Atacama
The boundaries of these nations intersect in the Atacama Desert, an arid and saline region. This area covers the Chilean provinces of Antofagasta and Atacama, the Argentine territory of Los Andes, and the south-western corner of the Bolivian department of Potosi. The region is characterized by the Puna de Atacama, a mountainous volcanic area ranging from 7,000 to 13,500 feet in elevation.

Historically, the province of Atacama was considered Bolivian territory before 1842. In 1843, Chile organized the area extending north to the 26th parallel into the province of Atacama. Today, this desolate region is a major source of mineral wealth, including silver, copper, lead, nickel, cobalt, iron, borax, and nitrates.

## Infrastructure and Communications
To overcome the isolation of the region, several international agreements were made to develop railway networks. Beyond the Arica-to-La Paz line (295 m.), Argentina undertook the extension of its northern railway from Jujuy to the Bolivian frontier town of Tupiza. 

Additionally, Bolivia sought communication with the outside world through other neighbors. A 1903 treaty with Brazil provided for a 180 m. railway around the falls of the Madeira to give north-eastern Bolivia access to the Amazon, supported by a cash payment of £2,000,000 from Brazil. Within Bolivia, a 54 m. line from Guaqui on Lake Titicaca to Alto de La Paz connects the capital to the Peruvian port of Mollendo.

## Other Boundary Relations
Bolivia's boundaries also extend to Brazil. The frontier was originally determined by the treaties of Madrid (1750) and San Ildefonso (1777), later modified in 1867 and 1903. A significant dispute occurred over the Acre rubber-producing territory, where Brazilian settlers resented Bolivian authority, leading to a declaration of independence by the state of Acre. This was settled by the Treaty of Petropolis in November 1903, in which Bolivia ceded a portion of the Acre province to Brazil in exchange for a cash indemnity of $10,000,000.

In the south-east, the drainage basin extends from the Sierras de Chiquitos to the Argentine frontier. The Pilcomayo river flows through the Bolivian Chaco toward the Paraguay river, while the Bermejo, an Argentine river, receives the Tarija (or Rio Grande) as a tributary from the Bolivian uplands.
