# America

The history of the Americas is characterized by diverse religious developments, the establishment of various cities and educational institutions, and the influence of missionary work.

## Early Religious Foundations and Missions
In North America, the first Baptist Church was established in the Providence settlement on Narragansett Bay. This was led by Roger Williams, who had been sentenced to banishment by the Massachusetts Court in October 1635 due to his separatistic views. After fleeing to the wilderness in January 1636 and receiving land from the natives, Williams founded a commonwealth based on civil democracy and absolute religious liberty. In March 1639, Williams and eleven others restored believers' baptism, rejecting infant baptism as a perversion of Christian ordinance. Ezekiel Holliman first baptized Williams, who then baptized the others. Although Williams later became a "seeker," believing apostolic organization had been lost, he maintained friendly relations with the Providence Baptists.

Additionally, the Order of the Jesuits served as the chief Christian missionaries in North America, contributing to the church's educational and missionary reach.

## Methodism and Francis Asbury
Methodism was introduced to the American colonies three years prior to October 1771, when Francis Asbury arrived in Philadelphia. At that time, there were fewer than 300 converts. Asbury, an English clergyman born in 1745 and enrolled as an itinerant minister by John Wesley in 1767, significantly expanded the movement, doubling membership within a year. In 1772, Wesley appointed him "general assistant" for America.

During the War of Independence, Methodists faced suspicion of Loyalism because they refused to take a prescribed oath. While many ministers returned to England, Asbury remained, despite being arrested and threatened. The Maryland authorities eventually forced him into exile in Delaware for two years. He was reappointed to supervise American Methodist congregations in 1782, and in 1784, John Wesley appointed him and the Rev. Thomas Coke as "bishops" or superintendents of the church in the United States. Asbury's *Journals* provide a commentary on the social and industrial history of the United States during its first forty years.

Other notable figures in the movement included Peter Cartwright (1785-1872), a "backwoods preacher" born in Virginia. Cartwright was ordained as a deacon by Bishop Asbury in 1806. Throughout his career, which included serving as presiding elder in the Wabash, Green river, and Cumberland districts, Cartwright preached approximately 14,600 times and baptized some 12,000 persons.

## Cities and Institutions
Several American cities and institutions bear the legacy of these developments or reflect regional growth:

*   **Asbury Park, New Jersey:** Founded in 1869 and named in honor of the Rev. Francis Asbury, this Atlantic Ocean seaside resort was incorporated as a borough in 1874 and chartered as a city in 1897. It is known for its beach, a one-mile boardwalk, and its status as a popular summer destination.
*   **Greencastle, Indiana:** Settled around 1820 and chartered as a city in 1861, Greencastle is the county-seat of Putnam county. It is an industrial center producing lumber, pumps, and tin-plate, and serves as a shipping point for beef cattle. The city is home to de Pauw University, a Methodist Episcopal institution founded in 1837 as Indiana Asbury University. The university includes the Asbury College of Liberal Arts and a School of Music and Art.

## Central America and Costa Rica
In Central America, Costa Rica was a province of the Spanish captaincy-general of Guatemala until 1821. The conquest was completed by 1530, and it became a separate province ten years later. While Governor Juan Vazquez de Coronado (appointed 1562) was considered humane, subsequent maladministration led to the enslavement of Indians and a focus on the quest for gold. By 1718, Governor Diego de la Haya y Fernandez reported that no province of Spanish America was in a more wretched condition. By the period between 1797 and 1809, Governor Tomas de Acosta noted that Indians were often clothed in bark and sometimes had to borrow such attire to attend church.

## Cultural Contributions
The United States also produced significant figures in the arts, such as Dudley Buck (1839-1909). Born in Hartford, Connecticut, Buck studied in Paris, Dresden, and Leipzig. He served as an organist in Hartford, Chicago, and Boston, and later at Holy Trinity church in New York. He became a well-known composer of church music, symphonies, and operas, including the comic opera *Deseret* (1880) and the cantata *Columbus* (1876).
