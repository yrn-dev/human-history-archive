# Stockbroker

A stockbroker is a specific type of broker who contracts for the sale of shares and stocks. While they fall under the broader category of mercantile agents, they differ from brokers proper primarily because stocks and shares are not classified as "goods." Consequently, the requirement for a memorandum in writing mandated by the Sale of Goods Act 1893 does not apply to them. This allows principals in a contract for the sale of shares and stocks to bring actions even in the absence of a written memorandum.

## Definition and Etymology
The term "broker" is derived from the Latin *brocca* (spit or spike) and *broccare* (to broach), which led to the Old French *vendre à broche* (to retail, such as wine from a tap), eventually evolving into the general sense of dealing. In a primary sense, a broker is a general mercantile agent whose role is to bring together intending buyers and sellers to facilitate a contract in exchange for remuneration, known as commission or brokerage. 

While brokers originally negotiated only the purchase and sale of commodities (such as produce, wool, or cotton), the modern use of the term includes various mercantile agents, including bill-brokers, ship-brokers, insurance-brokers, and stockbrokers. Pawnbrokers are distinguished from brokers in that they deal as principals rather than acting as agents.

## Legal Relations and Duties
A broker's primary function is to bring a third party and a principal into privity of contract; unlike a factor, a broker does not possess the principal's goods and cannot buy or sell in their own name unless expressly authorized. Once the contract is formed, the broker typically drops out of the transaction.

The broker owes several duties to the principal:
*   **Performance:** They must carry out instructions with skill, diligence, and perfect good faith.
*   **Commercial Accuracy:** They must ensure the bargain's terms—including price, quantity, and quality—accord with the principal's orders.
*   **Enforceability:** They must ensure the resulting contract is legally enforceable by the principal against the third party.
*   **Conflict of Interest:** They must not accept commissions from the third party or enter positions where their interests oppose those of the principal.

Regarding third parties, a broker who signs a contract note specifically "as broker" on behalf of a principal is not personally liable. However, if the broker makes the contract in a manner that makes them a party to it, the third party may sue either the broker or the principal.

## Stock Exchange Rules and Customs
The employment of a stockbroker is governed by the rules and customs of the Stock Exchange. These rules can modify ordinary law. For example, among members of an exchange, all are treated as principals, and no agents are recognized. A broker employed by a non-member to buy on the market is treated as buying for themselves and is therefore personally liable on the contract. In "futures" contracts, the broker must follow weekly settlement rules; if the principal fails to take delivery, the broker must pay the seller any difference between the contract and market price at the delivery date.

Some customs may be illegal and thus not binding on a client. An example is the Stock Exchange practice of disregarding Leeman's Act (1867), which requires the registered numbers of joint-stock bank shares to be stated for a contract to be valid. While such a contract may be unenforceable, a broker is still entitled to be indemnified by a client who instructed them to buy shares according to that specific practice.

## The "Bucketshop"
The term "bucketshop" is an American slang financial term originating in Chicago. It refers to the business of an inferior class of stockbroker who is not a member of an official exchange. Rather than attempting actual purchases or sales, these brokers conduct speculative operations for clients who deposit a cover or margin; these operations are essentially wagers or bets between the broker and the client. 

The term arose when the Chicago Board of Trade forbade "options" in grain of less than 5,000 bushels, leading to the creation of an "Open Board of Trade" for small gamblers in a neighboring street. The name stems from the lift used by Board of Trade members, which was sent down to bring up "bucketfuls" of smaller speculators when business was slack.
