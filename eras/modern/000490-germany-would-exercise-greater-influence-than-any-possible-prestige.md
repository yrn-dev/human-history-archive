# Germany: Influence and Prestige in European History

The historical trajectory of Germany and its relationship with neighboring powers has been characterized by a shifting balance between formal prestige—often derived from titles or traditions—and practical influence exerted through diplomacy, military force, and economic control.

## The Congress of Vienna and Austrian Hegemony
Following the conquests of Napoleon, the international congress summoned in Vienna in September 1814 sought to re-establish the European balance of power. For Austria, the result was a victory for the diplomacy of Metternich. While Metternich could not prevent Russia from retaining the grand-duchy of Warsaw, he successfully used a secret treaty with France and Great Britain on January 3, 1815, to stop Prussia from absorbing all of Saxony. Austria also regained Dalmatia and Illyria, and added Lombardy to Venetia to create a kingdom under the Habsburg crown.

In Germany, the settlement was particularly significant. Despite protests from the Holy See, the Holy Empire was not restored. Austria instead preferred a *Staatenbund*, a loose confederation of sovereign states under Austrian presidency. Metternich believed this body would be "powerless for offence" but "powerful for defence," thereby guaranteeing the peace of central Europe and ensuring Austrian preponderance. He argued that Austrian diplomacy, supported by the weight of Habsburg power outside German borders, would exercise a greater influence than any prestige derived from a "venerable title" that had become associated with practical impotence and unlimited pretensions.

However, this period also saw Austria abdicate its role as the bulwark of Germany against France. By relinquishing claims to other outlying territories in western Germany and the Belgian provinces, and by accepting the establishment of Prussia in the Rhine provinces, Austria accelerated its own gravitation toward the Slavs.

## The Struggle for German Unification
The prestige of the imperial tradition and the army served as the primary "sheet-anchors" for the Habsburg monarchy during subsequent storms. In Germany, the prestige of the imperial tradition was especially valuable in delaying settlements until the victory of Radetzky at Novara allowed Austria to once again back its policies with military force.

During the meeting of the parliament of united Germany in Frankfort on May 18, 1848, a dilemma arose regarding Austria's place in a united Germany. The Austrian government refused any settlement that would split the monarchy in half by including only its German provinces. Conversely, German patriots were unwilling to include a vast non-German population within Germany. Although the exclusion of Austria and the offering of the crown to Frederick William of Prussia was a possible solution, German statesmen remained influenced by the "shadow of the Holy Empire" and the tradition of Austrian hegemony. This hesitation lasted until Austria was again able to re-establish the system devised at the Congress of Vienna.

## Economic Influence and the Reichsbank
In the later Imperial era, German influence was exerted through powerful financial institutions. The Reichsbank became the most powerful banking institution in Germany, managed by a directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire. The government maintained complete control through the chancellor, while shareholders participated via a committee requiring a minimum holding of three shares.

Following the introduction of uniform coinage through laws passed between 1871 and 1873, the influence of the Imperial Bank permeated all separate kingdoms of the empire through its branches. The bank assisted business primarily through note circulation and the development of a clearing system known as *Giro-Verkehr*. While the bank received deposits and allowed cheques, notes remained the primary method for ordinary business payments in Germany.

## Global Ambitions and the Decline of Prestige
Unlike the English model, Germany did not seek colonies for white settlement. Due to a shortage of labor in German agriculture and a desire for the highest possible population concentration in the fatherland for military and industrial purposes, Germany lacked the settlers for such endeavors. As expressed by Baron von Rechenberg, former governor of German East Africa, Germany sought strategic positions and raw materials (specifically from Central Africa) rather than territory for settlement.

In the lead-up to the Great War, Britain attempted to exercise a "mitigating and modifying influence" to procure accommodation regarding German grievances over Anglo-French agreements in Morocco and Egypt. However, British leadership remained wary that if Germany's intentions were malignant, such diplomatic processes would be useless.

By the time of the conflict in southern Russia, German prestige began to crumble. Although Teutonic corps of occupation held the region, they were described as "mere islets" ruling more by prestige than physical power. As the Allied fleets opened the Black Sea following Turkey's surrender, disaffection grew from the Ukraine to the Caucasus, and the Don Cossacks remained unsubdued. Simultaneously, the Habsburg realm faced a desperate condition, with non-German and non-Magyar peoples in a state of suppressed revolt.
