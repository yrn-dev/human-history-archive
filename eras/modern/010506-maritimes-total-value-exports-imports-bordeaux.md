# Bordeaux: Maritime Trade and Commerce

Bordeaux serves as the fourth largest port in France, with its commercial activity conducted primarily by sea. The port is formed by the basin of the Garonne and extends 5-1/2 miles in length, with an average width of 550 yards. The Pont de Bordeaux divides the port into two distinct sections: the southern portion is utilized only by small craft, while the northern portion is accessible to vessels drawing between 21 and 26 feet, depending on the tide.

## Port Infrastructure and Capacity
The harbor is equipped with sloping wharves and quays on both sides, which are served by railway lines. The facility can accommodate between 1,000 and 1,200 vessels. At the northern extremity of the harbor on the left bank, there is a floating basin covering 25 acres. This basin is designed to receive the largest vessels and features over 1,900 yards of quays, a repairing dock, and elaborate machinery for unloading and loading goods. Further expansion of the port began in 1907 with the construction of new docks behind this basin.

## Trade Volume and Value
The total value of the imports and exports of Bordeaux averages between 25 and 26 million sterling annually. Of this total, exports account for 13-1/2 million sterling. The city maintains commercial relations with nearly all countries, though its primary partners are the United States, Portugal, Argentina, Spain, and Great Britain. The South American service of the Messageries Maritimes represents the most important line of steamers utilizing the port.

## Exports and the Wine Trade
Bordeaux is the center of the trade in "Bordeaux" wines, and the wine-cellars located along the quays are a principal sight of the city. The sales of wine account for approximately one quarter of the total export value. Other primary exports include:
*   Brandy
*   Hides and skins
*   Sugar and rice
*   Woollen and cotton goods
*   Chemicals
*   Oil-cake and pitwood
*   Salt-fish
*   Fruit, potatoes, and other vegetables

## Imports and Maritime Activities
The chief imports arriving at the port include:
*   Wool and fish
*   Timber and rice
*   Wine
*   Rubber and coal
*   Oil-grains
*   Hardware
*   Chemicals
*   Agricultural and other machinery

Beyond general commerce, a large fleet is dispatched annually from Bordeaux to the cod-fisheries of Iceland and Newfoundland.

## Related Industries
The most significant industry in the region is the refitting and building of ships, including merchant vessels, torpedo-boats, and ironclads. The city also produces railway carriages. Additionally, there are various industries subsidiary to the wine trade, including cooperage, wine-mixing, and the manufacture of capsules, corks, bottles, wooden cases, and straw envelopes.
