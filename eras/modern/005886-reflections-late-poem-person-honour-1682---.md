# Poetical Reflections on a Late Poem ... by a Person of Honour, 1682

*Poetical Reflections on a late Poem ... by a Person of Honour, 1682* is a literary work written by Buckingham. The piece was composed as a direct response to the celebrated lines in *Absalom and Achitophel*, a poem by John Dryden in which Buckingham had been described under the character of Zimri.

## Context and Authorship
The author of the *Reflections*, Buckingham, was a figure of the 17th century often compared to Alcibiades. He was regarded by his contemporaries as irresistible, possessing good looks and a reputation for being generous, good-natured, and good-humoured. He was recognized as a leader of fashion and an unsurpassed mimic.

Contemporary accounts of Buckingham highlight his versatility and social standing. Reresby and Burnet both testified that he was the first gentleman of wit and person they had ever seen. Dean Lockier noted his grace in the presence-chamber and his unrivaled skills in fencing, dancing, and riding. His interests were diverse; he was a patron to Wycherley, Sprat, Cowley, and Matthew Clifford. He also engaged in chemistry, spending years attempting to find the philosopher's stone, and established glass works at Lambeth, the output of which received praise from Evelyn. Additionally, his biographer Brian Fairfax noted that Buckingham spent significant sums on the construction of *insanae substructions*.

## Relationship with John Dryden
The impetus for the *Reflections* was the work of John Dryden, who is described as the master of expression in 17th-century English poetry, prose, and drama. Dryden's satirical power reached its peak in works such as *Mac Flecknoe*, *The Medal*, and *Absalom and Achitophel*. 

*Absalom and Achitophel* (published in two parts in 1681 and 1682) is noted for its rhetorical and metrical skill, as well as a mixture of "Olympian unfairness" and "deadly good temper." In this poem, Dryden presented various figures of the era, including the earl of Shaftesbury as Achitophel and the minstrel-king of Judah as Charles. It was within this specific work that Dryden utilized the character of Zimri to describe Buckingham, prompting Buckingham to write the *Poetical Reflections* in 1682.
