# France: Regional Geography and Departments

The geography of southern and south-central France is characterized by the central plateau, the Massif Central, and a complex network of river systems and limestone table-lands known as *causses*. This region is divided into several departments, each with distinct topographical and economic profiles.

## The River Systems of the South
The Garonne river serves as a primary drainage point for several major tributaries in southern France. Among these is the Lot, a river approximately 300 meters in length that rises in the Cévennes on the Mont du Goulet at an altitude of 4,918 feet. The Lot flows westward across the central plateau through the departments of Lozère, Aveyron, Lot, and Lot-et-Garonne, eventually entering the Garonne at Aiguillon. Its volume is significantly increased at Entraygues, where it is joined by its largest tributary, the Truyère. Other tributaries include the Dourdou de Bozouls (or du Nord) and the Célé. The Lot is canalized for 160 meters between the Garonne and Bouquiès.

In the east, the Doubs river rises in the Jura at the Noirmont ridge (3,074 feet) and flows into the Saône. Its 269-meter course includes the lake of St Point and the Falls of the Doubs, and it forms a portion of the frontier between France and Switzerland.

## The Department of Aveyron
Aveyron is a department of southern France located on the southern border of the central plateau. It corresponds nearly to the old district of Rouergue, which was a countship established in the early 9th century and united with Toulouse toward the end of the 11th century. The region's earliest known inhabitants were the Celtic Rutheni, who are credited with the area's numerous megalithic monuments.

The department is divided into four zones by three chief rivers—all tributaries of the Garonne: the Lot in the north, the Aveyron in the center, and the Tarn in the south. 
* **North-east:** The pastoral plateau of the Viadene is dominated by the volcanic mountains of Aubrac, which contain the department's highest summit at 4,760 feet.
* **Central Belt:** Between the Lot and Aveyron rivers lies a region of monotonous limestone table-lands (*causses*) broken by beautiful gorges.
* **Segala Region:** Located between the Tarn and Aveyron, this area is bordered by the heights of Palanges and Levezou and is traversed by the valley of the Viaur.
* **South:** The area south of the Tarn is largely occupied by the plateau of Larzac, situated between the Causse St Affrique and the Causse Noir. The latter features Montpellier-le-Vieux, a chaos of rocks resembling a ruined city.

## The Department of Lot
Formed in 1790 from the district of Quercy in the old province of Guyenne, the department of Lot extends over the western portion of the Massif Central. It slopes toward the south-west, with altitudes ranging from 213 feet to 2,560 feet. The department is characterized by several limestone plateaus: the Causse de Martel in the north, the Causse de Gramat (or de Rocamadour) in the center, and the Causse de Cahors in the south. These *causses* are often arid due to *igues*—clefts and chasms in the limestone—the most famous of which is the Gouffre de Padirac.

The city of Cahors, the department's capital, is situated on a rocky peninsula formed by a bend in the river Lot. It is noted for the Pont Valentré, a 14th-century fortified bridge, and the 12th-century cathedral of St Étienne. Other significant structures include the palace of Pope John XXII and the Logis du Roi.

## Other Southern and Central Departments
**Lozère:** The highest French department in average elevation, Lozère is composed mainly of Gévaudan and parts of the old dioceses of Alais and Uzès. It features three regions: the Cévennes (including Mont Aigoual at over 5,100 feet), the *causses*, and various mountain tracts. The peak of Finiels is the highest point at 5,584 feet.

**Dordogne:** Formed in 1790 from Perigord, Agenais, and parts of Angoumois and Limousin, this department is situated on the western slopes of the Massif Central. It is known for its fertile river valleys, the truffles of Perigord, and the cultivation of tobacco and vines, particularly near Bergerac.

**Lot-et-Garonne:** Also formed in 1790, this department consists of parts of Gascony and the old province of Guienne. It is one of the richest agricultural departments, producing wheat, maize, tobacco, and noted apricots from Tonneins and Nicole.

**Correze:** A south-central department formed from the province of Limousin. It is a hilly tableland where the Plateau de Millevaches separates the basins of the Garonne and the Loire. Its economy is based on agriculture (rye, buckwheat, and wheat) and industry, including a government fire-arms manufactory at Tulle.

## Eastern Districts
In eastern France, the Bresse district embraces portions of the Jura, Saône-et-Loire, and Ain departments. A plain varying from 600 to 800 feet above sea level, it is celebrated for its table poultry. Originally belonging to the lords of Bâgé and later the house of Savoy, it was ceded to France in 1601 via the treaty of Lyons.
