# France and Corsica

Corsica is a large Mediterranean island that currently forms a department of France. It is located north of Sardinia, separated by the strait of Bonifacio, and lies between 41 deg. 21' and 43 deg. N. and 8 deg. 30' and 9 deg. 30' E. The island covers an area of 3,367 square miles and had a population of 291,160 in 1906. Geographically, the island is characterized by forest-clad mountains with a central ridge running from the northwest to the southwest. Its highest peaks include Monts Cinto (8,881 ft.), Rotondo (8,612 ft.), Paglia Orba (8,284 ft.), Padro (7,581 ft.), and d'Oro (7,485 ft.). The eastern coast between Porto Vecchio and Bastia features a tract of fertile but unhealthy low country.

## Early History and Transitions of Power
During the collapse of the Western Roman Empire, the Vandals and the Gothic allies of the Roman emperors disputed possession of the island until 534, when Cyril, lieutenant to Belisarius, conquered Corsica for the East Roman empire. This period was marked by excessive taxation and raids by Lombards and Goths. In 713, the first descent of Mussulmans from northern Africa occurred.

The island passed to the Franks after Charlemagne overthrew the Lombard power in Italy in 774. Throughout the 9th century, the island faced repeated Moorish incursions from Spain. In 828, Boniface II, count of the Tuscan march, defended the island against African Mussulmans and established a fortress in the south, which became the nucleus of the town of Bonifacio.

In the early 15th century, France and Genoa vied for control. In 1407, Leonello Lomellino served as governor with the title of count of Corsica bestowed by Charles VI of France. However, Vincentello d' Istria, who served the king of Aragon, captured Bastia and Cinarca, leading to a period where most of the island—except for Calvi and Bonifacio—was independent of France by 1410. Later, during the Great Schism, Corsican clergy were divided between the suffragans of Genoa (supporting Benedict XIII) and those of Pisa (supporting John XXIII). Vincentello returned with an Aragonese force, capturing Ajaccio and Cinarca and building a castle at Corte by 1419.

## French Conquest and the Bonaparte Family
Following the French conquest of Corsica, Carlo Buonaparte (1746-1785) became an assessor to the royal court of Ajaccio. A Voltairian by conviction, Carlo encouraged his children to pursue careers in France. His wife, Letizia Ramolino, was a devout woman who sought to bind her children to Corsica. Their sons included Joseph, born in 1768, and Napoleon, born in 1769.

For twenty years, Corsica remained a dependency of the French crown. Following the Revolution of 1789, the island was incorporated into France as a separate department. This period saw the rise of the Jacobin Club in Corsican towns, which often usurped regular government functions.

## Political Conflict and British Occupation
The political landscape of the island was split between the democratic Jacobins and the more conservative party led by Paoli. Paoli, initially acclaimed as the "father of the country" upon his return in 1790, grew dissatisfied with the methods of the Terror. After being summoned by the Convention in 1793, Paoli rebelled against the dominant French faction and was declared *hors la loi* on June 26.

Lucien Bonaparte (1775-1840), an ardent supporter of the anti-clerical and democratic movement, became a leading speaker in the Jacobin club at Ajaccio. He urged his brothers to break with Paoli and led a deputation to France to denounce him. When the Paolists gained the upper hand, the Bonapartes fled to Toulon.

By the summer of 1794, the British government, responding to Paoli's appeal for assistance, reduced the island through hard fighting. In June 1794, the Corsican assembly offered sovereignty to King George III. The British occupation lasted until October 1796, when Napoleon Bonaparte sent an expedition that returned the island to French hands. While Great Britain briefly re-occupied the island in 1814, it was restored to the French crown in the settlement of 1815.

## Notable Figures in French and Corsican History
Several individuals of Corsican descent rose to prominence in French service:
* **Joseph Bonaparte (1768-1844):** A barrister and councillor of Ajaccio who served as a minister to the courts of Parma and Rome. He later served as a member for Corsica in the Council of Five Hundred.
* **Raphael Casabianca (1738-1825):** A general who took the side of France against Genoa in 1769. He defended Calvi against the English and later entered the senate.
* **Louis de Casabianca (1762-1798):** A naval captain who represented Corsica in the Convention and died during the battle of the Nile while commanding the *Orient*.
* **Étienne Cabet (1788-1856):** A French communist born in Dijon who served as *procureur-général* in Corsica under Louis Philippe before being dismissed for his criticisms of the government.
