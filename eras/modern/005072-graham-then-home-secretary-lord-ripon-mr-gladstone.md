# Political Secession of 1834

The political landscape of the 1830s was marked by significant shifts in cabinet loyalty, particularly regarding the administration of the Whig party and the handling of ecclesiastical matters in Ireland. A notable turning point occurred in 1834, centered on the government's proposal concerning the Irish Church.

## The Irish Church Question and Cabinet Secession
In 1834, the government proposed to appropriate surplus revenues from the church to be used for educational purposes. This proposal became a catalyst for a major rift within the cabinet. The issue led to the secession of several members from the government, marking a definitive and final separation from the Whig party for some of the involved statesmen.

Among those who seceded from the cabinet during this event were:
*   Sir James Graham
*   The earl of Ripon
*   The duke of Richmond
*   Lord Stanley

The nature of this political departure was noted by contemporaries; O'Connell described the secession in a couplet from Canning's *Loves of the Triangles*, referring to the group as "six insides" in a "Derby dilly" gliding down "romantic Ashbourne."

## Related Political Figures
The individuals involved in the 1834 secession held various high-ranking positions. Lord Stanley, for instance, had been promoted to secretary for the colonies with a seat in the cabinet in 1833, shortly before the introduction of the Irish Church Temporalities Bill. In that role, Stanley was responsible for the practical implementation of the emancipation of slaves, delivering a speech on May 14, 1833, to introduce the bill for freeing slaves in the West Indies.

Other figures mentioned in the archive's records of this era include Lord Ripon, who later appeared as a venerable figure in the government of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman.

## Context of Other Political Administrations
While the 1834 secession was a pivotal moment for the Whigs, the archive records various other Liberal and Conservative administrations. For example, Mr. Gladstone served as a prominent leader whose cabinets included figures such as Lord Kimberley, who held roles as Lord Privy Seal, secretary of state for the colonies, and secretary of state for India. 

In later years, the Liberal Party saw the rise of Herbert Henry Asquith. Asquith served as home secretary in a Liberal ministry after being selected by Mr. Gladstone in August 1892. As home secretary, Asquith was noted for appointing women as factory inspectors and opening Trafalgar Square for Labour demonstrations. Asquith eventually became prime minister on April 8, 1908, following the resignation of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman.
