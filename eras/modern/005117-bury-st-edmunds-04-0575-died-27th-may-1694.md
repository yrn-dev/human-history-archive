# Bury St Edmunds

Bury St Edmunds, also known as Beodricesworth or St Edmund's Bury, is a town that some suggest may have been the Roman Villa Faustina. Historically, it was one of the royal towns of the Saxons.

## Early History and Religious Significance
The town's early celebrity was largely due to the reputed miracles performed at the shrine of the martyr king, Edmund. King Sigebert of the East Angles founded a monastery in the town around 633. In 903, this site became the burial place of King Edmund, who had been slain by the Danes approximately 870. By 925, the fame of St Edmund had spread extensively, leading to the town being renamed St Edmund's Bury.

In 1020, Sweyn destroyed the existing monastery and ejected the secular priests to build a Benedictine abbey on the site. The town's governance was heavily influenced by the abbey; in 942 or 945, King Edmund granted the abbot and convent jurisdiction over the entire town, exempting it from all secular services. In 1020, Canute freed the town from episcopal control, and Edward the Confessor later designated the abbot as the lord of the franchise.

## Development as a Borough
Bury St Edmunds gradually achieved the status of a borough through various grants from the abbots. Royal charters further defined its commercial and legal standing:
* **1235:** Henry III granted the abbot two annual fairs, including one in December and the great St Matthew's fair (the latter was abolished by the Fairs Act of 1871).
* **1405:** Henry IV granted an additional fair.
* **1562:** Elizabeth confirmed charters previously granted to the abbots.
* **1606:** James I granted a charter of incorporation, along with a market and an annual fair held during Easter week.
* **1608 and 1614:** Further charters were granted by James I.
* **1668 and 1684:** Additional charters were granted by Charles II.

James I also granted the corporation the reversion of two markets (held on Wednesday and Saturday) and the fairs in fee farm. Economically, the town formerly supported a large woollen trade.

## Political Representation
The town served as a site for Parliaments in 1272, 1296, and 1446. However, it did not have parliamentary representation until 1608, when James I conferred the privilege of sending two members. This representation was later reduced to one member by the Redistribution Act of 1885.

The borough was frequently represented by members of the Hervey family, who held the estate of Ickworth in Suffolk since the 15th century. Notable representatives included:
* **Sir Thomas Hervey:** Member of parliament who died on May 27, 1694.
* **John Hervey, 1st Earl of Bristol:** Became member of parliament in March 1694.
* **Carr, Lord Hervey (1691-1723):** Member from 1713 to 1722.
* **Thomas Hervey (1699-1775):** Member from 1733 to 1747.
* **Felton Hervey (1712-1773):** Also served as a member for the family borough.
* **Frederick William Hervey, 2nd Marquess of Bristol:** Member from 1830 to 1859.
* **Frederick William Fane Hervey:** Member from 1906 to 1907.

## Other Notable Figures
The town is associated with Edmund Calamy "the elder" (1600-1666), an English Presbyterian divine of Huguenot descent. Calamy moved to Bury St Edmunds and served as a lecturer there for ten years. He eventually retired from this position when his bishop, Wren, insisted on the observance of specific ceremonial articles.
