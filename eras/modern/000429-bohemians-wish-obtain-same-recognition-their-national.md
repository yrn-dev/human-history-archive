# Bohemian National Recognition and the Habsburg Monarchy

The struggle for national recognition and autonomy in Bohemia was a central theme of its political history under the rule of the Habsburg dynasty. This conflict manifested through disputes over constitutional rights, the status of the national language, and the desire for a level of independence comparable to that granted to other territories within the empire.

## Accession of the Habsburgs and Early Governance
The permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne began on October 23, 1526, when the diet elected Ferdinand of Habsburg as king. While Ferdinand showed initial moderation in religious matters, he focused on establishing the hereditary right of his dynasty. In 1541, following a great fire in Prague that destroyed state documents, Ferdinand replaced a charter stating he was king by election with one asserting his hereditary rights through his wife, Anna. This change caused significant dissatisfaction and contributed to subsequent troubles.

Later, under King Matthias, the question of nationality became predominant during a general diet in 1615. This meeting reaffirmed enactments enforcing the use of the national language and decreed that Bohemian be the official ("authorized") language of the country. However, tensions rose again when Matthias's successor, Ferdinand, duke of Styria—a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome—was crowned in 1617. This period saw a shift toward ultramontane policies, leading to a conflict between the Catholic Church and Bohemian Protestants, which precipitated violent events at the Hradcany palace in May 1618.

## Erosion of Autonomy
Following the Thirty Years' War, the autonomy of Bohemia was progressively limited. In 1720, the estates sanctioned the female succession to the throne and recognized the Pragmatic Sanction, which proclaimed the indivisibility of the Habsburg realm. Under Maria Theresa and her son Joseph II, internal administration changes further reduced Bohemian autonomy. A 1749 decree abolished separate Bohemian law-courts, and an Austro-Bohemian chancellor was later appointed to control the administration of both Bohemia and the German domains of the house of Habsburg.

By the end of 1849, constitutional government in Bohemia had ceased entirely. A severe reaction followed, during which attempts to obtain liberty and self-government were suppressed. The authorities made a determined effort to exterminate the national language; German became the exclusive language of government offices and schools, and Bohemian newspapers were suppressed. Even the society of the Bohemian museum was forced to hold meetings under police supervision.

## 19th Century Constitutional Struggles
In 1871, there was a brief possibility that the Bohemians would see their ancient constitution re-established in a modernized form. The Austrian prime minister, Count Karl Hohenwart, sought an agreement that would satisfy Bohemian claims while keeping them connected to the Habsburg empire. On September 14, 1871, an imperial message to the diet of Prague recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia, with the sovereign expressing willingness to confirm this by taking the coronation oath.

This attempt failed due to various influences, and by 1872, a government with a pronounced German tendency took office in Vienna. Consequently, Bohemians temporarily refused to attend parliamentary assemblies in Prague and Vienna. In 1879, Count Eduard Taaffe became prime minister and persuaded Bohemian representatives to return to the parliament in Vienna. They agreed to do so on the condition that this step did not prejudice their view that Bohemia, along with Moravia and Silesia, constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. In return, Taaffe's government removed some of the grossest anomalies in the electoral system, which had previously favored the German minority, and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.

## The Language Question and National Independence
The title "emperor of Austria," established in 1804 by Francis II, was often resented by Slavs and Magyars, as it implied the superiority of German elements and ignored national rights. Bohemians, in particular, sought the same recognition of national independence that had been conceded to Hungary via the compact of 1867.

In the late 19th century, Count Badeni attempted to conciliate Bohemia. While he did not believe reopening the question of autonomy was feasible, he sought to remedy grievances. In early 1897, Badeni issued a decree requiring government officials in Bohemia to prove knowledge of both the Bohemian and German languages. This move faced violent opposition from German inhabitants of Austria, leading to the fall of Badeni's cabinet at the end of 1897. His successors, Count Thun and Count Clary, repealed the decrees that had granted equal rights to the Bohemian language. This triggered troubles in Prague, which were severely repressed by Austrian authorities. Despite this, Bohemians continued to assert their national rights and oppose the central government in Vienna.
