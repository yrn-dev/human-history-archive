# Japan

## Early Relations and Regional Diplomacy
In early historical periods, Japan engaged in diplomatic missions involving the kingdoms of Korea. Plenipotentiaries were dispatched from Japan to negotiate with Shiragi, who pledged to recognize Mimana as a dependency of Japan. Following these negotiations, a war-party gained ascendancy in Japan, leading to the deployment of a formidable army consisting of several tens of thousands of men, commanded by seven second-rank generals and two first-rank generals. Despite this show of force, the King tendered his submission without a blow being struck.

Japan did not attempt to re-establish its *miyake* in Mimana, and Shiragi ceased sending envoys to Yamato except on special occasions. Relations with the Korean kingdoms remained friendly, primarily because the peninsula served as the route for the arts, crafts, and literature of China's Tang dynasty to reach Japan. Due to the distance of Yamato from the port of Anato (now Nagato) and the dissolution of the office in Mimana, a new office called the Dazai-fu was established in Tsukushi (Kyushu).

## Religious Missions and the Jesuits
During the late 16th century, Jesuit missionaries operated in Japan. By October 1597, there were 125 Jesuits in the country, though only eleven departed Nagasaki by sea that month. Local authorities initially remained silent about the presence of disguised sailors posing as Jesuits. However, following rumors that Hideyoshi intended to visit Kyushu, efficient measures were taken to expel the fathers. This resulted in the destruction of several residences, seminaries, and 137 churches across Kyushu. All Jesuits in Japan were eventually assembled at Nagasaki for deportation to Macao, a process that was interrupted by the death of Hideyoshi.

## Governance in Kamakura
Between 1224 and 1284, Japan was governed by Hojo rulers. While Tokimasa and Yoshitoki are not regarded as great administrators in terms of moral aspects, the four rulers from Yasutoki to Tokimune are noted for governing the nation with clemency and justice. A characteristic of this administration in Kamakura was the devotion of the first half of every month to judicial proceedings. To facilitate this, a bell was hung at the gate of the Record Office, which petitioners or suitors could strike to receive immediate attention.

## The Russo-Japanese War and its Aftermath
The conflict between Russia and Japan had significant international repercussions. During the war, the British battleship *Asahi* operated in the region. Captain Pakenham, the sole European in a company of Japanese sailors, remained aboard the vessel for fourteen months, participating in the vigil outside Port Arthur and the final battle in the Sea of Japan. Admiral Togo later recommended Pakenham for the highest honor bestowed by the Emperor in recognition of his impassive conduct during heavy shelling on the open after-bridge.

The war resulted in a loss of Russian prestige, which influenced European diplomacy. In the Balkan Peninsula, Count Goluchowski observed the terms of the Muerzsteg programme despite this loss of prestige, until his retirement in October 1906.

Following the conflict, the Treaty of Portsmouth established the positions of Japan and Russia in Manchuria. To ensure continuous peace, the two powers signed a convention on July 30, 1906, pledging to respect the rights accrued under the Portsmouth Treaty. Despite this, both nations continued to expand their infrastructure in the region. Russia worked on doubling the Siberian Railway and building a second line along the Amur. Japan developed three lines to command access to central Manchuria: one from Dalny to Kwanchengtsz, one from Fusan via Wiju to Mukden, and a third from the northeastern coast of Korea via Hoiryong to Kilin.
