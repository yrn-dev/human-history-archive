# The Buccaneers of the West Indies

The buccaneers were a roving community of sailors and adventurers who, for three-quarters of a century, acted as a scourge to the dominions and trade of Spanish-America. Composed of English, French, and Dutch sailors, and occasionally supplemented by bands of native Indians, they functioned as a mercenary navy. They were available for employment by any nation wishing to oppose the power of Spain, provided they were permitted to share in the resulting plunder. The community was noted for its extraordinary skill in seamanship, as well as its daring and cruelty.

## Rise and Territorial Bases
The growth of the buccaneer community was closely tied to the possession of strategic islands. In 1641, the Governor-General for the French West India Islands took possession of Tortuga, expelling the English. While Spain eventually attacked Tortuga eight years later—massacring settlers while many buccaneers were absent—the survivors returned and entered a state of open hostility with Spanish arms. 

The stability of the buccaneers' bases fluctuated; the Spaniards regained Tortuga from the French in 1654, though it returned to French hands six years later. The community's survival was further secured by the 1655 conquest of Jamaica by the navy of the English Commonwealth. The buccaneers themselves aided in these territorial shifts, assisting the French in the taking and re-taking of Tortuga and providing similar influence in favor of the English navy during the conquest of Jamaica.

## Principles of Union and Internal Dynamics
The buccaneer community was bound together by two primary principles: a hatred of the Spaniard and a desire for gain and adventure. The latter was often an insufficient bond for men of different nationalities, as private ventures under the colors of separate European powers frequently offered alternative opportunities for booty. The hatred of Spain served as a more valid union; while the buccaneers displayed extreme savagery toward Spanish subjects, they rarely turned that violence upon one another.

Despite this external unity, the internal structure of the community was unstable. While the immense spoil from the capture of wealthy cities was divided equally, the "dream of equality" was short-lived. Following captures, gambling and debauchery often left half of the conquerors in pressing want. This created a divide where those who had retained their wealth wished to return home, while those who had lost theirs demanded renewed attacks.

## Major Expeditions and Conflicts
One of the most significant operations occurred in 1671, when approximately 2,000 buccaneers, commissioned by the governor of Jamaica, Thomas Modyford, attacked the Isthmus of Panama. This route was critical for transporting bullion from South American mines to Porto Bello for shipment to Spain. After a difficult journey via canoes and on foot, the buccaneers routed the defenders of Panama, took the city, and burnt it. The sack was marked by great barbarities, though the Spaniards had removed the treasure before the city fell. 

The aftermath of the Panama expedition led to internal strife. The leader, Morgan, was accused of defrauding his followers, and many buccaneers died of starvation during the return to Jamaica. Consequently, Modyford was recalled, and Morgan was imprisoned in the Tower in 1672. Although Morgan later returned to Jamaica as lieutenant-governor in 1674, he remained unpopular and spent his later years suppressing the buccaneers.

## The Decline of the Community
The power of the buccaneers reached its height around 1685. In the Bay of Panama, they were joined by large numbers of men who had crossed the isthmus under Townley and others. During this period, a Spanish fleet of fourteen sail encountered ten buccaneer vessels in the Bay of Panama, though they did not engage. 

The decline of the buccaneers was driven by both external and internal factors. The viceroy of Peru, seeing his colony's trade cut off and towns burned, resolved to end the raids through vigorous means. Internally, the combination of buccaneers had become too extensive for its own work, and the differing nationalities of its members became a source of growing discord. The separation of the French and English buccaneers, who had previously presented a united front in 1685, marked the beginning of the final epoch of their history.

## Historical Records and Documentation
The history of the buccaneers is documented in several works, though some are noted to be received with caution. These include:
* *De Americaensche Zee Roovers* (1678) by Exquemelin, later translated as *The Bucaniers of America* (English) and *Histoire des avanturiers qui se sont signalez dans les Indes* (French).
* *Voyages* by Dampier.
* *Monarchs of the Main, &c.* (1855) by Geo. W. Thornbury.
* *Voyage and Description of the Isthmus of America* (1699) by Lionel Wafer.
* Works by Père Charlevoix and Raynal's *History of the Settlements and Trade of the Europeans in the East and West Indies*.
* Captain James Burney's *History of the Buccaneers of America* (1816), which is regarded as a really authentic narrative.
* The *Calendar of State Papers*, Colonial Series.
