# The Early Constitutional Development of Bolivia

## The Constitution of 1826
On May 25, 1826, a project for a constitution for Bolivia was presented to the congress of that state. This document was prepared by Bolivar and was accompanied by an address detailing his opinions on the most expedient form of government for newly established republics. 

The constitution was presented to a new congress that had been formally installed at Chuquisaca on that same day, May 25, 1826, following the dissolution of the first general assembly of deputies of Bolivia on October 6, 1825. A committee appointed to examine Bolivar's prepared constitution issued a favorable report, leading the congress to approve the document and declare it the constitution of the republic. The people subsequently swore to it.

## Executive Power and Early Governance
The 1826 constitution contained a highly controversial provision regarding executive authority: it proposed lodging this power in a president for life. This president would be without responsibility and would possess the power to nominate his own successor. This feature alarmed republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires, as well as friends of liberty. In Peru, Bolivar faced accusations that he intended to render himself a perpetual dictator of a confederacy uniting Peru, Bolivia, and Colombia.

Under this constitutional framework, General Sucre was chosen as president for life. However, Sucre accepted the appointment for only two years and did so on the express condition that 2,000 Colombian troops be allowed to remain with him.

## Political Instability and Subsequent Reforms
The stability of the new republic was short-lived. By the end of 1827, Colombian troops and General Sucre were driven from La Paz following repeated risings. In April 1828, a new congress formed at Chuquisaca, which modified Bolivar's constitution and selected Marshal Santa Cruz as president. 

Following a revolution led by General Blanco that briefly overturned the government, quiet was restored in 1831. Santa Cruz then promulgated a code of laws bearing his name and organized the country's financial affairs. Later, in 1835 and 1836, Santa Cruz intervened in Peru, defeating General Gamarra and naming himself protector of Peru with the goal of creating a confederation of the two nations. This led to interference from Chile, resulting in three years of fighting. After Santa Cruz was defeated at the battle of Jungay in June 1839 and exiled, General Velasco became the provisional chief of Bolivia. Eventually, the Santa Cruz party revolted and installed General Ballivian in power. Ballivian remained president until 1848, after which General Belzu led a successful military revolution.

## Evolution of the Bolivian State
By the early 20th century, the government of Bolivia was characterized as a centralized or "unitarian" republic. While representative in form, it was considered autocratic in certain important particulars. The constitution in force in 1908 had been adopted on October 28, 1880.

Under this system, the executive branch consists of a president and two vice-presidents elected by direct popular vote for four-year terms, with no eligibility for re-election in the immediately succeeding term. The president is assisted by five ministers of state covering foreign relations and worship, finance and industry, interior and fomento, justice and public instruction, and war and colonization. 

The legislative branch is a national congress comprising two houses:
* **The Senate:** 16 members (two from each department) elected by direct popular vote for six-year terms.
* **The Chamber of Deputies:** 72 members elected for four-year terms.

The Chamber of Deputies is responsible for electing the justices of the supreme court and prosecuting impeachment trials, while the Senate serves as the court. Suffrage is limited to male citizens who can read and write and meet specific age and property or income requirements (owning real estate or earning 200 bolivianos a year, excluding servant compensation). This restricted electoral body resulted in the country being practically ruled by a political oligarchy.

## Territorial and Commercial Relations
Bolivia possesses no sea-coast and relies on usufruct granted by Chile for seaport access. On May 18, 1895, Chile and Bolivia signed a treaty in Santiago to strengthen friendship and provide Bolivia with free access to the sea for commercial prosperity. 

Under this treaty, Chile agreed that if it acquired permanent sovereignty over Arica and Tacna (via the treaty of Ancon with Peru or direct arrangement), it would transfer those territories to Bolivia in exchange for an indemnity of $5,000,000 silver. If this transfer occurred, Chile would move its frontier north of Camerones to Vitor. If Chile failed to obtain Tacna and Arica, it bound itself to cede the roadstead (caleta) of Vitor, or another analogous one, along with $5,000,000 silver. Supplementary protocols required that the ceded port fully satisfy Bolivia's present and future commercial requirements.
