# Czechs of all Parties

The Czechs constituted a significant political and ethnic force within the Austrian dominions, particularly in Bohemia, where they represented over three-fifths of the population. Their political history is characterized by a struggle for national recognition, linguistic equality, and the restoration of the Bohemian kingdom.

## National Aspirations and the Bohemian Kingdom
The primary objective of the Czech national movement was the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom in its fullest extent, encompassing Silesia and Moravia. Led by figures such as Palacky and Rieger, the movement demanded that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia in Prague, following the tradition of his predecessors. They sought a position within the monarchy similar to that obtained by Hungary.

This cause was supported not only by Czechs but also by several great nobles of German descent, including Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg (cardinal archbishop of Prague), Count Leo Thun, and Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz. These nobles hoped that a self-governing Bohemian kingdom would preserve their power against the influence of German Liberals. In 1868, a "declaration" outlining these claims was presented to the diet at Prague and signed by eighty-one members, including bishops and feudal nobles. The declaration asserted that no foreign assembly was qualified to impose taxes in Bohemia and that a separate settlement must be made with the region.

## Political Divisions: Old and Young Czechs
The Czech political landscape was divided between two primary factions: the Old Czechs and the Young Czechs (also known as Radicals). 

The Old Czechs, led by Rieger, maintained an alliance with the clergy and feudalists and pursued a policy of passive opposition. In contrast, the Young Czechs emerged as a more impatient group that disliked the alliance with the aristocracy. They advocated for universal suffrage, recalled Hussite traditions, and were willing to take their seats in the diet to pursue political reform alongside the Germans.

Tensions between the two groups peaked in the late 19th century. In 1890, when Taaffe attempted to reconcile opposing parties through conferences in Vienna, the Young Czechs were not invited. They rejected the resulting agreement—which proposed a revision of the electoral law and a separation between German and Czech districts—on the grounds that it threatened the unity of the Bohemian country. This led to a struggle in the 1891 elections, in which the Young Czechs were completely victorious over the Old Czechs.

## Parliamentary Activity and Conflict
In the Reichsrath, Czech representation fluctuated. Parliamentary tables from 1897 and 1901 show the Czech presence divided among several groups:
*   **Young Czechs:** 60 seats (1897) and 53 seats (1901).
*   **Radical Young Czechs:** 1 seat (1897) and 4 seats (1901).
*   **Agrarian Czechs:** 1 seat (1897) and 6 seats (1901).
*   **Clerical Czechs:** 1 seat (1897) and 2 seats (1901).

Overall, the Czechs held 63 seats in 1897 and 65 in 1901. At other times, they formed part of the "iron ring" under Taaffe, consisting of Clericals, Poles, and Czechs, which governed against the constitutionalists.

Conflict with German parties often centered on language. While Germans advocated for centralization and proposed dividing Bohemia into separate German and Czech language districts, the Czechs insisted on the "indissoluble unity" of the Bohemian kingdom. They demanded that Czech and German be recognized as equal and parallel languages for all official purposes throughout the territory. Under Dr. von Koerber (until December 1904), the Czechs utilized "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction to block business in the Chamber, including one sitting that lasted 57 hours without interruption.

## Military and External Actions
Beyond the domestic politics of the Austrian Empire, Czechs were active in Russia. During a period of instability, a Czech expedition gained the admiration of the local population. The Czechs seized Vladivostok, an action that was welcomed by those planning anti-Bolshevik demonstrations. Because they were a Slavic, Russian-speaking people, they were able to fight oppressive Russian elements without having their motives misunderstood, eventually gaining control of the city.
