# The Substance of the Bacterial Cell

The internal composition and physical characteristics of the bacterial cell are revealed through specific preparation and staining techniques. When these cells are suitably prepared and stained, their internal structure and the various substances they contain become visible under microscopic examination.

## Protoplasmic Composition
In larger bacterial forms, the cell contains a mass of homogeneous protoplasm. This protoplasm is characterized by the presence of irregular spaces known as vacuoles, which enclose a watery fluid. The protoplasm itself may be tinged with various coloring matters, such as yellow or bright red.

Within the protoplasm, one or more deeply-staining granules are usually scattered. While these granules are common, the protoplasm may occasionally contain other substances.

## Specialized Internal Substances
Various specific substances have been identified within the bacterial cell depending on the form of the bacterium:

*   **Starch-like Substances:** Some forms contain a starch-like substance that stains deep blue when treated with iodine. This occurrence has been clearly demonstrated by Ward and others, even in instances where the bacterium was grown on a medium that contained no starch.
*   **Glycogen or Amylo-dextrin:** In other forms, a substance that turns brown with iodine has been observed, which is likely amylo-dextrin or glycogen.
*   **Lipids and Minerals:** Oil and fat drops have been shown to occur within the cell. Additionally, sulphur-bacteria contain numerous fine granules of sulphur.

## The Cell Membrane and Wall
The bacterial cell is always enclosed by a definite cell-membrane, a fact demonstrated by the plasmolysing experiments of Fischer and others. This membrane is generally a modification of the general protoplasm and consists of a proteid substance; it typically does not contain chitin (as found in fungi) or give reactions of cellulose (unlike the cell-walls of higher plants). However, there are exceptions, such as *B. tuberculosis*, where analysis reveals a large amount of cellulose.

In certain forms, the cell-walls swell into a gelatinous mass, creating a clear, transparent space around the cell in an unstained condition. When this swollen wall is regular and dense, it is referred to as a "capsule."

## Staining Methodology
The study of bacterial substance relies heavily on staining methods to make the organisms visible against tissues. Basic aniline dyes in solution are used almost exclusively because they possess a special affinity for bacterial protoplasm. 

The primary goal of these methods is to color the bacteria as deeply as possible while keeping the surrounding tissues faintly colored. To achieve this, practitioners may use:
*   Simple watery solutions of dye.
*   Weak alkali or the application of heat to increase staining power.
*   Mordants to help fix the stain to the bacteria.

Following the staining process, decolorizing agents—such as alcohol or acids of varying concentrations—are used to remove excess stain from the tissues. Different bacteria react differently to these processes; some absorb stains rapidly while others slowly, and some resist decolorization more than others. In specific methods, such as Gram's method or those used for the tubercle bacillus, a solution of iodine is used before decolorizing to ensure the bacteria remain colored while the stain is entirely removed from the tissues.
