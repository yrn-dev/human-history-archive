# Asiatic Research

Asiatic research in the late 19th century was characterized by extensive explorations of the Tibetan plateau, the Pamirs, and Central Asia. These journeys were often driven by the goal of penetrating the guarded portals of Lhasa and improving the geographical mapping of the region.

## Tibetan Explorations and the Quest for Lhasa
Between 1886 and 1897, several explorers attempted to enter Lhasa, though many were unsuccessful. In 1886-1887, Pierre G. Bonvalot crossed the Tibetan plateau from north to south, accompanied by Prince Henri d'Orleans, but failed to enter the city. Similarly, the American traveller W.W. Rockhill, who conducted journeys between 1889 and 1891, attempted to reach Lhasa without success. Rockhill is noted for achieving greatness in the annals of Asiatic research through both his explorations and his writings, including his *Diary of a Journey through Mongolia and Tibet in 1891 and 1892*.

In 1891, Hamilton Bower undertook a famous journey from Leh to Peking. While Bower also failed to penetrate the portals of Lhasa, he provided a splendid addition to existing Tibetan mapping with the help of a native surveyor. His findings were later published in *Diary of a Journey across Tibet* (1894).

Other notable figures in the field included the French explorer Dutreuil de Rhins, who worked in Tibet from 1891 to 1893 and eventually sacrificed his life to his work. St George Littledale conducted his first great Tibetan journey during these same years, ending in Peking; his second journey in 1894-1895 brought him almost within sight of the sacred walls of Lhasa, though he remained unable to pass inside.

## Central Asian and Desert Research
Professor Sven Hedin, a Swede, is regarded as one of the greatest modern Asiatic explorers, second perhaps only to Prjevalsky. Between 1894 and 1896, Hedin conducted monumental investigations in the glacial regions of the Sarikol mountains and traveled through Tibet and the deserts of Takla Makan. His work resulted in the *Scientific Results of a Journey in Central Asia* (1899-1902), which included a general map of Tibet on a scale of 1:1,000,000.

Earlier research in the region included the work of Nicolas Prjevalski, who explored Asia between 1879 and 1889. During a journey from 1872 to 1873, Prjevalski shot twenty wild Yaks south of the Koko Nor, noting that while they appeared formidable, they were apathetic and stupid, though very hard to kill.

## Geographical and Boundary Surveys
From 1896 to 1898, British cavalry officers Captain M.S. Wellby (18th Hussars) and Captain H. Deasy (16th Lancers) provided valuable service to geography by striking out new lines in Tibet. Captain Deasy continued the Pamir triangulation into the sources of the Zarafshan and the plains of Kashgar. This triangulation had previously been carried across the Hindu Kush by R.A. Wahab and Sir T.H. Holdich during the Pamir Boundary Commission of 1895.

Other regional research included George N. (later Lord) Curzon's time in the Pamirs during the early 1890s. In Asia Minor, W.J. Hamilton conducted *Researches in Asia Minor* (1843). Hamilton observed that the population and prosperity of Sinope were lower than expected for its location between Trebizond and Constantinople, noting a general appearance of privation throughout the peninsula.

## Bibliography of Asiatic Research
The bibliography of Asia since 1880 is described as voluminous, comprising Survey Reports, Gazetteers of the government of India, and records from the Asiatic Society, Bengal, and the Royal Asiatic Society. Key published works include:
*   **Sven Hedin**: *Scientific Results of a Journey in Central Asia* (1905-1907).
*   **H. H. P. Deasy**: *In Tibet and Chinese Turkestan* (1901).
*   **M. S. Wellby**: *Through Unknown Tibet* (1898).
*   **W. Blunt**: "A Visit to Jebel Shammar" (1880).
*   **Captain F. Younghusband**: *The Heart of a Continent* (1893).

Additional authorities for the region include the works of C. Texier, P. Tchihatcheff, and W.M. Ramsay.
