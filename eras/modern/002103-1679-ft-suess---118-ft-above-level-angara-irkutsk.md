# Lake Baikal

Lake Baikal is a large lake located in East Siberia, serving as the largest fresh-water basin in Eurasia and ranking as the sixth largest lake in the world. It is known as *Bai-kul* to Turkish tribes and *Dalai-nor* to the Mongols. Geographically, the lake separates the government of Transbaikalia from the government of Irkutsk. It stretches from south-west to north-east, specifically between 51deg 29' to 55deg 50' N. latitude and 103deg 40' to 110deg E. longitude.

## Physical Dimensions and Altitude
The lake has a total area of 13,200 square miles, a size nearly equivalent to that of Switzerland. It measures 386 miles in length, with a width varying between 20 and 50 miles. The coastline consists of a western shore measuring 525 miles and an eastern shore measuring 640 miles. 

The altitude of the lake has been subject to different estimates. Chersky estimated it at 1587 feet, while Suess estimated it at 1679 feet, which is 118 feet above the level of the Angara at Irkutsk. However, a more correct altitude is considered to be 1500 feet. The water level experiences slight oscillations; for example, following five weeks of heavy rain in 1869, the level rose by 7 feet, resulting in the submergence of a large territory at the mouth of the Selenga.

## Geography and Geology
Lake Baikal is situated entirely within the Alpine zone that fringes the high plateau of Asia to the north-west, with its southern extremity penetrating the plateau itself. Except for the delta of the Selenga, the lake is surrounded by high mountains. These include:
*   **The South:** The Khamar-daban border-ridge, featuring a mountain summit 5300 feet above the lake.
*   **The North-West:** The Onot and Baikal (also Primorskiy) ridges, which strike the shore diagonally.
*   **The North-East:** An Alpine complex of mountains that remain unexplored.
*   **The East:** The Barguzin range, which impinges obliquely.
*   **The South-East:** The Ulanburgasu mountains, which intrude into the Selenga delta.
*   **Between the Irkut and Angara:** A massive highland characterized by deep ravines.

Geologically, the lake had a much greater extension in previous ages, stretching into the lower valleys of the Barguzin, the Upper Angara, and the valley of the Irkut. Earthquakes are frequent along the shores, and volcanic activity occurred during the Quaternary Age or at the end of the Tertiary, leaving lava streams across the Khamar-daban and Sayan mountains and the Irkut valley.

## Hydrology
The lake is fed by over 300 streams, primarily short mountain torrents. Major inflows include the Selenga on the south-east, the Barguzin on the east, and the Upper Angara at the north-east extremity. The only outflow is the lower Angara, which exits through a rocky cleft on the western shore. While the Irkut river once reached Lake Baikal, it no longer does; it now turns north through a mountain gap to join the Angara at Irkutsk.

## Navigation and Transport
Navigation on the lake is hindered by a lack of good ports and bays, as well as sudden storms. Listvinichnoe, located on the western shore near the Angara outflow, serves as the principal port, though it is an open roadstead. Steamers operate weekly from Listvinichnoe to Misovaya (Posolskoe) on the opposite shore, and frequently to the mouth of the Selenga. From the Selenga, steamers travel as far as Bilyutai near the Mongolian frontier to transport imported tea from Kiakhta. Conversely, timber, soda, salt, cedar nuts, and grain are shipped via rafts down the Uda, Chikoi, and Khilok tributaries.

In winter, the lake is covered in ice 3 to 4 feet thick, allowing for sledge crossings between Misovaya and Listvinichnoe. A powerful ice-breaker is also used to ferry trains. A year-round highway was constructed around the southern shore between 1863 and 1864, partly through the blasting of cliffs; since 1905, this route has been followed by the trans-Siberian railway.

## Regional Population: The Buriats
The Buriats, a Mongolian race, inhabit the vicinity of Lake Baikal, primarily within the Trans-Baikal Territory and the government of Irkutsk. They are organized into clans and tribes, often named after their localities, and further subdivided by kinship. Physically, they are described as broad-shouldered and inclined to stoutness, with high cheekbones, thick lips, broad flat noses, small slanting eyes, and scanty beards. Men wear a pigtail similar to the Chinese and shave their heads. Their clothing consists of silk and cotton gowns in summer and sheepskins and furs in winter.

Their primary occupation is rearing horses and cattle, with the Buriat horse noted for its endurance. In some areas, they have adopted Russian agricultural methods and are considered better farmers than the Russians in Irkutsk due to their skill in irrigation and manuring. They are also adept at fishing and trapping.

The Buriats speak a Mongolian language with three distinct dialects. Literacy is common, particularly among the Trans-Baikal Buriats who use books translated from Tibetan. Religiously, they are divided:
*   **Buddhists:** The majority, led by the Khambo Lama who resides at Goose Lake (Guisinoe Ozero).
*   **Shamanists:** Their most sacred site is the Shamanic stone at the mouth of the Angara.
*   **Christians:** Several thousand Buriats around the lake follow Christianity.
