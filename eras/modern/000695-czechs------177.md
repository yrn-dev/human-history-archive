# Czechs

The Czechs are a Slavic people primarily associated with the regions of Bohemia and Moravia. Their history in these areas has been characterized by linguistic and political contests, particularly regarding their status relative to German populations.

## Political and Linguistic Struggles in Bohemia and Moravia
In Bohemia and Moravia, the Czechs and Germans were nearly equal in number. During a twenty-year period, there was a constant encroachment by the Czechs upon the Germans, a process aided in part by government action. A significant shift occurred with an 1880 ordinance requiring that business brought before law courts or government offices be handled in the language in which it was introduced, granting the Czech language a position within the government service. This was expanded in 1886, when business introduced in Czech was permitted in the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid frequent translations.

Educational advancements furthered this trend. A large number of Czech elementary and middle schools were established, and Czech classes were introduced into German schools. In 1882, Czech classes began at the university of Prague, an event viewed by Germans as a desecration of the oldest German university.

## Parliamentary Representation and Conflict
The parliamentary landscape for the Czechs involved various political factions. In one recorded distribution, the Czech representation included 28 Czech Agrarians, 18 Young Czechs, 17 Czech Clericals, 9 Czech National Socialists, 7 Old Czechs, 2 Realists, and one unattached member. In another parliamentary table from 1897 and 1901, the Czechs were represented by Young Czechs (60 in 1897; 53 in 1901), Radical Young Czechs (1 in 1897; 4 in 1901), Clerical Czechs (1 in 1897; 2 in 1901), and Agrarian Czechs (1 in 1897; 6 in 1901).

To manage racial tensions, constituencies were divided by race based on tax-paying capacity. In mixed districts, separate registers ensured that Czechs voted only for Czech candidates, preventing them from achieving victory over Germans.

Political tensions peaked under Dr. von Koerber, who attempted to broker a compromise between Czechs and Germans in 1902 and 1903. Koerber proposed dividing Bohemia into ten districts: five Czech, three German, and two mixed. He also proposed that of 234 district tribunals, 133 be Czech and 94 German. The Czechs rejected this, demanding that the Czech and German languages be placed on an equal footing for all official purposes throughout Bohemia. Following this refusal, the Czechs employed "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction to block business in parliament, including one sitting that lasted 57 hours without interruption.

## The Czech Expedition in Siberia
During a period of global conflict, a Czech army operated in Siberia. This force was noted for its democratic nature; while leaders were trained, they attained their positions through popular choice. The expedition was influenced by Professor Masaryk in America and Kenneth Miller of the New York Y.M.C.A., who managed complicated travel arrangements for the Czech trains.

While traveling via the Trans-Siberian railway, the Czechs engaged in study, reading, and athletics, as Bohemians are considered the world's champion gymnasts. They distributed the speeches of President Wilson to the Russian and Siberian peasants to demonstrate the sanity of their cause and the ideals of democracy.

The Czechs gained the confidence of the Russian people, which facilitated their success in the region. This was exemplified by the seizure of Vladivostok, where the Czechs freed an anti-Bolshevik city. Because they were a Slavic, Russian-speaking people, their intervention was welcomed by the local population. It was noted that the Czechs were more effective in gaining the trust of the Russians than non-Slavic Allied forces, such as the Japanese.
