# Research and Exploration in Western and Eastern Fields

The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw a significant expansion of human knowledge across diverse fields of research, ranging from the physical geography of Asia and the Middle East to the study of ancient texts, palaeontology, and botany.

## Geographical Research in Arabia and Asia

Since 1875, additional light has been shed on the oases and physiography of the great deserts of Arabia. Key contributions were made by Charles Doughty and Wilfrid S. Blunt in northern Arabia between 1877 and 1878, followed approximately ten years later by E. Glaser and G. Schweinfurth in the south-west. In 1884-1885, Colonel S.B. Miles conducted an adventurous journey through Oman. Further investigations into ancient Semitic history were carried out by Theodore Bent, who explored Hadramut from 1894 to 1895 and the Bahrein Islands in 1888.

In Northern Asia, Russian organized surveys have provided new revelations regarding the forest-clad mountain regions and vast steppes of Siberia, suggesting future economic resources for Russian enterprise. A notable 1892 expedition by Baron Toll through regions watered by the Lena provided material to help elucidate the geological history of the world, specifically proving the primeval existence of a boreal zone of the Jurassic sea around the North Pole.

In the Farther East, geographical research progressed rapidly following the 1874 investigation into the route between China and Burma, during which Augustus Margary lost his life. Subsequent explorers included Captain William Gill, who traveled across China to Burma and eastern Tibet, and E.C. Baber, who explored the western provinces of the "Celestial empire" in 1877-1878. Archibald Colquhoun traveled from Talifu to Peking in 1881, and Mark Bell illustrated ancient trade routes while crossing the continent in 1887. 

Further exploration occurred in Burma and Siam; Prince Henri d'Orleans was among the first to travel to the mountains hiding the sources of the Mekong, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy. James M'Carthy worked on the map-making of Siam, while Warrington Smyth and Curzon contributed knowledge of its coast districts. Between 1900 and 1904, C.C. Manifold mapped and explored the upper basin of the Yang-tsze river. Additionally, the Russo-Japanese war, which ended in 1905, advanced knowledge of the Russo-Chinese borderland.

## Palaeontology and Astronomy

In the field of palaeontology, the study of archaic type morphology has improved the understanding of general evolution, though discoveries have provided little light on the relationships of living Teleostean groups. Significant work was produced by R. H. Traquair regarding the fishes *Lanasius*, *Birkenia*, *Ateleaspis*, *Lanarkia*, *Drepanaspis*, *Thelodus*, and *Palaeospondylus*. Additionally, Bashford Dean and A. S. Woodward researched the primitive shark *Cladoselache*, while L. Hussakof, E. W. Claypole, C. R. Eastman, J. S. Newberry, and the aforementioned Woodward and Dean studied the Arthrodira.

In the study of the universe, research is divided into Astrophysics, which concerns physical constitution, and Astronomy proper (or "Astrometry"), which deals with the dimensions, mutual relations, and motions of heavenly bodies. These sciences share fundamental principles. The universe is composed of two types of matter: incandescent bodies, which are visible by their own light, and dark bodies. Dark bodies are identified either by their attraction upon incandescent bodies or by reflecting light from nearby incandescent bodies. The ethereal medium is excluded from astronomy as it is capable of experimental study.

## Textual Criticism and Historical Records

In the late 19th century, Rendel Harris and Chase investigated the "Western" text, proposing that its phenomena resulted from retranslation into Greek from early versions—Harris arguing for Latin influence and Chase for Syriac. While they were noted for exaggerating the traceability of retranslation and overvaluing *Codex Bezae* as the best witness to a "Western" text that is not a unity, Harris's work is considered historically important for stimulating new lines of research in textual criticism.

Regarding historical records, the editor of Gibbon’s *History* noted that since the original publication, some fragments and classical works had come to light, and there was an increase in available Oriental literature.

## Botany and Early Human Development

The plant *Medicago sativa*, known as lucerne in England and alfalfa in America (an Arabic term meaning "the best fodder"), is a native of the eastern Mediterranean. It is a perennial herb with a hollow stem, trifoliolate leaves, and a tap-root that can extend 15 feet or more. Introduced to Italy in the 1st century A.D. and cultivated in Britain around 1650, it was taken to South America and Mexico in the 16th century. Its cultivation in the Western States of the American Union began primarily in the mid-19th century.

Regarding early human history, research indicates that between 15,000 and 12,000 B.C., people in Western Asia, North Africa, or a now-submerged Mediterranean valley began domesticating animals and practicing cultivation. During this period, they developed rudely modelled pottery, basketwork, woven plant fibre textiles, and polished stone implements.

## The Evolution of Scientific Research

During the 13th and 14th centuries, research in Europe was often detached and secretive, influenced by a tradition of isolated investigation brought by the Arabs. Alchemists, who worked closely with herbalists, medicine-makers, and metal and glass workers, sought power—such as the elixir of life or the manufacture of gold—rather than pure knowledge. However, their practical research led to discoveries in metallurgy, dyes, poisons, and the creation of lenses and clear glass.
