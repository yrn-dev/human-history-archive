# Fauna and Geography of the Southern Andean Plateaus

## The Chinchilla and Plateau Fauna
The colder plateau regions of the Andes, spanning across Bolivia, northern Argentina, and Chile, are inhabited by the chinchilla (*C. laniger*). This animal is highly prized for its fur. In Bolivia, the government has encouraged the production of the chinchilla and has prohibited the exportation of live animals. Other animals found on the higher and colder plateaus include llamas and alpacas, which receive significant breeding attention.

The fauna of these plateau and neighboring districts is closely linked. In addition to the chinchilla, the plateau species of the viscacha (*Lagidium cuvieri*) and the South American otter (*Lutra paranensis*) are hunted for their skins. Other indigenous fauna in the broader region include the puma, jaguar, wildcat, coati, tapir (or *anta*), sloth, ant-bear, paca (*Coelogenys paca*), and capybara. In the wooded Andean foothills, a rare species of bear known as the spectacled bear (*Ursus ornatus*) can be found.

## Regional Geography and Geology
The geography of the region includes diverse terrains, such as the well-wooded and mountainous country of the Bio-Bio province in southern Chile. This province is bounded to the east by Argentina and exports timber to a large extent. The Bio-Bio river, which rises in the Pino Hachado pass across the Andes, flows north-westerly to the Pacific at Concepcion, where it forms an excellent harbour.

Geologically, strata from the Eocene period occur in Argentina, Chile, and Brazil, known as the Patagonia-Megellanian series. Additionally, rocks from the Bajocian stage are recognized in Argentina, Chile, and Bolivia.

## Political and Diplomatic History
The relationship between Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia has been marked by territorial disputes and diplomatic negotiations. A long-standing dispute existed between Chile and Bolivia regarding the occupation of the former Bolivian provinces of Arica and Tacna under the Parto de Tregna of April 4, 1884. 

By 1895, there was a possibility of Chile conceding a sea outlet to Bolivia in exchange for the recognition of Chilean ownership of Tacna and Arica. However, Chile changed its attitude after the discovery of secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina. The hope for Bolivian support from Argentina ended following the final settlement of the boundary dispute between Argentina and Chile. Consequently, a treaty was ratified in 1905. Under this agreement, Bolivia ceded the maritime provinces occupied by Chile since the war of 1881, leaving the republic without a seaport. In return, Chile agreed to:
* Construct a railway at its own expense from Arica to the Bolivian capital, La Paz.
* Provide Bolivians with free transit through Chilean territory to specific coastal towns.
* Pay a cash indemnity to Bolivia and provide pecuniary assistance for the construction of other necessary railways.

## Infrastructure and Communication
Communication between large cities and frontiers has been facilitated by the construction of public highways, though these roads are often rough, almost impassable, and feature dangerous river crossings. Subsidized stage coaches operate on some of these routes.

Telegraph service was established in 1880. By 1904, there were 3,115 meters of lines in operation, with 1,936 meters belonging to the state and 1,179 meters belonging to private corporations. Some of these private lines, specifically those of the Oruro and Antofagasta railway, are located partly within Chilean territory. Large cities maintain communication with the outside world through Peru, Chile, and Argentina. Furthermore, special agreements exist with Argentina, Chile, and Peru for the transmission of Bolivian foreign mails.

## Historical Figures and Tribes
The region's history includes the Calchaqui, a now-extinct tribe of South American Indians who occupied northern Argentina. They achieved a high degree of civilization and resisted the first Spanish colonists arriving from Chile.

In the early 19th century, Jose Miguel Carrera (1785–1821) emerged as a principal leader in the fight for Chilean independence. Born in Santiago, Carrera used his military experience in the Spanish army during the Napoleonic war to lead the nationalist government through a series of coup d'etats. However, rivalry with Bernardo O'Higgins led to the defeat of nationalist forces at Rancagua in 1814. Carrera was later excluded from the 1817 liberation expedition led by O'Higgins and Jose de San Martin. After the Argentine government blocked his attempts to return to Chile and his brothers were executed at Mendoza by the San Martin party, Carrera organized a rebellion in Argentina. He was eventually captured and shot at Mendoza on September 4, 1821.
