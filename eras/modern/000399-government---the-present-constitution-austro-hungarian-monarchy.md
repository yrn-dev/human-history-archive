# Government of the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy

The Austro-Hungarian Monarchy, often referred to unofficially as the Dual Monarchy, is a central European state bounded by the German Empire, Russia, Rumania, Servia, Turkey, Montenegro, the Adriatic Sea, Italy, Switzerland, and Liechtenstein. As of 1905, it occupied an area of 239,977 square miles, representing approximately one-sixteenth of the total area of Europe. The monarchy consists of two independent states: the "lands of St Stephen's Crown" (unofficially called Hungary or Transleithania) and the kingdoms and lands represented in the council of the empire, the *Reichsrat* (unofficially called Austria or Cisleithania).

## Constitutional Foundations
The present constitution of the monarchy is based upon three primary legal pillars:
1. **The Pragmatic Sanction (1713):** Promulgated by Emperor Charles VI on April 19, 1713, this sanction settled the succession of the throne within the dynasty of Habsburg-Lorraine. Succession descends by right of lineal succession and primogeniture to male heirs; should the male line become extinct, it passes to the female line. This document also established the indivisibility and indissolubility of the monarchy.
2. **The Diploma of Emperor Francis Joseph I (1860):** Issued on October 20, 1860, this diploma introduced the constitutional form of government.
3. **The Ausgleich or "Compromise" (1867):** Concluded on February 8, 1867, this agreement regulated the relations between Austria and Hungary.

Under this structure, Austria and Hungary are completely independent of one another, each maintaining its own government and parliament. The unity of the Dual Monarchy is expressed through a common head of state—who holds the titles of Emperor of Austria and Apostolic King of Hungary—and the joint administration of specific affairs.

## Common Administration and Joint Affairs
The two states share a common administration for affairs that affect both halves of the monarchy, specifically:
* **Foreign Affairs:** This includes consular and diplomatic representation abroad.
* **Military:** The army and navy are common, though this excludes the special army of each individual state and the annual voting of recruits.
* **Finance:** Joint administration is maintained in so far as it concerns joint expenditure.

## Economic and Commercial Unity
The economic relationship between the two states is governed by a customs and commercial agreement first concluded in 1867 and renewable every ten years. Under the compact renewed in 1899, the monarchy constitutes a single customs and commercial territory, which includes the principality of Liechtenstein and Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Common principles are applied to commercial affairs, customs legislation, and legislation on duties connected to industrial production (specifically mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer). The states also share the determination of coinage and legal tender, as well as the principles regulating the Austro-Hungarian Bank. Additionally, ordinances are shared regarding railways that affect the interests of both states.

## The Austro-Hungarian Bank and Currency
The Austro-Hungarian Bank serves as the central financial institution. Originally an entirely Austrian institution in 1867 with branches in Hungary, it was converted into a joint Austro-Hungarian bank in 1877 as a concession to Hungary. This arrangement, renewed in 1887, established head offices in both Vienna and Pest, with management divided between the two countries.

Historically, the currency consisted of paper money (notes issued by the bank) and silver (the gulden). Because the government issued *Staatsnoten* (state notes) totaling 312 million gulden during the war of 1866, the bank's charter was violated, and the currency became inconvertible paper. A reform operation begun in 1892 sought to resume cash payments, redeem the *Staatsnoten*, and substitute a gold standard for the silver standard.

## Political Crises and Territorial Changes
The period between 1903 and 1907 is regarded as a critical era of development. The original interpretation of the dual system was discredited by the introduction of universal suffrage in Austria and the Austro-Hungarian crisis of 1903-1906. Tensions persisted between Habsburg imperialism, which sought to preserve military and economic unity, and Magyar nationalism, which aimed to maintain ascendancy in an independent Hungary.

Territorially, the monarchy expanded through the Berlin Treaty of 1878, which placed the principalities of Bosnia and Herzegovina (population 1,591,036 as of 1895) under Austro-Hungarian administration. Their annexation in 1908 was recognized by the Powers in 1909.
