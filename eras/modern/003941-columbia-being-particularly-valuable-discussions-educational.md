# Columbia and Educational Developments

The name Columbia is associated with several distinct entities in the archive's records, most notably Columbia University in New York City and the province of British Columbia in Canada, both of which feature prominently in discussions of educational administration, institutional growth, and academic scholarship.

## Columbia University and College

Columbia College, now known as Columbia University, underwent significant expansion and structural evolution during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Under the presidency of Frederick Augustus Porter Barnard, who served as the institution's tenth president from 1864 until shortly before his death in 1889, the college experienced rapid growth. During Barnard's tenure, the student enrollment increased from approximately 150 to more than 1,000. 

Barnard's administration was marked by the establishment of new departments, the great extension of the elective system, and the creation of more adequate provisions for original research and graduate study. Barnard was also an advocate for extending educational privileges to women; consequently, Barnard College for women was established in his honor immediately following his death. His contributions to the institution were further documented in his annual reports to the Board of Trustees, which are described as being particularly detailed.

The university continued to be a center for academic leadership into the 20th century. Nicholas Murray Butler, a graduate of Columbia College (1882) and a Ph.D. recipient from the same institution (1884), held various roles there, including assistant in philosophy, tutor, and adjunct professor of philosophy, ethics, and psychology. Butler served as the dean of the faculty of philosophy from 1890 to 1902 and eventually succeeded Seth Low as president of Columbia University in 1901. Butler was also the personal planner and organizer of the New York college for the training of teachers, which later became the Teachers' College of Columbia University, serving as its first president from 1887 to 1891.

Columbia University also served as a training ground for specialized scholars. For example, Edward Washburn Hopkins graduated from the university in 1878 and served as an instructor there from 1881 to 1885 before pursuing further studies in Leipzig and later becoming a professor of Sanskrit and comparative philology at Yale University.

## Education in British Columbia

In the province of British Columbia, the educational system is characterized by government-maintained, free, and undenominational schools. These schools are categorized into three distinct classes: common, graded, and high. The administration of these schools is handled by trustees who are selected by the ratepayers within each school district, with a superintendent of education operating under the provincial secretary.

Higher education in the province is limited; the archive notes there is only one college in British Columbia: the McGill University College of British Columbia located at Vancouver. This institution serves as one of the colleges of McGill University, which is headquartered in Montreal.

Additionally, religious groups have maintained an interest in the region. The Church of England founded the bishopric of British Columbia in 1857 through the benefactions of a Victorian baroness. The Baptist community in Canada also has a presence in British Columbia, though they are listed as one of four distinct sections of Canadian Baptists.
