# Churches and Ecclesiastical Architecture of Bristol

The city of Bristol contains a remarkable collection of architectural antiquities, the majority of which are ecclesiastical in nature. This concentration of historic religious buildings is attributed primarily to the influence of a few great baronial families during the city's early history—specifically the Berkeleys and the earls of Gloucester—and later to the contributions of prominent merchants, including the Framptons, Shipwards, and Canyngs.

## The See and Cathedral of Bristol
The see of Bristol was established in 1542 by Henry VIII. In 1836, it was united with the see of Gloucester, though the two were separated again in 1896. The diocese encompasses a small but populous part of Somerset, as well as portions of Wiltshire and Gloucestershire.

The Bristol cathedral is situated on the south side of College Green, positioned above the area known as Canons' Marsh, which borders the Floating Harbour. The structure features a nave, a choir with aisles, short transepts, two western towers, a central tower, an eastern Lady chapel, and various other chapels. To the south, the cathedral includes a cloister court and a chapter-house. The nave is a modern addition completed by Street in 1877, designed to imitate the 14th-century choir, which is noted for the curious skeleton-vaulting found in its aisles.

The cathedral serves as the site for several notable interments and memorials:
*   The Berkeleys are buried in canopied tombs featuring effigies in chain mail.
*   Fine tombs are present for crosiered abbots.
*   Memorials exist for Lady Hesketh (a friend of Cowper), Sterne's Eliza (Elizabeth Draper), and Bishop Butler.
*   A fine epitaph written by William Mason for his wife, who died in 1767, is located here.

The cathedral was originally the church of the abbey of St Augustine, founded by Fitz-Harding in 1142. Several original elements of the abbey remain largely intact, including the abbot's gateway, the vestibule to the chapter-house, and the stately entrance gateway with its sculptured mouldings. The chapter-house is particularly distinguished, described as one of the finest Norman chambers in Europe and characterized by Byzantine exuberance in its decoration.

## Other City Churches and Chapels
On the north side of College Green is the Mayor's chapel, originally known as St Mark's. This small, ornate building is devoted to the services of the corporation and the mayor, and its architecture is mainly Perpendicular and Decorated.

Additionally, the city features other significant churches located south of the business centre. Connected to the centre by Bristol Bridge, an island formed between the New Course of the Avon and the Floating Harbour houses two of the city's finest churches: St Mary Redcliffe and the Temple.

## Religious History and Movements
Beyond its architectural heritage, Bristol served as a center for various religious movements and figures. The city was the center of the Methodist revival led by Wesley and Whitefield. In the 19th century, the city was home to Lant Carpenter (1780–1840), a Unitarian minister who moved to Bristol in 1817. Carpenter sought to broaden English Unitarianism and replaced the rite of baptism, which he viewed as a superstition, with a form of infant dedication.
