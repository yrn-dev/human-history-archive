# Regimental Accessories

Regimental accessories consist of the various buildings and facilities required for administrative, military, and domestic purposes beyond the primary accommodation of troops. These structures are essential components of barracks, which are generally permanent brick or stone buildings used for peace occupations, though corrugated iron hut barracks lined with wood are sometimes used in connection with training grounds.

## Battalion Barrack Facilities
A battalion barrack includes a variety of specialized buildings for military operations and administration. These include regimental offices, a guard house, and a small-arm ammunition store. For the health and training of the troops, barracks provide a medical inspection block with a dispensary—where a medical officer treats the sick or refers them to a hospital—as well as a drill and gymnastic hall.

Logistical support is provided through a fire-engine house, a vehicle shed, and a storehouse for mobilization equipment. The quartermaster is responsible for various stores, including those for clothing, coal, meat, bread, signalling, and musketry, as well as general small stores. Maintenance is handled in dedicated workshops for tailors, shoemakers, painters, glaziers, plumbers, carpenters, and armourers. For recreation and exercise, the battalion barrack includes a parade ground, a recreation ground, and a fives court.

## Officers' Quarters and Mess
The officers' mess is typically situated at a distance from the men's barracks, often overlooking a cricket ground or the parade ground. The building features an entrance hall with a band alcove for guest nights, a mess-room (dining-room), and an anteroom (reading-room). To accommodate lantern lights, the kitchen and billiard-room are located at the rear. The mess also includes a mess office and facilities for the mess-man's department and waiters' department, such as a cellarage, plate-closet, and pantry, along with quarters for the mess-man.

Officers' living quarters are generally located in a storey above the mess or in wings extending from the building's frontage. Field officers are allotted two rooms, while other officers have one large room with a partitioned bedroom. Each officer is assigned a soldier servant who has a small room for cleaning. Bathrooms are centrally located and supplied with hot water from the mess kitchen. The commanding officer is provided with a detached house containing a kitchen, servants' hall, bathroom, seven bed- and dressing-rooms, and three sitting-rooms. The quartermaster is provided with a smaller detached house featuring four bedrooms, two sitting-rooms, a kitchen, and a bath. Other married officers must arrange their own housing, though they are usually granted a lodging allowance.

## Cavalry-Specific Accessories
Cavalry barracks are organized to allow men to reach their horses quickly. While barrack-rooms were formerly built over stables, this was abandoned for sanitary reasons. Troop stables are now detached buildings where each horse is provided 1500 cubic feet of air space. These stables feature ridge ventilation, skylights, and windows hinged at the bottom with hopper checks to prevent direct draughts. Fresh air is supplied to horses lying down via traversed air-ducts below the mangers.

Equipment in the stables includes cast-iron hay-racks and mangers, with horses separated by bails and chains. Each stable contains a troop store for gear and spare saddles, and an expense forage store for the day's rations. Drainage is managed via blue Staffordshire paving bricks graded toward a collecting channel. Outside the blocks, there are dung-pits, litter-sheds, horse-troughs, and cement concrete yards.

Additional cavalry facilities include:
* **Officers' Stables:** Separate blocks with one row of stalls divided by partitions and separate saddle-rooms.
* **Infirmary Stables:** Located in a separate enclosure, providing 2000 cubic feet of air space per horse in loose boxes or stalls.
* **Support Buildings:** A detached forge and shoeing shed, a granary and forage barn (holding a fortnight's supply), and a horse-powered chaff-cutter.
* **Training Areas:** A large covered riding school and several open maneges for riding instruction and exercise.

## Garrison and Hospital Accessories
Large military stations require general garrison buildings in addition to regimental ones. Military hospitals, built on the pavilion system with connecting corridors to ensure air circulation, are classified into three grades:
* **First-grade:** Large central hospitals for important districts, fully equipped for all operations. They include officer wards and may have attached military families' hospitals for women and children or separate isolation hospitals for infectious cases.
* **Second-grade:** Smaller, independent hospitals with operation rooms.
* **Third-grade:** Reception stations for small stations that act as feeders to larger hospitals and handle non-transportable or accident cases.

Hospital layouts feature a centrally placed administration block with ward blocks on either side. Ordinary large wards provide 1200 cubic feet of air space per patient, while isolation wards are placed in a retired position outside the main enclosure.

## International Variations
In France and Germany, barracks are often constructed as a single large block of three or four storeys containing all accessories and accommodation for a complete regiment or battalion, including officers and non-commissioned officers. While some modern barracks use a pavilion system of separate blocks, the single block is preferred for its architectural appeal, compactness, and ease of supervision. In the German army, the state provides a furnished officers' mess-house for every regiment. Married quarters are provided for some non-commissioned officers, but not for privates.

American barracks generally utilize the separate block system. These facilities center around a soldiers' club or post-exchange, which serves as a combined gymnasium, sergeants' mess, and recreation establishment with an attached bath-house. Canteens for liquor sales were abolished in 1901.
