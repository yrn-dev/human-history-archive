# Paul François Nicolas Barras

Paul François Nicolas Barras, Comte de (1755–1829), was a prominent figure of the French Revolution and a member of the French Directory from 1795 to 1799. Born at Fox-Amphoux to a noble family from Provence, Barras began his military career at age sixteen as a "gentleman cadet" in the regiment of Languedoc.

## Early Military Career and the Revolution
In 1776, Barras embarked for India, eventually reaching Pondicherry. He participated in the city's defense, which concluded with its capitulation to the British on October 18, 1778. Following his release, he returned to France and later took part in a second expedition to the East Indies between 1782 and 1783. After leaving the army, he spent several years engaged in frivolities typical of his nature and social class.

With the outbreak of the Revolution in 1789, Barras adopted the democratic cause and served as one of the administrators for the department of the Var. By June 1792, he held a seat in the high national court at Orleans. Later that year, following the start of the war with the kingdom of Sardinia, he became a deputy for the department of the Var in the Convention (the third national assembly of France) and served as commissioner to the French army of Italy. In January 1793, Barras voted with the majority in favor of the death of Louis XVI. During missions to the south-east districts of France, he became acquainted with Bonaparte during the siege of Toulon.

## Rise to Power and the Directory
In 1794, Barras aligned himself with those seeking to overthrow the Robespierre faction. Their success during the *coup d'etat* of 9 Thermidor (July 27) elevated him to the front rank of political influence. Following this event, Barras visited the prison where the dauphin was held; his account of the visit noted that the child suffered from extreme neglect, though it did not mention allegations that the child had been walled in.

The following year, when the National Guards of Paris threatened the Convention, Barras was appointed to command the troops tasked with the Convention's defense. He nominated Bonaparte as one of his subalterns, leading to the vigorous measures of 13 Vendemiaire (October 5, 1795), which dispersed royalists and malcontents near the Tuileries. Consequently, Barras became one of the five Directors controlling the executive of the French republic.

## Relationship with Bonaparte and Political Decline
Barras maintained intimate relations with Josephine de Beauharnais and helped facilitate her marriage to Bonaparte. While evidence is considered defective, some claim Barras procured Bonaparte's appointment to command the army of Italy in early 1796. 

The stability of the Directory was bolstered by Bonaparte's achievements. In the summer of 1797, when constitutional and royalist opposition grew, Bonaparte sent the Jacobin General Augereau to repress the movement via the *coup d'etat* of 18 Fructidor (September 4). However, the subsequent heavy-handed governance by Barras and the violent Jacobins made the Directory odious. 

Bonaparte eventually overthrew the Directory during the *coup d'etat* of 18-19 Brumaire (November 9-10). Barras, recognizing the need for change, was an accomplice in Bonaparte's designs to some extent, though he did not suspect the contriver's full ambition. Following the transition, the three Consuls who replaced the five Directors sidelined Barras, ending his political career. He spent his final years in "voluptuous ease," having amassed a large fortune. He is noted as one of the men of the Revolution who did the most to degrade the movement, with his notorious private and public immorality contributing to the downfall of the Directory.

## Memoirs and Historical Sources
Barras left behind memoirs in a rough state, which were drawn up by his literary executor, M. Rousselin de St Albin. While the extent of the alterations made by the executor is not fully known, M. George Duruy provided satisfactory proofs of the documents' genuineness when editing them for publication in 1895. However, the *Barras Memoirs* are cited as an example of incorrectness; for instance, the writer claimed the royalist defending force at Toulon numbered 30,000 men when it was actually fewer than 12,000, and the text sought to minimize Bonaparte's role in the city's capture.

Other historical sources regarding Barras include the memoirs of de Lescure, Larevelliere-Lepeaux, and Gohier, as well as works by A. Vandal, A. Sorel, and Sciout.
