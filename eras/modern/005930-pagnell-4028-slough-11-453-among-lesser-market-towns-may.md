# Buckinghamshire: Population, Administration, and Industry

## Population and Administration
The ancient county of Buckinghamshire covers an area of 475,682 acres. Its population was recorded as 185,284 in 1891 and grew to 195,764 by 1901. The administrative county encompasses a slightly larger area of 479,358 acres and contains 230 civil parishes. 

The county is organized into eight hundreds. Three of these—Desborough, Burnham, and Stoke—are known as the "Chiltern Hundreds," while the hundred of Aylesbury is referred to by its ancient designation, the "three hundreds of Aylesbury." Administratively, the county is divided into thirteen petty sessional divisions and falls within the midland circuit, where assizes are conducted at Aylesbury. While there is one court of quarter sessions, the municipal boroughs of Wycombe and Buckingham maintain their own separate commissions of the peace.

Ecclesiastically, the county is almost entirely within the diocese of Oxford, with 215 ecclesiastical parishes located wholly or partially within its borders. Politically, the county is split into three parliamentary divisions: Southern (Wycombe), Mid (Aylesbury), and Northern (Buckingham), each of which returns one member. Additionally, the county contains a small portion of the parliamentary borough of Windsor, which is primarily located in Berkshire.

## Economy and Industry
Buckinghamshire has historically been characterized as an agricultural county rather than a manufacturing one, gaining fame for its cattle and corn. An old proverb references "Buckinghamshire bread and beef," and the vale of Aylesbury is noted by Fuller for producing the largest bodied sheep in England.

The county's industrial history includes several key trades:
* **Lace-making:** This profitable industry was introduced by Fleming refugees fleeing the Alva persecution. Olney and Newport Pagnell were particularly famous for this craft. The parish of Hanslape is reported to have made an annual profit between £8,000 and £9,000 from lace manufacture. However, the trade suffered due to the monopolies of James I, leading to a petition to the high sheriff in 1623 regarding the decay of bone lace-making.
* **Straw-plait:** Introduced during the reign of George I, this industry previously employed a significant portion of the population.

## Notable Institutions and Political History
The most prominent institution in the county is Eton College, a famous public school established by Henry VI.

The county's parliamentary representation has evolved significantly over time. First represented by two members in 1290, the number of representatives grew as towns gained representative rights, reaching a total of fourteen members (including boroughs) by 1603. This number was reduced to eleven by the Reform Act of 1832. Following the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885, the boroughs lost their representation, and the county returned three members for its three divisions.
