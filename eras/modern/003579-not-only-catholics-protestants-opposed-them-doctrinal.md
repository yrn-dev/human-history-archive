# The Concept and Development of Dogma

The term "dogma" has undergone significant evolution in meaning, particularly within the contexts of Roman Catholic and Protestant theology. While it was once frequently used interchangeably with "doctrine," it eventually developed into a more precise term denoting truths defined by specific authorities.

## Evolution of the Term in the Early Modern Period
In the early modern period, both Roman Catholic and Protestant writers used "dogma" frequently when referring to heretics. For instance, the Augsburg Confession stated that Protestants had carefully avoided *nova dogmata*. During this time, the term often remained synonymous with doctrine. Philip Melanchthon, in his 1535 preface to *Loci communes*, used the phrase *de ullo dogmate* to refer to any point of doctrine. Similarly, the Council of Trent (1545–1562) used the term in the context of reforming dogmas (*reformandis dogmatibus*), maintaining the identification of dogma with doctrine.

However, by the time the collected *decreta* of Trent were published in 1564, the term began to shift toward a narrower meaning: truth defined by church authority. This marked the beginning of dogma's separation from the broader category of all doctrines.

## Dogma in Protestant Scholasticism
Under Protestant scholasticism (both Lutheran and Reformed), a highly definite conception of dogma emerged. Protestant creeds affirmed that authority resided only in Scripture; consequently, theologians defined dogma as the entire contents of the Bible, including historical, scientific, and doctrinal texts. In this view, dogma is revealed and infallibly true because it is the body of doctrines and facts propounded by God Himself. Johann Gerhard, a representative of Lutheran scholasticism, asserted that every true dogma appears in plain terms somewhere in Scripture.

The Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577) reinforced this by positioning Scripture as the sole *regula* (rule) by which all *dogmata* are to be tested. Later, some Protestant scholars, such as F. Loofs, proposed a "generally accepted position" that dogma is any doctrine enforced within a specific church community. However, this view faced criticism from others, such as A. Schweizer, who argued that Protestantism should not use the term "dogma" at all, except when referring to its imperfect past.

## Roman Catholic Definitions and Papal Authority
In Roman Catholic theology, the definition of dogma has remained complex. A distinction is sometimes made between "dogmas strictly" and "dogmatic truths," as well as between "material dogmas" (all traditional or scriptural truth) and "formal" dogmas (those ecclesiastically formulated). 

The role of the papacy is central to the Roman Catholic understanding of dogma. The Vatican decrees of 1870 affirmed the personal infallibility of the pope as *divinitus revelatum dogma*. This established that the pope and the council use "dogma" to describe what is definitely formulated by authority. Furthermore, the church defines faith—which is inseparable from dogma—as being revealed in either Scripture or unwritten tradition, and taught via the church's ordinary *magisterium* or formulated decrees. This implies that unformulated teachings on points of doctrine are as *de fide* as those that have been formally formulated.

The frontier of dogma has proven dynamic. For example, the Immaculate Conception of the Virgin was favored by the church but was not considered a dogma until the definition provided by Pius IX in 1854.

## Dogmatic Theology and Divergent Views
"Dogmatic theology" is the modern name for the systematic study of dogma or Christian doctrine. The term gained vitality through various theologians; Lukas Friedrich Reinhard and Andreas van Essen used the title in the mid-17th century, and F. Buddeus is credited with giving the expression its supremacy. In Roman Catholic scholarship, the related term "positive theology" has also been employed, such as by Petau and Joseph Turmel.

Despite these shared academic terms, the Catholic and Protestant definitions of dogma often exist on different planes. While the Catholic view emphasizes authoritative formulation, the Protestant scholastic view identified dogma with the total text of the Bible. In modern Protestant church life, the name "dogma" has largely lost its original connotation of immediate divine authority.

## Other Doctrinal Disagreements
Beyond the definition of dogma, Catholics and Protestants have differed on specific doctrinal applications. Catholic doctrine teaches that the Fall caused all animate and inanimate things to be subject to evil spirits, a belief supported by the epistles of St Paul to the Romans and Timothy. This leads to the practice of exorcism (to cast out devils) and benediction (to prevent them from entering). Orthodox Protestants have condemned these formulae as magic. In response, Catholics have argued that such accusations mirror those made by the Pharisees against Christ, who was accused of casting out devils by Beelzebub.
