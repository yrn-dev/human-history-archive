# Pars Compendii Theologiae and the Compendium

The *Pars Compendii Theologiae* and the *Compendium Studii Theologiae* are theological manuscripts held within the British Museum. These works are associated with a body of manuscripts that include various treatises on physics, mathematics, and logic.

## Related Manuscripts in the British Museum
The British Museum houses several important manuscripts related to these theological works. Among the more significant manuscripts are:
*   The *Compendium Studii Theologiae*.
*   The *Quinta Pars Compendii Theologiae*, which exists as a fragment.
*   *Baconis Physica*, located among the additional manuscripts.
*   The *Communia Naturalium*, an extensive work focusing on the fundamental notions of physics.

Other related works are distributed across different institutions, such as the *De Communibus Mathematicae* (found in the Bodleian and the Sloane collection) and the *Summulae Dialectices* (found in the Bodleian). Additionally, glosses upon the metaphysics and physics of Aristotle are located in the library at Amiens.

## Context of the Author
The works are linked to Bacon, who achieved significant renown during his time in Paris, where he earned the degree of doctor of theology and was given the title *doctor mirabilis*. Around 1250, Bacon returned to Oxford and likely entered the Franciscan order. Despite his fame, he faced suspicions regarding his orthodoxy and accusations of dealing in the black arts.

In 1257, Bonaventura, the general of the order, interdicted Bacon's lectures at Oxford and ordered him to be supervised in Paris. For ten years, Bacon remained under supervision, facing strict prohibitions against writing for publication and enduring great privations.

## Papal Intervention and Later Works
Bacon's situation changed in 1266 when Pope Clement IV (formerly the papal legate Guy de Foulques) ordered him to write a treatise on the sciences. Despite a lack of funds, skilled copyists, and materials, Bacon completed three large treatises in approximately eighteen months: the *Opus Majus*, *Opus Minus*, and *Opus Tertium*.

Following these works, Bacon was permitted to return to Oxford in 1268. He continued his work in experimental science and the creation of complete treatises. He viewed the works sent to the pope as preliminaries that established principles for application to the sciences. One such encyclopaedic effort is the *Compendium Studii Philosophiae* (1271), in which Bacon criticized the vices and ignorance of monks and clergy, as well as the insufficiency of contemporary studies.
