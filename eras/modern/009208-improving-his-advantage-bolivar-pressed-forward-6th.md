# Simon Bolivar and the Liberation of South America

Simon Bolivar (1783–1830) was a central figure in the independence of South America. Born on July 24, 1783, in Caracas, Venezuela, he was the son of Maria Concepcion Palacios y Sojo and Juan Vicente Bolivar y Ponte, both of whom came from noble Venezuelan families. Bolivar's early adulthood was marked by extensive study and travel in Europe, including several years in Madrid and time spent in Paris, where he witnessed the final stages of the Revolution.

After marrying the daughter of Don N. Toro in 1801 and returning to Venezuela to manage his estate, Bolivar was driven back to Europe by the death of his wife from yellow fever. He returned home in 1809 via the United States, where he observed the functioning of free institutions. Shortly after his arrival in Venezuela, he joined the cause of independence. He was a promoter of the April 1810 insurrection in Caracas and served as a colonel under the revolutionary junta, which included a mission to the court of Great Britain.

## The Republic of Colombia and Early Military Campaigns
Following Venezuela's declaration of independence on July 5, 1811, war broke out with the advance of Spanish troops under Monteverde. Bolivar commanded the post of Puerto Cabello but was forced to evacuate due to a lack of support and the inaction of Miranda, allowing the Spaniards to regain control.

Later, Bolivar returned to Angostura, where he was hailed as the father and deliverer of his country. On December 17, 1819, he obtained the enactment of a fundamental law that united the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar serving as president. The government was provisionally seated at Rosario de Cucuta.

Leading a well-appointed army, Bolivar gained advantages over the Spanish forces under Morillo. Although a six-month armistice was concluded at Truxillo on November 25, 1820, the contest resumed after Morillo was replaced by General Torre. Bolivar achieved a decisive victory at Carabobo, routing the Spaniards and forcing the remnants of their army to retreat to Puerto Cabello, where they surrendered to Paez two years later. This battle effectively ended the war in Venezuela. Bolivar entered Caracas on June 29, 1821, and the constitution of Colombia was adopted on August 30, 1821, with Bolivar as president and Santander as vice-president.

## Campaigns in Ecuador and Peru
Bolivar sought to complete the liberation of the region by expelling Spaniards from Peru and Ecuador. He led an army to Quito in Ecuador, where his colleague Sucre routed the Spaniards at the battle of Pichincha, allowing republicans to enter Quito in June 1822.

Bolivar then advanced on Lima. The royalists evacuated the capital upon his approach, and Bolivar was invested with absolute power as dictator. However, intrigues among republican factions in Peru forced him to withdraw to Truxillo, and the capital was re-occupied by the Spaniards under Canterac. By June 1824, the liberating army was reorganized. Bolivar pressed forward and defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin on August 6. While Bolivar returned to Lima, Sucre pursued the royalists into Upper Peru, achieving a decisive victory at Ayacucho. This victory dispersed the Spanish force, leaving only the castles of Callao—maintained by Rodil for over a year—under Spanish control.

## The State of Bolivia and Later Political Struggles
In June 1825, Bolivar visited Upper Peru. Having detached itself from the government of Buenos Aires, the region became a separate state named Bolivia in Bolivar's honor. In August 1825, the first congress of the new republic declared Bolivar its perpetual protector and requested that he prepare a constitution. This constitution was later approved by a congress installed at Chuquisaca on May 25, 1826. Under this constitution, General Sucre was chosen as president for life, though he accepted the role for only two years on the condition that 2,000 Colombian troops remain with him.

In September 1826, Bolivar left Lima, entrusting the government to a council led by Santa Cruz. He arrived in Bogota on November 14, assuming extraordinary powers to address rebellions. He traveled to Venezuela to stop bloodshed, reaching Puerto Cabello on December 31 and issuing a general amnesty the following day. After meeting with Paez, he established headquarters in Caracas to manage disturbances in the northern departments.

Despite being re-elected president in January 1827, Bolivar formally resigned the presidency in February, expressing a desire to retire to his patrimonial estate to refute claims of ambition. However, Santander and a resolution of congress urged him to resume his functions to appease the country's agitations. Bolivar agreed to return to Bogota to take the oaths, issuing decrees for a general amnesty, a national convention at Ocana, and the establishment of constitutional order throughout Colombia.
