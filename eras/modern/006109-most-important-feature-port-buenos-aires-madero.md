# The Madero Docks and the Port of Buenos Aires

The port of Buenos Aires, located on the west shore of the La Plata estuary approximately 155 miles above its mouth, is a critical maritime hub for Argentina. Because the estuary is shallow at this point, vessels can only enter the docks via artificial channels that require constant dredging to remove the great quantity of silt deposited by the river. The most significant feature of this port is the "Madero docks," a costly artificial construction designed to enlarge and improve shipping facilities.

## Development and Construction
Prior to the creation of the new port, ocean-going vessels with a draught exceeding 15 feet were forced to anchor in the outer roads, some 12 miles from the city. At that time, communication with the shore relied on small boats and steam tenders that connected to carts on the beach or long landing piers. 

Initial efforts to improve the port began in 1872 at the "Boca," the port located on the Riachuelo. These early improvements involved dredging a 12-meter channel to deep water and establishing landing facilities at a cost of nearly £1,500,000. However, these measures proved insufficient. In 1887, work commenced on a more ambitious plan executed by Sir John Hawkshaw. This project involved the creation of two basins and four docks situated in front of the city, occupying 3 miles of reclaimed shoreline. These facilities are connected to deep water through two dredged channels.

## Facilities and Infrastructure
The Madero docks include several specialized features to handle high volumes of trade. The north basin contains two dry docks, and the new quays are equipped with 24 warehouses and hydraulic cranes. Additionally, the port features 28 miles of railway connections and sidings. 

The financial investment in these works was substantial. Up to 1908, the total cost of the new port works was approximately £8,000,000 sterling ($40,000,000 gold). In September 1908, congress decided to borrow an additional £5,000,000 for further necessary extensions. Maintenance is a continuous expense; in 1908, £560,000 was voted for the constant dredging of the channels and allied purposes.

## Trade and Economic Impact
The port of Buenos Aires, along with Ensenada, concentrates an immense volume of business because the navigation of the Paraná is difficult and impossible for larger ocean-going steamers. Consequently, the great trunk railways of the republic converge at these ports to transport the products of the fertile soil to transatlantic steamship lines.

In 1907, the port recorded 29,178 shipping entries with an aggregate of 13,335,737 tons. The movement of merchandise for that year consisted of 4,360,000 tons of imports and 2,900,000 tons of produce exports. Financially, the port generated revenues of $5,452,000 gold in 1907, with working expenses of $2,213,000 gold. This resulted in a profit of $3,229,000, which represented approximately 8% of the construction cost.

## Regional Context
While Buenos Aires is a primary hub, the province of Buenos Aires—the largest and most populous in the republic—possesses other maritime interests. Bahia Blanca is noted as one of the best natural ports on the Argentine coast and serves as a shipping port for the southern districts of the province and the national territories of Neuquen and La Pampa, exporting large quantities of wool and wheat. Other river ports in the region include San Nicolas on the Paraná and the artificial basins at Ensenada (La Plata).
