# The Himalayan Range: Geology and Geography

The Himalayan range is noted as being the best known of all the Asiatic ranges from a geological perspective. The evidence provided by the range indicates that its elevation was produced by an overthrust from the north. This process occurred as if the high land of central Asia had been pushed southward, moving against and over the unyielding mass of old rocks found in the Indian peninsula. Consequently, the edges of the over-riding strata were folded and crumpled.

## Geological Composition and Formation
Asia can be viewed as consisting of a northern mass and a southern mass, both of which were too rigid to crumple, though not too strong to fracture. Between these two masses lay an intermediate belt of softer rock capable of folding. Due to the contraction of the earth's interior, the outer crust was forced to accommodate a smaller nucleus, causing the central softer belt to yield by crumpling while the rigid masses to the north and south yielded by faulting.

During the Mesozoic era, a land mass existed in the north of Asia and another in the south, separated by a sea where ordinary marine sediments were deposited. The current belt of folding corresponds fairly closely with the location of this central sea. In specific regions such as Kumaon, the principal geological formations consist of granite, gneiss, slate, sandstone, and limestone.

## Regional Geography and Topography
The Himalayas encompass diverse and extreme terrains. In the administrative division of Kumaon, the landscape is described as a maze of mountains, including some of the loftiest known. Within a tract measuring 40 meters in breadth and 140 meters in length, there are more than thirty peaks that exceed elevations of 18,000 feet.

In the Punjab's Kulu subdivision, the Himalayan system includes the mid-Himalayan ranges and the Bara Bangahal, which rise to a mean elevation of 18,000 feet. The region also features the Rohtang pass, situated 13,326 feet above the sea. The Beas river drains this basin, descending from the Rohtang pass with an average fall of 125 feet per mile through a landscape of precipitous cliffs, gorges, and cataracts.

To the east, the main chain of the Himalayas rises abruptly from the alluvial plain of the Brahmaputra valley. The Himalayan system is joined at its eastern extremity to the Patkai range.

## Flora, Fauna, and Natural Resources
The Himalayan forests contain valuable timber under official supervision, with primary tree species including the fir, cypress, alder, *saindan*, sal (or iron-wood), and the three-leaved Himalayan pine, also known as chir.

The range is home to specialized wildlife, such as the markhor (*Capra falconeri*), a large wild goat characterized by a long shaggy winter coat and spirally twisted horns. The markhor's range extends from the Pir-Panjal range of Kashmir westwards into Afghanistan, Hunza, Astor, Baltistan, and the trans-Indus ranges of the Punjab. Additionally, the Himalayan brown bear (*U. a. isabellinus*) and the Himalayan black bear (*U. torquatus*) are found in the region.

Mineral wealth is present in the range; Kumaon contains mines of asbestos, lead, gypsum, copper, and iron, though these are not thoroughly worked. Similarly, great mineral wealth exists in Kulu, though development is hindered by a lack of labour and the difficulty of transport.

## Climate and Hydrology
The climate of the Himalayas varies by elevation and location. In Kumaon, the climate is generally mild except in deep valleys and submontane strips. The outer Himalayan range receives double the rainfall of the central hills—an average of 80 inches compared to 40 inches—as it is the first area struck by the monsoon. Snow is a regular occurrence on higher ridges during winter.

The range serves as a critical watershed. In Kumaon, rivers such as the Kailganga, Pindar, and Sarda (Kali) rise on the southern slope of the Tibetan watershed north of the highest peaks, flowing down through valleys of extraordinary depth and rapid declivity to join the Alaknanda.

## Historical and Strategic Significance
The Himalayan range has historically functioned as a formidable geographic barrier. While central Asiatic tides of immigration—including the Huns, Goths, Jats, Yue-chi, and Sacae—entered the Indian peninsula via the Kabul valley and Makran gateways, they never overflowed the Himalayan barrier. Those tides that moved toward the Himalaya broke against its farther buttresses, leaving ethnographical flotsam in the northern valleys but failing to penetrate the peninsula through eastern passes.
