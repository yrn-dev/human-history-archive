# Bourbon Intrigues and British Attacks

The history of the Bourbon dynasty is marked by periods of exile, political instability, and conflict with opposing powers, most notably during the Napoleonic era and the French Revolution. These tensions manifested in various plots and military confrontations across Europe.

## The Bourbon Dynasty in Southern Italy

In early 1806, Napoleon dispatched a French force to Naples with the objective of expelling the Bourbon dynasty from southern Italy. Napoleon promised the Neapolitan crown to his brother, Joseph, should he choose to accept it. While the French quickly conquered the mainland, certain strongholds—specifically Reggio, Gaeta, and the rock of Scylla—resisted for several months.

The Bourbon court eventually retired to Sicily, where they were granted protection by a British force. Following a decree on March 30, 1806, Napoleon proclaimed Joseph as the king of Naples, though Joseph maintained his claims to the French throne.

## Challenges to Joseph's Reign

The administration of Joseph in Naples was fraught with significant difficulties. According to the memoirs of Count Miot de Melito, the minister of war, Joseph faced a degraded and fickle populace and a treasury that was almost bankrupt. Beyond these internal systemic issues, the new monarch had to contend with frequent attacks launched by the British from their base in Sicily, as well as various Bourbon plots and intrigues.

The stability of Joseph's throne was severely shaken on July 3 by a victory achieved by General Stuart at Maida. However, the subsequent surrender of Gaeta allowed Massena to move south and subdue the region of Calabria. Despite these conflicts, Joseph implemented several reforms during his brief reign, including the reorganization of educational, financial, and judicial systems, the reform of monastic orders, the abolition of feudal relics, and the initiation of public works.

## The Bourbons and the French Revolution

During the period of the French National Assembly, the French state was encumbered by a court and a king determined to create mischief. The queen maintained close correspondence with other exiled princes, including the Duke of Bourbon and the Count of Artois. Together, they attempted to persuade Prussia and Austria to launch attacks against the new French nation.

## The Return of Napoleon and the Bourbon Restoration

Following a period of restored Bourbon kingship, Napoleon returned to France after eleven months of exile on Elba. He had evaded the British ships that were monitoring the island and landed at Cannes. His return to Paris was described as a triumphal procession, during which he walked on white Bourbon cockades.

For a period known as "the Hundred Days," Napoleon regained mastery of France. This created a dilemma for the French people, who found themselves caught between the "dull weight" of the restored old kingship and an adventurer who had betrayed the republic. The allies of the time refused to consider further republican experiments, insisting that the choice was strictly between Napoleon and the Bourbons.
