# Northern Asia

## Geography and Boundaries
The northern boundary of Asia is defined by the Arctic Ocean, with a coastline situated between 70 and 75 degrees North. The most northern point of the continent is Cape Chelyuskin (also known as Cape Sivero-Vostochnyi), located at 78 degrees North. To the east, the continent reaches its furthest point at East Cape (Vostochnyi or Dezhnev) at 190 degrees East, at the entrance of the Bering Strait.

The western boundary is marked by the Mediterranean shore at Cape Baba (26 degrees East), extending through the Red Sea to the strait of Bab-el-Mandeb. It also follows the Black Sea and the Caucasus range toward the Caspian, then moves north along the Ural Mountains to meet the Arctic Ocean opposite the island of Novaya Zemlya. The eastern boundary follows the Northern Pacific and China Sea, characterized by the recession of the Yellow Sea and the Sea of Okhotsk, and the projection of the Korean and Kamchatka peninsulas.

## Exploration and Scientific Surveys
In northern Asia and Siberia, organized Russian surveys have provided revelations that have altered the geographical understanding of the region's forest-clad mountains and vast steppes. These surveys suggest that the area holds a new world of economic resources for Russian enterprise. A notable 1892 expedition led by Baron Toll through the Lena river regions collected material that helps clarify the geological history of the world, specifically proving the primeval existence of a boreal zone of the Jurassic sea surrounding the North Pole.

Other significant Russian explorers in Asia during the 19th century include Potanin, P.K. Kozlov, Lessar, and Annentkov, the latter of whom constructed a railway to bridge the Trans-Caspian deserts. Between 1889 and 1894, explorers Pevtsov and Roborovski, following Prjevalsky, expanded knowledge regarding the northern borders of Tibet and the topography of western Chinese Turkestan.

Earlier maritime exploration of the north-east passage to eastern Asia was attempted by the Dutch navigator Willem Barents in the late 16th century. Barents reached the west coast of Novaya Zemlya in 1594. During a subsequent journey, his vessel was beset by ice, forcing his party to winter in the north. Barents died on June 30, 1597. His winter house and various relics were later discovered in 1871.

## Populations and Culture
The northern half of Asia is inhabited largely by Mongolians who are almost entirely shepherds, herdsmen, or hunters. The most peaceful of these groups are those residing in Siberia, who are considered the least advanced. Between the 40th and 50th circles of North latitude, a zone is occupied by tribes known as Tatars—and to the ancients as Scythians—which include the Turks, Moguls, Mongols proper, and Manchus.

The Turks are Mahommedans whose tribes extend to the Caspian, the borders of Persia and Afghanistan (via the Oxus), and into Russia under the name of Kirghiz. They use Persian letters. The Mongols and Manchus are primarily Buddhist and use letters derived from ancient Syriac. It is noted that the Manchus are losing their peculiar language and nomadic habits as they fall under the influence of Chinese civilization. In contrast, the Chinese branch of the Mongolian family are traders and agriculturists who are settled and partially Buddhist; they utilize a monosyllabic, uninflected language with writing consisting of symbols representing words.

## Historical Migrations and Conflicts
The predatory habits of the Manchu, Mongolian, and Turkish populations of northern Asia, including their irruptions into Europe and other continental regions, have had remarkable effects on world history. For example, the Scythians once drove the Cimmerians from their territory. While the Cimmerians fled into Asia Minor, the Scythian pursuers followed a route east of Mount Caucasus near the Caspian sea, which led them into Media.

Regarding the Indian peninsula, trans-border surveys indicate that central Asiatic tides of immigration flowed into the region through only two gateways: the west (Makran) and the north-west (Kabul valley). The Kabul valley route, including passes across the Hindu Kush from Kaoshan to Khawak, served as the entry point for various central Asian hordes, including the Huns, Goths, Jats, Yue-chi, and Sacae. While some originated in eastern Asia, none entered India through eastern passes, as the Himalayan barrier prevented such overflow.

In more recent history, Russia and Japan engaged in conflict over northern Asia. Russia refused to grant Japan rights regarding Manchuria and conducted aggressions in northern Korea while prolonging negotiations. Japan, facing the choice between permanent effacement in Asia or war, chose war.
