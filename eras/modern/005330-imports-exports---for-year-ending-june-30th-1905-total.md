## United Kingdom Dairy and Brewing Trade

During the last decade of the 19th century, the United Kingdom demonstrated an inability to produce sufficient dairy products for its own population. Butter imports rose from 102,500 tons in the year ending June 30, 1891, to 170,700 tons by June 30, 1900, reflecting an average annual increase of 6,800 tons. This growth was generally uniform, though occasionally disrupted by deficient seasons in foreign countries and colonies. For example, in 1896, a drought in Australia caused colonial supplies to fall by 5,000 tons, though this was offset by an increase of over 16,500 tons from foreign sources. Conversely, in 1900, foreign imports decreased by over 8,000 tons, while colonial supplies rose by 15,000 tons over the previous year.

Between 1891 and 1900, colonial butter imports increased by 34,600 tons and foreign imports by 33,600 tons. Specifically, the Australasian colonies increased their quota by 13,400 tons and Canada by 11,100 tons. Among foreign nations, Denmark showed the most significant development, increasing its supply by 28,678 tons, followed by Russia (7,207 tons) and Holland (6,589 tons). Sweden's exports declined after 1896, sending 1,400 tons less in 1900 than in 1891.

The proportion of home-made butter in the United Kingdom fell from 45.4% in 1891 to 32.8% in 1900. The ten-year average for butter consumption indicates that 37.6% was home-made, 7.3% was imported from colonies, and 55.1% was imported from foreign sources.

Regarding cheese, Canada dominated the colonial import market. In 1900, out of a total colonial import of 128,600 tons, New Zealand provided only 4,000 tons, and Australia sent only small quantities.

In the brewing industry, the United Kingdom exported 520,826 barrels of beer and imported 57,194 barrels in the period recorded, with these figures remaining nearly stationary for thirty years.
