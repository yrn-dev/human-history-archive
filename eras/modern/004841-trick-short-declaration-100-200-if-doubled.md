# Bridge

Bridge is a card game with several variations, including Auction Bridge, Three-handed Bridge, and ordinary bridge. The game involves the dealing of cards, the declaration of trumps or no-trump (*sans atout*), and a scoring system based on tricks and honours.

## Auction Bridge
Introduced at the Bath Club in London in 1907 and settled by a joint committee of the Bath and Portland clubs in August 1908, Auction Bridge incorporates elements of poker. Unlike ordinary bridge, the declaration is not limited to the dealer or their partner. 

The dealer must declare a win of at least one "odd" trick. Following this, players in turn—starting from the dealer's left—may pass, double, redouble, or overcall with a declaration of higher value. A declaration is considered of higher value if it involves a greater number of tricks in a suit of lower value (for example, two spades against one club). Doubling and redoubling affect the score but not the declaration; thus, a call of two diamonds overcalls a doubled one no-trump.

The partner who made the first call in the final trump suit (or no-trump) plays the combined hands, while the other partner serves as the dummy.

## Three-handed Bridge
In this version, three players participate. The player who cuts the lowest card deals and takes the dummy for one deal, with players rotating the dummy role. The dealer declares without seeing the dummy's cards. If the dealer declares trumps, adversaries may view their hands; dummy's hand is only exposed after the first card is led.

If the dealer passes the declaration to the dummy (the right-hand adversary), that player examines the dummy's hand and declares trumps without exposing it. In this variation, declarations are forced: *sans atout* must be declared with three or four aces; otherwise, the longest suit is declared. If suits are equal in length, the strongest is chosen (ace counting eleven, king, queen, and knave counting ten). If length and strength are equal, the suit with the higher trick value is chosen.

The game is 30, and the first player to win two games wins the rubber. Fifty points are scored for each game won and fifty more for the rubber. Only tricks won in a player's own deal score toward the game; other tricks are scored above the line with honours, slam, and chicane.

## Scoring and Honours
In standard play, the game consists of 30 points made by tricks alone. Each trick above six counts points based on the declaration: 2 for spades, 4 for clubs, 6 for diamonds, 8 for hearts, and 12 for no-trump. A rubber ends when one side wins two games, and the winners add 100 points to their score.

Honours consist of the ace, king, queen, knave, and ten in a suit declaration. Scoring for conjoint honours is as follows:
* 3 honours: twice the value of a trick.
* 4 honours: 4 times the value of a trick.
* 5 honours: 5 times the value of a trick.

If a player holds 4 honours in their own hand, they score 4 additional honours; if they hold 5, they score 10. In no-trump hands, partners conjointly holding 3 aces score 30, and 4 aces score 40. Four aces in a single hand count 100.

Additional points are awarded for:
* **Chicane**: scored if a player holds no trump, equal to simple honours.
* **Grand slam**: 40 points for winning all tricks.
* **Little slam**: 20 points for winning 12 tricks.

In Auction Bridge, if a player fails to win the declared number of tricks, opponents score 50 points above the line for each under-trick (100 or 200 if doubled or redoubled). Loss on a one-spade declaration is limited to 100 points.

## Doubling and Play
Doubling increases the value of each trick. In ordinary bridge, doubling makes each trick worth twice as many points, and redoubling makes them worth four times as much, though the value of a trick is limited to 100 points. Doubling does not alter scores for honours, chicane, or slam.

Strategic doubling often depends on the player's hand. A leader typically doubles a no-trump declaration when holding an established suit of seven cards (including ace, king, and queen) or six cards (including king, queen, and knave) with two aces in other suits. Doubling by the third hand generally indicates a very strong suit.

In the play of the hand, the main object of a no-trump declaration is to bring in a long suit. Favourable conditions include one hand holding at least five cards of the suit and the two hands combined holding eight. The dealer is encouraged to play false cards to provide as little information as possible about their hand.
