# The Gun as a Thermo-Dynamic Machine

## Interior Ballistics and Thermodynamic Principles
A gun can be characterized as a simple heat-engine or thermo-dynamic machine. Unlike a standard gas-engine or steam-engine, which operates through a series of periodic cycles, a gun performs its work in a single stroke. The study of this process falls under the branch of interior ballistics. This field investigates the dynamics of the shot's movement up the bore, the stress placed upon the gun's material, the work realized by the expansion of the powder, and the relations between the temperature, volume, and pressure of the powder-gas inside the bore.

## Accuracy and Ballistic Variables
Achieving absolute certainty in hitting a specific spot every time is unattainable, regardless of the care taken in pointing and laying according to theory and practice. Several non-eliminable causes of error contribute to this, including variations in the muzzle-velocity, variations in the air, and the steadiness of the shot during its flight. To calculate the accuracy of a gun, the laws of probability are applied to 50% zones shown in range tables, utilizing as much actual practice as is available.

## Machine-Gun Mechanisms and Operation
Modern machine-guns are automatic weapons designed to deliver small shells or bullets at a high rate of fire, either in quick succession or by volleys. While older mechanisms were hand-operated, modern versions utilize the force of recoil or the gas from the explosion to eject fired cartridge cases, load the weapon, and lock and unlock the breech mechanism. These weapons are categorized into three general types:
* **Small types:** These approximate the "automatic rifle," which is expected to replace the magazine rifle for infantry.
* **Medium types:** The standard machine-gun of the present day, which uses ordinary rifle ammunition and is fired from various carriages.
* **Large types:** Known generically as "pompoms," these fire light artillery projectiles and are viewed by some artillery experts as the gun of the future.

## Thermal Management in Machine-Guns
A primary difficulty in single-barrel machine-guns is the heating of the barrel. For example, in a Maxim gun, 600 rounds of rapid fire (approximately 1.5 minutes) can raise the 7½ pints of water in the water-jacket to the boiling point. Continuous operation results in the evaporation of about 1.5 pints of water for every 1,000 rounds. The waste of energy due to heat expended on the water alone is equivalent to approximately 20 horse-power (294 foot tons per minute). 

While the water-jacket is effective at controlling barrel temperature, providing water during service can be troublesome. Other designs attempt to manage heat differently; the Hotchkiss gun utilizes a radiator, while the Colt gun employs a very heavy barrel.

## Tactical Employment and Limitations
The mechanism of a machine-gun is typically set to a rate of fire between 350 and 700 rounds per minute. Because of the strain on the weapon and the waste of ammunition caused by prolonged firing, armies maintain the axiom that fire should be delivered in "bursts" and only on favorable targets. 

Operating these weapons presents several challenges:
* **Fire Proportioning:** It is difficult to proportion fire to a target because there are only two rates of fire: slow single shots and rapid automatic. Firing a single shot requires skill, as the gun may fire some 7 shots in one second.
* **Aiming:** Vibration during rapid fire can make aiming difficult, even when the gun is on a firmly fixed mounting.
* **Psychological Effect:** The "drumming sound" of machine-guns produces an immense moral effect on combatants, comparable in some stages of battle to the boom of artillery.

Historically, many military powers distrusted the machine-gun, and for years it was described as a "diabolical weapon before which nothing could live." Early British instruction simply labeled it a "weapon of opportunity." However, after the Japanese observed the effective use of Russian machine-guns at Liao-Yang during the war of 1904-05, they introduced the weapon into their field armies.

## Equipment and Transport
Modern armies have largely moved away from simple wheeled carriages for machine-guns, adopting table mountings or tripods instead. Wheeled vehicles are now reserved primarily for transporting the equipment. Since the Russo-Japanese War, there has been a trend to remove shields to reduce visibility. In that conflict, the Japanese found it advisable to use unprotected field guns and protected machine-guns, though they later reversed this procedure.
