# A. S. Brown (London, 1901)

## Overview
A. S. Brown is the author of the work *Madeira and the Canary Islands, with the Azores*, published in London in 1901. This text is cited as a source for those seeking a general account of these specific island groups.

## Comparative Island Flora and Fauna
While the works of Brown and Drouet focus on the Atlantic islands, the archive contains data on the flora and fauna of other island groups for comparison:

### Pacific Islands
The Kermadec islands, a British possession in the Pacific (approximately 30° S., 178° W.), feature flora and fauna that mostly belong to those of New Zealand. The largest island in this group, Raoul (or Sunday Island), is noted for being thickly wooded.

### The Bahamas
The Bahamas possess a flora that is more diverse than its fauna. The islands feature various soil types, including a white soil along shores, a reddish clay, and a black soil of decayed vegetable matter found in forested areas. New Providence contains a "pine barrens" area. Andros Island and the Abaco Islands are noted for large timber, such as bullet, iron, lignum vitae, mastic, and mahogany woods. The islands produce a wide array of spices and fruits, including pomegranates, citrons, lemons, oranges, olives, tamarinds, bananas, pine-apples, and others. Indigenous plants include cotton, which was used by aborigines for cloth. The fauna is relatively poor, though mentions exist of a small coney and a breed of non-barking dogs used by aborigines.

### The Balearic Isles
The flora and fauna of the Balearic archipelago resemble those found on the Mediterranean coasts of France or Spain. The region's climate is generally healthy and favourable to plant life, though it varies based on shelter from wind and altitude.

## Regional Biological Boundaries
The archive notes the existence of "Wallace's line," named after the naturalist A.R. Wallace. This line of physical separation follows the Straits of Macassar (approximately 120 deg. E.) between Celebes and Borneo. To the west of this line, flora and fauna are essentially Asiatic in type, whereas the Australian element becomes predominant to the south and east.
