# The Bar and Parliamentary Houses

## The Bar in English Law and Parliament
In the English legal system, the term "bar" refers to a partition that divides law-courts into two distinct sections. This division was created to separate the general public, suitors, their advocates, and prisoners from the officials and members of the court. While this division is theoretically maintained, specific rules govern who may sit within the bar. Those entitled to do so include solicitors, serjeants (until the order ceased to exist), barristers with patents of precedence, and king's counsel. Conversely, the general public and other members of the bar remain outside. In criminal trials, the accused is required to stand forward at the bar, while parties in civil suits appearing in person are permitted to stand on the floor within the bar.

A "bar" also exists within the British Parliament. In the House of Commons, this is a literal bar consisting of a long brass rod stored in a tube and pulled out when necessary to establish the technical boundary of the House. This boundary serves two primary functions: those charged with violating the privileges of the House appear before it, and members who have been returned at bye-elections sit below it while they await their introduction to the House and the taking of the oath of allegiance. Additionally, in the House of Lords, "the bar" is the designation for the location where members of the House of Commons and Mr. Speaker stand when they are summoned by Black Rod.

## Judicial and Legislative Houses in England
The House of Lords includes specific judicial roles, such as the Lords of Appeal in Ordinary. Limited to four individuals, these members must have practiced at the bar for at least fifteen years or held high judicial office. They sit as members of the House of Lords to adjudicate cases in a legal capacity and assist the judicial committee of the Privy Council with appeals. These officials are life peers; they are nominated and appointed as Lords of Appeal in Ordinary by the style of Baron rather than being "created" as barons. They receive a salary of £6000 a year and are entitled to a writ of summons to sit and vote in the House of Lords.

## The Imperial Diet of Japan
The Imperial Diet of Japan consists of two Houses: the House of Peers and the House of Representatives. The opening, prolongation of sessions, closing, and prorogation of the Diet occur simultaneously in both Houses. If the House of Representatives is ordered to dissolve, the House of Peers is prorogued at the same time. Following a dissolution, an Imperial decree orders the election of new members, and the new House must be convoked within five months.

Both Houses of the Imperial Diet operate under specific procedural rules:
* **Quorum and Voting:** No vote can be taken and no debate opened unless at least one-third of the total membership is present. Votes are decided by absolute majority, with the President providing the casting vote in the event of a tie.
* **Deliberations:** While deliberations are generally held in public, they may be held in secret sitting if the House passes a resolution or the Government demands it.
* **Powers:** Both Houses may receive petitions from subjects, present addresses to the Emperor, and enact rules for their own internal management.

## Other Legislative and Representative Bodies
In the context of the British North America Act of 1867, British Columbia sends seven members to the lower house of the federal parliament in Ottawa, as well as three senators. 

In the constitutional structure involving the Austrian Herrenhaus (Upper House) and the Hungarian Table of Magnates (Upper House), as well as the Austrian and Hungarian Houses of Representatives, political control and voting on money for common affairs are handled by Delegations. Each delegation consists of sixty members chosen for one year; one-third are chosen by the Upper Houses and two-thirds by the Houses of Representatives. These delegations are summoned annually by the monarch to Budapest and Vienna. If the two delegations cannot agree on a decision after three written interchanges, a common plenary sitting of an equal number of members from both delegations is held to decide the question by a common vote. Such decisions require the sanction of the monarch to be valid.

## The Household and Social Structure
Outside of formal legislative houses, the concept of the "house" extends to the traditional household. In Hindu life, the householder has a traditional duty of hospitality to offer meals to strangers before midday. In these households, the mistress of the house does not sit for her own meal until every other member is fed, often eating well after midday to ensure a hungry stranger can be accommodated. This type of household structure has prevailed since Neolithic days in the Far East, China, and India, though it has been replaced in the West by industrialism and municipal organization.
