# AVIARY

An aviary (derived from the Latin *avis*, meaning bird) is a structure designed to keep birds in a state of captivity. Older writers referred to such structures as "volaries." While the practice of keeping birds in cages is ancient, structures that qualify as aviaries were likely first utilized by the ancient Romans.

## Historical Development
In the period between 116 and 127 B.C., during the time of Varro, "ornithones" (from the Greek *ornis, ornithos*) were common. These structures served two primary purposes: pleasure and utility. Some were used to keep song-birds, such as nightingales, for pleasure, while others were used by poulterers and owners to fatten birds—such as thrushes—for market or the table. Varro maintained an aviary exclusively for song-birds, whereas Lucullus utilized a combined aviary for both pleasure and delicacies. Despite this, keeping birds for pleasure was rare compared to the common practice of fattening birds for food in large ornithones located in the city or at Sabinum.

The modern practice of using aviaries for pleasure and the study of avian habits is of a more recent date. This trend began with 15th-century geographical research, as explorers brought back beautiful bird species that owners wished to study at home. By the 16th century, aviaries were common in the gardens of the wealthy; the canary-bird was introduced to Europe during the early part of this century, and Francis Bacon mentioned aviaries in his 1597 essay on gardening. An example of an early outdoor aviary was held by Elizabeth of Bohemia, daughter of James I of England, at Coombe Abbey near Coventry. This structure featured a roof and back made of natural rock and housed various species from many different countries.

## Scientific Importance and Aviculture
Aviculture is regarded as both a pastime and a pursuit of scientific importance. Many bird species from around the world have unknown habits because they are rare, reside in inaccessible native haunts, or possess shy and retiring natures that make wild study impossible. In large, suitably designed aviaries, researchers can accurately record and observe behaviors such as courtship, incubation, moult, display, and nesting habits. To reveal these natural secrets, aviaries must be of sufficient size and design; birds will not exhibit these behaviors in small aviaries or cages.

## Construction and Design
Aviaries vary greatly in size, ranging from the "Crystal Palace aviary" (named for its shape) to structures as large as a church. While the term is occasionally applied to a room in a house with wire-netting over the windows, it generally refers to outdoor structures made of wire-netting supported by woodwork or iron frameworks.

### General Requirements
For hardy birds, wire-netting combined with rough wooden shelters, rock-work, or growing shrubs is sufficient. However, delicate tropical species require a wooden or brick house that can be artificially warmed and shut during cold months. To protect birds from biting winds from the north, north-west, and north-east, aviaries should have a southern or southeastern aspect and be sheltered by high walls, banks, or belts of fir-trees.

The exterior of an aviary should include:
*   Turfed areas planted with deciduous and evergreen shrubs.
*   A gravel path.
*   An abundance of pure water for bathing and drinking.

A highly useful design involves multiple compartments with a rear passage, allowing a keeper to visit one section without disturbing birds in others. A sample four-compartment plan includes an inner house of 10 feet square and an outer flight of double that area. These outer flights are turfed and planted, while the gravel path is covered by a glazed roof for protection from wet weather. Water basins should be supplied via an underground pipe and cock operated from outside, connected to an overflow pipe and a properly laid drain.

### Specialized Considerations for Parrots
When housing parrots, most vegetation except grass is useless, as larger parrots will bite through living trees and the woodwork of their enclosure. Consequently, the most suitable materials for parrot aviaries are iron, stone, or brick. Additionally, the wire-netting must be of the stoutest gauge to withstand their strong bills.

## Management of Aviary Birds
Most wild birds will breed in aviaries if provided with appropriate nesting sites that imitate their natural surroundings.
*   **General Birds:** Many species will use evergreen shrubs or large bundles of brushwood fixed in sheltered spots. Those that naturally nest in crevices or holes will use baskets or small boxes fastened to the roof or walls.
*   **Parrots:** Because wild parrots scrape slight hollows in rocks or hollow trees without building nests, they require boxes or hollow logs with small entrance holes near the top.

### Popular Species
Finches and finch-like birds are among the most popular foreign aviary birds. 
*   **African Weaver-birds:** These are generally hardy and eat primarily canary-seed. Males possess brilliant colors and sometimes long tail-feathers during the nuptial season, which are replaced by somber, sparrow-like plumage in winter.
*   **Australasian Grass-finches:** These include the beautiful grass-finch (*Poephila mirabilis*), which features colors such as blue, mauve, green, crimson, and yellow. They feed on millet and canary seeds and enjoy flowering grasses.
*   **African Waxbills:** Including the "cordon-bleu" (crimson-eared waxbill), these birds are very beautiful but delicate, requiring artificial warmth during winter and special care upon initial import.
