# Bolivia

## Agricultural Production and Constraints
The agricultural resources of Bolivia are varied and possess great value, though their development has been slow and hesitating. In the warm and temperate valleys of the Andes, the cultivation of vegetables, fruits, and cereals followed the establishment of mining settlements. In the tropical districts of eastern Bolivia, rice is grown, although production levels are insufficient to meet local requirements. Tobacco of fair quality is produced in the warm eastern regions, including the valleys of Cochabamba and La Paz (specifically the *yungas*). Cacao of a superior grade is grown in the warm Andean valleys of Cochabamba and La Paz, as well as in the department of Beni, where missions planted large orchards. Additionally, coffee of the best flavour is produced in some warmer districts of the eastern Andes.

Two indigenous products receive significant attention: coca and quinoa. Quinoa is a staple food for the natives and is grown in large quantities. Coca is highly esteemed by the natives, who masticate the leaf, and is exported for medicinal purposes; it is cultivated extensively in La Paz and Cochabamba, particularly in the province of Yungas.

Sugar-cane was introduced early in the republic's history. While climatic conditions in eastern Bolivia are highly favourable for this crop, the product is heavily taxed and restricted to a small home market. Due to limited demand for sugar, the cultivation of cane is chiefly devoted to the manufacture of rum.

## Finance and Taxation
Bolivian finance is characterized by a lack of published itemized returns; information is primarily drawn from estimates presented to congress by cabinet ministers. While taxation is not generally considered heavy, the system is complex. National revenues are derived from export duties on rubber, coca, and minerals (excluding silver), as well as mining taxes, taxes on stock company profits, excise taxes on sugar, tobacco, wines, and spirits, and fees for patents and licences.

Although Bolivia is sometimes called a "free-trade country," this is considered a misnomer. In addition to *ad valorem* import rates of 10 to 40%, the state imposes a 1-1/2% consular fee for invoices exceeding 200 bolivianos, a consumption tax of 10 centavos per quintal, and fees for viseing certificates for merchandise in transit. Furthermore, monopolies on tobacco and spirits are subject to special "octroi" taxes.

At the local level, departmental revenues—estimated at 2,296,172 bolivianos in 1903—come from land taxes, excise, tithes, tolls, inheritance taxes, stamp taxes, and mining grants. Municipal revenues in 1902 aggregated 2,317,670 bolivianos, derived from market and consumption taxes, ground rents, leases, a lighting tax, cemetery fees, and taxes on skins and hides. Trade is further burdened by a multiplication of taxes that resembles the old colonial *alcabala* tax, which serves to increase the cost of goods and restrict commerce.

## Commerce and Trade
Foreign trade in Bolivia is comparatively unimportant, and statistical returns are often unsatisfactory. In 1904, imports were valued at L1,734,551, consisting of machinery, hardware, provisions, live-stock, clothing, woollens, cottons, spirits, and wines. Exports in 1903 totaled L2,453,638, dominated by tin and its ores (L1,039,298) and silver and its ores (L636,743), followed by rubber (L260,559), copper ores (L157,609), cinchona (L9,197), coca (L28,907), bismuth (L16,354), and other minerals (L20,948).

The import trade is restricted by the poverty of the population. Exports are limited by the cost of transportation, the tariffs of neighbouring countries, and a geographical position that restricts trade primarily to pastoral, forest, and mineral products.

## Geography and Transport
The loss of its maritime department has left Bolivia without direct access to the ocean, relying instead on the ports of Lake Titicaca (notably Guaqui) and the affluents of the Paraguay and Madeira rivers. Because these routes require transhipment in foreign territories, transport costs are increased, allowing neighbouring nations to exclude Bolivia from direct commercial intercourse with other countries.

Access to the Amazon via the Acre river was lost after Brazil closed the Purus and Acre rivers to foreign vessels and acquired the port of Puerto Acre and adjacent territory in 1903. Consequently, the outlet to the Amazon is restricted to the Madeira river, where navigation is interrupted by falls. The port of Villa Bella is reached via a costly and long portage. Other connections include Puerto Suarez on Lake Caceres, which handles the bulk of trade in that direction, and Puerto Pacheco on Bahia Negra.

## Political History and Diplomacy
In January 1871, President Melgarejo was deposed by a revolution led by Colonel Augustin Morales. Following Morales' murder in November 1872, Colonel Adolfo Ballivian assumed the presidency. Under Ballivian, Bolivia entered a secret agreement with Peru on February 6, 1873, for mutual defence and the preservation of territorial integrity against exterior aggression, specifically the perceived encroachment of Chile.

This tension stemmed from a 1866 treaty with Chile, where Bolivia conceded the 24th parallel as a boundary and allowed Chile a half share of customs and trading facilities between the 23rd and 24th parallels. Chile was also permitted to export and mine in this district without hindrance or tax. In 1870, Bolivia further granted an Anglo-Chilean company the right to work nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel for $10,000. These agreements created significant discontent within Bolivia and Peru.
