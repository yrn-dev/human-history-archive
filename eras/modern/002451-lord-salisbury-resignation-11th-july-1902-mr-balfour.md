# Arthur James Balfour and the Transition of 1902

## Accession to the Premiership
On July 11, 1902, Lord Salisbury resigned as prime minister, ending a seventeen-year period of dominance in which the Liberal Party had failed to exercise effective control over affairs. Mr. Arthur James Balfour succeeded him as prime minister with the cordial approval of all sections of the Unionist party. This transition marked the end of an epoch; while the Conservative members who acclaimed Balfour's accession were unwilling to return to their constituencies—given that the 1900 Parliament was only two years old—the period was characterized by a widespread feeling that change was impending and a vast alienation of public opinion.

## Cabinet Reconstitution and Early Administration
Within a few weeks of taking office, Mr. Balfour reconstituted the cabinet. He assumed the roles of lord privy seal and first lord of the treasury. The leadership of the House of Lords was assigned to the duke of Devonshire, who also remained lord president of the council. Other key appointments included:
* **Foreign Secretary:** Lord Lansdowne
* **Chancellor of the Exchequer:** Mr. (later Lord) Ritchie, replacing Sir Michael Hicks-Beach
* **Colonial Secretary:** Mr. J. Chamberlain
* **Postmaster-General:** Austen Chamberlain (with a seat in the cabinet)
* **Home Secretary:** Mr. Akers-Douglas
* **Secretary for India:** Lord George Hamilton
* **War Minister:** Mr. St John Brodrick
* **Secretary for Scotland:** Lord Balfour of Burleigh
* **Lord Lieutenant of Ireland:** Lord Dudley
* **President of the Board of Education:** Lord Londonderry (with Sir William Anson as parliamentary secretary)
* **Chief Secretary for Ireland:** Mr. G. Wyndham
* **President of the Board of Trade:** Gerald Balfour, Mr. Balfour's brother.

## Political Background and Experience
Arthur James Balfour, born July 25, 1848, was the eldest son of James Maitland Balfour and Lady Blanche Gascoyne Cecil. Educated at Eton and Trinity College, Cambridge, he entered Parliament in 1874 as the M.P. for Hertford. His early career included serving as private secretary to Lord Salisbury during the latter's tenure as foreign minister in 1878, which provided him experience in international politics during the settlement of the Russo-Turkish conflict.

Balfour's path to the premiership involved several departmental "apprenticeships." He served as president of the Local Government Board (June 1885–January 1886) and secretary for Scotland (from July 1886). He gained significant prominence as chief secretary for Ireland, where he implemented a policy of "coercion"—the fearless administration of the Crimes Act—combined with remedial legislation. During this turbulent period, he broke down the Plan of Campaign and opposed the Home Rule party. Later, from 1891, he served as first lord of the treasury and leader of the House of Commons.

## Fiscal Crisis and Cabinet Instability
Balfour's premiership was heavily impacted by internal disputes over fiscal policy. On October 1, Mr. Balfour delivered a speech at Sheffield where he declared his intention to reverse traditional fiscal policy by removing the axiom that import duties should only be levied for revenue purposes. While this was welcomed by the National Union of Conservative Associations and Mr. Chamberlain, it was rejected by free-trade Unionists. Consequently, the duke of Devonshire resigned the following day.

To conciliate the duke, Balfour reconstituted the ministry, appointing Mr. Alfred Lyttelton as colonial secretary, Austen Chamberlain as chancellor of the exchequer, and Lord Salisbury (son of the late premier) as lord privy seal. However, tensions persisted. In September 1905, Balfour published a pamphlet on "Insular Free Trade," arguing for the "freedom to negotiate" to increase exchange. This led to further resignations, including those of Mr. Ritchie, Lord George Hamilton, Lord Balfour of Burleigh, and Mr. Arthur Elliot. Mr. Chamberlain also resigned, following a published correspondence that revealed Balfour's sympathy for retaliatory duties but his disbelief that the country was ready for the taxation of food.

## Decline and End of Administration
The final session of 1905 was marked by attempts to avoid debates on the fiscal question, including an instance where Mr. Balfour and his supporters left the House, allowing a motion hostile to tariff reform to pass *nem. con*. While the government passed the Aliens Bill, the Unemployed Bill, and the Scottish Churches Bill, it suffered a "complete fiasco" regarding redistribution proposals, which were withdrawn due to a procedural blunder.

The administration's instability culminated on July 19, when the government was defeated by a minority of four on the estimates for the Irish Land Commission. Although the government initially decided to hold on rather than resign or dissolve, the events of the session had foreshadowed the end of Balfour's tenure.
