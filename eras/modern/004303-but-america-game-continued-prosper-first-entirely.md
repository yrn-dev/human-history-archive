# American Sports and Games

The United States has developed a variety of popular sports and games, ranging from indoor gymnasium activities and table games to national outdoor pastimes. These activities vary in their origins, with some deriving from English traditions and others emerging from domestic innovation.

## Base-Ball
Base-ball is the national summer sport of the United States, and it is also popular in Japan and throughout Canada. The origins of the game are a subject of debate among authorities. Some suggest it is derived from rounders, an old English game with several variations played in America during the colonial period. Others argue the resemblance to rounders is coincidental and that the game originated in the United States, specifically in Cooperstown, New York, in 1839, where Abner Doubleday—later a general in the U.S. army—is said to have devised the scheme for play.

During the beginning of the 19th century, schoolboys in the North Atlantic states played a popular game called "One Old Cat," which involved three boys who took turns batting and fielding; a run was scored if the batsman ran to a single base and back without being put out. This game was modified into "Two Old Cat," "Three Old Cat," and "Four Old Cat," which featured four, six, and eight players respectively. A further development of this was known as town-ball, leading to the 1833 organization of the Olympic Town-Ball Club of Philadelphia.

Organized club matches first appeared near New York, including the founding of the Washington Baseball Club in 1843. In 1845, the Knickerbocker Baseball Club drew up the first regular code of rules, which were used in matches against the Empire, Gotham Eagle, and several Brooklyn clubs, including the Atlantic, Putnam, Eckford, and Excelsior. By 1858, the first National Association was organized. While its laws were similar to the modern code, the pitcher was required to deliver the ball with a full toss (no approach throw was allowed), and the ball itself was "livelier" and larger. The game's rapid spread led to the creation of famous clubs such as the Red Stockings of Cincinnati, the Forest City of Cleveland, the Maple Leaf of Guelph, and the Beacon and Lowell of Boston. Over time, sharp rivalry between top teams introduced semi-professionalism to the sport.

## Basket-Ball
Basket-ball was invented in 1891 by James Naismith, an instructor at the Young Men's Christian Association training-school gymnasium in Springfield, Massachusetts. The game was created to provide a gymnasium class with an activity that was not too rough for indoor play and could replace football and baseball during the winter months to break monotony. The concept was first published in the school paper, the *Triangle*. It became a highly popular indoor game in America for both men and girls before spreading to England and other regions. While adapted for the open air, it is typically played in the cold season on a gymnasium floor.

## Pool
Pool is played in America using a six-pocket table and fifteen numbered balls. There are various versions of the game, the most popular being Continuous Pool. This is an expanded version of Fifteen-Ball Pool where balls are arranged as in English pyramids. 

In Continuous Pool, the game is played for a series of points, typically 100. The balls are reset after each frame (a frame consists of fifteen balls), and the player who pockets the final ball decides whether they or their opponent will break. In Fifteen-Ball Pool, a single frame constitutes a full game. 

The rules of the game include the following:
* **Winning:** The winner is the player who pockets the majority of the fifteen balls. Each ball is worth one point.
* **Calling Balls:** Numbers are used to distinguish the balls; a player must "call" the specific ball they intend to pocket and the pocket they intend to drive it into.
* **The Break:** The player who breaks must send at least two balls to the cushion or forfeit three points. To avoid giving the second player too great an advantage, the usual method is to strike a corner ball hard enough to hit the cushion but not hard enough to break up the balls.
* **Accidental Pockets:** If a ball is pocketed by chance during the same play in which a called ball was legitimately put down, it is counted. All other accidentally pocketed balls are replaced on the table.
