# John Myles and the Growth of the Baptist Church in Massachusetts

## Arrival and Settlement in Massachusetts
John Myles (1621–1683) was a Welsh Baptist who had previously served as one of Cromwell's Tryers. In 1663, seeking refuge from the intolerance of the government of Charles II, Myles and his congregation emigrated to Massachusetts. The group was permitted to settle in Rehoboth. Even after the authorities discovered that the settlers were Baptists, they were allowed to remain in the colony, provided that they established their meeting-place at a considerable distance from that of the standing order.

## Influence and Denominational Growth
Once established, Myles did much to promote the growth of the Baptist Church within Massachusetts. His efforts and service to the denomination extended to Boston and other areas. During this period, other individuals and groups were also adopting Baptist views. For example, as early as 1654, Thomas Gould of Charlestown appeared to share antipaedobaptist views and was in close touch with President Dunster. 

By May 1665, several English Baptists who had settled near Boston, along with others who had adopted Baptist beliefs, joined with those already baptized in England to form a church covenant; this group included Thomas Gould.

## Persecution and Legal Struggles
Despite the growth of the movement, the Baptist church in Boston faced severe persecution. Members were frequently fined and imprisoned, and they were denied the use of a meeting-house they had constructed. 

The struggle for recognition continued even after the Act of Toleration (1689) took effect in England. The Boston Baptists pleaded for the privileges granted by the Act, but their requests were initially in vain. It took the earnest efforts of other dissenters and English Baptists to secure a recognition of their right to exist. Even a mandate issued by Charles II in July 1679—which sharply rebuked Massachusetts authorities for denying others the liberties that the authorities themselves had sought during their own exile—produced little effect in easing the persecution.
