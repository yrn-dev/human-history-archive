# The Ledger in Book-keeping Systems

The ledger serves as the center of all book-keeping systems. While other books are maintained for practical convenience and are frequently referred to as "subsidiary books," the ledger is the primary record where transactions are classified after being first recorded in books of entry (also known as books of first entry).

## Evolution and Fundamental Principles
In the early stages of book-keeping, the ledger was not a complete record of all transactions, but rather contained only those accounts deemed necessary to keep accessible, such as personal accounts involving other business houses. Initially, transactions that reduced indebtedness were recorded by date beneath those that created the debt, with the reduction subtracted from the total. This was later replaced by a more convenient system of keeping two separate accounts: one for the creation of indebtedness and a "contra account" for the reduction or extinguishing of that debt. These were placed on opposite sides of the ledger, leading to the development of the Dr. and Cr. (debit and credit) accounts used today.

The essential principle of the double-entry system is the creation of a complete record of every business transaction. Because transactions are cross-dealings—where one party receives a benefit and another imparts one—an entry of equal amount must be made on both the debit and credit sides of the ledger. Consequently, the aggregate amount on the debit side must always equal the aggregate amount on the credit side. A list of all ledger balances used to verify this mechanical agreement is called a trial balance. However, a trial balance does not prove absolute accuracy, as it cannot detect omitted transactions, records entered into the wrong accounts, incorrect monetary values, or fictitious entries.

## Ledger Specialization and Subdivision
To allow a large staff to work simultaneously, the ledger is generally divided into sections:

*   **Cash Book:** This separate book binds the cash and bank accounts together, using parallel columns to show movements of office cash and bank cash. A third column for discounts can be added to remove the need for a separate discount journal. For businesses that pay by cheque and deposit all receipts into a bank, a separate "petty cash book" is used for petty payments.
*   **Personal Accounts:** These are often separated from nominal and real accounts. They are further subdivided into "sales ledgers" (or "sold ledgers") for customers and "purchases ledgers" (or "bought ledgers") for trade creditors.
*   **General Ledger:** This contains nominal and real accounts. It may be further split into a nominal ledger and a private ledger. The private ledger—often managed by the principal or a confidential employee—typically contains the trading and profit and loss accounts, even though these are strictly nominal. To make these various ledgers self-balancing and to localize clerical errors, "adjustment accounts" are employed to complete the double-entry record in each ledger.

## Modern Ledger Applications
Recent modifications in book-keeping aim to increase speed, reduce clerical work, and increase certainty.

### Tabular Book-keeping
Tabular book-keeping substitutes the usual form of books with those ruled with numerous columns. When applied to ledgers, this system allows a very large number of personal accounts to be recorded on a single opening of a ledger, provided that the number of entries for each individual is small.

### The Slip System
The slip system is a modern method that mirrors ancient Babylonian records kept on movable tablets. It can be applied to ledgers in several ways:
*   **Direct Record:** In some cases, original slips (duplicates created via carbon sheets) are filed with guide-cards and constitute the ledger record themselves. This means the record is scattered over many sheets rather than a single page. This is only practical when transactions per individual are few and the volume of personal accounts is so large that avoiding duplicated clerical work is essential.
*   **Card and Loose-leaf Ledgers:** The more common application involves card ledgers, where each account is on an independent sheet of cardboard stored in cabinets or drawers. This allows "dead matter" to be continuously eliminated from the active record. Loose-leaf ledgers are another variation of this system.

## Legislative Requirements
The legality and implementation of ledger systems vary by country:
*   **France and Belgium:** These nations require a journal, which can make the use of numerous books of first entry difficult due to duplication. France also requires an inventory and a letter book.
*   **Germany:** Statutory requirements include the use of bound books with consecutively numbered pages, which makes the legal status of loose-leaf or card ledgers doubtful.
*   **Spain and Portugal:** The Commercial Code of Spain requires a ledger, journal, inventory, invoice book, and letter book. Portugal requires a ledger, journal, balance book, and copy-letter book.
*   **Holland:** Law requires the recording of commercial transactions and letters, as well as an annual balance sheet.
