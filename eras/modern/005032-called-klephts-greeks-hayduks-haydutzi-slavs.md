# Klephts and Haiduks

The Klephts and Haiduks (also referred to as *hayduks* or *haydutzi*) were irregular fighters and brigands operating in the Balkan peninsula under Turkish rule. While the term "Klepht" was used by the Greeks, the Slavs used the terms *hayduk* or *haydutzi*. These groups emerged as a response to the conditions of the Ottoman conquest, which had reduced the Christian races of the plains to a state of serfdom.

## Nature and Role
In the broader context of brigandage, a brigand is defined as an outlaw who conducts warfare like a partisan or irregular soldier, utilizing surprises and skirmishes. Such figures typically support their operations through plunder, the extortion of blackmail, and the capture of prisoners for ransom, often employing violence or killing those unable to pay.

However, in the Balkan peninsula, the Klephts and Haiduks viewed themselves as representatives of their people against oppressors. They are described as champions of freedom who maintained a struggle against alien tyranny, finding refuge in the mountains where the spirit of liberty continued to exist. Because the invader often reduces a nation to anarchy and subsequently suffers from the resulting disorder, opponents are frequently labeled as brigands.

## Relations with the Armatoli
To maintain order and repress the activities of the Klephts, the ruling authorities granted permission to a portion of the population to carry arms. These individuals were known as "armatoli." Despite their official purpose, the armatoli were often allies of the Klephts rather than their enemies.

## Cultural Legacy and Poetry
The adventurous and romantic life of the Klephts and Haiduks, characterized by solitude in nature and frequent tragedy, inspired a spontaneous and indigenous form of poetry known as Klephtic ballads. These anonymous works, composed in the language of the people, are considered the most genuine Greek poetry of the epoch.

The ballads frequently personify nature, giving voice to rocks, rivers, trees, and mountains to sing of the Klepht's prowess or mourn his death. For example, the mountain Olympia is depicted as a place where the "footstep of the Turk" has never desecrated its valleys and where a Klepht resides beneath every tree. 

The poetry often features romanticized or primitive luxury; for instance, Romaic ballads describe the horses of the Klephts as being gold shod. The ballads also capture the tragedy of the warrior's end, such as the dying Klepht who requests a lofty tomb with a window so that nightingales and swallows may signal the arrival of spring and May. Another account describes the wounded Vervos, who asks his silver-shod bay horse to dig his tomb and deliver his arms to his comrades and a handkerchief to his beloved.
