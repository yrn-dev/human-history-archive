# Special Fracture in Young People

## Bone Structure and Diastasis
The long bones of growing persons and children differ from fully developed bones in their composition, consisting of a shaft with cartilaginous ends where bone is developed. Due to this specific anatomy, injury can cause the end of the bone to become detached. This specific variety of fracture is known as diastasis. Regardless of the quality of treatment received, a diastasis fracture may result in the stiffness of the neighboring joint or an arrest of the bone's growth.

## Types of Fractures in Youth
Fractures are generally categorized by the nature of the violence that caused them. A fracture from direct violence occurs where the bone is struck and is generally transverse. A fracture from indirect violence occurs as a consequence of a strain applied to the bone and is more or less oblique. Additionally, fractures can be caused by muscular action, such as a violent cough causing a rib to break.

While fully developed bones typically break fairly across, the soft bones found in young people may simply be bent; this is referred to as a willow fracture or a green stick fracture.

## Classification and Complications
Fractures are classified as either simple or compound. A simple fracture is analogous to a subcutaneous laceration in the soft parts. A compound fracture is analogous to an open wound in the soft parts, creating a communication between the injured bone and the external air. This open wound may be caused by the force that fractured the bone (such as a cart-wheel passing over a limb) or by a sharp point of the fractured bone piercing through the skin.

Compound fractures carry significantly higher risks because germs of suppuration can reach the seat of the fracture. This can lead to:
*   Septic inflammation and suppuration.
*   Osteomyelitis.
*   Blood-poisoning.
*   Necrosis (the death of large pieces of the bone).
*   Delayed union.

## Consolidation and Union
The process of bone healing involves consolidation. When consolidation occurs very slowly, or not at all, it is termed delayed union. This may be caused by constitutional or local factors. If the bones remain in a good position, treatment for delayed union requires patience, general health-measures, and massage.

An ununited fracture occurs when nature makes no attempt to consolidate the parts after several months or weeks. This condition can be caused by several factors:
*   The broken ends were not brought close enough together.
*   The seat of the fracture was constantly disturbed.
*   A tendon or muscle was interposed between the broken ends.
*   The patient possesses a constitutional defect.

In cases of ununited fractures—except those caused by constitutional defects—treatment involves cutting down to the broken ends, sawing off a thin slice to freshen them, and fixing them in place using a screw or wire. ## General Treatment and Recovery
To achieve firm bony union, the fragments must have accurate apposition. The act of putting the broken ends together is called "setting the fracture," and splints are used to provide the necessary rest. It is generally advisable to fix the joint above or below the fracture with the splint.

Modern improvements in treatment suggest that simple fractures should not be kept in splints for extended periods. Instead, they are frequently "taken down" to allow for gentle movements and massage, which helps swelling and pain disappear and ensures a more complete recovery. Surgeons may use X-rays to verify that fractured surfaces are in close apposition. If ordinary methods cannot achieve this, the surgeon may cut down to the broken ends and fix them with a permanent strong wire suture. In compound fractures where fragments cannot be easily adjusted, immediate wiring is considered a necessary part of primary treatment.

## Related Bone Conditions in Young People
Young people are also susceptible to acute ostitis, also known as septic osteomyelitis. This is a serious disease caused by the cultivation of virulent germs—specifically *Staphylococcus pyogenes aureus*—in the marrow and delicate growing tissue of the bone. These germs, which collect in grape-like clusters and have a yellow tinge, usually enter the bone via the blood-stream from the gullet, the membrane lining the mouth, or other parts of the alimentary canal. While these germs may circulate harmlessly in a healthy individual, an injured bone provides an attractive focus for the germs to settle.
