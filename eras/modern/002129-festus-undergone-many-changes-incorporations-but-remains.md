# Festus

The term "Festus" appears in the archive's records in three distinct contexts: as a voluminous poetic work, as a Roman procurator in Judea, and as a reference in classical scholarship.

## The Poem *Festus*
*Festus* is a voluminous poem written by the English poet Philip James Bailey (1816–1902). Born in Nottingham, Bailey was educated locally before matriculating at Glasgow University at age sixteen. Although he entered Lincoln's Inn in 1835, he did not make a serious practice of law. Instead, he settled at Basford, where he spent three years composing *Festus*, which was published anonymously in 1839.

The work achieved immediate success in both America and England, seeing nearly thirty editions in the United States and a dozen in England. By 1889, Bailey published a "Jubilee Edition," noting that the poem remained known to both younger and older generations. While Bailey published other verses, he is regarded essentially as a man of one book.

Thematically, *Festus* is described as a vast pageant of philosophy and theology. Divided into approximately twelve sections, the poem attempts to represent the relationship between man and God, preach the immortality of the soul, emphasize the benignity of Providence, and postulate a "gospel of faith and reason combined." Despite its dignified thought and fine lines, the work is noted for a certain incoherency and an ambitious theme that may make it difficult to read for anyone other than a sympathetic student. The poem is cited as a singular example of a work virtually completed in the author's youth and never reinforced or supplanted by his later achievements.

## Festus as Procurator of Judea
In the historical records of Judea, Festus served as a Roman procurator, succeeding Felix. His administration was characterized by a focus on correcting disturbances within the country. He was active in capturing and destroying a large number of robbers.

Upon his arrival in Judea, the region was afflicted by robbers who plundered and set fire to villages. This period saw the rise of the sicarii, robbers named after the *sicae* (sickles) or small, crooked swords they used—weapons similar in length to Persian *acinacae*. The sicarii would mingle with crowds during festivals when people gathered from various regions to worship God, allowing them to easily slay their targets. They also targeted the villages of their enemies for plunder and arson.

In response to these threats, Festus deployed forces of footmen and horsemen to attack a group that had been led by an impostor. This individual had promised his followers freedom from their miseries and deliverance if they followed him into the wilderness. The forces sent by Festus destroyed both the impostor and his followers.

Festus was eventually succeeded by Albinus, who is described as having been a wicked and tyrannical leader. Unlike Festus, Albinus plundered the substance of the people, burdened the nation with taxes, and allowed prisoners to be redeemed for money.

## Chronology and Scholarship
There is scholarly debate regarding the exact date of Festus's arrival as procurator. Some arguments suggest the earliest possible date for his arrival was A.D. 58, based on the timing of missionary journeys and a two-year imprisonment at Caesarea. However, an alternative calculation suggests his arrival could be placed in A.D. 50 if the missionary journeys began in A.D. 48 and were spread over an additional year.

Other evidence, such as Eusebius's *Chronicle*, places Festus's arrival in "Nero 2" (October 55-56). However, this is contested on the grounds that Nero 2 actually corresponds to September 56–September 57, and it is questioned whether Eusebius had any authority for this date other than Josephus, who provided no precise year.

Additionally, the name Festus appears in classical citations as a source regarding *bilingues Brutates*.
