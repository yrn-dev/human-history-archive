# Methods of Producing Active Immunity and Organism Cultivation

## Principles of Active Immunity
The artificial establishment of active immunity is based on the established fact that recovery from certain infective diseases provides protection against subsequent attacks for varying lengths of time. This principle leads to the practice of producing a modified attack of a disease as a preventative measure, a concept previously utilized in smallpox inoculation. 

Immunity likely results from specific substances introduced into the system during a disease rather than from the disease itself. Consequently, if doses of the poison (defined in the widest sense) are properly adjusted, immunity can be achieved without the occurrence of any disease symptoms.

## Methods of Attenuation
One primary method for producing active immunity is inoculation with an "attenuated virus," which consists of bacteria whose virulence has been diminished. Early methods of attenuation were developed using the anthrax bacillus, though this organism is considered somewhat exceptional due to the relative stability of its virulence.

Generally, these methods involve growing the organism outside the body under unsuitable conditions. Examples of such conditions include:
*   Exposure to temperatures higher than the optimum.
*   Growth in the presence of weak antiseptics.
*   Growth on ordinary artificial media, where virulence often diminishes. This process can be accelerated by passing a current of air over the surface of the growth.

Additionally, the virulence of a bacterium for a specific animal may be reduced by passing it through the body of an animal of a different species. By using these methods, cultures of varying virulence can be obtained. Immunity is gradually increased by inoculating with vaccines of increasing virulence, potentially reaching a very high degree by ultimately using "supervirulent" cultures. This supervirulent character is typically achieved through the method of passage.

## Other Immunity Methods and Specificity
A second method for producing immunity is the injection of the bacterium in a dead condition, which can produce immunity against the living organism. To ensure immunity reaches a high degree, injections must ultimately use either a large dose of toxin or the bacterium in a very virulent state. In these instances, the immunity is generally specific, meaning it applies only to the toxin or bacterium used to produce it.

Conversely, non-specific immunity or increased tissue resistance can be produced locally (such as in the peritoneum) through injections of nucleic acid, peptone, non-pathogenic organisms, and other substances. This type of immunity lacks specific character and cannot be transferred to another animal. Furthermore, some organisms exhibit antagonistic actions; for example, the products of *B. pyocyaneus* provide a certain protective action against *B. anthracis*, though this has not led to important practical applications.

## Cultivation of Bacteria
The methods of cultivation vary depending on the organism's relationship with oxygen. Organisms that grow in the presence of oxygen follow standard modes of cultivation. However, strictly anaerobic bacteria grow only in the absence of oxygen, requiring specific methods to exclude this gas:
*   **Deep Cultures:** Inoculation is made deep within a solid medium, particularly if the medium contains a reducing agent like glucose.
*   **Surface and Fluid Cultures:** When using the plate method for surface growth or for cultures in fluids, air is displaced by an indifferent gas, typically hydrogen.
