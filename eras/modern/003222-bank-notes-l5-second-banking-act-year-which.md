# Bank Notes and Banking Legislation in England

The history of bank notes and the regulation of banking institutions in England is characterized by a long period of restriction designed to protect the monopoly of the Bank of England, followed by gradual legislative shifts in the 19th century.

## Origins and Early Development of Bank Notes
While the use of notes was current in China around A.D. 800, the bank note was adapted for use in Europe by a Swede named Palmstruck. The Bank of Sweden (the *Riksbank*), established in 1656 as the earliest state bank of Sweden, issued the first bank note in 1658. A French government *enquete* in 1729 recognized this as an admirable Swedish invention intended to facilitate commerce.

In England, the ability to issue notes was historically fundamental to the banking system. According to Bagehot in *Lombard Street*, issuing notes served as the introduction to deposit-banking; consequently, banks located outside of London could scarcely exist without the power to issue notes.

## Early Restrictions on Joint-Stock Banking
The Bank of England was incorporated by the act of 1694. For over a century, subsequent legislation strictly limited the competition. The act of 1697 declared that no joint-stock bank was to be established within England while the Bank of England continued. This was further reinforced by the act of 1708, which prohibited any company or partnership exceeding six persons in England from borrowing or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand, or at any time less than six months from the borrowing. These restrictions were confirmed by the act of 1800.

## Legislative Changes of 1826 and 1833
Significant changes to these restrictions began in 1826. An act passed that year prohibited the issuance of "bank notes under L5." Additionally, a second Banking Act of 1826 allowed for the establishment of co-partnerships consisting of more than six persons—which were necessarily joint-stock companies—provided they were located beyond 65 miles from London.

Further liberalization occurred with the act of 1833, which permitted the establishment of joint-stock banks within the 65-mile limit and removed various restrictions regarding the amounts of notes for less than L50. However, joint-stock banks within the 65-mile radius were still not permitted the power to issue notes.

## The Bank Act of 1844 and the Note-Issue Monopoly
The Bank Act of 1844 sought to concentrate note issues within the Bank of England to secure its monopoly as the sole issuer in England and Wales. Under this act, the Bank of England became the only source for legal tender notes. 

The act established specific geographic zones for the privilege of issuing notes:
* **Within a 3-mile radius of the city of London:** The Bank of England held an absolute monopoly.
* **Between 3 and 65 miles from the city:** A concurrent right existed for banks established before 1844 that were issuing notes at that date and consisted of six or fewer persons.
* **Beyond the 65-mile radius:** The privilege was available to all banks established before 1844 that issued notes at that time, provided they had not lost the right through abandonment of business, bankruptcy, or temporary suspension of issue.

By 1906, nearly all provincial banks in England had lost their right of issue. The act fixed the maximum circulation for English issuing banks based on the average circulation of the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. As of October 1844, this included 207 private banks (with an authorized issue of L5,153,417) and 72 joint-stock banks (with an authorized issue of L3,478,230).

## Management of the Note Issue and Specie
The Bank Act of 1844 divided the Bank of England's accounts into two departments: the "issue department" and the "banking department." The notes held in the banking department are technically referred to as the "Reserve." If the demand for notes or specie exceeds the amount in the Reserve, the government must grant permission to "suspend the Bank Act" to meet the demand, regardless of the specie available in the issue department. Between 1844 and 1906, this authority was granted three times: during the crises of 1847, 1857, and 1866.

This division of specie into two parts is noted to have increased the liability to constant fluctuations in the Bank's rate of discount. From the start of the act in 1844 until the end of 1906, there were more than 400 changes in the rate.
