# House of the Reichsrath

The House (also referred to as the Lower House of the Reichsrath) served as a central legislative body in the Austrian parliamentary system. It was characterized by a complex representation of various ethnic groups and political factions, functioning as a site of significant tension between centralizing government policies and the demands of different nationalities.

## Composition and Racial Representation
The House was designed to represent the various "kingdoms and lands represented in the Reichsrath." At one stage of its history, it was noted that all races were represented within the House except for the Czechs. The distribution of constituencies was based on the tax-paying capacity of the various races. In districts where multiple ethnicities resided, separate registers and constituencies were established; this ensured that electors of a specific race could only vote for a candidate of their own race. Consequently, while internal political struggles occurred—such as Czech Radicals opposing Young Czechs or Clerical Germans opposing Liberal Germans—it was impossible for one race to achieve a victory over another (for example, Czechs over Germans or Poles over Ruthenes).

The racial and political breakdown of the House included:
*   **Czechs:** 177 members, including Young Czechs (18), Czech Agrarians (28), Czech Clericals (17), Old Czechs (7), and Czech National Socialists (9).
*   **Germans:** Divided among German Conservatives (including Christian Socialists with 96 and German Agrarians with 19) and German Liberals (including Populists with 29 and Progressives with 15).
*   **Poles:** Including Democrats (26), Populists (18), Conservatives (15), and the Centre (12).
*   **Ruthenes:** 72 members, including National Democrats (25).
*   **Slovenes:** 30 members, primarily Clericals (17).
*   **Southern Slav Club:** Including Croats and Serbs (20) and Slovene Liberals (37).
*   **Italians:** 15 members, including Clerical Populists (11).
*   **Rumanians:** 5 members.
*   **Jews:** 5 members, including Zionists (4).
*   **Social Democrats:** 82 members of all races.

## Legislative Functions and the Ausgleich
The House was the venue for the ratification of the Ausgleich, which was passed almost unanimously. Following this, fundamental laws were established to create a parliamentary government. These laws introduced the responsibility of ministers, complete control over the budget, and guarantees for personal liberties and rights.

The House also played a role in selecting the Delegation. Unlike in Hungary, where the Delegation was elected by each House as a whole, the Austrian Delegation was chosen by representatives of separate territories. For instance, Galicians chose 7 delegates, Bohemians chose 10, and Trieste chose 1. This system meant the Delegation was not representative of the House's majority but instead included various groups that opposed the government.

## Political Dynamics and the "Iron Ring"
The balance of power in the House shifted frequently based on the alliances of ethnic and political blocs. The Poles held significant influence; their threat to leave the House, potentially followed by the Tirolese, Dalmatians, and Slovenes, prevented German Liberals from introducing direct elections to the Reichsrath.

Under the leadership of Taaffe, a period of "unpolitical politics" emerged. Taaffe formed a coalition known as the "iron ring," consisting of the Right, Poles, and Czechs. This combination allowed him to govern against the constitutionalists (Liberals and Radicals). During this era, Smolka served as the president of the Lower House. Taaffe's strategy often involved playing different parties against one another to reduce the overall influence of parliament and clear a path for government action.

## Conflict and Obstruction
The House frequently experienced instability due to ethnic tensions, particularly regarding the Czechs. While the government attempted various reconciliations, the Czechs sought the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom. In 1890, Taaffe attempted to reach an agreement between the German party, the Feudal party, and the Czechs regarding electoral law and the separation of districts. However, this failed because the Young Czechs were excluded from the conference and subsequently used obstruction to block the measures.

At other times, the House faced total collapse. In March of one year, after Galician demands were rejected, the Polish club, Slovenes, and Tirolese all left the House. This left only 110 members—Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia—making the parliament impossible to govern.

## Electoral Reforms and the Curia System
The method of electing members to the House was a point of intense contention. In 1873, a new electoral law transferred the right of election from the diets to the direct vote of the people. This was a blow to the Federalists, as it prevented territories from formally refusing to appoint deputies. The law passed 120 to 2, despite the withdrawal of the Poles and a petition of 250,000 names from Bohemia.

Later, opposition to the extension of popular rights was led by the German nominees of the *curia* of large landed proprietors. These Constitutional Landed Proprietors fought to maintain their privileges against the rise of the Christian Socialists (the Lueger party), though their opposition was ultimately unsuccessful due to the disapproval of the emperor.
