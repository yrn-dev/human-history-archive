# Wallace's Line and the Biological Boundaries of Asia and Australia

The geographical and biological transition between the Asian and Australian continents is defined by a specific physical separation known as Wallace's line. This boundary was first indicated by the naturalist A.R. Wallace and is marked by the channel between Celebes and Borneo, known as the Straits of Macassar. This line follows approximately 120 deg. E.

## Biological Distribution
The significance of Wallace's line lies in the distinct difference in flora and fauna on either side of the boundary. To the west of the line, the flora and fauna are essentially Asiatic in their type. To the south and east, the Australian element becomes distinctly marked and eventually predominant. 

The Tropical Pacific region is defined as including all islands east of Wallace's line, as well as New Guinea, the islands of the tropical Pacific up to the Sandwich group, and Australia, with the exception of its south-eastern portion.

## Regional Fauna and Flora
Beyond the specific boundary of Wallace's line, other biological groupings exist across the Old World. The "palaeotropical" region combines the mostly tropical landmasses of the Old World, excluding the Australian region (Austrogaea). This palaeotropical region is divided into two subregions: the Indian and the Ethiopian. The Ethiopian subregion includes Madagascar and the whole of Africa, except for the Barbary States, but includes Arabia.

In terms of insects, those found in southern Asia—including China, Siam, the Malayan Islands, and India south of the Himalaya—belong to a single group. These Indo-Malayan types are met south of 30 deg. N., while Japan is categorized within the Europeo-Asiatic group.

## Human Populations and Racial Diversity
Human distribution in the Asiatic area is characterized by complexity rather than loose generalizations. While there are general tendencies, there have been "leakages," "admixtures," and "eddies" between main areas. For example, the Ainu in Japan are a wavy-haired, fairish, hairy-skinned race who more closely resemble Europeans in facial type than the surrounding yellow Japanese. 

Additionally, "Asiatic negroids" are found as a streak of negroid blood in parts of India and south Persia. Primitive black people also inhabit the Andaman Islands, despite being far from Africa and Australia. There is little to no proof that the negroes, the Australians, the Asiatic negroids, and the black people of the Andaman Islands share a single origin, though they have lived under similar conditions for vast periods.

## Australian Territories and Culture
The Australian territory of Papua (British New Guinea) includes the Louisiade Archipelago. This chain of over eighty islands extends south-eastward from the easternmost promontory of New Guinea. The islands are rich in tropical forest products, and gold has been discovered on Misima (St Aignan) and the chief island, Tagula (South-east). The natives of these islands are of Papuan type and practice cannibalism.

Among the Australian aborigines and other peoples is the use of the boomerang, a sickle-shaped missile weapon made of wood. There are two main types: the non-return (or war) boomerang and the return boomerang. In Australia, the return boomerang is always curved at an angle of 90 deg. or more. While similar instruments exist among the Dravidians of South India (made of ivory or steel) and the Hopis of Arizona, it remains uncertain if the Indian and Egyptian versions have a real resemblance to the Australian return boomerang.
