# Bohemia

## Geography and Topography
The interior of Bohemia is characterized as an undulating plateau, generally exceeding 1,000 feet in height, formed by a series of terraces that slope gradually from south to north. While some have compared the interior to a deep basin, its lowest points are located in the north within the valley of the Elbe. The region is divided into highland (south of a line passing through Hohenmauth-Prague-Komotau) and lowland (north of that same line).

The interior features several mountain ranges. In the middle lies the Brdywald (2,798 ft.). The north-west part contains the Tepler Gebirge (2,657 ft.), the Karsbader Gebirge (3,057 ft.), and the Kaiserwald (3,238 ft.). The northern corner is occupied by the Mittelgebirge (2,739 ft.), which is a volcanic massif extending on both sides of the Elbe.

## Mineral Resources and Industry
Bohemia possesses nearly every useful metal and mineral, with the sole exception of salt, which is entirely absent. Lignite and coal are the most important minerals in terms of value and quantity. Rich lignite fields are located in the north-east corner near Teplitz, Ossegg, Falkenau, Dux, and Brux. Coal mining occurs around Pilsen, Kladno, Buschtehrad, Schlan, Rakonitz, Nurschan, and Radnitz; the latter contains the oldest mines in Bohemia, dating to the 17th century.

The iron industry is a major center for the region, with foundries located around Konigshof and Kladno, and iron ores found at Nucic and Krusnahora. Other extracted minerals include:
* **Silver:** Extracted at Joachimsthal and Pribram (the medieval mines at Kuttenberg are now abandoned).
* **Lead:** Extracted at Pribram.
* **Tin:** Found at Graupen in the Erzgebirge, the only location in Austria where it is found.
* **Other minerals:** Antimony (Milleschau near Tabor), uranium and radium (near Joachimsthal), graphite (Budweis and Krumau), and porcelain-earth (near Carlsbad). 

Additionally, the region produces copper, cobalt, nickel, sulphur, alum, arsenic, building stone, and precious stones such as the Bohemian garnet (pyrope). Large quantities of asphalt and peat—particularly in the south-west—are also collected.

## Population and Demographics
As of 1900, Bohemia had a population of 6,318,280, with a density of 315 inhabitants per square mile. This made it the second most populous Austrian province after Galicia and third in density behind Lower Austria and Silesia. In 1800, the population was slightly over 3,000,000.

The population is divided by nationality: approximately 65% are Czechs and 35% are Germans. Czechs primarily occupy the south, south-east, and middle regions. Germans are concentrated in the large towns and near the borders, particularly in the west and north. There are German-speaking enclaves in Czech districts, while Czechs have tended to move into German manufacturing and mining districts. Despite industrial development, there is a steady flow of emigrants to Russia, Germany, other monarchy provinces, and America.

Religiously, 96% of the population belongs to the Roman Catholic Church. The region is organized into the archbishopric of Prague and three bishoprics: Leitmeritz, Koniggratz, and Budweis.

## Administration and Urban Centers
Budweis (Czech *Bud[ve]jovice*) serves as the principal commercial center of South Bohemia. Located 80 miles south-south-west of Prague, it is a river port at the junction of the Moldau and Maltsch. As of 1900, its population was 39,630; by 1908, the population was 60% Czech. The town is a center for a German enclave and features a diverse industry including the manufacture of machinery, chemicals, paper, matches, bricks, tiles, boat-building, bell-founding, and black-lead pencils. It is also known for its beer, which is mostly exported to America. Notable landmarks include a Renaissance-style town hall (1730), a cathedral with a detached tower (1500), and the 19th-century Frauenberg castle.

## History and Political Conflict
Bohemia was ruled by King Charles (died 1378), who founded the university of Prague and established the *nove mesto* (new town), which tripled the city's size and population. Charles attempted to codify the laws of Bohemia, though the nobility resisted.

In later centuries, Bohemia was a primary theater of the Thirty Years' War, beginning in its capital. The region was pillaged by Swedish troops, and the 1648 capture of part of Prague by General Konigsmark marked the war's end. In 1720, the estates of Bohemia recognized the Pragmatic Sanction, allowing female succession to the throne. This led to a conflict upon the death of Emperor Charles VI, when Charles, elector of Bavaria, invaded and occupied Prague in 1741. Maria Theresa eventually recovered the territory. Under Maria Theresa and Joseph II, Bohemian autonomy was limited; for example, a 1749 decree abolished separate Bohemian law-courts.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, ethnic tensions rose between Czechs and Germans. Government ordinances in 1880 and 1886 began allowing the Czech language in government offices and high courts in Prague and Bruenn. The introduction of Czech classes at the university of Prague in 1882 was viewed by Germans as a "desecration" of the institution. While educated Czechs were generally bilingual, Germans increasingly refused to learn Czech after 1867, leading to professional friction regarding government appointments.
