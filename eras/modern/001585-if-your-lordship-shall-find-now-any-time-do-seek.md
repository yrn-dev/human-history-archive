# Professional and Personal Conduct of Francis Bacon

## Career Ambitions and Public Service
Francis Bacon expressed a specific philosophy regarding his pursuit of professional advancement and public office. In correspondence, he asserted that if his lordship found him seeking or affecting any position for which someone closer to his lordship was more convenient, he should be considered a "most dishonest man." 

Bacon indicated that if he were not carried on in his career, he would pursue alternative means of sustenance. His proposed alternatives included selling his inheritance to purchase a lease of quick revenue or acquiring an office of gain that could be executed by a deputy. By doing so, he intended to relinquish the care of service and instead become a "true pioneer" in the "mine of truth" or a "sorry bookmaker."

## Legal Challenges and Defense
In a letter to the king dated March 25, 1621, Bacon addressed a "tempest" that had fallen upon him. He defended his character and professional conduct, stating that he had never been the author of "immoderate counsel" and had always desired for things to be carried out via *suavibus modis*. He denied being a "haughty," "intolerable," or "hateful man" in his carriage or conversation, and further denied being an "avaricious oppressor of the people."

Regarding accusations of briberies and gifts, Bacon expressed hope that when the "book of hearts" was opened, he would not be found to have a "corrupt heart" or a "depraved habit" of taking rewards to pervert justice, though he admitted to being frail and partaking in the "abuse of the times." Eventually, after understanding the particulars of the charges—not formally from the House, but enough to inform his memory and conscience—Bacon confessed that there was sufficient matter to move him to desert his defense and to move his lordships to censure and condemn him.

## Later Appointments and Associations
Bacon's professional timeline includes his appointment as the treasurer of Gray's Inn in October 1608, an event whose tercentenary was celebrated in 1908. Archive records also link him to correspondence with the queen, Essex, and Puckering. The history of Essex is documented in Spedding's *Letters and Life* and is also recounted by J. Bruce in the introduction to the *Correspondence of James VI. with Sir Robert Cecil*.
