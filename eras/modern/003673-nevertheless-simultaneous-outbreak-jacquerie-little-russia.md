# Jacquerie in Little-Russia and the Bar Confederation

The outbreak of a *jacquerie* in Little-Russia occurred simultaneously with the formation of the Bar Confederation, a movement that significantly impacted the political and military landscape of the eastern provinces of Poland and Lithuania in the late 18th century.

## The Bar Confederation
The Bar Confederation was established in 1768 at the little fortress of Bar, located in Podolia. It was formed by a group of Polish nobles and gentry, specifically Adam Krasinski (the bishop of Kamenets), Osip Pulawski, and Michael Krasinski. The primary objective of the confederation was to protect the external and internal independence of Poland against the aggressions of the Russian government, which was represented in Warsaw by Prince Nicholas Repnin.

Initially, King Stanislaus attempted to act as a mediator between the Russian government and the confederates. However, after determining that mediation was impossible, the king dispatched a force to oppose the confederates. This force, led by two generals and the grand hetman Ksawery Branicki, successfully captured Bar.

## Expansion and the Little-Russian Jacquerie
Despite the fall of Bar, the confederation expanded throughout Lithuania and the eastern provinces of Poland. This extension was contributed to by a simultaneous outbreak of a *jacquerie* in Little-Russia. 

Following this expansion, the confederates sought international assistance, which helped trigger a war between Turkey and Russia. The situation became so critical that Frederick II advised Catherine to reach terms with the confederates. Under the leadership of Prince Charles Radziwill, Michael Pac, and Ignaty Malchewsky, the confederate bands ravaged the land and won several engagements against Russian forces. Eventually, the confederates began ignoring King Stanislaus entirely, sending their own envoys to the principal powers of Europe.

## Diplomatic Efforts and Decline
In 1770, the Council of the Confederation moved its seat from Silesia to Hungary. From this new location, the council engaged in diplomatic negotiations with Turkey, Austria, and France to establish a league against Russia. 

The court of Versailles appointed Dumouriez as the commander-in-chief of the confederates. While Dumouriez reorganized the army, allowing it to maintain a gallant but hopeless struggle for several years, he is described as an adroit adventurer who did not distinguish himself as a politician or soldier. One of his significant blunders occurred when he labeled King Stanislaus a tyrant and a traitor at the exact moment the king was preparing to join the Confederation. Consequently, the king returned to the Russian faction, and the Confederation lost the confidence of the European powers.

## Context of the Jacquerie
The term *jacquerie* refers to violent peasant uprisings. Historical precedents for such outbreaks include the French "Jacquerie" of 1358, where peasants burnt châteaux and devastated the countryside. Similar patterns of peasant unrest were seen in the English insurgents under Wat Tyler in 1381, the German Peasant Wars of the late fifteenth century, and the Anabaptist insurrection of 1525.

In later centuries, similar peasant-driven violence occurred in other regions. For example, in 1846, a "hideous *jacquerie*" took place in Galicia for three or four days following a rising in support of the Austrian government against Polish nobles. In these instances, as in the broader history of the Russian masses, a simple loyalty to the monarchy or social order could be transformed into a "pitiless intolerance of social injustice" under sufficient provocation.
