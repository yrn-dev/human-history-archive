# The Fejervary Programme

The Fejervary programme was a political initiative introduced in Hungary during the early 20th century, centered on the introduction of universal suffrage. The programme emerged from a period of intense conflict between the crown and the Hungarian government, as well as tensions between the two halves of the Dual Monarchy.

## Origins and Political Context
Between January 1905 and April 1906, a deadlock existed between the crown and Hungary. The Coalition, which held the majority in the Chamber, refused to take office unless the crown granted specific demands, including customs separation from Austria and the "Magyar words of command." The crown declined these requests, as granting either would have wrecked the dual system as it had been interpreted since 1867.

Because the Tisza cabinet could not be relieved of its functions until June 1905, it was succeeded by a non-parliamentary administration. This administration was led by General Baron Fejervary, the former minister for national defence, who served as premier.

## The Universal Suffrage Scheme
Since the Coalition refused to take office on terms acceptable to the crown, Baron Fejervary sought the crown's consent for a scheme drafted by the minister of the interior, Mr. Joseph Kristoffy. The core of this scheme was to subject the dispute between the crown and the Coalition to the test of universal suffrage by radically reforming the franchise in Hungary.

The proposal was sanctioned by the crown in September 1905. This move was a tactical maneuver by the monarchy; the king-emperor could hold the threat of an appeal to the disfranchised majority *in terrorem* over the Magyars. The ascendancy of the Magyars in Hungary was based on a narrow and artificial franchise, and the Magyars were in a minority within their own country.

## Reaction of the Coalition
The Fejervary programme alarmed the Coalition. The Magyar nobility and gentry, who concentrated political power in their hands, feared that universal suffrage would destroy their hegemony. Furthermore, they believed that granting political equality to non-Magyars would undermine the supremacy of the Magyar race itself.

The Coalition did not yield immediately. However, they eventually came to terms with the crown and formed an administration after the Chamber was dissolved by military force on February 19, 1906, and an open breach of the constitution became a possibility. While the depression of trade and the miserable state of public finances contributed to their decision, the primary motive for the Coalition was to escape the menace of universal suffrage or to ensure it was introduced in a manner that safeguarded Magyar supremacy over other Hungarian races.

## Impact on the Dual Monarchy
The introduction of the Universal Suffrage Bill by Joseph Kristoffy was the catalyst that brought Opposition leaders in the Hungarian parliament to terms, making the agreement of 1907 possible. However, this agreement was merely a truce between Habsburg imperialism, which sought to preserve the military and economic unity of the Dual Monarchy, and Magyar nationalism, which sought to maintain ascendancy in an independent Hungary.

The Fejervary programme also had implications for Austria. In August 1905, the crown considered the proposal for universal suffrage, and by September, it was sanctioned. The monarch's decision to support the programme in Hungary indicated that he had already decided that universal suffrage should be introduced in Austria as well, recognizing that he could not oppose in Cisleithania what he sought to further in Hungary. This contributed to a rapid movement toward universal suffrage within the Austrian half of the Monarchy.

## Subsequent Developments
Following the Fejervary administration, the Wekerle ministry took office on April 9, 1906. Although the Wekerle ministry was pledged to a measure of franchise reform, they showed no eagerness to fulfill this pledge. Nevertheless, the granting of universal suffrage in Austria made such a change inevitable in Hungary. Other critical issues remained unsettled, including the "Magyar word of command" and the renewal of the charter of the Austro-Hungarian State Bank, the latter of which became a point of acute conflict by 1909.
