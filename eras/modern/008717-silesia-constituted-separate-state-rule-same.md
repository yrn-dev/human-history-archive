# Silesia and the Constitutional Status of Bohemia

## Military Conflicts and Territorial Shifts
During the mid-18th century, Silesia was a primary objective of Frederick the Great, who fought specifically to secure the region. In 1742, during a campaign involving French, Saxon, and Prussian forces, Frederick's military movements were focused on maintaining a connection to Silesia. While Frederick conducted a foray that brought Prussian outposts before Vienna, Prince Charles of Lorraine attempted to cut off the Prussians from Silesia. During this period, Hungarian levies entered Upper Silesia via the Jablunka Pass. Frederick eventually fell back to Kuttenberg in Bohemia, which allowed him to remain in contact with both Broglie and Silesia, particularly after the surrender of Glatz. Frederick's success in depriving Maria Theresa of Austria of Silesia earned him her respect and later influenced his efforts to incite a joint attack with Catherine the Second of Russia against Poland.

## 19th Century Constitutional Disputes
In the late 19th century, the political status of Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia became a point of contention within the Habsburg empire. Bohemians sought the re-establishment of their ancient constitution in a modernized form. In September 1871, an imperial message addressed to the diet of Prague indicated that the sovereign recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia, citing the fidelity of its population and the glory the Bohemian crown had provided to his ancestors. The sovereign expressed a willingness to confirm this by taking the coronation oath.

However, these efforts to reconcile Bohemia with Austria failed due to various influences. By 1872, a government with a strong German tendency took office in Vienna, leading Bohemians to temporarily boycott parliamentary assemblies in both Prague and Vienna.

## The Government of Count Taaffe
In 1879, Count Eduard Taaffe became the Austrian prime minister and successfully persuaded Bohemian representatives to return to the deliberations of the parliament in Vienna. The representatives agreed to participate, but they did so under the specific condition that their participation was without prejudice to their own political view. This view held that Bohemia, together with Moravia and Silesia, constituted a separate state, although it remained under the rule of the same sovereign as Hungary and Austria.

In response to this concession, the government of Count Taaffe implemented several changes:
* The government removed some of the most gross anomalies in the Bohemian electoral system, which had previously been heavily biased toward the German minority.
* The government consented to the establishment of a Bohemian university at Prague, an action that significantly aided the intellectual development of the region.
