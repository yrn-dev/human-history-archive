# Education and Religion

The relationship between education and religion has historically functioned as a primary synthetic force in the development of human societies, serving to counteract base and individual impulses. While these two influences have been closely interwoven to enable the growth of larger human cooperations, the nineteenth century saw an intellectual and theological conflict that resulted in a "disentanglement" of religious teaching from formal education. This divorce is characterized as a temporary dislocation that has contributed to a "de-civilization" of minds, manifesting in brutal nationalism and selfish profit-seeking in industrial life.

## Education and Religion in Baden

In the grand-duchy of Baden, educational establishments are numerous and flourishing, with public education managed entirely by the government. The region hosts a celebrated technical college at Karlsruhe and two universities: a Roman Catholic university at Freiburg-im-Breisgau and a Protestant university at Heidelberg.

The history of education in Baden has been deeply entwined with religious and political struggle. Early efforts to improve education were championed by Charles Frederick, a wise and liberal ruler who reigned from 1738. Later, under Grand-duke Leopold (who succeeded Louis in 1830), Liberal reforms were introduced into education, criminal law, and the constitution.

However, the internal politics of Baden centered largely on religious questions. On June 28, 1859, a concordat was signed with the Holy See, which facilitated the establishment of religious orders and placed education under the oversight of the clergy. This sparked a constitutional struggle that ended in 1863 with a victory for Liberal principles; while priests retained a share in management, the communes became responsible for education.

Further Liberal changes occurred in 1867 under the premiership of Julius von Jolly, including the introduction of compulsory education. That same year, a law required all candidates for the priesthood to pass government examinations. This was resisted by the archbishop of Freiburg, and following the archbishop's death in April 1868, the see remained vacant. Tensions escalated after 1870 with the proclamation of the dogma of papal infallibility, leading to the "Kulturkampf" in Baden. During this period, the government encouraged the formation of Old Catholic communities. Reconciliation with Rome was not achieved until 1880, and the archbishopric of Freiburg was refilled in 1882.

The religious structure of Baden is overseen by the grand-duke, who is a Protestant. The Evangelical Church is governed by a synod and a nominated council consisting of a "prelate" and 7 nominated and 48 elected clerical and lay members. The Roman Catholic archbishop of Freiburg serves as the metropolitan of the Upper Rhine.

## Other Regional Systems

In other jurisdictions, the intersection of religion and education varies:

*   **Belgium:** While the constitution provides for absolute liberty of conscience and no state religion, the population is predominantly Roman Catholic. Monastic and conventual houses are numerous and are primarily engaged in eleemosynary and educational work.
*   **Tegucigalpa Region:** In this area, Roman Catholicism is the creed of a very large majority, though the constitution grants complete liberty to all religious communities. No church receives special privileges or public funds. Education is compulsory, secular, and free for children between seven and fifteen years of age. The state maintains a university and central institute at Tegucigalpa, a school of jurisprudence at Comayagua, and secondary colleges with special teacher schools in each department.

## Historical and Theoretical Perspectives

In the early Christian era, followers of the faith used the practice of superstitious public or private rites—often performed out of habit and education—as opportunities to declare their opposition to idolatry, thereby fortifying their attachment to their faith.

From a theoretical perspective, some visions of a future world state suggest a system sustained by universal education on a scale and quality beyond current experience. In such a vision, education would be a lifelong process where adults become self-educators and student teachers, and at least ten percent of the adult population would work within the educational organization. This system would be based on a simplified, universal world religion—not a specialized form like Buddhism, Islam, or Christianity—but a "pure and undefiled" religion centered on brotherhood, creative service, self-forgetfulness, the Kingdom of Heaven, and the Eightfold Way. The goal of such education would be to turn human motives away from the obsession of self toward the service of human unity, power, and knowledge.
