# RULE IV.--CUTTING AWAY WRECK

## Definition and General Average
Rule IV specifically addresses the financial and legal implications of "cutting away the wreck." According to the established rules of maritime conduct, any loss or damage that results from the act of cutting away a wreck, or the remains of spars or other items that had previously been carried away by sea-peril, shall not be made good as General Average (G.A.).

This rule exists within a broader framework of maritime regulations regarding sacrifice and damage. For comparison, other rules dictate that damage caused by sacrifices made for the common safety, or damage resulting from the extinguishing of a shipboard fire (including beaching or scuttling), is generally made good as G.A. However, Rule IV creates a specific exception for the removal of wreckage and spars already lost to sea-peril.

## Marking and Buoying of Wrecks
To ensure the safety of other vessels, specific protocols are followed for the buoying and marking of wrecks in open seas, estuaries, or harbor approaches. Wreck buoys are colored green and marked with the word "Wreck" in white letters. Ideally, these buoys are placed near the side of the wreck closest to the mid-channel.

When a wreck-marking vessel is employed, it typically features green top sides with "Wreck" painted in white. Its signaling requirements include:
*   **Daytime:** Three balls on a yard 20 feet above the sea. Two balls are placed vertically at one end, and one ball is placed at the other end (the single ball indicates the side nearest the wreck).
*   **Nighttime:** Three white fixed lights arranged in the same pattern as the daytime balls, though ordinary riding lights are not shown.
*   **Fog:** A gong or bell is rung in quick succession at intervals not exceeding one minute, where practicable.

In rivers, harbors, or narrow waters under local jurisdiction, these rules may be varied. A marking vessel may instead carry a cross-yard on a mast with two balls (day) or two lights (night) placed horizontally between 6 and 12 feet apart. If only an open boat or barge is used, a ball or flag may be displayed during the day. In some regions, such as those under the Mersey Dock and Harbour Board, a uniform system of shape and color is used to assist mariners at night.

## Legal Status of Wreckage
The legal ownership and recovery of property lost at sea vary by jurisdiction:

*   **England:** Under the Merchant Shipping Act 1894, "wreck" includes everything found on the shore of a tidal river or the sea. Finders must report discoveries to the receiver of wreck or face a penalty. While the finder is entitled to a salvage reward, the property belongs to the crown or its grantee unless the true owner claims it within one year.
*   **United States:** Unclaimed wreck generally becomes the property of the state after one year. Some American courts have held that without a decree, a finder is entitled only to salvage rights, with the court retaining the rest for the state if no owner is found.
*   **Scotland:** Theoretically, the crown holds the right to lost property. Under the Burgh Police (Scotland) Act 1892, finders must deliver goods to the police. If no owner is found within six months, magistrates may hand the items to the finder.

## Historical Context of Wrecks
Historical accounts illustrate the strategic and cultural impact of shipwrecks. During the conflict at Salamis, the Persians occupied the island of Psyttaleia specifically because it was expected that men and the wrecks of ships would be cast up on its shores during the sea-fight. Following the battle, the Hellenes towed back as many wrecks as possible to prepare for further fighting, though many were carried by the West Wind to the strand in Attica known as Colias.

In other ancient cultures, such as the Tauroi, shipwrecked persons were treated as victims for sacrifice to the "Maiden" (identified by the Tauroi as Iphigeneia, daughter of Agamemnon). These victims were either struck in the head with a club and pushed from a cliff or buried in the earth.
