# Military Operations in Italy: Traun and the Spanish Conflicts

## The Campaigns of Traun
In the mid-18th century, the Austrian commander Traun operated in Italy, where he initially held his own with ease against the forces of the Neapolitans and Spaniards. During this period, a British squadron forced Naples to withdraw its troops to focus on home defence. Consequently, Spain, lacking the strength to advance in the Po valley, dispatched a second army to Italy via France.

The geopolitical situation was marked by complex complications; Sardinia had allied with Austria, yet neither state was officially at war with France. This resulted in combats occurring in the Isere valley between Spanish and Sardinian troops, while the French remained uninvolved.

In February 1743, the Spaniards achieved a Pyrrhic victory over Traun at Campo Santo on the Panaro. However, this was followed by six months of inaction. Traun was eventually joined by Lobkowitz, who brought reinforcements from Germany, allowing the Austrians to drive the enemy back to Rimini. Simultaneously, the Spanish-Piedmontese war continued in the Alps with little result, though the king of Sardinia personally won a combat at Casteldelfino.

## Expansion of the Conflict (1744–1747)
By 1744, the conflict expanded with the start of the Second Silesian War. France, which had previously acted as an auxiliary and was officially at war only with England, declared war directly upon Sardinia and Austria in April 1744. As part of this escalation, the prince de Conti was tasked with leading a fresh army to assist the Spaniards in Lombardy and Piedmont.

By 1745, the campaign in Italy shifted from a "war of posts." A secret treaty in March 1745 allied Naples, Spain, France, and the Genoese republic. The allies concentrated a force of 80,000 men between the Tanaro and the Scrivia. While a march on Piacenza distracted the Austrian commander, the allies defeated the Sardinians at Bassignano on September 27, subsequently capturing Casale, Valenza, and Alessandria.

However, these gains were not sustained. In early 1746, Austrian troops entering from Tirol attacked Franco-Spanish winter quarters, forcing a French garrison of 6,000 men at Asti to capitulate. Count Browne, leading an Austrian corps, struck the allies on the lower Po and severed their communications with the main body in Piedmont. This destroyed the allied concentration, forcing the Spaniards to entrench themselves at Piacenza. Although the French and Spaniards eventually joined forces again, they were pursued by the army of the king of Sardinia and faced the principal Austrian army, leading to a hard-fought pitched battle at Piacenza on June 16.

## Other Spanish Military Expeditions in Italy
Earlier historical records indicate Spanish involvement in Italy during the late 15th and early 16th centuries. When Catholic sovereigns decided to support the Aragonese house of Naples against Charles VIII of France, Gonzalo was chosen to command the Spanish expedition, eventually earning the title "the Great Captain."

Gonzalo held command in Italy twice. His first term (1495–1498) involved aiding Ferdinand of Naples in recovering his kingdom. He returned in 1501 as part of a coalition with Louis XII of France to divide and spoliate the kingdom of Naples, serving as viceroy and general until 1507. 

Operating primarily in Calabria, Gonzalo employed mountain warfare. His forces—consisting of light cavalry and foot soldiers armed with crossbows, arquebuses, or sword and buckler—were veterans of the Granadine war. They utilized "guerrillero warfare" to wear down French forces, which were composed largely of men-at-arms and Swiss pikemen. Gonzalo's troops were noted for their endurance, long marches, and vigilance, which allowed them to better withstand the heat than their opponents.
