# British Naval Operations and the Interception of Convoys (1739–1748)

During the mid-18th century, the British government employed energetic naval policies to disrupt enemy communications and protect trade, specifically targeting French and Spanish convoys. These operations were characterized by a shift toward more coherent strategies between 1747 and the conclusion of the war in October 1748.

## The Interception of French Convoys
In 1747, the British government adopted a more creditable approach to naval warfare than it had in the previous year, when it had allowed a French expedition under M. d'Anville to fail primarily due to its own weakness. Upon receiving information in the spring that an important convoy destined for the West and East Indies was set to sail from L'Orient, the British deployed an overwhelming force under the command of Anson. This force intercepted the convoy in the English Channel on May 3, where the convoy was crushed and either captured or driven back.

Later that year, on October 14, another French convoy was intercepted in the Bay of Biscay. This convoy was protected by a French squadron of eight ships under Admiral Desherbiers de l'Etenduere. They were met by a well-directed British squadron of superior numbers, consisting of fourteen ships under the command of Sir Edward Hawke. Although the high quality of the French ships and the gallant resistance of Desherbiers partially counteracted the British numerical advantage, the merchant vessels were left with minimal protection. While some continued toward the West Indies, most were subsequently intercepted and captured in those waters. This sequence of disasters led the French government to conclude it was helpless at sea, ending further efforts.

## Operations Against Spain
Great Britain declared war on Spain on October 23, 1739, based on the belief that Spanish colonies would fall quickly. The initial strategy involved combined operations from the east and west. Admiral Edward Vernon was tasked with assaulting the colonies from the West Indies, while Commodore George Anson (later Lord Anson) was ordered to round Cape Horn to attack the Pacific coast.

The initial scheme suffered from dockyard corruption, bad preparations, delays, and squabbles among military and naval officers. While Admiral Vernon captured the ill-defended Spanish harbor of Porto Bello on November 21, 1739, he failed to prevent Spanish convoys from reaching Europe. Furthermore, Spanish privateers caused destructive effects on British trade in European waters and the West Indies. A subsequent attack on Cartagena (March 9–April 24, 1741) failed with a heavy loss of life due to the incompetence of military officers, the insolence of the admiral, and the preparations made by the Spanish admiral, Don Bias de Leso.

## The Expedition of George Anson
Commodore Anson's expedition sailed late on September 18, 1740, with a force of six ships that was less strong and more poorly provided than intended. Despite these setbacks, Anson's cruise became a feat of endurance and resolution. He harried the coasts of Peru and Chile and captured a Spanish galleon of immense value near the Philippines. In August 1742, Anson conducted research in the archipelago known as the Ladrones (or the Marianas), landing on the island of Tinian. By the time he returned to England on June 15, 1744, he was alone with his flagship, the "Centurion"; the other vessels had either been lost or failed to round Cape Horn.

## Final Engagements and Peace
The final naval operations occurred in the West Indies in 1748. A British squadron under Sir Charles Knowles attacked the coast of Cuba, while Admiral Regio commanded a Spanish naval force at Havana. Both sides sought to protect their own trade and intercept the other's. The British were particularly motivated by the prospect of capturing the Spanish homeward-bound convoy, which carried bullion from American mines.

On October 1, 1748, the two squadrons met in the Bahama Channel. Although the action was indecisive compared to later British successes, the advantage lay with Sir Charles Knowles. However, he was unable to pursue the victory further upon receiving news that peace had been established in Europe. The powers involved were exhausted, and the peace was arranged on terms of mutual restoration of conquests. Because the French had achieved military success in Flanders and conquests in the Bay of Bengal, and because nothing had been taken from Spain, the French were able to negotiate on equal terms.
