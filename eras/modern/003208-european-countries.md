# EUROPEAN COUNTRIES

## Imperial Expansion and Global Competition
During the latter quarter of the nineteenth century, an increase in European populations forced governments to seek staple foods abroad. Simultaneously, the rise of scientific industrialism created a demand for new raw materials, including rubber, fats, greases, and other previously disregarded substances. While Great Britain, Portugal, and Holland already held significant commercial advantages through their control of tropical and semi-tropical products, other nations began seeking unannexed raw-material areas or Oriental countries that could be profitably modernized. Following 1871, Germany began this pursuit, followed by France and later Italy.

This drive led various European foreign offices to compete with the British to acquire undeveloped regions and to carve up civilized, populous countries in Asia for exploitation. Great Powers sought glories similar to the British ruling class's imperialism in India and the profitable Dutch possessions in the East Indies, directing their interests toward China, Japan, Further India, Persia, and the disintegrating Ottoman Empire.

## Interventions in Asia
European powers engaged in several territorial seizures in China and surrounding regions. In 1898, Germany seized Kiau Chau, and Britain responded by seizing Wei-hai-wei. The following year, the Russians took possession of Port Arthur. These actions, along with the Russian seizure of Manchuria and the British invasion of Tibet in 1904, fueled a flame of hatred toward Europeans in China.

In 1900, a political society known as the Boxers, which sought the expulsion of Europeans, broke out into violence. Supported by a dowager empress of great force of character, the Boxers murdered 250 Europeans and approximately 30,000 Christians. They also attacked and besieged European legations in Pekin. In response, a combined force of Europeans launched a punitive expedition to Pekin to rescue the legations, during which they stole a large amount of valuable property.

## Relations with Japan
Japan first came into contact with Europe in the sixteenth century. In 1542, Portuguese arrived in a Chinese junk, and in 1549, Francis Xavier, a Jesuit missionary, began teaching there. Initially, Japan welcomed European intercourse, and many converted to Christianity. William Adams of Gillingham, Kent, became a trusted adviser to the Japanese and taught them to build large ships, which were subsequently used for voyages to Peru and India.

However, relations deteriorated due to quarrels between English and Dutch Protestants, Portuguese Jesuits, and Spanish Dominicans. The Jesuits, during a period of ascendancy, persecuted Buddhists with acrimony. The Japanese eventually concluded that Europeans and Christianity were an intolerable nuisance, viewing Catholic Christianity as a cloak for the political ambitions of the Spanish monarchy—which already held the Philippine Islands—and the Pope. Following a conclusive persecution of Christians, Japan was absolutely closed to Europeans in 1638 and remained so for over 200 years.

## Political Shifts and Conflict in Europe
The European political landscape shifted from the "patchwork" of the Treaty of Westphalia (1648) to that of the Treaty of Vienna (1815). During the seventeenth, eighteenth, and early nineteenth centuries, the sailing ship spread European influence globally while material knowledge and ideas about the world grew.

In 1821, Greeks rose in insurrection against the Turks. After six years of war and protests from liberal opinion, volunteers from various European countries joined the insurgents. Eventually, Russia, France, and Britain took joint action. The French and English destroyed the Turkish fleet at the battle of Navarino in 1827, and the Tsar invaded Turkey. Under the Treaty of Adrianople (1829), Greece was declared free. Rather than resuming republican traditions, Greece was given a German king, Prince Otto of Bavaria. Additionally, Christian governors were established in Serbia and the Danubian provinces (now Roumania).

## Scientific and Technical Standards
European countries have utilized different systems of measurement. In France and most European nations, barometric height is measured in millimetres. In England, English inches are used. Russia used half-lines (one-twentieth of an English inch) until 1869, after which it adopted the metric barometric scale. Some European countries have used the Paris line (0.088814 in.).

In the field of zoology, European countries established professional societies, such as the Societe Entomologique de France (1832) and the Entomological Society of London (1834). Local naturalists investigated the insect fauna of European countries, while geographical exploration brought exotic materials to museums. Notable studies include those by H.T. Stainton and O. Staudinger on European Lepidoptera, A.H. Haliday and H. Loew on European Diptera, and John Curtis on British insects.

## Natural History and Health
Regarding the flora and fauna of Greenland, there was a long-held belief that they were essentially European. However, evidence suggests land mammals in Greenland are more American than European; for example, the musk-ox and white polar wolf came from America, while the polar bear and Arctic hare are circumpolar. Of the 61 bird species breeding there, eight are European-Asiatic and four are American. Among the 310 species of vascular plants, 44 are European-Asiatic and 40 are American.

Additionally, European children born in regions along the Tigris and Euphrates rivers are susceptible to a disease known as the "Bagdad date-mark" or "Aleppo button." While native children usually have one boil, European children often have several, which almost invariably appear on the face and cause serious disfigurement. Adult foreign women visiting the region rarely escape disfigurement if they stay for a significant length of time.
