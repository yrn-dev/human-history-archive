# Natural Resources and Geography of the Indian Subcontinent

## Timber and Forestry
The availability of economically useful timber in India is limited due to the extremely hot and moist climate, which reduces the durability of the wood. Among the available species, teak (_Tectona grandis_) is considered the finest timber. It is found in its greatest perfection in the forests of the Indian peninsula and the west coasts of Burma, where rainfall is heaviest. Teak grows to heights of 100 to 150 feet and is often mixed with bamboos and other trees.

The sal tree (_Shorea robusta_) provides a very durable wood and is more gregarious than the teak. It is most abundant along the skirts of the Himalaya, extending from Assam to the Punjab, and is also found in central India, where teak also extends. Other useful woods found in the plains include the sissoo (_Dalbergia_), toon (_Cedrela_), and babool (_Acacia_). In the Himalaya proper, the deodar (_Cedrus deodara_) is the only timber in ordinary use. Additionally, sandalwood (_Santalum_) is found in southern India, and various types of bamboo are present throughout the country. Recent introductions include the cinchona, which has been successful, and American mahogany, which shows promise for timber use as it reaches a large size.

## Physical Geography and Plains
British India is situated approximately between the 70th and 95th meridians, extending from the Indian Ocean to the Tibetan table-land. The Indian peninsula south of 25 deg. N is a table-land with elevations ranging from 1,000 to 3,000 feet, though the highest points on the west rise to over 8,000 feet.

The great plain of northern India lies between the foot of the Himalayan slope of the Tibetan plateau and the peninsula's table-land, stretching from the Indus delta in the west to the deltas of the Brahmaputra and Ganges in the east. Including its prolongation up the valley of Assam, this plain covers about 500,000 square miles and contains the most civilized, populous, and richest districts of India. The surface is so finely comminuted that it is possible to travel over 2,000 meters from the Bay of Bengal, through the Punjab, and down the Indus without encountering a pebble.

## Mineral Productions
Coal is the most valuable mineral production in the region. The Tertiary coal seams of Assam are noted for their extraordinary thickness and purity compared to the Gondwana coal of the peninsula. In Upper Assam, the "Thick Seam" of Margherita averages 50 feet and can reach 80 feet in some areas. Assays show that Assam coal has an average ash percentage of 3.8, significantly lower than the 16.3 found in Raniganj coal. These coal seams are frequently associated with petroleum springs. While gold is found in alluvial deposits, exploration results have not been promising.

## Historical Migration and Invasions
Geographical surveys indicate that central Asiatic immigration into the peninsula occurred through two primary gateways: the west (Makran) and the north-west (the Kabul valley). The Kabul valley route, featuring passes across the Hindu Kush from the Kaoshan to the Khawak, served as the entry point for various hordes, including the Huns, Goths, Jats, Yue-chi, and Sacae, who entered the Punjab. Later historic invasions typically followed the route from Kabul to Delhi and Peshawar.

During the fifth century A.D., India was afflicted by the Ephthalites, or White Huns, who terrorized the region and levied tribute from small Indian princes. They spent summers in western Turkestan and descended through the passes every autumn. Knowledge of the Indian Hunas is derived from inscriptions found from central India to the Punjab, coins, and the accounts of the Chinese pilgrim Hsuan Tsang. The Greek monk Cosmas Indicopleustes, visiting around 530, described a White Hun king named Gollas (likely Mihiragula or Mihirakula) who used war elephants and cavalry to exact oppressive tribute.

## Administrative Divisions
For administration, the Indian empire is divided into four minor commissionerships and nine great provinces. These provinces include:
*   **Governors:** Bombay and Madras.
*   **Lieut.-governors:** Burma, the Punjab, United Provinces (Oudh and Agra), Eastern Bengal and Assam, and Bengal.
*   **Chief Commissioner:** The Central Provinces.
*   **Agent to the Governor-General:** The N.-W. Frontier Province.

The supreme executive authority is the viceroy in council, which consists of a commander-in-chief and six ordinary members. For legislation, the governor-general's council includes fifteen members nominated by the crown. In England, the secretary of state for India, assisted by a council of at least ten members, manages the administration and controls revenue expenditure.
