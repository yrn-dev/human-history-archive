# AXOLOTL

The axolotl is the Mexican name for larval salamanders of the genus *Amblystoma*. Known to the people of Mexico from the remotest times, the animal has been regularly brought from neighboring lakes to the Mexico market as a wholesome and agreeable article of food. Francisco Hernandez (1514-1578) referred to the creature as *atolocatl*, *lusus aquarum*, *piscis ludicrus*, or *Gyrinus edulis*.

## Physical Characteristics
The axolotl is a nearly exact image of the larvae of the British newt, though it is significantly larger, growing to a length of eleven inches. It possesses a plump, moderately long body with a low dorsal crest and a strongly compressed tail. Its head is large and thick, featuring a large pendent upper lip and small eyes that lack lids. It has two pairs of well-developed limbs with free digits.

The most characteristic feature of the axolotl is the presence of three large appendages on each side of the back of the head. These external gills are fringed with filaments that, when fully developed, resemble black ostrich feathers; these organs allow the animal to breathe oxygen dissolved in the water. The axolotl's coloration is typically blackish, brownish grey, or dark olive-grey, often marked with round black dots. Its jaws contain small teeth in several rows, and the front of the palate features an elongate patch of teeth on the palatine and vomerine bones.

## Habitat and Ecology
Axolotls occur in abundance in Lakes Chalco and Xochimilco, which are situated approximately 10 feet higher than Lake Texcoco. These lakes are described as a "paradise" for the creatures, characterized by stable water levels, a constant abundance of food, and numerous hiding places among the mud, reeds, roots of floating islands, and banks. Lake Xochimilco specifically contains powerful springs, though its water is often muddy and dark, filled with worms, *Daphniae*, larvae of insects, fish, and decomposing vegetable matter.

The axolotls breed in early February, fastening their eggs to water plants. By June, the larvae grow into large creatures ready for market. While they may take to the rushes in late summer and become scarce in autumn, no axolotls in these lakes have ever been known to metamorphose or leave the water. H. Gadow suggested that the extreme attractions of their environment induce them to remain in their larval state, retaining features not connected with sexual maturity.

## Scientific Classification and Metamorphosis
In the beginning of the 19th century, Humboldt brought specimens of the axolotl to the scientist Cuvier. Cuvier recognized that these animals were not related to permanent gill-breathers (Perennibranchiates) like *Proteus* and *Siren*, but were instead the larval form of an air-breathing salamander. This opinion was initially ignored by most systematists. When it was discovered that the axolotl could breed while still possessing gills, the genus *Siredon* (named by J. Wagler) was maintained as a permanent gill-breather.

However, subsequent discoveries established that *Siredon* is actually the larval form of the salamander *Amblystoma*. The genus *Amblystoma* was established by J. J. Tschudi in 1838 for various North American salamanders. The "perfect" or imago form, such as *Amblystoma tigrinum*, differs from the larval axolotl in several ways: it possesses movable eyelids, a smooth and shiny body with vertical side grooves, and a feebly compressed tail. Its palate dentition consists of small teeth in a single row forming a transverse series behind the inner nares. Unlike the axolotl, the *Amblystoma* leaves the water after completing metamorphosis, a process marked by the loss of the gills.

## History of Study in Europe
Original specimens from the vicinity of Mexico City arrived at the Jardin d'Acclimatation in Paris in late 1863, sent by order of Marshal Forey. Thirty-four specimens were delivered, including one albino. Six of these were given to Professor A. Dumeril at the Jardin des Plantes. 

Between 1865 and 1868, and again in 1870, these original specimens and their offspring produced between 9,000 and 10,000 larvae. This abundance allowed the Paris Museum to distribute over a thousand examples to dealers and institutions across Europe, including the London Zoological Gardens in 1864. It is believed that most axolotls kept in Europe between 1870 and 1880 descended from a stock bred in Paris and distributed by the dealer P. Carbonnier. Recent decreases in their numbers in France and England are attributed to close in-breeding.

Experimental observations at the Jardin des Plantes revealed the latent tendency of the axolotl to transform. Between 1865 and 1870, twenty-nine individuals from the second generation lost their gills and dorsal crests, developed movable eyelids, and assumed the yellow spots of *Amblystoma tigrinum*. While these transformed individuals did not initially breed, it was reported by Professor E. Blanchard in 1876 that an axolotl in its *Amblystoma* state had successfully spawned.
