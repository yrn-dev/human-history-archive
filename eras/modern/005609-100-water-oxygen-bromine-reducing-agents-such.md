# Bromine and Its Compounds

## Physical Properties of Bromine
At ordinary temperatures, bromine is a mobile liquid characterized by a fine red colour, appearing almost black when present in thick layers. It solidifies into a dark brown solid at -21° C. The specific gravity of bromine is 3.18828 (at 0/4°), with a specific heat of 0.1071. Its latent heat of vaporization is 45.6 calories, and its latent heat of fusion is 16.185 calories.

Bromine boils at 59° C, though specific measurements vary based on the drying agent used: 57.65° C when dried over sulphuric acid and 58.85° C when dried over phosphorus pentoxide (under a pressure of 755.8 mm.). The resulting deep red vapour is poisonous and exerts an irritating action on the respiratory organs. The specific heat of this vapour is 0.05504 at constant pressure and 0.04251 at constant volume.

## Solubility and Chemical Reactivity
Bromine is soluble in several substances, including ether, chloroform, alcohol, and water. Its solubility in water at 15° C is 3.226 grammes per 100 grammes of solution, a value that increases slightly if potassium bromide is present. This orange-red solution is permanent in the dark but decomposes into oxygen and hydrobromic acid when exposed to light, eventually becoming colourless. Cooling the aqueous solution produces hyacinth-red octahedra of a crystalline hydrate (composition $\text{Br}\cdot4\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or $\text{Br}_2\cdot8\text{H}_2\text{O}$).

Chemically, bromine's properties are generally intermediate between those of iodine and chlorine. It does not combine directly with carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen. Union with hydrogen requires a catalytic agent or high temperatures. Bromine reacts with other elements to form bromides, sometimes with explosive violence; for instance, phosphorus inflames in its vapour and detonates in its liquid form. While potassium reacts energetically, sodium requires heating to 200° C to react.

## Bromine in Chemistry and Industry
In analytical chemistry, bromine water is primarily used for its oxidizing action. Both bromine and bromine water possess the ability to bleach organic colouring matters. In organic chemistry, bromine serves as an oxidizing and substituting agent and is used to prepare addition compounds. These reactions may occur in liquid, vapour, or solution forms, sometimes utilizing "bromine carriers." Sunlight can accelerate, retard, or alter the products of the action of bromine vapour on organic compounds.

Certain reactions require nascent bromine, which can be produced using solutions of bromate and sodium bromide with a calculated amount of sulphuric acid. Common diluents for bromine include water, acetic acid, chloroform, ether, hydrochloric acid, and carbon bisulphide. Excess bromine is typically removed via shaking with mercury, heating, or using sulphurous acid.

While R. Wagner and others proposed using bromine for gold extraction, the high cost of the element has limited its general application.

## Hydrobromic Acid and Bromides
Hydrobromic acid ($\text{HBr}$) is the sole compound formed from hydrogen and bromine. It is a colourless gas at ordinary temperatures that fumes strongly in moist air and has an acid reaction. It can be condensed into a liquid (boiling point -64.9° C) or cooled into colourless crystals (melting point -88.5° C). When saturated at 0° C, its aqueous solution has a specific gravity of 1.78. A constant boiling point solution (boiling at 126° C under atmospheric pressure) contains 48% acid.

$\text{HBr}$ can be prepared by:
* Passing bromine vapour and hydrogen gas through a heated platinum spiral.
* Dropping bromine onto a mixture of water and amorphous phosphorus.
* Passing sulphuretted hydrogen through bromine water.
* The action of bromine on saturated hydrocarbons like paraffin-wax.

Bromides are formed when hydrobromic acid reacts with carbonates, hydroxides, and metallic oxides, or through the direct union of metals with bromine. Most metallic bromides are solids that volatilize and fuse upon heating. While most are water-soluble, lead bromide (soluble only in hot water), palladious bromide, mercurous bromide, and silver bromide are exceptions. Non-metallic bromides are typically liquids that decompose readily in water.

## Oxy-acids of Bromine
No oxides of bromine have been isolated, but three oxy-acids are known:

* **Hypobromous acid ($\text{HBrO}$):** A very unstable, light yellow aqueous solution with strong bleaching properties. It is obtained by distilling a dilute solution of precipitated mercuric oxide and bromine water *in vacuo* at approximately 40° C. Upon heating, it breaks up into oxygen and bromine.
* **Bromous acid ($\text{HBrO}_2$):** Formed by adding bromine to a saturated solution of silver nitrate.
* **Bromic acid ($\text{HBrO}_3$):** This acid is known only as an aqueous solution and is very unstable, decomposing into bromine, oxygen, and water when heated to 100° C. It can be produced by passing chlorine through bromine water, the action of bromine on silver salt in water, or adding diluted sulphuric acid to the barium salt. It is rapidly converted to hydrobromic acid by reducing agents such as sulphur-dioxide and sulphuretted hydrogen.

The salts of bromic acid are called bromates; they are generally difficult to dissolve in water and evolve oxygen when decomposed by heat.
