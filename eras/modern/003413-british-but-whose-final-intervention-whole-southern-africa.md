# British Interests and Administration in Southern and Central Africa

British involvement in southern and central Africa during the 19th and early 20th centuries was characterized by a series of capitulations, settlements, treaties, and the establishment of protectorates. These activities resulted in a wide array of territories under British influence or direct control, ranging from the Cape of Good Hope to the regions north of the Zambezi river.

## Territories of Southern Africa
The British presence in the southern portion of the continent began with the first occupation of the Cape of Good Hope from 1795 to 1803, followed by a capitulation in 1806; however, the present limits of this territory were not attained until 1895. Other significant settlements and annexations in the region include Natal (settled in 1824, with the submission of the Natal Boers in 1843), Basutoland (annexed in 1868), and Zululand (annexed in 1887 and incorporated into Natal by 1897). Additionally, Bechuanaland became a protectorate between 1885 and 1891, with its southern portion annexed to the Cape Colony in 1895.

## British Central Africa
The term "British Central Africa" was originally used by Sir H.H. Johnston to describe all territories under British influence north of the Zambezi river that were intended for a single administration. By 1893, the name was officially confined to the British protectorate located on the Shiré and around Lake Nyasa. This territory is defined by boundaries between 8° 25' S. on Lake Tanganyika and 17° 6' S. on the river Shiré, and between 36° 10' E. (Mlanje district) and 26° 30' E. (river Luengwe-Kafukwe).

In 1907, the official title of this protectorate was changed to the Nyasaland Protectorate. Other divisions in the region include North Eastern Rhodesia and North Western Rhodesia (Barotseland), both of which were territories of the British South Africa Company. For the purpose of general designation, "British Central Africa" remains the most convenient term for the region encompassing the Nyasaland Protectorate, North Eastern Rhodesia, and parts of North Western Rhodesia.

## Mapping and Surveying
Since 1880, international boundaries in Africa have been carefully surveyed and marked. In the states of British South Africa, each maintains its own surveyor-general. A reconnaissance survey, based on careful triangulation superintended by Sir D. Gill, has been in progress since 1903 and reached within 70 miles of Lake Tanganyika by 1907. This modern survey has superseded older maps, including those of the Transvaal (1899), the Cape Colony, and the Orange River State (1902-1904). Furthermore, the British general staff provided a map of southern Rhodesia in 1909 and a map of north-eastern Rhodesia compiled by C. L. Beri.

## European Intervention and Diplomacy
British intervention in East Africa was significantly influenced by Sir John Kirk, a naturalist and administrator. Kirk served as the physician and naturalist for David Livingstone's second expedition to Central Africa and was among the first four white men to see Lake Nyassa in 1859. Later, as consul-general and agent in Zanzibar, Kirk acted as the virtual ruler of the country for much of the critical period of European intervention.

Kirk exerted a controlling influence over Sultan Seyyid Bargash, securing a treaty on June 5, 1873, to abolish the slave trade. In 1877, the Sultan gave Kirk a declaration promising not to cede territory to any power other than Great Britain, though the British government ignored this at the time. When Germany claimed districts in 1885, Kirk intervened to prevent the Sultan from protesting in Berlin and managed the subsequent negotiations to checkmate German designs in Zanzibar while maintaining the Arab form of government. By May 1887, Kirk successfully obtained a portion of the mainland for Britain.

## Regional Demographics and Culture
The regions of southern Africa were inhabited by various groups prior to and during the period of European intervention. A study of Bantu languages suggests that approximately 3,000 years ago, a powerful tribe of negroes speaking the Bantu mother-language pushed from the heart of Africa into the southern half of the continent. This group assimilated or absorbed the majority of preceding populations, which included Forest-negroes, Nilotic-negroes, Hottentots, and Bushmen. 

Modern Bantu-speaking tribes in these regions exhibit great diversity in physical characteristics. These include the Zulu-Kaffirs, the Ova-herero, the Ba-tonga, the people of the Great Lakes, and the Babangi of the upper Congo. Some Bantu-speaking groups are dwarfish and belong to the Forest Pygmies, while others show relationships to Hottentots or resemble West African negroes.
