# Great Pamir and the Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895

The Great Pamir is a region characterized by lofty valleys situated between ranges of hills. These valleys feature floors that are more or less flat, typically measuring no more than five or six miles in width, and provide luxuriant summer pasture. According to information obtained from guides on the Great Pamir and corroborated at Yangi-Hissar, the term "Pamir" is a Khokandi-Turki word meaning "wilderness"—a place that is waste, abandoned, or depopulated, yet remains capable of habitation. Historically, the region was inhabited by Kirghiz, as evidenced by the ruins of burial-grounds and villages; during that period, the valleys were known by their specific village names rather than as "Pamir."

## Boundary Demarcations and the 1895 Commission

The determination of boundaries in this region was driven by the Russian advance toward the Oxus and the Turkoman deserts, which necessitated a definite border between the kingdom of Afghanistan and Russia's trans-Caspian conquests. While the north-west boundary was established by the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886, the eastern limits required further definition.

The main channel of the Oxus river serves as the northern boundary of Afghanistan, separating it from Russia. This boundary continues from Khamiab to the river's source in Victoria Lake of the Great Pamir. Beyond Victoria Lake, the Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895 demarcated a boundary line that extends to the glaciers and snowfields overlooking the Chinese border. This 1895 commission was also responsible for carrying out Pamir triangulation across the Hindu Kush, a task performed by Colonels R.A. Wahab and Sir T.H. Holdich.

## Regional Geography and Administration

The province of Badakshan, which includes the district of Wakhan, is located on the north-east frontier of Afghanistan and adjoins Russian territory. An 1873 Anglo-Russian agreement acknowledged that Badakshan and Wakhan belonged fully to the amir of Kabul, limiting the province to the southern or left bank of the Oxus.

Geographically, Badakshan is described as having a "bottle-shaped" extension into the Pamirs. It is narrow at the "neck," represented by the northern slopes of the Hindu Kush, and swells eastward to include portions of the little and great Pamirs. The province's boundaries are defined as follows:
* **West:** A line running southwards from the junction of the Oxus and Kunduz rivers, touching the eastern water-divide of the Tashkurghan river (Koh-i-Chungar), and crossing the Sarkhab affluent of the Khanabad before striking the Hindu Kush.
* **South:** The boundary follows the crest of the Hindu Kush to the Khawak pass. While the area east of Khawak is occupied by Kafirs, the exact northern extent of their territory beyond the main watershed is not ascertainable. The boundary becomes definite again at the Dorah pass, which connects Ishkashim and Zebak at the bend of the Oxus with the Lutku valley leading to Chitral.
* **East:** The boundary follows the crest of the Hindu Kush until it meets the Sarikol and Muztagh ranges, which separate India and Russia from China. It then skirts the head of the Tagdumbash Pamir before merging into the Pamir boundary and turning west along the Oxus.

## Exploration and Surveying

The rugged Sarikol range intervenes between Chinese Turkestan and the Russian Pamirs, though the actual dividing line has remained indefinite. Between 1894 and 1896, Professor Sven Hedin conducted investigations into the glacial regions of the Sarikol mountains. Other notable figures in the region include George N. (Lord) Curzon, who was in the Pamirs during the early 1890s, and Captain H. Deasy, who continued the Pamir triangulation into the sources of the Zarafshan and the plains of Kashgar.

Scientific measurement in the region has faced significant challenges. While Russia utilized high-precision triangulation in Europe, the arid trans-Caspian deserts proved an insuperable obstacle to these rigorous methods. Consequently, Russian geography in Central Asia relies on means other than direct measurement for coordinate values. The astronomical observatory at Tashkent serves as the starting point for the trans-Caspian triangulation, which is classified as second-class and extends beyond Osh to the Pamir frontier.
