# Literary Forgeries and the Case of W. H. Ireland

The history of autograph collection and literary production has frequently been marked by the emergence of forgeries. These frauds are often driven by the high prices that rare or favorite autographs can realize, as well as the rivalry between collectors. While many such forgeries—including false letters attributed to popular authors and heroes such as Thackeray, Burns, and Nelson—are too palpably spurious to deceive experienced observers, some have been executed with greater skill.

## The Forgeries of W. H. Ireland and Chatterton

Among the notable instances of literary fabrication are the works of W. H. Ireland and Chatterton. The Shakespearean forgeries perpetrated by W. H. Ireland were conducted with a literary intent rather than as a purely autographic venture. These works, along with the forgeries of Chatterton, are characterized as literary inventions; however, both are described as poor performances.

The scarcity of genuine autograph documents from William Shakespeare makes such forgeries a recurring issue. Aside from two signatures on property conveyances in Blackfriars and three signatures on the three sheets of his will, there is a remarkable dearth of his handwriting. Consequently, other signatures professing to be his that appear in books are dismissed as imitations.

## Other Notable Autographic Frauds

Beyond the works of Ireland and Chatterton, other sophisticated frauds have appeared in modern times. In the middle of the 19th century, a series of letters attributed to Shelley and Byron was fabricated. These documents were produced with complete seals and postmarks and were published as *bona fide* documents.

## The Nature of the Epistle and Pseudonymous Works

The production of forgeries is often linked to the distinction between a letter and an epistle. While a letter is a spontaneous, private, and non-literary production, an epistle is a literary form and an artistic effort intended for a public audience and permanence. Because of the posthumous value placed on the letters of great men, epistles have sometimes been written under eminent names, either genuinely or as forgeries. This practice of pseudonymous epistles was particularly common during the early Roman empire, with works frequently attached to the names of Cicero, Aristotle, Plato, and Demosthenes. For example, six epistles associated with Aeschines are identified as forgeries, which were subsequently used by the composer of twelve epistles bearing Aeschines' name.

## Historical and Political Forgeries

Forgeries have also appeared in historical and religious contexts. The *Constitutum* (the Donation of Constantine) was critically challenged by Laurentius Valla in 1440, sparking a controversy that lasted until the end of the 18th century. While Roman Catholic scholars, such as Cardinal Hergenrother and Grauert, have attempted to prove the fraud was not committed in Rome—suggesting instead it was written by a Frank in the 9th century or concocted at the abbey of St Denis after 840—available evidence confirms the forgery originated in Rome.

In contrast, modern criticism of the Book of Daniel suggests that while it is a production of the reign of Antiochus Epiphanes and lacks historical accuracy, it is not a forgery. Instead, it is characterized as a consolatory political pamphlet intended to provide hope to God's people during their distress.
