# 1700 and the Rise of Spain Consequent upon the Accession of the French

The year 1700 marked a pivotal transition in European geopolitics characterized by the end of the Hapsburg dynasty's rule in Spain and the subsequent rise of French influence over Spanish territories. This transition was triggered by the death of the last Hapsburg heir, King Charles II, on November 1, 1700.

## The Succession of Philip of Bourbon
The future of Spain's possessions had been a subject of significant diplomatic maneuvering and instability. Prior to his death, Charles II had been persuaded to name Philip, Duke of Anjou—the second son of the dauphin and grandson of Louis XIV of France—as his sole successor. This appointment ensured that Philip of Bourbon would become the sovereign of Spain and, consequently, the sovereign of the Netherlands.

Although Louis XIV had formally renounced such claims under the Treaty of the Pyrenees, he took immediate steps to support his grandson's claims upon the death of Charles II. In practice, Louis XIV governed in the name of Philip.

## Impact on the Netherlands and Belgium
The accession of the French claimant brought immediate administrative changes to the Netherlands, specifically Belgium. The absolutist system utilized in France was suddenly introduced to Belgium. This new régime was organized by the Count of Bergeyck, who was assisted by French generals.

However, this French-led administration was short-lived. The War of the Spanish Succession broke out in 1702, leading England and the United Provinces to form an alliance against Louis XIV. The conflict was eventually resolved through three treaties between 1713 and 1714: the treaties of Utrecht, Rastadt, and Bade. These agreements determined that Belgium would be transferred to the Austrian branch of the Hapsburgs to serve as a protective barrier for the United Provinces against French threats. Following a provisional occupation by the Dutch, the Hapsburgs of Austria were placed in full possession of Belgium via the Treaty of Antwerp (the "Treaty of the Barriers") in 1715 and the Hague Convention of 1718.

## European Conflict and the Treaty of Utrecht
The Spanish question provided Louis XIV with an opportunity to recover territories lost during the war with the Grand Alliance (1688–1697), which had ended with the Treaty of Ryswick. While Louis XIV's astute diplomacy successfully won the inheritance for Philip, it plunged France and Europe into an immense war starting in 1700.

The struggle concluded with the Peace of Utrecht in 1713. Under the terms of this peace, the French prince Philip retained the Spanish crown, but France was required to make further concessions of territory.

## Consequences for the Buccaneers
The political shift of 1700 had a direct impact on the maritime communities of the West Indies. For three-quarters of a century, French and English buccaneers had maintained a bond of unity, collaborating in schemes of aggression against a common foe. This unity had already been strained by the war between England and France that began in 1689.

During the brief peace from 1697 to 1700, both England and France attempted to gain the favor of the Spanish king. However, the resumption of hostilities in 1700, coupled with the rise of Spain following the accession of the French claimant, effectively closed the career of the buccaneers. While they had appeared significant one last time as auxiliaries to French regular forces during the siege of Carthagena, the community faced rapid extinction.
