# Lower Austria

Lower Austria (German: *Niederoesterreich* or *Oesterreich unter der Enns*, meaning "Austria below the river Enns") is an archduchy and crownland of Austria. It covers an area of 7,654 square miles and is bounded to the north by Bohemia and Moravia, to the west by Bohemia and Upper Austria, to the south by Styria, and to the east by Hungary.

## Geography and Hydrography
The province is divided into two primary parts by the Danube river, which enters at the most westerly point and exits at the eastern extremity near Pressburg. Lower Austria belongs to the watershed of the Danube; with the exception of the Lainsitz (a tributary of the Moldau), the Danube receives all other rivers in the province. Navigable rivers include the Danube, the March, and the Enns.

The province's right-bank affluents include the Enns, Ybbs, Erlauf, Pielach, Traisen, Wien, Schwechat, Fischa, and Leitha. The left-bank affluents consist of the Isper, Krems, Kamp, Goellersau, and the March. Notable small Alpine lakes include the Lunzer See and the Erlaufsee. The region is also known for various mineral springs: thermal springs at Voeslau, iron springs at Pyrawarth, iodine springs at Deutsch-Altenburg, and sulphur springs at Baden.

## Topography
The province is characterized by two distinct regions:
*   **The North:** Known as the *Waldviertel*, this is a low hilly country that continues the Moravian and Bohemian plateau. It reaches an altitude of 3,478 feet at the Peilstein in the Weinsberger Wald, descending through the Manhartsgebirge (1,758 feet) and the Gfoehler Wald (2,368 feet). Its south-easterly offshoots include the Bisamberg (1,180 feet) near Vienna.
*   **The South:** This region is primarily mountainous and hilly, occupied by the Lower Austrian Alps and their offshoots. The highest summit in the province is the Schneeberg (6,806 feet). Other principal groups include the Raxalpe (6,589 feet), the Oetscher (6,205 feet), the Duerrenstein (6,156 feet), and the Voralpe (5,802 feet). To the east of the Semmering ridge lie the Leithagebirge (1,674 feet) and the Wechsel (5,700 feet). The Wiener Wald, which reaches 2,929 feet at the Schoepfl, ends in the Leopoldsberg (1,380 feet) and Kahlenberg (1,404 feet) northwest of Vienna.

The Carpathians separate Lower Austria from Hungary and Transylvania, meeting the Alps at the Leitha Mountains at Pressburg.

## Climate, Soil, and Agriculture
The climate is generally healthy and moderate, though it is subject to rapid temperature changes and varies based on surface configuration. 

The soil is of moderate fertility and does not fully meet the needs of the province's thick population. Land use is distributed as follows:
*   Arable land: 43.4%
*   Woods: 34.2%
*   Gardens and meadows: 13.1%
*   Pastures: 3.2%
*   Vineyards: 2%

Vineyards, particularly those on the sunny slopes of the Wiener Wald, produce good wine. While cattle-rearing is not well developed, fish and game are plentiful. Mining is of slight importance, with graphite found near Muehldorf and small amounts of iron-ore and coal extracted in the Alpine foothill region.

## Industry and Economy
Lower Austria is among the front rank of Austrian provinces industrially, alongside Moravia and Bohemia. The center of commerce and industrial activity is the capital, Vienna. Additionally, many factories are located in the region from the Semmering up to the Wiener Wald, utilizing the area's abundant waters for motive power.

Principal industries include:
*   Textile and metallurgic industries (all branches)
*   Chemicals, brewing, and milling
*   Paper, silk, and leather
*   Cloth, millinery, and *objets de luxe*
*   Foodstuffs, tobacco, and sugar
*   Musical and physical instruments

Specific towns contribute specialized products; for example, Krems (population 12,657 in 1900) produces vinegar, mustard, steel goods, and a specific white lead known as *Kremser Weiss*. Klosterneuburg (population 11,595 in 1900) serves as an important pioneer station and houses an academy for fruit and wine cultivation.

## History and Roman Influence
In ancient times, the territory between the Danube, the Inn, and the Save—including Lower Austria—was known as Noricum and Pannonia. The Danube served as a frontier of the Roman Empire for a long period. 

A significant Roman site in Lower Austria is Carnuntum, located near Hainburg between Petronell and Deutsch-Altenburg. Originally part of Noricum and later Pannonia, it was a Celtic town that became a center for Roman fortifications from Brigetio to Vindobona (Vienna). It served as a mart for amber from the north and was created a municipium by Hadrian. Marcus Aurelius resided there from 172 to 175 during the war against the Marcomanni, and Septimius Severus was proclaimed emperor there in 193. The site was destroyed by Germans in the 4th century and later by Hungarians in the middle ages.
