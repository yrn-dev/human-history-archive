# Brigandage

Brigandage is a form of lawlessness characterized by outlaws who conduct warfare as irregular or partisan soldiers. The term "brigand" is believed to derive from the Old French *brigan*, a form of the Italian *brigante*, meaning a partisan or irregular soldier. Similarly, the word "bandit" shares this meaning. In Italy, a *bandito* was specifically a man declared an outlaw by a proclamation, or *bando*.

## Nature and Methods of Brigandage
The brigand operates through surprises and skirmishes, making the activity self-supporting through plunder, the extortion of blackmail, and the capturing of prisoners for ransom. Violence is used to enforce demands, and prisoners who are unable to pay are killed. 

While often viewed as mere malefactors, brigands have sometimes been the last resource for people subject to invasion. For example, Spanish irregular levies resisting the French from 1808 to 1814 and Calabrians fighting for Ferdinand of Naples were labeled brigands by their enemies. Similarly, in the Balkan peninsula under Turkish rule, the *klephts* (Greeks) and *hayduks* or *haydutzi* (Slavs) viewed themselves as representatives of their people against oppressors. In such regions, the "armatoli" were populations permitted to carry arms to repress the klephts, though they often became allies of the brigands instead.

## Conditions Favoring Brigandage
The development of brigandage is primarily fostered by bad administration and the availability of convenient hiding places, such as forests and mountains. Notable haunts include the Apennines and mountains of Calabria in Italy, the Sierras of Spain, the highlands of Scotland (home to "gentlemen reavers"), and the forests of England. Other examples include the "maquis" (bush) of Corsica and the bush of Australia.

In Italy, brigandage was chronic due to the survival of feudal power, the helplessness of the peasantry, and a fragmented system of small states that allowed brigands to flee across borders to avoid pursuit. In Naples and Sicily, "manuténgoli" (maintainers)—including corrupt officials, political parties, and terrorized or profiting peasants—provided essential support by selling food and clothes to the bands. In the East, the Kurds are described as wild and lawless and given to brigandage, partly due to being treated with mistrust by Persians and Turks.

## Brigandage in Spain
In Spain, brigandage was endemic in and south of the Sierra Morena, while in the north it flourished during periods of weak government, civil war, or foreign invasion. It reached its peak in Catalonia, originating from peasant strife against feudal landlords. A traditional hero of this period was Roque Guinart. 

Catalan brigandage was stimulated by the War of the Succession (1700-1714) and the 1640 revolt against the house of Austria. It was suppressed through the efforts of Pedro Veciana, a country gentleman and hereditary *balio* of the archbishop of Tarragona, who armed his servants to form a band called the Mozos of Veciana. The government of Philip V subsequently commissioned Veciana to raise the "escuadra de Cataluna," a special police corps. Later, the organization of "La Guardia Civil" by the duke of Ahumada around 1844 further kept brigandage down. 

A notable instance of local resistance occurred at the close of the Carlist War in 1874, when a gang of charcoal-burners surprised one of the worst bands in Catalonia and battered its members to death with boxwood cudgels on the ruins of the castle of San Martin de Centellas.

## Historical and Social Impact
In the sixth, seventh, and eighth centuries, western Europe experienced a period of confusion and universal insecurity characterized by brigandage and unpunished crimes. This lawlessness forced individuals to seek protection from powerful leaders—such as barbaric chiefs, bishops, or landowners—leading to the development of the feudal system.

Despite the romanticized portrayal of brigandage in popular verse (such as the *ottava rima* poems of southern Italy) or ballads like those of Robin Hood, the reality was often grim. Many bands were recruited by men fleeing the gallows after committing homicide. Internal conflicts were constant, and weaker members were often robbed and terrorized by bullies within their own bands.
