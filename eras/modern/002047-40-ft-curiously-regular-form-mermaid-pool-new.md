# Pools and Pool-Based Systems

The term "pool" appears across various contexts in the archive, referring to natural geological formations, artificial bodies of water, competitive sporting structures, gaming mechanics, and financial stakes.

## Natural and Artificial Water Formations

In the physical geography of certain island groups, coral formations create low-lying landscapes. In New Providence, a natural phenomenon known as "banana holes" occurs in the limestone. These holes are characterized by a curiously regular form and reach depths of approximately 40 ft. A deeper example of this phenomenon is the Mermaid's Pool in New Providence, which is partially filled with water. Other water features in these regions include an artificial lake in New Providence used by turtle-catchers—noted for its extraordinary phosphorescence—and a fresh or nearly fresh swampy lagoon in Eleuthera.

In eastern France, the district of Dombes is an undulating plateau characterized by an impervious surface of boulder clay and glacial relics. This geography has led to the creation of numerous rain-water pools, which cover some 23,000 acres of the region's 282,000 total acres. These pools, which range from 35 to 250 acres in size, were largely created artificially, many dating back to the 15th century or earlier. Landed proprietors established them for fish-breeding, which was viewed as a more secure revenue source than agriculture during disturbed times. While this policy led to depopulation and disease, the pools remain profitable for rearing pike, tench, and principally carp.

In Australia, the Darling River—which rises in Queensland and flows into New South Wales—is marked by a series of shallow pools during the dry season.

In historical accounts of the New City, the sheep pool of Bethesda was located beneath a hospital with five porticoes or cloisters on the small hill of Bezetha, situated on the north side of the temple.

## Competitive and Sporting Pools

In the context of fencing, specifically the epee tournaments, a "pool" is a method used to determine a victor. Competitors are divided into pools, typically consisting of six or eight fencers. Each member of the pool fights an assault for the first hit against every other member. The winner is the individual who is hit the least often, or not at all. In tournaments with many competitors, preliminary rounds are held, and fresh pools are formed from the top two, three, or four fencers of each preliminary pool until a final pool determines the overall victor.

In American billiards, Pool is played on a six-pocket table using fifteen numbered balls. One popular variety is Continuous Pool, an expanded version of Fifteen-Ball Pool. In this version, the game is played for a series of points (generally 100). The player who "breaks" must send at least two balls to the cushion or forfeit three points. Players must "call" the ball they intend to pocket and the specific pocket they intend to use.

## Financial and Gaming Pools

In the card game known as Bank (also called Russian Bank or Polish Bank), a pool is formed by each of the five or six players contributing an arranged stake. Players bet against a "turn-up" card; winners take the amount from the pool, while losers pay into it. If the pool is exhausted, a fresh stake is contributed. Penalties for touching cards before betting result in a fine paid to the pool.
