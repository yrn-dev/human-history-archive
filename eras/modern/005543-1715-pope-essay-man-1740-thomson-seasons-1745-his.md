# Pope's Essay on Man and Thomson's Seasons

The works of Alexander Pope and James Thomson represent significant milestones in English literature, exerting a broad influence that extended across Europe and into the development of German and French poetry during the 18th century.

## James Thomson and The Seasons
James Thomson's *The Seasons* (1726–1730) is regarded as the classic of descriptive poetry and the most successful specimen of its kind in world literature. Thomson was the first poet of considerable eminence to treat external nature as an all-sufficient subject, utilizing a single appeal to landscape and the emotions it evokes to sustain a long poem. 

While some critics, such as Coleridge, viewed Thomson as a good rather than a great poet, noting that descriptive poetry often fails to engage the highest powers of the imagination, Thomson is credited with producing comprehensive beauties and nobly-massed effects unknown to his predecessors. His work is characterized by a skillfully varied catalogue of natural phenomena; a primary example of this art is his description of twilight in the "fading many-coloured woods" of autumn. Despite these achievements, the work has been criticized for a certain monotony and a lack of progress toward a final crisis of passion or rapture.

## Influence on European Literature
The example set by Thomson was immediately fruitful on the European continent, particularly in France and Germany.

### Impact in France
In France, *The Seasons* prompted four different translations. J. F. de Saint-Lambert (1716–1803) produced an imitation titled *Les Saisons* (1769), which remained popular for fifty years; Voltaire remarked that it was the only poem of its generation that would reach posterity, though Madame du Deffand described Saint-Lambert as "froid, fade et faux." Other descriptive poets followed, including J. A. Roucher, who wrote *Les Mois* in 1779, and the Abbé Jacques Delille. Delille, viewed by contemporaries as a Virgil, published *Les Géorgiques* (1769), *Les Jardins* (1782), and *L'Homme des champs* (1803). His work *Trois règnes de la nature* (1809) is considered the masterpiece of the descriptive school, though like Thomson, Delille struggled with coherency and monotony. The vogue for this style of poetry eventually ended following the witty satire of Marie Joseph Chénier in *Discours sur les poèmes descriptifs*.

### Impact in Germany
In Germany, the poet Barthold Heinrich Brockes (1680–1747) served as a pioneer in introducing the new English poetry of nature to his countrymen. Brockes translated both Thomson's *Seasons* (1745) and Pope's *Essay on Man* (1740). Brockes' own poetry, while sometimes crude or artificial, shifted German literature away from bombastic imitations of Marini toward a clear and simple diction. His work introduced a religious interpretation of natural phenomena and a reverential attitude toward nature, which prepared the way for the poet Klopstock.

## Alexander Pope and Literary Connections
Alexander Pope's influence was felt not only through his poetry but also through his connections to other literary and political figures. Pope was influenced by the French poet Boileau, who was the first to reduce French versification to rule. 

Pope's interactions with Bolingbroke provide insight into the political entanglements of the era. Bolingbroke had written *The Patriot King* for Frederick, prince of Wales, and entrusted the work to Pope. It was later discovered that Pope had secretly printed 1,500 copies of the poem, leading Bolingbroke to publish a corrected version in 1749. Bolingbroke was present at Pope's death in May 1744.

## Related Works and Collaborations
The influence of Thomson extended into other artistic mediums. For instance, the English musical historian Charles Burney (1726–1814) wrote music for Thomson's *Alfred*, which was produced at the Drury Lane theatre on March 30, 1745. In England, Thomson's descriptive style was widely imitated by other writers, including Akenside, Armstrong, Shenstone (in *The Schoolmistress*, 1742), the anonymous author of *Albania* (1737), and Goldsmith (in *The Deserted Village*, 1770).
