# British North America

British North America comprises a significant portion of the British Empire's territories in the Western Hemisphere. Its geographical extent and economic structure are characterized by a mix of dominion governance and colonial administration, with a population and landmass that contribute substantially to the empire's global reach.

## Geography and Demographics
The territories of British North America are situated primarily in the northern hemisphere. Canada alone occupies 3,653,946 square miles in the western hemisphere. While much of the empire's land is located in temperate zones suitable for white settlement, the portions of North America that extend into Arctic regions are noted as exceptions to this suitability.

As of 1901, the white population of British North America was recorded at 5,500,000. This population is mainly of Anglo-Saxon race, though it also includes some Spanish, Dutch, and French peoples.

## The Dominion of Canada
Canada represents a major administrative and financial hub within British North America. The dominion possesses a robust banking system consisting of 29 banks, many of which are large and managed according to the Scottish principle. These institutions maintain more than 1,200 offices, with combined capitals of nearly £19,000,000 and deposits totaling approximately £140,000,000.

Within the dominion, provinces such as British Columbia operate under specific financial arrangements. In 1905, British Columbia received an annual contribution of $307,076 from the dominion government. The province's total revenue for that year was $2,920,461, derived from the dominion contribution as well as income tax (which operates on a sliding scale), timber dues, sales of public land, and provincial taxes on personal and real property. The provincial expenditure for 1905 was $2,302,417. 

The capital investment in British Columbia was estimated in 1899 at $307,385,000. This figure includes $100,000,000 in timber, $47,500,000 in telegraphs and railways, and $10,500,000 in smelters and mining plant, alongside provincial and municipal assessments and private wealth.

## British Honduras
British Honduras is located in the region of the Caribbean Sea, bordering the bay or gulf of Honduras. The territory's commerce and finance saw growth between 1901 and 1905, with the tonnage of vessels accommodated at its ports increasing from 300,000 to 496,465. During this period, imports rose from £252,500 to £386,123, while exports increased from £285,500 to £377,623.

The export economy of British Honduras relies on fruit, timber, and other vegetable products, as well as sponges, turtles, tortoiseshell, deerskins, and rum. Its principal imports include hardware, cotton goods, spirits, wine, beer, groceries, and specie. The sea-borne trade is primarily shared between the United States and Great Britain.

Financial administration in British Honduras underwent a significant change on October 14, 1894, when the American gold dollar was adopted as the standard coin, replacing the Guatemalan dollar and ending the legal tender status of silver from Central, South, and North America. The currency system includes government notes in denominations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 50, and 100 dollars, as well as silver pieces (5, 10, 25, and 50 cents) and bronze one-cent pieces. The British half sovereign and sovereign remain legal tender.

Banking services were established with the founding of the government savings bank in Belize in 1846, followed by branches in other principal towns and the establishment of the British Bank of Honduras in Belize in 1903.

The revenue of British Honduras, which is chiefly derived from customs, rose from £60,150 in 1901 to £68,335 in 1905. Expenditure for the same period rose from £51,210 to £61,800, with education and the cost of police cited as important items. As of 1905, the public debt stood at £34,736, representing the balance of three loans raised in 1885, 1887, and 1891 for public works in Belize; these loans are scheduled for repayment between 1916 and 1923.
