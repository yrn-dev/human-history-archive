# Municipal and Executive Appointments

The administration of cities and territories varies significantly across different jurisdictions, with some systems utilizing popular elections for leadership and others relying on appointments by national or state executives.

## Appointment by National Executive: Buenos Aires
In Buenos Aires, the government is vested in an *intendente municipal*, who serves as the mayor. This official is appointed by the national executive, though the appointment requires the approval of the senate. The city's legislative functions are handled by the *concejo deliberante* (legislative council), which is elected by the people and consists of two councillors from each parish. In this system, every ratepayer—regardless of whether they are a native or a foreigner—possesses the right to serve in the municipal council and to vote in municipal elections. Additionally, the higher municipal courts and the police force (which is a military organization) remain under the control of the national executive.

## Elected Mayoral Systems in the United States
Several American cities utilize elected mayors with varying powers of appointment and qualifications:

*   **Baltimore**: The mayor is elected for a four-year term. Candidates must be at least twenty-five years old and own property in the city valued at $2,000 or more, having paid taxes on that property for the two years prior to the election. The mayor has the authority to appoint heads of departments and sub-departments, subject to approval by the second branch of the council. When appointing a commission or board, the mayor must select members from more than one political party. The mayor also holds veto power over ordinances, which can only be overridden by a three-fourths affirmative vote from both branches of the council.
*   **Louisville**: The mayor is elected for a four-year term. Subject to the approval of the board of aldermen, the mayor appoints the members of the two principal executive boards (public safety and public works) as well as the controller.
*   **Buffalo**: Governed by an amended city charter of 1896, Buffalo elects its mayor for a four-year term. The mayor is responsible for appointing the heads of the principal executive departments, specifically those of fire, police, parks, civil service, and health.

## Other Executive and Administrative Appointments
Beyond mayoral roles, various national and regional systems employ appointed executives to maintain authority:

*   **Bolivia**: The national executive's authority extends to the smallest hamlets through a hierarchy of appointed officials. The president appoints the prefect, who is the supreme administration in each department and is solely responsible to the president. The prefect, in turn, appoints subordinate officials, including *alcaldes*. Other appointed officials include sub-prefects in provinces, *corregidores* in cantons, and *alcaldes* in rural districts. Additionally, the president appoints two *delegados nacionales* to govern national territories adjacent to Peru and Brazil.
*   **The Sarre**: This territory is governed by a five-member commission appointed by the League of Nations. The commission includes one French member, one native inhabitant of the Sarre, and three representatives from countries other than Germany and France. The League also appoints a Chairman to serve as the commission's executive.
*   **England (Historical)**: In 1215, a charter granted by John stipulated that the citizen chosen to be mayor had to be presented to the king or his justice for approval. This requirement led to the development of the Lord Mayor's Show, a pageant involving the mayor's journey to Westminster.
