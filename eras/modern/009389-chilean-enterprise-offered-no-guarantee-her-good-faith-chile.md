# Chile and the War of the Pacific

## Diplomatic Tensions and the Nitrate Dispute
In the mid-19th century, Chile and Bolivia established agreements regarding territorial boundaries and trade. A treaty from 1866 set the 24th parallel as the boundary of Chilean territory. Under this instrument, Bolivia agreed that Chile would receive full facilities for trading on the coast between the 23rd and 24th parallels and a half share of the customs. Additionally, Chile was permitted to mine and export products from this district without hindrance or tax from Bolivia. In 1870, for a sum of $10,000, an Anglo-Chilean company was granted the right by Bolivia to work certain nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel.

These arrangements created discontent within Bolivia and Peru, as Peruvian authorities were displeased with the establishment of Chilean capital in the rich Tarapaca district. On February 6, 1873, Bolivia and Peru entered into a secret agreement for mutual defense and the preservation of territorial integrity, aimed at preventing further Chilean encroachment.

## Escalation to War
Following the presidency of Adolfo Ballivian, Dr. Tomas Frias signed a treaty with Chile in which Chile agreed to withdraw its claim to half the duties in Bolivian ports, provided Chilean industries in Bolivian territory remained duty-free for twenty-five years. This treaty was not ratified. By 1876, President Hilarion Daza demanded a tax of 10 cents per quintal on all nitrates exported from Bolivia, threatening to seize nitrates if the levy was not paid.

Chile responded by sending its fleet to blockade the ports of Tocapilla, Cobija, and Antofagasta to protect Chilean property. Colonel Sotomayor occupied Antofagasta on February 14, 1879, and the Bolivian government declared war on March 1.

## The War of 1878–1881
Prior to the outbreak of hostilities, Peru offered to act as a mediator, but Chile rejected the offer. The Chilean government was aware of the secret treaty between Peru and Bolivia and viewed Peruvian intervention as questionable. Furthermore, a Peruvian law passed in 1875 had created a monopoly of the Tarapaca nitrate beds, which prejudiced Chilean enterprise and suggested a lack of good faith. Chile demanded an assurance of Peruvian neutrality and the annulment of the secret treaty; when these were refused, Chile declared war on Peru.

Chilean naval superiority allowed the landing of troops to face allied forces at Arica and other southern points. Chilean forces surprised and routed troops under Peruvian General Buendia at Pisagua, opening the way into southern Peru. General Daza led Bolivian troops in a disorderly retreat to Tacna after receiving news of the defeat at Pisagua. Following the fall of San Francisco and the evacuation of Iquique, the Chileans took complete possession of the Tarapaca province by the end of 1879.

The conflict continued with the battles of Miraflores and Chorillos in January 1881, characterized by heavy slaughter and savage excesses by Chilean troops. These events led to the surrender of Callao and Lima. Patricio Lynch, a Chilean naval officer and minister of marine, led a destructive naval raid and later commanded the Army of Occupation in Peru.

## Peace Treaties and Territorial Changes
Peru concluded a peace treaty with Chile on October 20, 1883. A separate peace was signed between Chile and Bolivia on December 11, 1883, in which Bolivia ceded its entire sea-coast, including the port of Cobija, to Chile.

A long-standing dispute remained regarding the occupation of Arica and Tacna under the Pacto de Tregna of April 4, 1884. In 1895, Chile considered conceding a sea outlet to Bolivia in exchange for recognition of Chilean ownership of Tacna and Arica, but this conciliatory attitude ended after the discovery of secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina. Consequently, Bolivia was forced to yield. A treaty ratified in 1905 required Bolivia to cede the maritime provinces occupied by Chile since the war of 1881, leaving the republic without a seaport.

## Post-War Infrastructure and Relations
Under the 1905 treaty and a 1904 agreement (ratified 1905), Chile undertook to construct a railway from Arica to La Paz at its own cost. Chile also agreed to provide Bolivians with free transit through Chilean territory to specific coastal towns, pay a cash indemnity, and provide pecuniary assistance for other interior railways. Specifically, Chile agreed to pay interest (not exceeding 5%) that Bolivia might guarantee on capital invested in certain interior railways, provided payments did not exceed £100,000 annually or £1,600,000 in total.

## Internal Chilean Politics
During this era, Chile experienced internal political instability. Jose Manuel Balmaceda, who served as president starting in 1886, was initially involved in agricultural and industrial enterprise. However, his imperious temper led to conflicts with national representatives. On January 1, 1891, Balmaceda refused to convoke the assembly and ordered the continued collection of taxes on his own authority. This triggered the Chilean Civil War of 1891, which resulted in his overthrow and subsequent suicide on September 18.
