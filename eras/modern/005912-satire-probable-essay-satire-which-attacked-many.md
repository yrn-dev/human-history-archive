# Satire

Satire is a literary form used for the criticism of men, manners, and institutions, ranging from aggressive personal attacks to philosophical dialogues and parodies. Its development across different cultures and eras shows a variety of forms, including the "medley" of early Roman works, the structured verse of the French tradition, and the boisterous attacks of the English Restoration.

## Origins and Roman Development
In Roman literature, Ennius is regarded as the inventor of satire, though in its original sense the term referred to a "miscellany" or "medley." The word *satura* was initially applied to musical and scenic performances in Rome before regular drama was introduced. Ennius's *saturae* consisted of four or six books containing writings on various subjects in different metres, including original compositions and metrical versions of the works of Sicilian Greeks, such as the gastronomic researches of Archestratus of Gela and the physical speculations of Epicharmus.

The character of the genre shifted with Lucilius, who first imparted the quality of censorious and aggressive criticism of manners and men to the form. Later, the Roman poet Horace wrote *Satires* inspired by the heart of Rome, though he later found peace and independence on a farm among the Sabine hills provided by Maecenas.

## French Satire and Boileau
In France, Boileau utilized satire to both attack contemporary figures and demonstrate the capabilities of the French language. His first satire (1660), imitating Juvenal, depicted a poet's farewell to Paris. He eventually produced twelve satires in which he parodied high-ranking writers such as Philippe Quinault, Jean Chapelain, the abbe Charles Cotin, and Georges de Scudery. Boileau's work introduced a regularity of verse structure and a vigour of expression previously unseen in French versification.

Boileau also wrote the *Dialogue des heros de roman* (1664), a prose satire that targeted the elaborate romances of Mlle de Scudery and La Calprenede. His later work included the 12th satire, *Sur l'equivoque* (c. 1705), which attacked the Jesuits. This specific work caused significant conflict; the Jesuit fathers successfully petitioned Louis XIV to withdraw the publication privilege and demanded the satire's suppression. Additionally, Boileau's 10th satire, *Sur les femmes*, provoked an *Apologie des femmes* from Charles Perrault and was denounced by the bishop Bossuet as being subversive of morality and incompatible with the spirit of Christianity.

## English Satire and the Restoration
During the English Restoration, satire took various forms, from the "horribly agile" and "fierce" attacks of Samuel Butler to the malicious songs of the Earl of Rochester. Butler's *Hudibras* (1663-1678) attacked the manners, religions, and wrangles of the popular party, utilizing short rhyming couplets known as "Hudibrastics." Other poets of the period, such as Andrew Marvell and John Oldham, transitioned from elegy and fantasy to satires that served more as documents than works of art; for example, Oldham wrote *Satires on the Jesuits* in 1681.

Other notable English satirists include:
* **Lord Mulgrave (Buckingham):** Author of the *Essay on Satire*, which attacked notable persons, including "sauntering Charles." Though often attributed to Dryden (who suffered physical attacks by Rochester's bravoes because of the reflections in the text), the work was written by Mulgrave.
* **William King:** Wrote *Rufinus, or An Historical Essay on the Favourite Ministry* (1712), a satire on the duke of Marlborough.
* **Another William King (1685-1763):** Wrote the mock-heroic poem *The Toast* (1736), which satirized the countess of Newburgh.
* **Grose:** Wrote *Advice to the Officers of the British Army* (1782) in the style of Swift, and included a satiric preface in *A Guide to Health, Beauty, Riches and Honour* (1783).

## Philosophical and Dialogue Satire
Satire has also been employed within the framework of philosophical dialogues, notably by Lucian. In *Bis accusatus* ("Twice Accused"), Lucian satirizes the popular belief that only gods are happy, with Zeus enumerating the burdens of governing the world. The work also includes a satire on the Socratic method of using short questions and answers, as well as parodies of Demosthenes and Plato. Lucian's *Icaromenippus* serves as an amusing satire on physical philosophers.
