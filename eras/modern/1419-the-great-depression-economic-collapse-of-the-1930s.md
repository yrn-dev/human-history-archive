# The Great Depression: The Global Economic Collapse of the 1930s

## Overview
The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world. While traditionally dated from the stock market crash of October 1929 to the onset of World War II, the crisis was not a single event but a systemic collapse of global finance, trade, and agriculture. 

The crisis was characterized by a catastrophic spiral: a collapse in asset prices led to bank failures, which contracted the money supply, leading to plummeted consumer demand, mass unemployment, and a sharp decline in industrial production. In the United States, unemployment peaked at nearly 25% in 1933. Globally, the crisis was exacerbated by the adherence to the Gold Standard, which prevented governments from expanding their money supplies to stimulate growth.

Historians and economists continue to debate the primary cause. The "Monetarist" school, led by Milton Friedman, argues that the Federal Reserve failed by allowing the money supply to shrink. Conversely, Keynesian economists argue that a collapse in aggregate demand and under-consumption were the primary drivers.

## Key Facts
*   **The Dust Bowl:** In North America, the economic crisis was compounded by a severe drought and poor farming practices, creating massive dust storms that displaced millions of "Okies" and "Arkies."
*   **Bank Failures:** In the U.S., thousands of banks failed between 1929 and 1933, wiping out the life savings of millions of citizens because deposits were not yet insured.
*   **The New Deal:** President Franklin D. Roosevelt introduced a series of programs (the "Alphabet Agencies") such as the WPA and CCC to provide immediate relief, recovery, and long-term structural reform.
*   **Global Impact:** Germany was hit particularly hard, as the withdrawal of American loans (under the Dawes Plan) contributed to the political instability that allowed the Nazi Party to rise to power.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Gold Standard Link:** Countries that abandoned the Gold Standard earlier (such as Great Britain in 1931) generally recovered from the Depression faster than those that clung to it.
2. **Deflationary Spiral:** Prices didn't just stop rising; they plummeted. This meant that while money had more purchasing power, debtors found it impossible to pay back loans that were fixed at pre-crash values.
3. **The 1937 Dip:** Economic recovery was fragile; when the Roosevelt administration tried to cut spending in 1937 to reduce the deficit, the economy suffered a sharp secondary contraction.
