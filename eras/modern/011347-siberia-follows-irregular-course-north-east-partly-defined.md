# The Southern Boundary of Siberia

## Geographical Course and Demarcation
Beyond Kashgar, the southern boundary of Siberia follows an irregular course toward the north-east. This boundary is defined in part by the Alatau and Tian-shan mountains. The line continues until it reaches a northerly point located at approximately 53 deg. N. lat., which is marked by the Sayan range to the west of Irkutsk.

From the Sayan range, the boundary deflects to the south-east. It reaches the Kerulen, an affluent of the Amur river, at a point identified in unofficial maps as approximately 49 deg. 20' N. lat. and 117 deg. 30' E. long. The boundary then follows the Kerulen affluent to its junction with the Amur river, and subsequently follows the Amur river to its junction with the Usuri.

The boundary then follows the Usuri river to its head, moving in a direction slightly west of south. Finally, the boundary strikes the Pacific coast at approximately 42 deg. 30' N. lat. at the mouth of the Tumen river. This point is located 100 m. south of the Amur bay, where the Russian port of Vladivostok is situated at the bay's head.

## Regional Mountain Ranges and Drainage
Siberia's geography is characterized by several significant mountain systems and drainage patterns:

*   **The Yablonoi Range:** Starting from the 115th meridian at its western end, a mountainous belt extends in an irregular line toward the extremity of Pamir. The Yablonoi range divides the waters of the river Lena, which flows through Siberia into the Arctic Sea, from the waters of the Amur river, which falls into the North Pacific.
*   **The Altai:** Located near the 50th parallel and rising to 10,000 or 12,000 ft., the Altai separates the waters of the great rivers of western Siberia from those collecting in the lakes of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria.
*   **The Ural Mountains:** A line of elevation continues west of the Altai to the Ural Mountains. This range divides the drainage of south-west Siberia from the great plains located north east of the Aral Sea.
*   **The Tian-shan and Alatau:** The Tian-shan (or Celestial Mountains) lie between 75 deg. and 95 deg. E. and 40 deg. and 43 deg. N., with some summits exceeding 20,000 ft. They form the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. The Ala-tau is a prolongation of the Tian-shan, rising to 14,000 or 15,000 ft. and acting as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes.

## Historical and Political Context
The region has been the site of various political and military movements. In the district of the Urals and across Siberia near the Manchurian frontier, the Cossack General Semenoff operated during April and May, closing the Trans-Siberian line to traffic via Harbin. This forced approximately twelve thousand Czech soldiers to use the single-track Amur Railway line to the north via Khabarovsk.

During this period, the Siberian Soviets used the poor condition of the Amur line and fictitious floods on the Amur as reasons to temporarily prevent the Czechs from proceeding to France. An international proletariat army of roughly three thousand men, consisting largely of Austrian prisoners of war, was enlisted to repulse Semenoff from the railway junction at Karuimskaya.

## Historical Descriptions of the Region
Historical accounts, such as those from Rashiduddin, describe the confines of "Ibir-Sibir" (Siberia). According to these sources, the region was bounded to the north by the "great river called Angara." Adjacent territories, such as the country of the Kirghiz and the territory of the Naimans, were noted for having many towns, villages, and nomad inhabitants, as well as the cultivation of wheat and the presence of grey squirrels and good iron.
