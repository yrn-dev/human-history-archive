# The Necessary and Immediate Results of Periodical Changes

In the study of natural and physical systems, periodical changes—whether atmospheric, astronomical, or biological—produce specific, immediate, and necessary results. These outcomes range from the generation of global wind patterns to the physical transformation of plant life and the mathematical oscillation of planetary motion.

## Atmospheric Pressure and Wind Systems
The necessary and immediate results of periodical changes in atmospheric pressure are winds. Generally, these winds blow from areas of greatest pressure toward areas of least pressure. However, this movement is subject to modifications in direction caused by the absolute motion of the air, which results from the earth revolving on its axis from west to east.

A primary example of this phenomenon is the south-west monsoon. During the hot half of the year, a great diminution of atmospheric pressure occurs over Asia, becoming strongly marked during May and April as temperatures rise. Simultaneously, relatively higher pressure exists over the equator and regions to its south. This pressure diminution increases as heat increases, reaching its maximum shortly after the solstice in July, which leads to the development of the south-west monsoon. 

As temperatures fall north of the equator and barometric pressure is restored and equalized within the tropics after the October equinox, the south-west winds subside. They are then succeeded by the north-east monsoon during the winter months, a result of the relatively greater atmospheric pressure occurring over Asia compared to the equatorial region.

## Botanical Development and Fertilization
In botany, the development of fruit is the result of the fertilization of the ovule. This process triggers several immediate changes within the parts of the flower. The stigma and anther, which are most directly involved in the process, rapidly decay and wither. While the style and filaments may remain for a period, the petals fall and the floral envelopes become dry. The sepals may either remain in an altered form or be deciduous.

The ovary undergoes enlargement to form the pericarp, and the ovules develop into seeds containing the embryo-plant. While "fruit" strictly refers to the mature ovary or pistil and its interior seeds, the term often includes other parts such as floral envelopes and bracts. For example, the fruit of the oak and hazel includes the ovary enveloped by bracts, while the fruit of the pear and apple consists of the floral receptacle and the ovary. In the case of the pine-apple, the fruit consists of the whole inflorescence. These are categorized as pseudocarps.

## Mathematical Representation of Periodic Variations
Periodic variations can be represented algebraically as the result of a series of harmonic motions. If an angle (L) increases uniformly with time (t) at a rate (n), the general expression is L = nt + L0. This angle completes a 360-degree revolution in a definite period; for instance, if the daily motion is 5 degrees, the round is completed every 72 days.

When an equation takes the form U = a sin (nt + L0), the value of U oscillates between the extreme values of -a and +a. These changes occur within the same period that the angle nt + L0 takes to complete a revolution, resulting in a simply periodic variation. However, the motion of an element of a planet's motion is typically the sum of an infinite series of these periodic quantities with different periods. This results in motion that is still periodic but appears irregular, similar to the motion of a pitching ship.

## Climate and Adaptation
The history of the world is marked by continuing changes in climate, characterized by slow fluctuations between cold and heat rather than a simple story of cooling. These changes have periodically stimulated or checked life, forcing every species of living thing to adapt more closely to its conditions. 

These fluctuations are not regular. Evidence in ice-worn rocks from the Archæozoic or Azoic Age indicates periods of intense cold, which have occurred alternately with warmer conditions. Additionally, the earth has experienced periodic cycles of great dryness and great wetness. Because conditions are always changing, there is no finality in adaptation, only a continuing urgency for fresh change.
