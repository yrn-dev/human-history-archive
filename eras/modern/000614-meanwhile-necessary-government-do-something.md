# Government Actions and Political Transitions

Across various historical contexts, governments have been required to implement specific measures to maintain stability, manage diverse populations, or respond to immediate strategic crises. These actions range from linguistic policies in multi-ethnic empires to the restructuring of city-states and the coordination of naval defenses.

## Language and National Identity in Austria
In the Austrian monarchy, the government found it necessary to take action for the Czechs and other Slavs, as the government depended on their support for their majority. This led to a shift in government influence that became more favorable to these groups regarding language. Consequently, the struggle of nationalities became the primary focus of Austrian public life.

The issue of language became a political matter, specifically concerning its use in schools, law courts, and public offices. While no general law established universal rules, German had served as the ordinary language of government since the time of Joseph II. German was the sole language of the army, the courts, and the central public offices in Vienna, and all laws were published in German. In most parts of the monarchy, including Galicia and Bohemia, German was the language of the "internal service," meaning all correspondence and books were kept in German. This created a network of German-speaking officialism; speaking and writing German was a prerequisite for any share in the government.

An exception existed in the Italian districts, including Trieste, Istria, Dalmatia, South Tirol, and Italy itself (Venetia and Lombardy), where Italian was used even for internal government service. Although officers were required to know Serbo-Croatian and commands were given in German, Italian remained the language of the Austrian navy. Following 1867, the military authorities ensured German remained the language of the army and the railway administration.

## Florentine Governance and Machiavelli
In Florence around the year 1519, Leo X and Cardinal Giulio de' Medici were perplexed by the management of the republic. Due to the gradual extinction of their family, they felt it necessary to provide the city with a semblance of self-government. They sought advice from several politicians, including Machiavelli.

Machiavelli responded with a treatise that blended his general principles of government with the present temper and past history of the city. He urged Leo X to establish a free and permanent state system for the republic, emphasizing the glory that would come to a man who conferred such benefits upon a people.

## Naval and Strategic Coordination
In different military contexts, governments and naval authorities have had to implement urgent protective and strategic measures. During a period of crisis, the Admiralty—specifically the First and Fourth Sea Lords—worked since the end of September to create necessary protective structures. By October 20, Prince Louis telegraphed the Commander-in-Chief that defenses for Scapa would leave the dockyards on October 24.

Additionally, coordination between the British and Japanese Admiralties involved strategic schemes in the vicinity of the Dutch East Indies and Sumatra. The Japanese Admiralty agreed to dispatch the First Southern Squadron to Fiji, though they noted it might be necessary to extend operations to the Marquesas Islands. They also requested that the British Admiralty make the necessary arrangements for the supply of coal for the *Idzumo*, *Asama*, and *Hizen*. Simultaneously, the British Admiralty issued urgent orders on November 4, 1914, for H.M.S. *Kent* to proceed to the Abrolhos Rocks to join Admiral Stoddart’s squadron, as it was necessary to move reinforcing ships and ensure the safety of the surviving ships of Admiral Cradock’s squadron.

## Global Governance and the League of Nations
Following the covenant of 1919, the League of Nations was created. However, it has been characterized as a partial league of states and governments that emphasizes sovereignty and nationality. Some perspectives suggest that for a satisfactory league of peoples to exist, it may be a necessary preliminary for each people to be in complete possession of its own household.

The vision for a secure world peace is seen as requiring a world law under a world government, where nationality is subordinated and sovereignty is merged. This transition is viewed as an educational task to bring a common interpretation of history to all men. While some countries, such as Switzerland or the United States, possess political constitutions that would not impede coalescence, others—such as the Turkish Empire before the Great War—involved "subject peoples" and dependent areas that would require breaking up before they could be adapted to a federal world system.
