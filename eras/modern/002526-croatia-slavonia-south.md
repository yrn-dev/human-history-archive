# Croatia-Slavonia

Croatia-Slavonia is a political division of the Balkan Peninsula located south of the Save and Kulpa rivers. As of 1909, the region encompassed an area of approximately 8,200 square miles with a population of about 1,200,000 people.

## Governance and the Ban
The administration of the region is linked to the historical office of the *ban*. Derived from the Persian word *ban*, meaning lord or master, the term was introduced to Europe by the Avars and used in south-eastern Europe—particularly southern Hungary—to describe governors of military districts known as *banats*. These positions were originally equivalent to the German *margrave* and possessed extensive powers, though they were gradually reduced in importance and number over time.

Since 1868, the governor of Croatia and Slavonia has held the title of ban of Croatia, Slavonia and Dalmatia. Unlike the historical military governors, the duties of the modern ban are civil. The ban is appointed by the emperor of Austria in his capacity as the king of Hungary and holds a seat in the upper house of the Hungarian parliament.

## Political History and Nationalism
The region has experienced various shifts in political control and national identity. In 1809, parts of Croatia were included in the Illyrian Provinces, which were ceded by Austria to Italy and governed by French troops in the interest of Napoleon. Following the cessation of effective French occupation around the end of 1813, these provinces reverted to Austria.

During the 19th century, the southern Slav populations grew restless under German-Magyar domination. Starting in 1836, the "Illyrian" movement was voiced through the *Illyrian National Gazette* of Ljudevit Gaj. Initially, this movement sought a somewhat shadowy Pan-Slav union. However, after the Austrian government interfered in 1844, the movement's objective shifted toward the revival of the "Triune Kingdom"—consisting of Croatia, Slavonia, and Dalmatia—independent of the Hungarian crown.

## Major Towns and Trade
The region includes several significant urban centers with distinct economic profiles:

*   **Brod**: Located in the county of Pozega on the left bank of the river Save, Brod is a town that dates at least from the 15th century. Its name is derived from the Servian word for ford (*brod*). The town has a history of being captured and recaptured during the wars between Austria and Turkey; notably, the Austrian army mustered here in 1879 for the occupation of Bosnia. Brod serves as a transit hub where the principal Bosnian railway meets the Hungarian system, facilitating trade in wood, spirits, wine, cereals, and prunes.
*   **Buccari (Bakar)**: A royal free town situated in the county of Modru[vs]-Fiume on a small bay of the Adriatic Sea. While the town possesses a harbor that provides good anchorage for small vessels, it lost much of its trade during the 19th century due to competition from Fiume. The local economy relies on a coasting trade in coal, wood, wine, and fish, with boatbuilding serving as the staple industry and tunny-fishery maintaining some importance.

## Regional Context
Croatia-Slavonia is part of a broader geographical and historical area often associated with Illyria. The term Illyria has been applied to a region extending from Fiume to Durazzo along the Adriatic shore, reaching inland to the Servian Morava and the Danube. This area includes the southern half of Croatia-Slavonia, as well as Montenegro, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Dalmatia.

In the context of post-war territorial adjustments, property of predominant historic interest to the former kingdoms of Croatia and Slavonia (along with Dalmatia, Bosnia, Herzegovina, and others) may be transferred without payment from the former Austrian Government.
