# The Lateral Division and Geography of Asia

Asia is characterized by a complex system of elevations and depressions that define its hydrography and physical structure. A primary feature of this geography is a lateral division occurring along the parallel of 40 deg. north.

## The Central Depression
Asia is divided laterally along the 40 deg. north parallel by a depression. This feature begins on the east of the Gobi desert and extends westwards through Mongolia into Chinese Turkestan. To the west of Kashgar, this central depression is limited by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol, the latter of which serves as the eastern face of the Pamir. 

The level of this depression, which was once a vast inland sea, likely never exceeds 2,000 ft. above sea level between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the sources of the Hwang-ho. Research indicates that in the central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, the level may actually be below sea level. South of the great continental divide, the land drops to this central depression of the Gobi, creating a vast, almost waterless interior space where local drainage is lost to swamps or deserts.

## The Great Continental Divide
North of the central line of the 40 deg. north parallel, a vast proportion of the continent remains at an altitude of only a few hundred feet. From the low flats of Siberia, the general level of the continent rises gradually toward a great central water-parting, or divide. This divide stretches from the Black Sea, through the Hindu Kush and the Elburz, to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, eventually reaching the Bering Strait in the extreme north-east.

This divide is not consistently marked by steep ranges; in some areas, it consists of intermediate highlands that form lacustrine regions with indefinite watersheds. Only specific sections of this divide rise to great heights, including the Altai, Khangai, Tian-shan, and Hindu Kush; other portions remain below 5,000 ft. in altitude.

## Major Mountain Ranges and Hydrography
Several significant mountain systems define the Asian landscape:

*   **Tian-shan (Celestial Mountains):** Located between 40 deg. and 43 deg. N. and between 75 deg. and 95 deg. E., these mountains form the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. Some summits are said to exceed 20,000 ft. The principal cultivated districts and few towns of central Asia are situated along the foot of this range.
*   **Ala-tau:** A prolongation of the Tian-shan that flanks the Syr on the north, rising to 14,000 or 15,000 ft. and acting as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes.
*   **Altai:** Situated near the 50th parallel and rising to 10,000 or 12,000 ft., this range separates the waters of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria from the great rivers of western Siberia.
*   **Yablonoi and Stanovor:** The Stanovor range (4,000 to 5,000 ft.) follows the southern coast from the borders of Manchuria to Kamchatka. The Yablonoi range divides the drainage of the river Amur (which flows into the North Pacific) from the river Lena (which flows into the Arctic Sea).
*   **Khingan:** This range stretches from north of Manchuria southward to the Chinese frontier near Peking.

In the east, a "strange mountain wilderness" divides China from Burma and Tibet. This region contains the sources of the Mekong, the Salween, the Yang-tsze-kiang, and the Hwang-ho (Yellow River). The Tsanpo of Tibet and the Brahmaputra are the same river. The Salween and Mekong flow parallel to each other for approximately 300 m. in narrow troughs before the Yang-tsze takes its course eastwards north of the 25 deg. parallel.

## Continental Boundaries and Form
Viewed as a whole, Asia has the form of a great isosceles spherical triangle. Its north-eastern apex is at East Cape (Vostochnyi) in the Bering Strait. Its two equal sides, each about 6,500 m. in length, extend to the extremity of the Malay peninsula in the east and the southern point of Arabia in the west. The base of this triangle is approximately 4,500 m. and is deeply indented by the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea.

The boundaries are defined as follows:
*   **North:** The Arctic Ocean, with the coast-line between 70 deg. and 75 deg. N. and the extreme northern point at Cape Sivero-Vostochnyi (78 deg. N.).
*   **West:** The extreme point is Cape Baba on the Mediterranean (26 deg. E.). The boundary follows the Mediterranean, the Red Sea to the strait of Bab-el-Mandeb, the Black Sea, and the Caucasus range (approximately 40 deg. N.) to the Caspian, then north along the Ural Mountains to the Arctic Ocean.
*   **East:** The most easterly point is East Cape (190 deg. E.). The boundary follows the coast of the China Sea and Northern Pacific, broken by the projections of Korea and Kamchatka.
*   **South:** The coast-line is irregular, with the peninsulas of Cambodia, Hindostan, and Arabia descending to about 10 deg. N., while the Malay peninsula extends within 1.5 degrees of the equator.
