# Repressions of Insurrections in Bordeaux (1653 and 1675)

## Civil Unrest in 17th-Century Bordeaux
The 17th century was characterized as a period of disturbance for the city of Bordeaux. During this era, the city served as the primary support for the Fronde. This instability manifested in open defiance of the crown, leading to specific instances where the state intervened to maintain order. On two separate occasions, in 1653 and 1675, troops were dispatched to the city for the purpose of repressing insurrections that had been sparked by royal measures.

## Religious and Social Context
Bordeaux's history of unrest was mirrored by religious tensions. The reformed religion had attracted many adherents within the city; however, following the massacre of St Bartholomew, nearly three hundred residents of Bordeaux lost their lives.

## Later Urban Development and Political Shifts
Following the disturbances of the 17th century, Bordeaux experienced a period of architectural and commercial growth in the middle of the 18th century. This progress was largely driven by the marquis de Tourny, the *intendant* of Guienne, who improved the city's infrastructure by laying out public squares and widening its streets.

The city's political volatility continued into the era of the French Revolution, where it served as the headquarters for the Girondists. During the subsequent Reign of Terror, Bordeaux suffered severely, with levels of hardship comparable to those seen in Marseilles and Lyons. Under the rule of Napoleon I, the city's commerce was greatly reduced.

## Subsequent Political Alignments
In 1814, Bordeaux declared its support for the house of Bourbon. This alignment was later recognized by Louis XVIII, who bestowed the title of duc de Bordeaux upon his grand-nephew, the comte de Chambord. In 1870, the city again played a role in national governance when the French government was transferred to Bordeaux from Tours as German forces approached the latter.
