## 19th Century Literary and Social Figures

Charlotte Brontë's life during the mid-19th century was marked by significant literary production and social engagements. Following the deaths of her siblings Branwell and Emily, she worked on the novel *Shirley*, which she completed after the death of her sister Anne and published in October 1849. During visits to London as a guest of her publisher, Mr. George Smith, she was introduced to Thackeray and later sat for a portrait by George Richmond. In August 1850, while visiting the English lakes as a guest of Sir James Kay-Shuttleworth, she met Matthew Arnold, Miss Martineau, and Mrs. Gaskell. 

Charlotte's personal life included a third marriage offer in 1851 from Mr. James Taylor. However, she eventually promised herself to Arthur Bell Nicholls (1817-1906), her father's curate, who had been a persistent suitor despite the discouragement of Mr. Brontë. They married on June 29, 1854, at Haworth church, with the ceremony performed by the Rev. Sutcliffe Sowden.

In the realm of social reform, Mary Carpenter (1807-1877) was an English educational reformer born in Exeter. Educated in her father's school for boys, she studied mathematics, Greek, and Latin. After her father gave up his school in 1829, she and her sisters opened a school for girls. Carpenter's philanthropic work was influenced by visits from the raja Rammohun Roy, who sparked her interest in India, and Dr. Joseph Tuckerman of Boston, who focused her attention on destitute children. In 1835, she helped organize a "Working and Visiting Society," serving as its secretary for twenty years. She later established a "Ragged School" and a night-school for adults in Lewin's Mead.

## Figures of Ancient Greece

In ancient Athens, Aspasia, the daughter of Axiochus and a native of Milêtus, was a woman of significant influence. Described as beautiful, ambitious, and well-educated, she was the mistress of Periklês. Aspasia possessed remarkable powers of criticism, conversation, and oratory, attracting distinguished Athenians such as Sokratês. While free citizen women of Athens lived in "strict and almost oriental recluseness" and were managed by male relatives, Aspasia belonged to the class of *hetæræ* (courtezans), who managed their own affairs and lived free lives. Along with Theodotê, Aspasia was among the few women in Greece, excluding Spartans, who exercised mental ascendency or inspired strong passion.

## Dutch Literature and the Aesthetic Revolution

The literature of Holland experienced an aesthetic revolution around 1880, moving away from a period of sluggish conventionality. Notable figures during this transition included the humorist Nicolaas Beets and the novelist Mrs. Gertrude Bosboom-Toussaint. Other significant novelists of the period included:
*   **Arnold Buning (b. 1846):** Author of the popular *Marine Sketches* (1880).
*   **M.G.L. van Loghem (b. 1849):** Writing as "Fiore delle Neve," he wrote *Een liefde in het Zuiden* (1881), *Liana* (1882), and *Van eene Sultane* (1884).
*   **Gerard Keller (b. 1829):** Author of *From Home* (1867).
*   **Johan Gram (b. 1833):** Known for *De Familie Schaffels* (1870).
*   **Hendrik de Veer (1829-1890):** Author of *Frans Holster* (1871).
*   **Justus van Maurik (b. 1846):** Known for plays and sketches of Amsterdam life in *Uit het Volk* (1879).
*   **N. Marie C. Sloot (b. 1853):** A writer of colonial novels born in Java.
*   **Adele Opzoomer (b. 1856):** Writing as A. C.S. Wallis, she achieved success with *In Days of Strife* (1877).

Despite his efforts to encourage her to live lavishly with the money he sent, she maintained her customary economies, stating that he would eventually be glad of her savings.
