# San Pedro y Pablo and Licancaur

## Geographic Context
San Pedro y Pablo and Licancaur are volcanic peaks located in the Andean cordilleras of South America. They are part of a series of snow-clad peaks that overlook the great Bolivian tableland, also known as the *alta-planicie*, from the west.

## The Bolivian Tableland
The *alta-planicie* is a vast lacustrine basin that once contained an inland sea, which had an outlet to the east through the La Paz gorge. This plateau extends from the Vilcanota knot in Peru in the north to the Serrania de Lipez on the Argentine frontier in the south. It measures approximately 500 meters in length and 80 meters in breadth, covering an area of about 40,000 square miles.

The elevation of the plateau averages about 12,650 feet near Lake Titicaca, though it descends by approximately 1,000 feet toward its southern end. The region is characterized by its inhospitable nature: the northern section, commonly referred to as the *puna*, is bleak, while the southern portion—the "desert of Lipez"—is barren and arid. This southern area is considered part of the great Puna de Atacama and features large saline depressions that fill with water during the rainy season. The highest peak within the plateau itself is the Cerro de Tahua, reaching 17,454 feet.

## Volcanic Peaks and Elevations
The western boundary of the tableland is formed by the Cordillera Occidental, which serves as a rampart and follows the outline of the coast, acting as a border between Bolivia and the northern provinces of Chile. This range contains several prominent snow-clad peaks. Among these are:
*   Huallatiri (21,654 ft.)
*   Sajama (21,047 ft.)
*   Parinacota (20,918 ft.)
*   Pomarape (20,505 ft.)
*   Licancaur (19,685 ft.)
*   San Pedro y Pablo (19,423 ft.)
*   Oyahua (19,226 ft.)
*   Lirima (19,128 ft.)

## Regional Physiography
The broader Andean system in this region is comprised of two great parallel chains with spurs and ranges extending to the east, projecting toward the Brazilian highlands in a great obtuse angle. Roughly two-fifths of Bolivia's total area is contained within these cordilleras. The system consists of parallel ranges that enclose deep ravines and elevated plateaus. To the north-east of Lake Titicaca, the landscape is characterized by the Nudo de Apolobamba, a knot of intersecting lofty ridges that include some of the highest peaks in South America.
