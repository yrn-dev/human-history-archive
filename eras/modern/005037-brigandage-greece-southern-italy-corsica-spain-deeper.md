# Brigandage in Greece, Southern Italy, Corsica, and Spain

Brigandage in Greece, southern Italy, Corsica, and Spain is characterized by deep historical roots and a long history of resistance to suppression. The persistence of this lawlessness in these four regions is attributed to two primary factors: the abundance of natural hiding places in mountains and forests, and a history of poor administration. In these areas, law enforcement officers were often viewed as dangers or deliberate enemies, leading to a lack of native cooperation. Furthermore, local potentates frequently protected brigands because it served their own interests.

## Brigandage in Greece
Under Turkish rule, the conditions in Greece were such that individuals were either official extortioners or victims; those who were not "klephts" fell into the latter category. Certain groups, such as the Mainotes and similar clans, lived by the sword under their own laws to avoid being flayed by the Turks. Following independence, Greece suffered from extreme maladministration under a nominal parliamentary government, where politicians utilized brigands for their own ends.

The state of Greek brigandage is illustrated by the captivity of S. Soteropoulos, an ex-minister, whose experiences were detailed in the 1868 English translation *The Brigands of the Morea*. The misfortunes of Soteropoulos prompted the government to take strong measures. When the peasantry observed the government's earnestness, they provided active support to the troops, which cleared the Morea. However, brigandage persisted elsewhere. In 1870, a party of English citizens—including Lord and Lady Muncaster, Mr. Vyner, Mr. Lloyd, Mr. Herbert, and Count de Boyl—were captured at Oropos near Marathon. Although Lord and Lady Muncaster were released to secure a ransom of £25,000, the Greek government pursued the brigands with troops, resulting in the murder of the remaining prisoners. The brigands were subsequently hunted down and executed, after which Greece became relatively free of the issue.

## Brigandage in Italy and Corsica
Conditions favoring brigandage were more prevalent and lasted longer in Italy than in other parts of western Europe, with the exception of Corsica (which is described as Italian in all but political allegiance). Until the mid-19th century, Italy's division into small states allowed brigands to escape pursuit by fleeing across borders. For example, Marco Sciarra of the Abruzzi avoided the Spanish viceroy of Naples around 1600 by crossing into the papal states. When the pope and viceroy collaborated against him, Sciarra took service with Venice, which allowed him to maintain contact with and visit his associates. Sciarra, who led 600 men, was eventually killed in a trap.

Sciarra followed the example of Benedetto Mangone, a brigand known for allowing a party of travelers, including the poet Torquato Tasso, to pass unharmed due to his reverence for poetry. Mangone was eventually captured and beaten to death with hammers in Naples. Such figures became heroes of popular verse written in *ottava rima*, including the popular history of the life and death of Pietro Mancino.

In Corsica, the "maquis" (bush) and the island's hills provided essential cover for brigands.

## Brigandage in Spain
Brigandage in Spain was endemic in and south of the Sierra Morena. In northern regions, it flourished during periods of civil war, foreign invasion, or weak government, though it was easily suppressed by capable administrations. The highest levels of brigandage occurred in Catalonia, originating from peasant struggles against the feudal exactions of landlords. This tradition produced the hero Roque Guinart, who appears in *Don Quixote*. Catalan brigandage was further stimulated by the War of the Succession (1700–1714) and the 1640 revolt against the house of Austria.

Suppression in Catalonia was achieved through unconventional means. Pedro Veciana, a hereditary *balio* of the archbishop of Tarragona, armed his farm-servants to resist brigands. Alongside other country gentlemen, he formed a group called the Mozos (Boys) of Veciana. After repelling an attack on the town of Valls, Veciana was commissioned by the government of Philip V to raise the "escuadra de Cataluna," a police corps that remained under the leadership of the Veciana family for five generations.

In central and northern Spain, the rural population generally supported the police when the government acted firmly. The establishment of "La Guardia Civil" by the duke of Ahumada around 1844 further reduced brigandage. Following the Carlist War in 1874, a remaining band in Catalonia was destroyed when a gang of charcoal-burners battered its members to death with boxwood cudgels at the ruins of the castle of San Martin de Centellas. In southern Spain, "bandoleros" enjoyed more sympathy and found local supporters similar to the Italian "manuténgoli." Additionally, tobacco smuggling from Gibraltar continues to sustain a lawless class prone to brigandage.

## General Conditions and Geography
The development of brigandage is primarily linked to bad administration and the availability of convenient hiding places. Various regions provided such refuges: the Apennines and mountains of Calabria for Italian "banditos"; the Sierras of Spain for "bandoleros" (banished men) and "salteadores" (raiders); the highlands of Scotland for "gentlemen reavers"; the forests of England for outlaws like those in the Robin Hood ballads; and the bush of Australia for bushrangers.

Ultimately, neither mountains nor forests provide lasting protection against an intelligent police force supported by a law-abiding community. Gibbon noted that when bands of brigands publicly defy justice, it indicates that the lowest ranks of society are abusing the excessive weakness of the government.
