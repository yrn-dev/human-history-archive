# Intermediaries in Trade, Culture, and Governance

The concept of the intermediary appears across various historical contexts, serving as a link between two parties, cultures, or entities. These roles range from commercial agents and publishing professionals to diplomatic aides and cultural conduits.

## The Book Trade and Publishing
In the history of the book trade, a distinction is made between the roles of the publisher and the bookseller. The publisher is the entity responsible for the production of books from the author's manuscripts and serves as the intermediary between the author and the bookseller. 

Conversely, booksellers, when defined in a restricted sense, act as the intermediaries between the author and publisher on one side, and the general public on the other. While publishers originally began as booksellers, they eventually attained a higher rank within the trade and exerted significant influence on the history of literature. 

Some modern developments have seen these functions merge. For example, in 1902, the London *Times* published supplementary volumes to the 9th edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* and sold them directly, amalgamating the roles of publisher and bookseller. Additionally, the *Times* Book Club, established in 1905, combined the business of bookselling with a subscription library, utilizing the publicity resources of a newspaper to drive book sales.

## Commercial and Cultural Intermediaries
Intermediaries have played vital roles in the economic and cultural development of various regions. In Antwerp, the city's rise as a great commercial center of the north by the end of the fifteenth century was supported by individuals acting as auxiliaries and intermediaries. These roles included charterers of vessels, forwarding agents, and brokers. Unlike in Bruges, where monopolies existed for money-changers and brokers, these professions were open to all in Antwerp.

On a broader scale, the Persian state often acted as an intermediary for the transmission of ideas and art due to its geographical position. When it functioned as a conquering empire, it overlapped India, Babylon, and Asia Minor. Through this position, Persia transmitted the cult of Mithra to western Europe and sent Greek sculpture to India.

In Belgium, the church's establishment of dioceses that included both Franks and Gallo-Romans—regardless of racial differences—prepared the inhabitants to serve as intermediaries between Teutonic and Latin civilizations. Because bishoprics were mostly located in the Romance section of the country, people from the Teutonic section were required to meet Walloons at their shared religious centers, which diminished racial and national differences and removed the linguistic frontier as a real barrier.

## Political and Religious Intermediaries
In governance and diplomacy, individuals have served as intermediaries to refine communication or facilitate administration. Maret, a secretary of state for the First Consul, served as a useful intermediary for Napoleon. He was noted for his ability to tone down the hard and abrupt language used in Napoleon's communications. Maret also assisted in drafting constitutions for the Italian and Batavian Republics, as well as a constitution for Spain.

In a religious context, the Shaykhis—a sect of extreme Shi'ites—held the doctrine of *Rukn-i-rabi'* ("the fourth support"). This doctrine taught that an intermediary, known as "the perfect Shi'ite," must exist at all times between the faithful followers and the twelfth Imam. This concept was modeled after the four successive *Babs* (or "gates") who served as the sole means of communication between the twelfth Imam and his partisans during the period of "minor occultation" (*Ghaybat-i-sughra*) from A.D. 874 to 940. Mirza 'Ali Muhammad of Shiraz assumed the title *Bab* in this specific sense.

## Perspectives on Intermediation
Not all historical figures viewed intermediaries favorably. In Scotland, Lyndsay expressed a Renaissance-era demand for first-hand impressions, particularly regarding theological matters. He argued that people should receive their lessons directly rather than through intermediaries, whom he claimed understood original texts no better than the "ravyng of the rukis." This belief fueled his persistent advocacy for the use of the vernacular language over the *lingua franca* of Europe.
