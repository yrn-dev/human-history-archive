# Christian Socialists

The Christian Socialists, also referred to as the Lueger party, were a political group in Austria that rose to prominence in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Led by Dr. Lueger, a man of significant oratorical power, the party established a predominant influence in Vienna, a city that had previously been a center of Liberalism.

## Political Growth and Leadership
The influence of Dr. Lueger was particularly strong in Vienna, where he eventually secured a two-thirds majority in the town council and was elected burgomaster. This election faced initial resistance from the emperor, who refused to confirm it and personally appealed to Lueger to withdraw. However, after the 1897 elections demonstrated Lueger's extensive following in the Reichsrath, his election as burgomaster was confirmed upon the advice of Badeni.

The Christian Socialists maintained a contentious relationship with other political factions. There was violent antipathy between the Christian Socialists and the German Nationalists; the transfer of their disputes from the Viennese Council Chamber to the Reichsrath was noted as being detrimental to the orderly conduct of parliamentary debate.

## Electoral Strategy and Franchise Reform
The Christian Socialists generally favored the extension of the franchise. During the consideration of reform measures, they were favorable toward such changes because they hoped to extend their own organization throughout the empire and gain seats at the expense of the German Populists and German Progressives. 

In 1893, a reform bill introduced by Taaffe proposed granting the franchise to anyone who could read and write and had resided in one place for six months. While this was supported by many Christian Socialists and anti-Semites, the bill ultimately failed. Later, in 1896, Count Badeni carried through a franchise reform bill that added a fifth *curia*, allowing almost anyone who had resided in one place for six months (and was not in domestic service) to vote.

## Parliamentary Presence and Alliances
The Christian Socialists' presence in the Reichsrath fluctuated over time. In 1897, the party held 28 seats, and by 1901, they held 23. Following a general election on May 14, 1907, the party experienced a significant increase in its numbers, rising from 27 to 67 seats. To establish the Christian Socialist or Lueger party as the strongest group in parliament, an amalgamation was formed between them and the conservative Catholic party. In a later classification of the Chamber, the Christian Socialists were listed as having 96 members.

The rise of the Christian Socialists signaled a shift in the leadership of the German element in parliament. The Constitutional Landed Proprietors, who had led the German element since the 1860s, viewed the ascent of the Christian Socialists as the end of their own political dominance.

## Ideology and Program
The program of the Christian Socialists contained strong nationalist elements. These were primarily directed against Hungarians and Jews. Dr. Lueger, in particular, distinguished himself through violent attacks on Hungary, which caused embarrassment to the government.
