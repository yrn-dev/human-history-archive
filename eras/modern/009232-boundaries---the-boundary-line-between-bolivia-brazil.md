# Boundaries of Bolivia

Bolivia is an inland republic located in South America. It was formerly known as the province of Charcas, or Upper Peru, and was once part of the Spanish vice-royalty of Peru. Geographically, it extends approximately from 9 deg. 44' to 22 deg. 50' S. latitude and from 58 deg. to 70 deg. W. longitude. It is bounded to the west by Peru and Chile, to the south by Argentina and Paraguay, and to the north and east by Brazil.

## Territorial Area and Disputes
Bolivia is the third largest political division on the South American continent. However, official estimates of its area vary widely, with figures cited at 640,226 and 703,633 sq. m., while Supan (1904) estimates the area at 515,156 sq. m. These discrepancies are attributed to repeated losses of territory resulting from boundary disputes with neighboring states.

## Boundary with Brazil
The boundary between Bolivia and Brazil originated from the limits of Portuguese and Spanish colonies established by the treaties of San Ildefonso (1777) and Madrid (1750). These limits were subsequently modified by treaties in 1867 and 1903.

The boundary line begins at the outlet of Bahia Negra into the Paraguay river (lat. 28 deg. 08' 35" S.). It ascends the river to a point on the west bank 9 kilometres below Fort Coimbra, then moves inland 4 kilometres to lat. 19 deg. 45' 36" S. and long. 58 deg. 04' 12.7" W. From there, it follows an irregular course north and east of north to Lakes Uberaba, Gaiba (or Mandiore), and then up the San Matias river. It continues north along the Sierra Ricardo Franco to the headwaters of the Rio Verde, a tributary of the Guapore. This specific section was turned inland from the Paraguay river to ensure that settlements on the west bank, including Corumba and Fort Coimbra, remained under Brazilian jurisdiction. In 1903, this area was modified by the recession of approximately 1158 sq. m. to Bolivia to improve commercial facilities on the Paraguay.

The line further follows the Guapore, Verde, Mamore, and Madeira rivers down to the mouth of the Abuna (approximately lat. 9 deg. 44' S.), as determined by the 1903 treaty. The original colonial frontier had extended down the Madeira to a point midway between the Amazon and the Beni, then ran due west to the Javary. A 1867 treaty shifted this starting point to the mouth of the Beni (lat. 10 deg. 20' S.) and established a straight line to the source of the Javary, which granted Brazil a large territory.

## The Acre Dispute and Treaty of Petropolis
A significant dispute arose over the rubber-producing territory of Acre. The region was rich in valuable rubber forests, and the majority of those working in the industry were Brazilians who resisted the authority of Bolivian officials. This tension led to a declaration of independence by the state of Acre and the dispatch of Bolivian troops in 1900 to restore order.

President Pando sought an amicable diplomatic settlement to avoid hostilities with Brazil. This was achieved in November 1903 through the Treaty of Petropolis. Under this agreement, Bolivia ceded a portion of the Acre province to Brazil in exchange for a cash indemnity of $10,000,000. The new boundary line was established to ascend the Abuna river to lat. 10 deg. 20' S., then run west along that parallel to the Rapirran river. It follows the Rapirran to its principal source, then due west to the Ituxy river, following that river west to its source. From there, it goes to the source of Bahia Creek, follows it to the Aquiry (or Acre) river, and then up that river to its source.

## Boundary with Chile
Bolivia's relationship with Chile was marked by long-standing disputes and conflict. In 1866, a treaty established the 24th parallel as the boundary of Chilean territory. Bolivia also agreed that Chile would receive full trading facilities and a half share of customs on the coast between the 23rd and 24th parallels, as well as the right to mine and export products from this district without tax. In 1870, Bolivia granted an Anglo-Chilean company the right to work nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel for $10,000.

Following the war of 1878-81, the railways of Bolivia's Litoral department (282 m. long) passed under Chilean control. A subsequent dispute concerned the Chilean occupation of the provinces of Arica and Tacna under the Parto de Tregna of April 4, 1884. Although there were prospects in 1895 for Chile to grant Bolivia an outlet to the sea in exchange for the recognition of Chilean ownership of Tacna and Arica, these talks failed after Chile discovered secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina.

A final settlement was ratified in 1905. Bolivia was compelled to cede the maritime provinces occupied by Chile since 1881, leaving the republic without a seaport. In return, Chile agreed to pay a cash indemnity, provide pecuniary assistance for interior railways, and construct a railway at its own cost from Arica to La Paz.
