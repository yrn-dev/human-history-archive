# London

## Milk Supply and Distribution
Providing a daily supply of milk to a population of five million people is an undertaking of great magnitude in London, particularly because only a minute proportion of the milk is produced within the metropolitan area. To meet this demand, there are approximately 5,000 proper dairies, along with numerous other businesses that sell milk alongside other commodities. The industry employs at least 25,000 people in distribution and related work, with some 12,000 traders engaged in milk-selling. The financial scale of this operation is significant; the paid-up capital of six major retail and distributing dairy companies exceeds one million sterling.

The supply chain relies heavily on the railway system and the use of refrigerators to reduce the temperature of the product, which allows milk to be transported from considerable distances. The primary sources of supply are located in the counties of Bucks, Berks, Cambridge, Dorset, Essex, Gloucester, Hants, Leicester, Notts, Northampton, Oxford, Stafford, Warwick, Wilts, and Derby. 

The logistics of this supply are extensive:
* **Production:** Approximately 4,300 farmers supply London. Based on an average of 28 cows per farm and a yield of 500 gallons per cow annually, the labor required for milking alone is equivalent to 12 battalions of 1,000 men each.
* **Transport:** Some 3,500 horses are used to move milk from farms to railway stations, and roughly 5,700 horses are employed for delivery within London.
* **Railway Volume:** The Great Western line delivers about 11,000,000 gallons annually, the London & North-Western railway delivers 9,000,000 gallons, and the Midland railway at St Pancras delivers 5,000,000 gallons.

At the beginning of the 20th century, the "cowkeeper"—the retailer who produced the milk they sold—began to disappear. This was due to favorable railway rates and improved treatment methods that made distant milk cheaper than milk produced in the metropolis. Consequently, the number of milk cows in the county of London dropped from 10,000 in 1889 to 5,144 in 1900.

The supply is highly sensitive to temperature. A change of ten degrees can inversely affect supply and demand. In extreme cold or heat, cows produce less milk while Londoners demand more. Conversely, a ten-degree fall in summer temperature can lead to an enlarged supply and lessened demand, sometimes resulting in a single firm having 600 gallons returned by carriers in one day. In such instances, cream separators provide invaluable assistance, as large-scale cheese making or setting for cream in London is considered impracticable.

## Aerial Defence and Security
During the period when Germans overran Belgium, London faced a serious threat of air attacks. It was known that the city was within range of Zeppelin sheds located in Cologne and Düsseldorf. At the time, the resources for aerial defence were wholly inadequate; there were no searchlights or anti-aircraft guns, and the Admiralty had only been able to "scrape and smuggle together" naval aeroplanes. 

By September 1914, the responsibility for the aerial defence of Great Britain was undertaken by the Admiralty because the War Office lacked the means to discharge it. While there were concerns that half a dozen Zeppelins might bomb London, military leadership noted that defending the vast area of London with artillery was impossible. It was determined that guns should be limited to points of military value. Consequently, priority for aerial guns and searchlights was given to more vulnerable points such as Portsmouth, Dover, and the Medway, as well as specific targets like magazines, power-houses, lock-gates, oil-tanks, and airship sheds.

## Finance and the Money Market
The money market in London, as described in Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street*, evolved after 1873. A significant development was the increase in the extent to which money is "used up" nightly. By 1888 or 1889, it became usual for bill-brokers to "allow" a rate for "money for the night," occasionally fixing terms independently of the banks.

Much of this "fugitive" money comes from two main sources:
1. Institutions that have recently received public subscriptions for loans or capital.
2. Balances left temporarily with banks or finance houses by foreign governments or other parties awaiting payments in London.

While this money is often cheap, it is not considered "good" in City slang because its availability is uncertain. It may be available for only a few days or, in some cases, for several months. Some less wealthy bill-brokers have used this money profitably for continuous weeks, despite the risk that the borrower will eventually "call" it.

## Industrial Fuel and Transport
In the vicinity of the Thames, several large cement manufacturers and contracting firms adopted petroleum as a fuel. While these adaptations were mechanically successful, they were discontinued when increased demand led to a rise in the price of liquid fuel. Similarly, the London, Brighton & South Coast railway and the Great Eastern railway (which adapted 120 locomotive engines) eventually returned to coal fuel due to short supplies and increasing prices of petroleum.
