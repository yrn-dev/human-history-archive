# Kavirondo Gulf and Regional Railway Infrastructure

## The Uganda Railway
The construction of the railway from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza represented the most significant engineering project undertaken in the protectorate. A preliminary survey for the line was conducted in 1892, with construction beginning in 1896. The route roughly follows the path of a trunk road from Mombasa, passing through Lake Naivasha and Kibwezi in the upper Sabaki basin, before reaching Berkeley Bay on Victoria Nyanza. After reaching the equator, the railway takes a more direct route across the Mau plateau, eventually terminating at Port Florence on the Kavirondo Gulf.

The railway is a mountain line characterized by gradients of one in sixty and one in fifty. It begins at Mombasa, crossing to the mainland via a bridge measuring half a mile in length. The line ascends the plateau to the Kikuyu Escarpment, reaching an altitude of 7,600 ft. above sea level at a distance of 346 m. from the start. From there, it descends across ravines using viaducts to reach the valley floor at 6,011 ft., before ascending the Mau escarpment to its highest point of 8,321 ft. In the final 100 m. of the journey, the altitude drops to 3,738 ft. at the Port Florence station.

## Technical Specifications and Cost
The railway is 584 m. long and utilizes a metre gauge (3.28 ft.). This differs from the 3 ft. 6 in. gauge used for lines in South and Central Africa and the Sudan. The British government funded the construction at a total cost of £5,331,000, which averages to approximately £9,500 per mile. The first locomotive arrived at Victoria Nyanza on December 26, 1901. The permanent way was largely finished by March 1903, coinciding with the resignation of Sir George Whitehouse, the engineer who managed the project from its inception.

## Socio-Economic Impact
The completion of the railway had profound effects on the region. By eliminating the need for the carriage of goods by men, the railway dealt a final death-blow to the slave trade in that portion of East Africa. Furthermore, it enabled the development and continued occupation of Uganda. Prior to the railway, such efforts were nearly impossible due to the prohibitive cost of transporting goods from the coast, which stood at £60 per ton.

## Comparative Railway Gauges in Other Regions
The use of metre gauge is seen in other colonial and national contexts. In Dahomey, a metre gauge railway runs from Kotonu to the Niger. Work began in 1900, reaching Toffo (55 m.) by 1902, Pauignan (120 m. from Kotonu) by 1905, and Paraku (a further 150 m. north) by 1907.

In India, the adoption of the narrow (metre) gauge over large areas was heavily influenced by Yule, the Deputy Consulting Engineer for Railways, as well as Colonels Dickens and R. Strachey. Supporters of the narrow gauge argued that costs were significantly lower, as the rails weighed only about two-thirds of those required for broad gauge. However, practical experience later revealed disadvantages, including lower durability, higher maintenance costs, and reduced carrying capacity. These issues, along with the labor required to shift goods at breaks of gauge and strategic concerns regarding Imperial defence, led to the modification of this policy.

## Other Global Railway Systems
In Denmark, the railway system is fairly complete, with the state owning about three-fifths of the total mileage (approximately 2,000). While the main lines are generally standard gauge (4 ft. 8½ in.), there is also a considerable amount of light narrow-gauge railway mileage.

In other regions, such as the state mentioned in the archive's records regarding the Bombay & Baroda and Rajputana railways, the state has constructed three of its own railways using three different gauges.
