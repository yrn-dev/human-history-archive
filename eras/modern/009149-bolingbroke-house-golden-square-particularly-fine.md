# Lord Bolingbroke

## Political Career and the Treaty of Utrecht
Lord Bolingbroke, also known as St John, was a prominent figure in the Tory administration. In August, after being created Baron St John of Lydiard Tregoze and Viscount Bolingbroke on July 7, he traveled to France to conduct negotiations. This resulted in the signing of a four-month armistice between France and England on the 19th, eventually leading to the Treaty of Utrecht, which was signed by all allies except the emperor on March 31, 1713. 

While the terms granted to England were perhaps not subject to much criticism, the process of peacemaking was characterized by underhand conspiracies and the violation of solemn international assurances, which left a lasting stain on national credit and honor. Bolingbroke is held primarily responsible for these actions, as well as for the abandonment of the Catalans. Additionally, a commercial treaty he established with France to create free trade was rejected in June.

## Relations with Harley and the Court
The Tory administration was based on the friendship between Bolingbroke and Harley, though this relationship gradually dissolved. In March 1711, an attempt on Bolingbroke's life by Guiscard resulted in Harley being wounded instead. By May, Harley was made lord treasurer and obtained the earldom of Oxford. Bolingbroke expressed disappointment in July when he received only his viscountcy rather than an extinct earldom in his family and was passed over for the Garter. Despite an attempt by Swift in September 1713 to reconcile the two men, the rift remained.

## Exile and the Pretender
Upon the accession of George I, Bolingbroke was immediately dismissed from office, though the illuminations and bonfire at his house in Golden Square were noted as being "particularly fine and remarkable." He retired to Bucklebury, where he is said to have written a response to the *Secret History of the White Staff*, which had accused him of Jacobitism. 

In March 1715, Bolingbroke unsuccessfully attempted to defend the previous ministry in the new parliament. Following the announcement that Walpole intended to attack the authors of the Treaty of Utrecht, Bolingbroke fled to Paris in disguise on March 28, 1715. Before leaving, he sent a letter from Dover to Lord Lansdowne protesting his innocence and challenging his enemies to provide evidence of criminal correspondence.

By July, Bolingbroke fully identified with the interests of the Pretender and became his secretary, though he was attainted on September 10. While he drew up James's declaration, the priests cancelled the assurances he had included regarding the security of the Church of England. Bolingbroke advised against the expedition of 1715, but his counsel was ignored in favor of Irish priests and ignorant refugees. Eventually, due to jealousies and petty intrigues, Bolingbroke was dismissed from his office upon the return of James. He subsequently renounced all efforts for the Pretender, telling Mary of Modena that he wished his arm might rot off if he ever used a sword or pen in their service again.

## Literary Legacy and Influence
Bolingbroke is described as a magnificent writer but a bad patriot. His "fine style" was later imitated by Burke in 1756 in a satire titled *A Vindication of Natural Society*. This work, written as a posthumous piece by Bolingbroke, presented a view of the evils and miseries resulting from artificial society. Burke used the piece to reduce to absurdity the political rationalism and deistic school of thought associated with Bolingbroke, which Burke believed could undermine the structure of political government and revealed religion.

## Succession
Following his unfortunate history and wasted genius, Bolingbroke was succeeded in his title by his nephew, Frederick, the 3rd Viscount St John. This succession occurred according to a special remainder; the title had originally been granted to Bolingbroke's father in 1716.
