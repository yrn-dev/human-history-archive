# Ash

The ash is a timber-tree found in various regions globally, including Britain, North America, Europe, and Asia. It is valued for its specific physical properties and its utility in various industrial and agricultural applications.

## Characteristics and Growth in Britain
In Britain, the ash is considered the second most valuable timber-tree, following the oak. Its value is derived primarily from its elasticity and toughness. To ensure the trunk grows free from large side-branches, it is best grown in masses where the soil is of high quality. The tree requires a naturally sheltered location—such as the steep banks of lakes, rivers, or glens—and a good deep loam with a gravelly subsoil; it does not succeed in wet and cold clay.

The ash is easily propagated from seeds and produces strong root shoots. While it requires significant light, it grows rapidly, and its terminal shoots can easily pierce through thickets of beech, a tree with which it is often associated. When mixed with beech, the ash grows well and can reach great girth and height. However, unmixed ash plantations are seldom satisfactory because the foliage fails to sufficiently cover the ground. Additionally, the ash is considered obnoxious as a hedgerow tree because it sends out a dense mass of roots horizontally just beneath the surface, which causes harm to the vegetation beneath its shade.

## Global Distribution
The ash appears in the flora of several different regions:
*   **North America:** In the United States, ash is found in the forests of western Kentucky and is also present in the alluvial regions, stream bottoms of the prairies, and drier portions of the swamps in Louisiana.
*   **Europe:** In Bulgaria, the ash is listed as one of the principal forest trees. In Denmark, the ash was abundant in the islands in ancient times, though it is now rare. However, in Bornholm, the ash is one of the most abundant trees, along with birch and pine.
*   **Asia:** Mountain ash is listed as one of the indigenous trees in certain regions.

## Industrial and Commercial Uses
The timber of the ash is highly utilized for its strength and flexibility. In Britain, coppice shoots are used to produce hoops, crates, whip-handles, and excellent hop-poles. The timber is also widely employed by wheelwrights, coach-builders, and for the creation of agricultural implements.

In the manufacture of carriage wheels, ash is specifically used for the felloes. Furthermore, an American method of constructing wheel rims involves using straight-grained ash (or hickory) bent into the required circle in two sections. This particular method is especially recommended for wheels utilizing indiarubber tires, as these do not require wood felloes as deep as those used for steel tires.
