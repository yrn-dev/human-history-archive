# Montenegro, Rumania and Servia

Montenegro, Rumania, and Servia are political divisions located within the Balkan Peninsula. These regions are characterized by a complex ethnographic history and have been the subject of extensive cartographic surveys by Austrian and Russian officers, as well as the British war office.

## Demographics and Geography
As of 1909, the area and population figures for these three entities were as follows:

*   **Servia**: This region covered an area of 18,782 square miles with a population of 2,493,770.
*   **Montenegro**: This region covered an area of 3,255 square miles with a population of 311,564.
*   **Rumania**: While the total area of the kingdom is not listed in the general summary, the Dobrudja region of Rumania covered 5,896 square miles with a population of 258,242.

The broader Balkan Peninsula is inhabited by various races whose ethnographic limits often do not align with political boundaries. The Slavonic population, which includes the Bulgars and Serbo-Croats, is the most numerous group in the region, exceeding 10,000,000 people. The Turkish population, which includes descendants of 14th and 15th-century Ottoman invaders and later colonists, has declined during the 19th century; it has almost entirely disappeared in Servia.

## Ethnography and Religion
The Serbo-Croats settled in the Balkan Peninsula during the 7th century after migrating from the Carpathians. While there is no valid racial or linguistic difference between the Serbs of the central region and the Croats of the north-west, a strong religious divide emerged over time. 

The Orthodox creed prevailed in the wild, mountainous interior where the Byzantine Church faced little competition. Consequently, Orthodox Serbs inhabit Montenegro, the kingdom of Servia, Herzegovina, parts of Bosnia, and "Old Servia" (comprising north-western Macedonia and Novibazar). These populations have historically been attracted to Russia and maintained constant communication with Servia and Montenegro. Their aspiration to create a great Servian kingdom has occasionally conflicted with loyalty to Austria.

## Political and International Relations
The foreign policy of Austria-Hungary has sought to maintain trade and influence within the Balkan states. This has been pursued through commercial treaties with Servia, Rumania, and Bulgaria, as well as the opening of the Danube for navigation and the construction of railways. However, the growth of Russian influence in Servia, particularly following the abdication of King Milan, has been a source of serious anxiety for the Austro-Hungarian government.

In the case of Rumania, international agreements have focused heavily on the Danube. The Congress of Berlin in 1878 extended the jurisdiction of the European commission from Isakcea to Galatz, granting the commission independence from territorial authority, though Rumania was granted a delegate. Further treaties in 1883 extended this jurisdiction to Braila. Despite these arrangements, certain regulations for navigation and river police between the Iron Gates and Braila were never put into force due to opposition from Rumania.

## Cartography and Documentation
The mapping of these regions has relied heavily on military surveys. The kingdom of Servia was surveyed between 1880 and 1891, with results published on a scale of 1:75,000. In eastern Rumania, surveys began in 1874 (scale 1:50,000), and a general map of the entire kingdom was published between 1906 and 1907 on a scale of 1:200,000. A map of Montenegro, published in Vienna in 1894 on a scale of 1:75,000, was based on surveys conducted by Russian and Austrian officers.
