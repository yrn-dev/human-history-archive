# Discussions of Principal Problems in Letters

The term "Letters" refers to various historical and philosophical correspondences across different disciplines, ranging from the study of psychology and ethics to early ecclesiastical history and the physical sciences.

## Psychology and Ethics in the Letters of Bailey
In the *Letters* (specifically the 3rd series), Bailey addresses several principal problems regarding ethics and psychology. While his general tendency leans toward the empirical school rather than the idealist school, he bases his psychological method entirely on introspection. Bailey rejects the doctrine of mental faculties, arguing that what are commonly called faculties are actually classified phenomena of consciousness or facts. He is also a severe critic of the use of metaphorical language when describing mental operations.

Regarding perception, Bailey posits that the perception of external things via sense organs is a direct mental act or phenomenon of consciousness that cannot be resolved into anything else. While he does not work this doctrine out in detail, he supports it through subtle criticisms of alternative theories.

In the realm of morals, Bailey advocates for utilitarianism, though he finds the term "utility" to be narrow and potentially sordid. He analyzes the formation of "complex" mental facts related to right, obligation, and duty. His moral theory is based on five specific facts:
1. Human susceptibility to pain and pleasure.
2. The liking or disliking of the causes of these feelings.
3. The desire to reciprocate received pain or pleasure.
4. The expectation that others will reciprocate.
5. A degree of sympathy for the same feelings in other people.

## The Epistles of Ignatius
The letters written by Ignatius, the bishop of Antioch and one of the "Apostolic Fathers," serve as the only trustworthy source of information regarding his career. These letters were written to various churches during his final journey from Antioch to Rome. 

The study of these letters presents a complex critical problem known as the "Problem of the Three Recensions," which involves three different versions of the texts:
*   **The Short Recension (Vossian):** This version contains letters to the Romans, Ephesians, Magnesians, Trallians, Philadelphians, Smyrnaeans, and Polycarp. It was derived from the Medicean MS. at Florence and published by Vossius in 1646. Various versions exist in Latin (by Robert Grosseteste, c. 1250), Armenian, Syriac, and Coptic.
*   **The Long Recension:** This version includes the seven letters mentioned above in an expanded form, plus several others. The Greek form contains thirteen letters, adding correspondence to the Tarsians, Philippians, Antiochians, Hero, and Mary of Cassobola, as well as a letter from Mary to Ignatius. The Latin form omits the letter of Mary of Cassobola but adds the *Laus Heronis*, two letters to the apostle John, and one to the Virgin Mary.
*   **The Syriac (Curetonian) Recension:** Produced by William Cureton in 1845 from three Syriac MSS, this version is considerably abbreviated and contains only three epistles: those to the Ephesians, the Romans, and Polycarp.

## Scientific and Philosophical Correspondence
Letters have also been primary vehicles for discussing problems in physics and mathematics. The letters of Descartes, particularly those written during his time in Holland, provide a detailed account of his mental history. The majority of these were addressed to Mersenne and focused on mathematics, musical theory, and physics. Descartes also maintained a philosophical correspondence with Princess Elizabeth of the Palatine, to whom he dedicated the *Principles of Philosophy*. His correspondents also included churchmen, Jesuits, professors, and Dutch friends such as Hoogheland and Constantijn Huygens.
