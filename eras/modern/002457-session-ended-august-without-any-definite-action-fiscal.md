# The Fiscal Question and the Balfour Government

The fiscal question was a central point of contention within the Unionist party during the early 20th century, leading to significant cabinet instability and eventually foreshadowing the end of the government under Prime Minister Mr. Balfour.

## Cabinet Divisions and Resignations
A parliamentary session ended in August without any definite action being taken on the fiscal question, although discussions continued within the cabinet. On September 16, Mr. Balfour published a pamphlet titled "Insular Free Trade." Shortly thereafter, on September 18, it was announced that Mr. Ritchie and Lord George Hamilton had resigned. They were followed a day or two later by Mr. Arthur Elliot and Lord Balfour of Burleigh. These individuals were identified as the "strait free-traders." Simultaneously, Mr. Chamberlain also resigned.

Correspondence published between Mr. Balfour and Mr. Chamberlain (dated September 9 and 16) indicated that Balfour was a sympathizer with retaliatory duties and some form of fiscal union with the colonies, provided it was practicable. However, Balfour was unwilling to support Mr. Chamberlain's specific scheme because he did not believe the country was ready to agree to the taxation of food required for a preferential tariff. Despite this, Balfour encouraged Mr. Chamberlain to use "missionary efforts" outside the government to test and convert public feeling. For his part, Mr. Chamberlain emphasized his parliamentary loyalty to the Prime Minister.

## The "Insular Free Trade" Manifesto
In his pamphlet, the Prime Minister reviewed economic history since the time of Cobden. He argued that the promises of early free-traders had been falsified and noted that England remained the only country that allowed free imports. Balfour asserted that he remained in harmony with the "true spirit of free-trade" by pleading for the "freedom to negotiate" to increase the freedom of exchange.

This manifesto was initially viewed as the government's platform and the point of dissent for the resigning free-trade members. However, the situation became confused by personal distrust and suspicion. Lord George Hamilton stated that Mr. Balfour had circulated a second, different document among colleagues that was in fuller agreement with Mr. Chamberlain. Despite this confusion, the duke of Devonshire remained in the cabinet, and his orthodoxy was seen as typical.

## The Struggle of 1903
Throughout the remainder of 1903, the struggle within the Unionist party persisted. Mr. Chamberlain campaigned across the country for a budget reorganization that included more taxes on imports, including food, with the goal of stimulating employment and improving the position of the working classes.

In opposition, the free-trade Unionists—represented chiefly by Lord James, Lord Goschen, Lord Hugh Cecil, and the duke of Devonshire—established a Free Food league to counter Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Reform league. At a meeting at Queen's Hall in London on November 24, the free-traders rejected food taxes, discredited Chamberlain's statistics, and repudiated Balfour's assumption that retaliation would be desirable.

By December, Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Commission was appointed. Both factions fought to capture the party and the Prime Minister. Balfour's "dialectical dexterity" in avoiding a definite expression of his fiscal opinions drew contemptuous criticism from the Liberal press. Balfour maintained that no definite action could be taken until the next parliament. While he did not discourage Chamberlain's campaign, he declined to go "the whole hog" with him.

## The Session of 1905 and Government Collapse
The events of the 1905 session foreshadowed the government's end. The opposition sought to force debates on the fiscal question in the House of Commons, while Mr. Balfour attempted to avoid these "traps." This avoidance peaked when Balfour and his supporters left the House, allowing a motion hostile to tariff reform to pass *nem. con*.

While the government successfully passed the Aliens Bill, the Unemployed Bill, and the Scottish Churches Bill, it suffered a "complete fiasco" regarding redistribution proposals, which were withdrawn due to a procedural blunder. Although a party meeting at the foreign office on July 17 resulted in verbal assurances of loyalty, the government was caught in a minority of four on the estimates for the Irish Land Commission two days later. Despite the uncertainty of whether to dissolve or resign, the government initially decided to hold on.
