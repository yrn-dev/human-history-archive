# The Baltic Sea

The Baltic is a body of water characterized by a large drainage area, which Sir J. Murray measured at 461,450 sq. sea m. (611,700 sq. English m.). Its drainage system includes the Neva river-basin in the east, as well as the Oder and Vistula in the south. In western Finland and Sweden, the drainage system is marked by narrow parallel troughs running at right angles to the coast.

## Surface Levels and Water Dynamics
The mean surface level of the Baltic varies depending on the point of measurement. Levellings from Swinemuende indicate that the surface is 0.066 metres (.216 ft.) below the level at Ostend and 0.093 metres (.305 ft.) below the surface of the North Sea at Amsterdam. Furthermore, measurements from Swinemuende through Eger to the Adriatic show the Baltic's mean surface level to be 0.499 metres (1.6 ft.) above that of the Adriatic Sea.

There is a notable variation in level across the sea itself; the mean surface rises approximately 0.5 metres (1.6 ft.) from the coast of Holstein to Memel. This is likely caused by prevailing westerly winds. When westerly winds are strong, this difference is exceeded, while easterly winds can cause the difference to disappear or reverse. Navigation in the Baltic is often challenged by short, irregular waves and destructive waves known as *Seebaren* (bores), which are likely caused by distant earthquakes.

## Bathymetry and Topography
The Baltic proper consists of a series of troughs and hollows. East of a plateau, the first is the Bornholm deep, located east of the island of Bornholm. This is separated from the Gotland deep by the Middelbank. To the northeast, the Gotland basin serves as the largest and deepest of all the basins, extending toward the Gulf of Finland. To the southeast of the Middelbank lies an isolated depression called the Danziger Tiefe.

Along the coast of Sweden, a deep channel runs northward from outside the island of Oland. This channel is cut off to the east and south by a bank that extends northward and eastward from near Karlskrona—the bank upon which the island of Gotland is situated. This channel connects to the Gotland deep at its northern end; the deepest hole in the Baltic, measuring 420 metres (230 fathoms), is located near the junction opposite Landsort.

## Geological History
The Baltic has undergone significant transformations due to glacial subsidence and upheaval. During the last great subsidence of glacial times, an arm of the sea submerged much of the littoral up to the Gulf of Bothnia, including the present-day lakes Malar, Hjelmar, and Vener. At this time, the northern Baltic was salty enough for oysters.

Subsequent upheaval restricted communication with the open sea to the Danish channels, causing the waters to become fresher and the oysters to disappear, though seals and various crustaceans and cold salt-water fishes became acclimatized. Further elevation transformed the area into a vast freshwater lake known as the *Ancylus* lake (named for the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*), which covered approximately 220,000 sq. m., including Lake Ladoga, a large part of Finland, and the current Baltic area.

A later subsidence re-established communication through the Danish channels, allowing the water to become salty enough for *Littorina littorea* and *Cardium edule*. During this epoch, the Gulf of Bothnia suffered greater depression than the Baltic proper, with deposits reaching 100 metres (328 ft.) near Hernosand compared to 25 metres (82 ft.) near Gotland. The process of elevation that gave the Baltic its current form is believed to be continuing. Dr. R. Sieger's isobasic lines indicate that while movement is nearly nil along the Gulf of Finland and the axial lines of the Baltic, it increases northwards toward the Gulf of Bothnia and toward the main ridge of the southern Sweden *massif*.

## Water Circulation and Salinity
Water circulation in the Baltic proper is analyzed separately from the channels connecting it to the North Sea, using the plateau between the coast of Mecklenburg and Ruegen and the islands of Moen and Falster as a dividing line. In the hollows and basins from Ruegen to the Gulfs of Finland and Bothnia, the upper layers of water (30 to 70 metres thick) maintain nearly the same salinity, with vertical circulation driven by convection currents.

Below these layers lie stagnant masses of salter water that are surcharged with carbonic acid and deficient in oxygen. These salter waters originated from outside the Baltic, passing over the plateau between Mecklenburg and Falster. Their horizontal movement is restricted by ridges that separate the deep hollows. The inflow into these deep basins is intermittent, characterized by long periods of reflux and flux.
