# Thomas Ball and the Lincoln Emancipation Group

## Overview of Thomas Ball
Thomas Ball (1819–) was an American sculptor born on June 3, 1819, in Charlestown, Massachusetts. The son of a house-and-sign-painter, Ball began his artistic career as a self-taught portrait painter. In 1851, he shifted his focus to sculpture, producing a bust of Jenny Lind as his earliest work. 

At the age of thirty-five, Ball traveled to Florence for study. He remained there for over thirty years, though his time in Italy was interrupted by a period of work in Boston, Massachusetts, between 1857 and 1865. While in Florence, he was part of an artistic colony that included Hiram Powers and the Brownings. Ball returned to the United States in 1897, establishing a studio in New York City and residing in Montclair, New Jersey. In 1891, he published an autobiographical work titled *My Three Score Years and Ten*.

## Major Sculptural Works
Ball's body of work includes early cabinet busts of musicians, a reflection of his own skills as an accomplished musician; he was the first person in America to sing "Elijah." His later career was defined by significant monumental art, which had a marked influence on the United States, particularly in New England.

His notable public works include:
*   **The Lincoln Emancipation group**: Located at Washington.
*   **Equestrian statue of Washington**: Situated in the Boston public gardens, this is considered probably his best work.
*   **Josiah Quincy**: Located in City Hall Square, Boston.
*   **Charles Sumner**: Located in the public gardens of Boston.
*   **Daniel Webster**: Located in Central Park, New York City.
*   **Washington monument**: Located in Methuen, Massachusetts.

## Edwin Forrest as "Coriolanus"
Among Ball's contributions to the arts was the sculpture of Edwin Forrest as "Coriolanus," which is located in the Actors' Home in Philadelphia. Edwin Forrest was a prominent figure in the American theater, though his performances were later compared to those of Edwin Booth. While some pronounced Booth's interpretations of Richelieu, Richard, and Hamlet to be superior to those of Forrest, the two were contemporaries in the field of tragedy. John Edward McCullough, an American actor born in Ireland, played second roles in tragedies in support of both Edwin Booth and Edwin Forrest; upon his death, Forrest left his prompt books to McCullough by will. Additionally, the actor Lawrence Barrett wrote a life of Edwin Forrest, published in the *American Actors Series* in Boston in 1881.
