# The Wekerle Ministry and the Hungarian Political Crisis (1905–1907)

The political landscape of the Dual Monarchy during the early 20th century was defined by a critical struggle between two opposing principles: Habsburg imperialism, which sought to maintain the military and economic unity of the Dual Monarchy, and Magyar nationalism, which aimed to maintain ascendancy within an independent Hungary.

## The Deadlock and the Fejervary Administration
Between January 1905 and April 1906, a deadlock occurred between the crown and Hungary, as well as between Hungary and Austria. The Coalition, despite holding a majority in the Chamber, refused to take office unless the crown granted specific demands, including customs separation from Austria and the use of Magyar words of command. The crown declined these concessions, viewing them as threats to the dual system established in 1867.

Consequently, the Tisza cabinet remained in power until June 1905, when it was replaced by a non-parliamentary administration led by General Baron Fejervary, the former minister for national defence. Because the Coalition would not accept the crown's terms, Fejervary implemented a scheme drafted by the minister of the interior, Mr. Joseph Kristoffy. This plan proposed that the dispute between the crown and the Coalition be settled through a radical reform of the franchise in Hungary, subjecting the conflict to the test of universal suffrage.

This proposal alarmed the Coalition. The political power in Hungary was concentrated in the hands of the Magyar nobility and gentry; universal suffrage threatened not only their hegemony but also the supremacy of the Magyar race by potentially granting political equality to non-Magyars.

## The Transition to the Wekerle Ministry
The Coalition did not yield immediately, but the situation changed after the Chamber was dissolved by military force on February 19, 1906. Facing a potential open breach of the constitution, and influenced by a depression in trade and the poor state of public finances, the Coalition came to terms with the crown. Their primary motivation was to avoid the menace of universal suffrage or to ensure its introduction in a form that would safeguard Magyar supremacy.

On April 8, 1906, a pact was concluded between the crown and the Coalition. The terms of these "capitulations" included:
* The suspension of all military questions until after universal suffrage was introduced.
* The voting of the normal contingent of recruits and estimates for 1905 and 1906.
* The voting of extraordinary military credits by the Hungarian Chamber.
* The ratification of commercial treaties concluded by Tisza.
* The election of the Quota-Deputation and the Hungarian Delegation.
* The introduction of suffrage reform at least as extensive as the Kristoffy scheme.

Following this agreement, the Wekerle ministry succeeded the administration of Baron Fejervary on April 9, 1906.

## Instability and the Agreement of 1907
Despite the transition, the Wekerle ministry contained elements that rendered a lasting compromise impossible. The ministry was pledged to franchise reform—a change made inevitable by the granting of universal suffrage in the Austrian half of the Monarchy—yet the ministry showed no eagerness to fulfill this pledge. Furthermore, the "burning question" of the Magyar word of command remained unsettled, except for the fixed determination of the king-emperor.

The relationship between the two halves of the monarchy remained critical. While an agreement was eventually reached in 1907, it served only as a truce. Key issues remained unresolved, including the renewal of the charter of the Austro-Hungarian State Bank.

## Economic Tensions and the Bank Charter
The issue of the state bank became a focal point of contention. In March 1908, Mr. Hallo proposed to the Hungarian parliament that the charter of the Austro-Hungarian Bank, set to expire at the end of 1910, should not be renewed. He suggested that if a convention between the banks of Austria and Hungary could not be negotiated, a separate Hungarian Bank should be established. This matter became acute again in the spring of 1909, causing sharp divisions of opinion within the Coalition cabinet.
