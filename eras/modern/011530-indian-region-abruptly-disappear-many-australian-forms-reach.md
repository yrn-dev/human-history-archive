# The Indian and Australian Zoological Regions

The distribution of animal and plant life across Asia and the adjacent islands is divided into distinct zoological and botanical provinces. Among these, the Indian region and the Australian region are separated by a boundary known as Wallace's line. This line marks a significant transition in the types of fauna present, particularly regarding mammals and birds.

## The Indian Region
The Indian region encompasses the south-eastern portion of Asia and includes the adjacent islands of Borneo, Java, Sumatra, and the Philippines. Botanically, this region extends into southern China, where approximately three-fifths of the species in Hong Kong are common to the Indian region. The area is characterized by high temperatures and heavy periodical summer rains.

The assemblage of birds in the Indian region is one of the most varied and richest globally, surpassed only by tropical America. Nearly every order is well represented, with the exception of the Struthiones (ostriches). Highly characteristic bird families of this region include the pheasants (_Phasianidae_), barbets (_Megalaeminae_), and vultures (_Vulturidae_), as well as the bulbuls (_Ixidae$), specifically the sub-group _Phyllornithinae_. The family _Eurylaemidae_ is entirely confined to this part of Asia. Other peculiar genera include _Buceros_, _Harpactes_, _Lophophorus_, _Euplocamus_, _Pajo_, and _Ceriornis_, along with specific crows such as _Dendrocitta_, _Urocissa_, and _Cissa_.

## The Australian Region
The Australian region includes Papua, Celebes, and other islands located east of Java and beyond Wallace's line. This region is characterized by the presence of mammals of the Marsupial order. In the Papuan Subregion, "doves" (forms entitled to the name) reach a high level of development; more than a quarter of all known species (some 300) are found there. This abundance is suggested to be a result of the absence of fruit-eating and forest-haunting mammals, which typically destroy eggs.

## The Boundary at Wallace's Line
The transition between the Indian and Australian regions is sharply defined. The margin of the Australian region is reached at the Molucca Islands and Celebes, where Marsupials occur. Conversely, no Marsupials have been found in the Indian region or in the islands of Borneo and Java, which lie to the west of Wallace's line.

This boundary is equally evident in the distribution of birds; those of the Indian region abruptly disappear at the line, while many Australian forms reach the line but do not pass it. Certain bird groups prevalent in the Indian region are entirely wanting in the Australian region, such as the bulbuls, barbets, vultures, and pheasants. Woodpeckers (_Picidae_) are weakly represented across the boundary, with only a few species out of 400 crossing into the Moluccas, Celebes, or Lombok.

Other mammalian distinctions exist at this boundary. Ruminants, Insectivora, Carnivora, and Quadrumana abound in the western part of the Archipelago but are absent from Celebes and the Moluccas. Furthermore, the genus _Sus_ reaches its eastern limit in New Guinea, and deer do not extend into that island.

## Regional Overlaps and Anomalies
While the primary regions are distinct, there are notable exceptions and overlaps:

*   **Celebes:** This island contains the singular ruminant _Anoa_ and a peculiar form of baboon, _Cynopithecus_. These animals, along with certain birds, appear to have no relation to Asiatic animals and are instead allied to those found in Africa.
*   **The Ethiopian Region:** The extreme south-west of the Asian continent—including southern Persia, Palestine, and Arabia, and extending to Sind and Baluchistan—belongs to the Ethiopian region. This area is characterized by hot, dry deserts and life forms that closely approach those of Africa. Ethiopian types, such as the hunting leopard and the lion, extend into western India. In birds, this influence is seen in the prevalence of stone chats and larks.
*   **The Palaearctic Region:** The northern portion of Asia, extending as far south as the Himalaya, is zoologically similar to Europe. This area, along with the strip of Africa north of the Atlas, forms the Palaearctic region. In Syria, Afghanistan, and Persia, the Europaeo-Asiatic fauna mingles with Indian and African forms.
