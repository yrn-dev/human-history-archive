# Institutions, Laws and Ceremonies of the Order of the Garter

The *Institutions, Laws and Ceremonies of the Order of the Garter* is a scholarly work published in 1672 by the English antiquarian Elias Ashmole. The text is described as being practically exhaustive and serves as an example of Ashmole's years of patient antiquarian research and diligence.

## Author and Context
Elias Ashmole (1617–1692) was born in Lichfield to a saddler and pursued a varied career as a solicitor and a commissioner of excise. Through his association with Captain George Wharton at Oxford, Ashmole obtained a king's commission as comptroller of the ordnance and captain of horse. Ashmole's intellectual interests evolved over time; while he was initially interested in astrology—influenced by Wharton and William Lilly—his focus eventually shifted toward antiquarian research and heraldry.

Ashmole held several high-ranking court positions, including accountant-general of excise, commissioner for Surinam, comptroller of the White Office, and Windsor herald. Although he was offered the office of Garter king-at-arms, he refused the position in favor of Sir William Dugdale, whom he married in 1668.

## Content and Contributions
In his work on the Order of the Garter, Ashmole provides detailed observations on the nature of knighthood and the origins of the order.

### Ensigns of Knighthood
Ashmole identifies the principal ensigns of knighthood, both ancient and modern, as consisting of the following items:
* A horse
* A gold ring
* A shield and lance
* A belt and sword
* Gilt spurs
* A gold chain or collar

### Origins of the Order
Ashmole presents a specific account regarding the institution of the Order of the Garter. He asserts that King Edward III used his own garter as a signal for a battle that ended in victory (which is conceived to be the battle of Crécy). Following this success, the King instituted the order, adopting the garter as the preeminent ensign and a symbol of society and unity.

## The Order of the Garter
The Order of the Garter is recognized as the oldest and most illustrious of the eight orders of knighthood in the United Kingdom. It was instituted in the middle of the 14th century during a period of magnificent court life and bright English chivalry.

While the exact year of its founding is debated due to the loss of original records prior to 1416, evidence from treasury and wardrobe accounts indicates the order was fully constituted by the autumn of 1348. Specifically, records from September 1347 to January 1349 note the issue of habits with garters and embroidered mottoes for St George's Day, and November 1348 records show the prince of Wales gifting twenty-four garters to the knights of the Society of the Garter. However, research by Sir Harris Nicolas suggests the order could not have existed before the summer of 1346, as the prince of Wales and other original companions were not knighted until the middle of that year.
