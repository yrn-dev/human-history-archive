# Political Aspirations of Bohemians and Galicians

In the history of the Habsburg Empire, the Bohemians and Galicians represented Slavic populations whose political aspirations were frequently at odds with the imperial administration and the dominant German and Magyar influences. Their efforts to obtain autonomy or independence were shaped by the constitutional arrangements of the empire and the shifting alliances within its parliament.

## The Impact of Dualism
The establishment of the principle of dualism divided the empire into two primary portions: one ruled by the Germans and the other by the Magyars. While this settlement granted the Hungarians nearly all they demanded—including more than an equal share of power despite paying less than one-third of common expenses—it had a restrictive effect on other ethnic groups. Specifically, the settlement deprived the Bohemians and Galicians of any hope of obtaining a similar level of independence. Under this political framework, the Slav races, including the Poles, Czechs, Slovenes, Serbs, and Croatians, were placed in a position of political inferiority.

## Nationalist Movements in Bohemia and Galicia
By 1868, nationalist movements gained significant ground in both Bohemia and Galicia, leading to direct challenges against the existing constitution.

In Galicia, the Polish population sought to increase the powers of the Galician diet. The "extreme party," led by Smolka, desired to imitate the Czechs by refusing to attend the Reichsrath in Vienna. While the extreme party was outvoted, a general declaration was formed stating that members from Galicia should cease attending the Reichsrath for matters that the Galician diet was qualified to handle. The Poles threatened to leave the Reichsrath entirely if these demands were not met.

In Bohemia, the Czechs were equally active. In 1868, they presented a "declaration" to the diet at Prague, which served as an official statement of their claims. Their demands included:
* The full restoration of the Bohemian kingdom.
* A separate settlement with Bohemia similar to the one granted to Hungary.
* The assertion that no foreign assembly was qualified to impose taxes in Bohemia.
* The claim that the diet was not qualified to elect representatives to Vienna.

This declaration was signed by eighty-one members, including bishops and many feudal nobles. The resulting agitation led to serious riots and the government's decree of exceptional laws.

## Parliamentary Conflict and the Reichsrath
The struggle for representation continued within the Reichsrath. The influence of the Poles was initially sufficient to secure concessions for the Federalists, as the Poles, Slovenes, Dalmatians, and Tirolese often acted in concert. This pressure prevented German Liberals from introducing direct elections to the Reichsrath.

However, tensions peaked in March 1870 when new Galician demands were definitively rejected. In response, the Polish club, along with the Slovenes and Tirolese, left the House. This left the parliament consisting only of Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia, a situation that made governing nearly impossible.

Efforts at reconciliation were attempted by Count Potocki and Count Taaffe. Potocki, serving as minister-president, traveled to Prague and summoned Rieger and Thun to Vienna to persuade the Czechs to accept the constitution. These attempts failed in despair, as the Czechs and Feudals remained committed to the declaration of 1868 and refused any compromise.

## Resistance and the Bohemian Diet
During a period of crisis involving the war between Germany and France in July, the emperor dissolved the Reichsrath and all diets to avoid alienating either the Germans or the Slavs. In Bohemia, the Czechs—supported by Clericals and Feudals—won a large majority in the subsequent elections. Upon taking their seats, the Czechs declared that they did not view the diet as the legal representative of the Bohemian kingdom, but rather as an informal assembly. Consequently, they refused to elect delegates for the Reichsrath, leading the Germans to leave the diet in turn.

## Historical Context of Bohemian Autonomy
The desire for Bohemian independence had deep historical roots. In the 15th century, George of Podebrad attempted to found a national dynasty and later offered the succession to Vladislav of Poland to secure aid against King Matthias of Hungary. Following the election of Vladislav in 1471, a treaty at Olmutz in 1478 eventually secured Bohemia to him, though Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia remained with Matthias for his lifetime.

Later, under the Habsburgs, the relationship between the crown and the Bohemian estates remained contentious. During the reign of Maximilian, the Bohemians used requests for warfare funds as leverage to demand religious concessions, specifically asking that the "Compacts"—which had granted limited freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists—cease to be part of the laws of the country. By the end of 1849, all constitutional government in Bohemia had ceased, and a period of severe reaction followed, during which attempts to obtain liberty and self-government were suppressed and the national language was targeted for extermination in favor of German.
