# Furness

Furness is a district of Lancashire, England, covering an area of approximately 250 square miles. It is geographically separated from the primary portion of Lancashire by Morecambe Bay. The district is bounded to the south and east by this inlet of the Irish Sea, to the southwest by the sea, to the west by Cumberland and the Duddon estuary, and to the north and east by Westmorland. Administratively, it comprises the majority of the North Lonsdale parliamentary division of Lancashire and includes the parliamentary borough of Barrow-in-Furness.

## Geography and Topography
The surface of Furness is almost entirely hilly. The northern half of the district is part of the Lake District and features eminences such as Wetherlam and the Old Man of Coniston. The principal rivers in the region are the Crake and the Leven, which flow southward into a shared estuary in Morecambe Bay; the Crake drains Coniston Lake, while the Leven drains Windermere. The Duddon estuary forms part of the western boundary.

Between Morecambe Bay and the Duddon lies Walney Island, which is 8 miles in length. Several smaller islands are located in the shallow strait separating Walney from the mainland. Because of the prominence of the Lake District, the name "Furness" is often associated in common thought with the area south of the Lakes. This is reflected in local place-names such as Broughton-in-Furness, Dalton-in-Furness, and Barrow-in-Furness.

## Iron Ore and Industrial Development
The peninsula situated between the Duddon estuary, Morecambe Bay, and the Leven estuary is rich in hematite iron ore. This resource was worked from very early times and was known and smelted by the Romans, the British, and the monks of Conishead Priory and Furness Abbey.

The presence of this ore led to the growth of the town of Barrow during the 19th century. Initially, Barrow served as a port for exporting ore to the midlands and South Wales. By 1847, exports had reached some 50,000 tons, though the population of the town at that time was only 325. The industrialization of the area accelerated with the opening of the first section of the Furness railway in 1846, which connected Barrow to the mines near Dalton.

The establishment of iron works by Messrs Schneider & Hannay in 1859, followed by the Barrow Haematite Steel Company in 1866, allowed for the production of steel using the Bessemer process. This specific process requires non-phosphoric ore, such as the hematite found in Furness. While hematite is also worked at Dalton, Askam, and Ulverston, local furnaces have eventually come to depend partly on ore imported from Spain. To facilitate the working of ore believed to extend under the sands of the Duddon estuary, a sea wall was constructed.

## Barrow-in-Furness
Barrow-in-Furness is a municipal, county, and parliamentary borough located on the seaward side of the hammer-shaped peninsula. It is situated 264-1/2 miles north-west by north from London. The population grew from 51,712 in 1891 to 57,586 in 1901.

The town is separated from Walney Island by a narrow channel. In 1904, the War Office undertook the construction of a strong fort on Walney, and a bridge connecting the island to the mainland was started in 1905. Within the channel lies Barrow Island, which was connected to the mainland through reclamation efforts that left only a narrow channel, which was then used for docks.

While the town's rise was primarily due to the hematite iron ore, other industries developed. Shipbuilding eventually surpassed steel works in importance, notably through the yard of Vickers, Sons & Maxim, which constructs various vessels, including vessels of war. Other local industries include engineering and wagon works, jute works, a paper-pulp factory, and a petroleum storage establishment.
