# The Days of the Week

The days of the week serve as the fundamental units for organizing calendars, religious observances, and historical chronologies. In the English language, the names of these days are derived from Saxon origins. The ancient Saxons adopted the week from an Eastern nation and replaced the names of Greek gods with their own divinities. These names correspond to Latin designations: Sunday (*Dies Solis*) is the Sun's day; Monday (*Dies Lunae*) is the Moon's day; Tuesday (*Dies Martis*) is Tiw's day; Wednesday (*Dies Mercurii*) is Woden's day; Thursday (*Dies Jovis*) is Thor's day; Friday (*Dies Veneris*) is Frigg's day; and Saturday (*Dies Saturni*) is Seterne's day. Despite the use of English names, Latin names are still retained for justiciary and legislative acts.

## Calendar Construction and the Dominical Letter

Connecting the week to the year is a primary challenge in calendar construction. Because the number of days in a year and the number of days in a week are prime to one another, successive years cannot begin on the same day. For instance, if a common year begins on a Sunday, the following year begins on a Monday; if a leap year begins on a Sunday, the following year begins on a Tuesday.

To manage this, the first seven letters of the alphabet (A through G) are used to denote the days of the week. These letters are placed beside the days of the year, starting with A on January 1st and cycling through G on January 7th. If Sunday is represented by a specific letter—such as E—then Monday is F, Tuesday is G, and Wednesday is A. The specific letter that represents Sunday for a given year is known as the Sunday Letter, or the Dominical Letter. Once this letter is identified, the letters for all other days of the week are established.

## Religious and Monastic Observances

The days of the week dictate specific rhythms of prayer and diet within various religious traditions. In the Catholic Breviary, the Psalter serves as the groundwork. Psalms 1 through 108 are recited at Matins, with twelve psalms recited each day from Monday to Saturday, and eighteen recited on Sunday. Omissions from this sequence are recited at Prime, Compline, and Lauds. Additionally, Psalms 148 through 150 are always used at Lauds.

Certain monastic orders have historically implemented strict dietary and liturgical schedules based on the day of the week to combat pride and avarice. In one such tradition, monks and lay brothers followed these rules:
*   **Sundays and Thursdays:** Cheese and eggs may be eaten. Mass is said on Sundays and festivals.
*   **Tuesdays and Saturdays:** Cooked vegetables are eaten.
*   **Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays:** Only bread and water are consumed.

These monks lived in separate small houses, performing manual labor and reading, and assembling in church specifically for Vespers and Matins.

## Historical and Cultural Events

The days of the week often mark critical turning points in military and political history. During the end of the Great War in Germany, a republic was declared in Berlin on Saturday the 9th. On Sunday the 10th, the Emperor sought refuge in Holland after leaving Army Headquarters at Spa. Following the acceptance of terms on the night of Sunday the 10th of November, the armistice was signed at 5 o'clock on the morning of Monday, 11th November.

Military operations have also been timed by the week. At Anzac, three-fifths of the force embarked on transports on a Saturday night, with the remainder embarking on Sunday night, and the final transports moving away by 5:30 a.m. on Monday morning. In other engagements, the 3rd Brigade was withdrawn on a Saturday, followed by the 2nd Brigade on Sunday evening, before the latter was ordered back to the line on Monday. In another instance, torrential rain from Saturday night through Sunday night aided a retreat by swelling the Isonzo and Tagliamento rivers and grounding enemy aeroplanes, a benefit that became clear when Monday morning broke with sunshine.

Politically, the onset of war has been marked by rapid shifts over a weekend. In one instance, a Cabinet meeting on Sunday morning occurred during the German violation of Luxemburg, followed by an ultimatum to Belgium that evening. By Monday, the majority of Mr. Asquith's colleagues viewed war as inevitable.

Culturally, the days of the week govern traditional celebrations. The Horn Dance, a medieval dance in Abbots Bromley, Staffordshire, involves men wearing deer skulls with antlers. While it originally took place on a Sunday, it is now typically celebrated on the Monday after "Wakes Sunday" (the Sunday following September 4th).
