# Turkey in European and Global Diplomacy

The geopolitical position of Turkey, particularly during the 18th and early 20th centuries, was characterized by its complex relationships with the Great Powers, its internal struggles for reform, and its role in the volatile Balkan region.

## Relations with Austria and Russia
Historically, Austria's policy toward the Ottoman power was shaped by its fear of Russian expansion. In the late 18th century, the ambition of Catherine of Russia to advance the empire of the tsars to the Black Sea and south of the Danube posed a more serious menace to Austria than the "decadent Ottoman power." Consequently, in July 1771, Kaunitz concluded a defensive alliance with the Porte. However, this alignment was inconsistent; Austria later supported Russian policy at Constantinople and acquired Bukovina from Turkey in 1777.

By the 19th century, Austria remained opposed to the creation of new Slav states on its southern frontier, fearing the influence of Russian agents and the Pan-Slav party. During the disturbances in the Balkans in the 1870s, Austria faced a political dilemma: while the Slav inhabitants of the Austrian empire sympathized with Christians in Turkey, the Hungarians—whose memories of early Turkish conquests had been erased by the events of 1848—were sympathetic to the Turks.

## The Balkan Insurrections and the Treaty of Berlin
In 1875, Andrassy drafted a note, accepted by the powers, requiring Turkey to institute reforms for the good government of its provinces. Although Turkey agreed, insurgents demanded a guarantee from the Powers that Turkey would keep her engagements. This guarantee was not given, leading to continued rebellion that spread to Bulgaria.

Following the advance of the Russian army on Constantinople, the Treaty of San Stefano (March 3, 1878) created an autonomous Bulgarian principality covering three-fifths of the Balkan Peninsula with 4,000,000 inhabitants. Fearing this would become a Russian dependency, the Great Powers intervened via the Treaty of Berlin (July 13, 1878). This treaty divided "Big Bulgaria" into three parts: the principality of Bulgaria, the autonomous province of Eastern Rumelia, and a portion including most of Macedonia and part of the vilayet of Adrianople, which remained under Turkish administration. While it was stipulated that laws similar to the organic law of Crete be introduced in European Turkey, the Porte never carried out this engagement.

## Internal Politics and the Young Turks
In the early 20th century, Turkey was divided between pro-German and pro-Entente parties. The "Young Turks," including leaders such as Djavid, Talaat, Halil, and Enver Pasha, sought to reform and revive their country. In 1911, following an attack by Italy on Turkey's Tripoli Province, Djavid Bey wrote to Winston Churchill suggesting that the attack by a Triple Alliance power provided an opportunity to establish a permanent alliance between England and Turkey.

Despite the "assiduous courting" of Turkey by Germany and the German condonation of Turkey's "most atrocious actions," many Turks—both old and young—initially welcomed a British alliance due to Britain's perceived disinterested course of action.

## Turkey and the First World War
At the outbreak of the First World War, Britain, France, and Russia offered to guarantee the integrity of the Turkish Empire in exchange for faithful neutrality. However, British policy was complicated by domestic pressures; the British Press and a Liberal House of Commons often denounced the Turk in unmeasured terms.

Strategically, Britain was concerned with naval assets. Two Turkish battleships were being built in Great Britain. One, the *Reshadieh*, was complete on the Tyne. Fearing the ships would fall into "bad hands" and be used against them, the British government placed military guards on the *Reshadieh* to prevent the Turkish crew from boarding and hoisting their flag.

As the war progressed, the "German grip on Turkey" tightened. British officials noted that Turkey's repeated breaches of neutrality and hostility tied the Mediterranean Fleet to the Dardanelles. This led some British leaders to suggest that the Allies should stop prioritizing Turkish integrity and instead support the Christian States of the Balkans, specifically allowing Bulgarians to regain territory lost in the second Balkan War, such as Adrianople.

## Post-War Settlements
In subsequent diplomatic arrangements, Germany recognized the British Protectorate over Egypt (declared December 18, 1914) and consented to the transfer of powers previously held by the Sultan of Turkey regarding the free navigation of the Suez Canal to Great Britain. Additionally, Germany accepted the arrangements made by the Allied and Associated Powers with Turkey and Bulgaria regarding German rights and interests in those countries.
