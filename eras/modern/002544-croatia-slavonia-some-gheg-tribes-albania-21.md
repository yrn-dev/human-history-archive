# Croatia-Slavonia and Regional Balkan Ethnography

## Croatia-Slavonia: Administration and Geography
Croatia-Slavonia is a political division of the Balkan Peninsula located south of the Save and Kulpa rivers. As of 1909, the region covered an area of approximately 8,200 square miles with a population of about 1,200,000, resulting in a population density of 146.3 per square mile. Geographically, the southern half of Croatia-Slavonia is included within the region known as Illyria, an area extending along the eastern shore of the Adriatic from Fiume to Durazzo and inland to the Servian Morava and the Danube.

The administration of the region involves a governor known as the ban of Croatia, Slavonia and Dalmatia. The title "ban" is derived from the Persian word *ban*, meaning master or lord, and was historically used by the Avars to denote governors of military districts called *banats*. Since 1868, the duties of the ban have been civil rather than military. The ban is appointed by the emperor of Austria in his capacity as the king of Hungary and holds a seat in the upper house of the Hungarian parliament.

Within Croatia-Slavonia, the royal free town of Buccari (known in Serbo-Croatian as Bakar) is located in the county of Modru-Fiume, seven miles southeast of Fiume on the Adriatic Sea. As of 1900, Buccari had a population of 1,870. The town's economy is centered on boatbuilding and a coasting trade involving coal, wood, wine, and fish, with the tunny-fishery being of some importance. The Hungarian state railway from Agram and Zákány terminates 2.5 miles from the town.

## Religious Affiliations in the Region
The religious landscape of the Balkan Peninsula was significantly altered by the Turkish conquest, which led to numerous conversions to Islam. While the majority of the peninsula's Christian population belongs to the Orthodox Church—with autocephalous churches in Montenegro, Servia, Bulgaria, and Greece—certain groups adhere to the Roman Catholic Church. These include the Serbo-Croats of Croatia-Slavonia and Dalmatia, as well as some of the Gheg tribes in Albania. Other Catholic populations include approximately 21% of Bosnians, a small number of Greeks in the islands, and a limited number of Bulgarians in Macedonia and the kingdom.

## The Albanians and the Gheg Tribes
The Albanian people, who refer to themselves as *Shkyipetar* (and are called *Arnauts* by Slavs and Turks), have a history of expansion, particularly in western Macedonia. Following the first Turkish invasion, many Albanian beys adopted Mahommedanism, with the majority of the population converting during the 16th and 17th centuries.

The Albanians are divided by stock; the Tosk race is found in southern Macedonia, extending to the shores of Lakes Ochrida and Prespa and the upper Bistritza valley. In northern Macedonia, the Albanians are of the Gheg stock. These Gheg populations have advanced in large numbers into the districts of Usküb, Kalkandelen, and Dibra, displacing the Slavonic population. In total, approximately 120,000 Albanians reside in Macedonia, of whom about 10,000 are Christians, primarily Orthodox Tosks.

## Historical and Ethnological Context
The broader region encompassing Croatia-Slavonia, Dalmatia, and Montenegro was historically associated with the Illyrians. Ancient Greek historians described the Illyrians as a barbarous people similar to the tribes of Thrace, noting practices such as human sacrifice and tattooing. Socially, women in Illyria appeared to hold high positions, including roles as queens.

Archaeological evidence from Croatia-Slavonia, Dalmatia, and Bosnia—including lake-dwellings at Butmir and cemeteries at Glasinac and Jezerine—reveals a civilization that developed from the neolithic age, with strong representations of the La Tène and Hallstatt cultures. Linguistically, ancient Illyrian languages were split into northern and southern groups; the southern group is believed to be represented today by the Albanian language.

## Political Transitions
The term "Illyria" saw a political revival in 1809 when Napoleon's French troops established the Illyrian Provinces, which included parts of Croatia, Carniola, Istria, Fiume, and Dalmatia. These territories reverted to Austria by the end of 1813, and a kingdom of Illyria existed as an Austrian crown-land from 1816 until 1849. In later diplomatic arrangements regarding the dismemberment of Austria, property of predominant historic interest to the former kingdoms of Slavonia and Croatia, among others, was identified as potentially transferable without payment.
