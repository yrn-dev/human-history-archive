# Foreign Policy of Austria

The foreign policy of Austria and Austria-Hungary has historically been driven by the need to maintain the internal stability of the Habsburg monarchy and manage the complex geopolitical pressures of Central and Eastern Europe.

## The Era of Stability and Metternich
From the conclusion of the Congress of Vienna until the revolutions of 1848, Austrian foreign policy was inseparably linked to Metternich. This period was defined by a "system of stability" designed to protect an artificial and unsound internal imperial structure. Because the triumph of national aspirations or Liberal principles in Europe or Germany could shatter the Habsburg monarchy, Metternich sought to prevent any movements that threatened the established order.

This objective manifested in several ways: the Carlsbad Decrees and the Vienna Final Act were utilized to keep Germany quiet; the Troppau Protocol served as cover for Austrian aggressions; and in the Eastern Question, Austria provided moral support to the "legitimate" authority of the sultan over "rebel" Greeks to maintain the integrity of Turkey. This policy was conducted under the reign of Francis II, who succeeded Leopold II in 1792. Francis II was characterized by an autocratic temper and intellectual and religious obscurantism.

## Relations with Germany and France (1866–1871)
Following the war of 1866, foreign policy under Chancellor Beust was influenced by the desire to recover positions lost in Germany and potentially revenge Koeniggraetz. During this time, Austria maintained a close friendship with France, highlighted by meetings between the emperor and Napoleon in Salzburg (1868) and Paris (1869), though the death of Maximilian in Mexico cast a shadow over this bond.

By 1870, discussions for a formal alliance with France against the North German Confederation took place, but no agreement was signed. The rapid German successes in the war of 1870 ended these ambitions. With the absorption of South Germany into the German Empire, the primary cause for friction vanished. Following Beust's retirement in 1871, Count Andrassy established a good understanding with Bismarck. This reconciliation was signaled by Emperor Francis Joseph's 1872 visit to Berlin, where the tsar was also present.

## The Alliance with Germany and Italy
In 1879, Austria-Hungary secured its European position through a formal alliance with Germany. Arranged by Bismarck and Andrassy, the treaty committed Germany to support Austria against a Russian attack, while Austria-Hungary pledged to assist Germany against a combined French and Russian attack. This alliance remained the foundation of Austro-Hungarian policy, surviving national discord and commercial differences.

The alliance was popular among German Austrians, some of whom viewed it as a "national alliance of the Teutonic races" and attempted to use it to influence internal struggles against the Slavs. However, both governments rejected this theory; Bismarck specifically avoided influencing internal Austrian affairs to avoid provoking the Poles and Czechs, who were inclined toward an alliance with France. The alliance was strengthened in 1882 when Italy joined, following the French occupation of Tunis. Full reconciliation with Italy was later completed when Francis Joseph visited Victor Emmanuel in Venice.

## The Eastern Question and the Balkans
Austria's approach to the East was historically shaped by the threat of Russian influence. In the late 18th century, the ambition of Catherine of Russia and the Russian advance toward the Black Sea led Kaunitz to conclude a defensive alliance with the Porte in 1771, as Russian control of the Danube was viewed as a more serious menace than the Ottoman power. However, Maria Theresa eventually shared in the spoliation of Poland to modify Russia's extreme claims in Turkey, acquiring Galicia and Lodomeria in 1772 and Bukovina in 1777.

In the later 19th century, an insurrection in Bosnia and Herzegovina created a difficult political problem. While the empire's Slav inhabitants sympathized with Christians in Turkey, the Hungarians sympathized with the Turks. A cardinal principle of Austrian policy was the refusal to allow new Slav states on its southern frontier, especially as disturbances were fomented by Russian agents. 

In 1875, Andrassy drafted a note requiring Turkey to institute reforms. When rebellion spread to Bulgaria and Russia took the lead, Austria entered a secret understanding with Russia in 1877, where Russia promised not to annex territory injurious to Austria. However, the Russian advance on Constantinople and the subsequent Peace of San Stefano violated these engagements, leading Andrassy to mobilize a portion of the army and request a credit of 60 million gulden.

## Later Policy and Annexations
By the late 19th century, Austria-Hungary's policy became more peaceful and unambitious, focusing on maintaining trade and influence in the Balkans through commercial treaties with Bulgaria, Servia, and Rumania, as well as the building of railways and the opening of the Danube for navigation. Anxiety grew over the growth of Russian influence in Servia following the abdication of King Milan.

In 1908, supported by Germany, Austria defied Europe by annexing the Yugo-Slav provinces of Bosnia and Herzegovina from the Turk. This action had the effect of estranging Italy, a former ally, and contributed to a defensive understanding between Britain, France, and Russia against German and Austrian aggressions.
