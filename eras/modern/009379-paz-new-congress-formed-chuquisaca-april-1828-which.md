# Political and Administrative History of Bolivia

## Early Governance and Constitutional Development
Following the achievement of independence, Bolivia experienced a period of political instability. The first general assembly of deputies dissolved on October 6, 1825. Subsequently, a new congress was installed at Chuquisaca on May 25, 1826, to review a constitution prepared by Bolivar. This document was approved by the congress and sworn to by the people. Under this constitution, General Sucre was selected as president for life, though he accepted the position for only two years and on the condition that 2,000 Colombian troops remain with him.

Despite these foundations, the country faced repeated risings. By the end of 1827, Colombian troops and General Sucre were driven from La Paz. In April 1828, another congress was formed at Chuquisaca. This body modified Bolivar's constitution and appointed Marshal Santa Cruz as president.

## The Era of Marshal Santa Cruz
The government of Santa Cruz faced a revolution led by General Blanco only a year after his appointment, which temporarily overturned the government and caused disorder. Order was restored by 1831, allowing Santa Cruz to implement a code of laws bearing his name and organize the nation's financial affairs. During this period, he established a treaty of commerce with Peru, leading to several years of peace.

In 1835, Santa Cruz intervened in a power struggle between two factions in Peru. After defeating General Gamarra in the spring of 1836, Santa Cruz pacified Peru, named himself its protector, and sought to create a confederation between the two nations. This move prompted active interference from the government of Chile, which supported Gamarra. Following three years of conflict, Santa Cruz was defeated at the battle of Jungay in June 1839 and exiled.

## Mid-19th Century Conflict and Leadership
Following the exile of Santa Cruz, Gamarra became the president of Peru, while General Velasco served as the provisional chief in Bolivia. However, the Santa Cruz party remained influential and successfully revolted, eventually installing General Ballivian in power. 

In August 1841, Gamarra attempted to annex the province of La Paz, besieging the capital. His army was totally routed in a battle with Ballivian, and Gamarra was killed. Ballivian subsequently attempted to invade Peru, but Chile intervened again to stop him. Ballivian held the presidency until 1848, when he retired to Valparaiso. He was succeeded by General Belzu, who took power after a successful military revolution at the end of 1848.

## Administrative Divisions and National Capitals
Bolivia is organized into one territory and eight departments: La Paz, El Beni, Oruro, Cochabamba, Santa Cruz, Potosi, Chuquisaca, and Tarija. These are further subdivided into 54 provinces, 415 cantons, 232 vice-cantons, 18 missions, and one colony. According to 1900 census data, the total population was 1,766,451, though the total area was later adjusted to 515,156 sq. m. following the loss of territory to Brazil in 1903.

The national capital has shifted between four provincial capitals: Sucre (also known as Chuquisaca), La Paz, Cochabamba, and Oruro. While Sucre was the chosen capital, a revolution in 1898 moved the capital to La Paz, which serves as the commercial metropolis and is more accessible.

## Late 19th Century and the Pando Administration
Political tension resurfaced in December when an attempt was made to establish Sucre as the perpetual capital. This triggered an open revolt in La Paz. On January 17 of the following year, government forces were defeated by insurgents led by Colonel Pando. Pando marched on Oruro, entering the town on April 11, 1899, after defeating government troops. Dr. Severo Alonso fled to Chile, and Colonel Pando formed a provisional government, eventually being elected president without opposition.

President Pando's administration focused on improving communications to revive mining and other industries. He also managed boundary disputes with Chile and Brazil. The conflict with Brazil centered on the rubber-producing Acre territory, where a majority of workers were Brazilians. After Acre declared independence and Bolivian troops were dispatched in 1900 to restore order, Pando sought an amicable diplomatic settlement. A treaty signed in November 1903 ceded a portion of the Acre province to Brazil in exchange for a cash indemnity of $10,000,000.
