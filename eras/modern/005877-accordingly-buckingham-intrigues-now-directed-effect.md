# Buckingham's Political Intrigues and Career

The political activities of Buckingham were characterized by shifting alliances, secret intrigues, and a recurring struggle for influence within the English government across different eras. His efforts often focused on the removal of political rivals and the pursuit of high office.

## Conflict with Clarendon
During a period when Buckingham was barred from high office due to the influence of Clarendon, he directed his intrigues toward effecting the chancellor's ruin. To achieve this, Buckingham organized parties in both houses of parliament to support a 1666 bill that prohibited the import of Irish cattle. This move was designed partly to oppose Clarendon and partly to thwart the duke of Ormonde. 

During the resulting debates, Buckingham asserted that anyone opposing the bill possessed either an "Irish understanding" or an "Irish interest." This led to a challenge from Lord Ossory; however, Buckingham avoided the encounter, and Ossory was subsequently sent to the Tower. Later, on December 19, during a conference between the two houses, Buckingham engaged in a physical scuffle with the marquess of Dorchester. During the fight, Buckingham pulled off Dorchester's periwig, and Dorchester ended the encounter with much of Buckingham's hair in his hand. Clarendon described the event as a flagrant misdemeanour that offended the dignity of the House of Lords. While both peers were sent to the Tower, they were released after apologizing. Following this, Buckingham attempted to vent his spite by claiming the title of Lord Roos, which was held by the son-in-law of Dorchester.

## Fall and Rise to Power
Buckingham's opposition to the government eventually cost him the favor of the king. He faced accusations of having cast the king's horoscope and engaging in treasonable intrigues. On February 25, 1667, his arrest was ordered and he was dismissed from all offices. He remained at large until June 27, when he surrendered and was imprisoned in the Tower. He was released by July 17 and restored to his appointments and royal favor on September 15.

Following his restoration, Buckingham took an active role in the prosecution of Clarendon. After Clarendon's fall, Buckingham became the chief minister, although he held no high office other than master of the horse, a position he purchased from the duke of Albermarle in 1668. His later career included being elected high steward of Oxford university in 1672 and chancellor of Cambridge in 1671. In his political and legal views, he supported the original jurisdiction of the Lords in Skinner's case, advised the declaration of indulgence in 1672, supported a scheme of comprehension in 1668, and favored religious toleration, for which he was praised by Richard Baxter.

## The Cabal
Buckingham was one of five ministers who formed a committee of the privy council known as the "Committee for Foreign Affairs," which eventually developed into the cabinet. This group became known as the "Cabal." The term "cabal"—derived from the French *cabale* and the theosophical interpretation of Hebrew scriptures (*Cabbala* or *Kabbalah*)—refers to a private organization engaged in secret intrigues. The term became stereotyped in English during the reign of Charles II because the initial letters of the five ministers who signed the 1673 treaty of alliance with France—Clifford, Arlington, Buckingham, Ashley, and Lauderdale—spelled the word "cabal."

## Early Career and Foreign Relations
In the early 17th century, Buckingham's influence was marked by volatile international relations. In the winter of 1621 and 1622, he was heavily influenced by Gondomar. In May 1622, Laud successfully argued against Buckingham's resolution to declare himself a Roman Catholic. By May 1622, an understanding was reached that the prince of Wales would visit Madrid the following year, where the Spanish court hoped to convert him to Roman Catholicism before he married the infanta Maria.

On February 17, 1623, Charles and Buckingham set out for Madrid, arriving on March 7. The mission was characterized by mutual deception: Charles and Buckingham hoped for the restitution of the Palatinate as a marriage gift, while the Spaniards sought Charles's conversion and other extreme concessions. Personal disputes between Buckingham, the grandees, and Olivares eventually led the two men to break entirely with Spain upon sailing from Santander in September.

## Military Enterprises and Popularity
As the virtual ruler of England, Buckingham attempted to initiate war despite a lack of funds. He sent 12,000 Englishmen under Count Mansfeld through France into the Palatinate. This enterprise ended in disastrous failure, as the troops lacked provisions and died in winter frosts. Buckingham also considered other schemes, including attacking Spain directly, attacking Spain's ally Genoa with an English fleet, and providing aid to Christian IV, the king of Denmark.

Despite these failures, Buckingham became the most popular man in England by positioning himself at the head of the popular movement against Spain. In February 1624, with the help of the heir apparent, he took an independent political position, leading King James to end all negotiations with Spain. Buckingham and his master then attempted to sway public opinion by sending a fleet to seize Spanish treasure-ships and attack Cadiz, and by traveling to the Hague to pawn the crown jewels to fund a Protestant alliance and provide money to the king of Denmark. These efforts also ended in failure; the fleet achieved nothing at Cadiz, the crown jewels yielded a small sum, and the funding for Denmark required an appeal to parliament. Additionally, the king of France was offended by the seizure of French vessels carrying goods for Spain and the treatment of English Roman Catholics.
