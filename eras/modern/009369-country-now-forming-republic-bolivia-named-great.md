# Bolivia

The republic of Bolivia, named after the liberator Simon Bolivar, was originally a portion of the empire of the Incas of Peru. Following the Spanish conquest of Peru in the 16th century, the native populations were subjected to oppression and tyranny. While the Spanish home government attempted to protect Indians from ill-usage and serfdom through the council of the Indies, these regulations were largely ignored by mine-owners and settlers. Although some claims suggest eight million Indians died due to forced labor in mines, this is considered a gross exaggeration; the significant decline in the Indian population was primarily caused by alcohol and European epidemics. In 1780-82, the Inca Tupac Amaru led an abortive insurrection directed more against Creole tyranny than Spanish rule. Consequently, the native population remained relatively indifferent to the 1814 colonial revolt that eventually led to South American independence.

## Ethnography and Geography
The chief ethnical element in Bolivia is the Aymara (anciently known as Colla), a tribe that formerly inhabited the valleys of the Andes and the region around Lake Titicaca. The Incas regarded the Lake Titicaca region as a "holy land," attributing the origin of Quichua civilization to it. The Aymaras possessed a considerable culture before their conquest by the Incas in the 13th and 14th centuries, as evidenced by the megalithic ruins of Tiahuanaco. By the time the Spaniards arrived, the Aymaras were in a decadent state but retained privileges such as their own language. Physically, the pure Aymara is characterized by a broad face, black eyes, rounded forehead, and a reddish complexion. Including half-breeds, they number approximately half a million in Bolivia.

Geographically, Bolivia's river systems are divided into three regions: the Central Plateau, La Plata, and the Amazon. The Amazon system covers more than half of the republic, featuring the Mamore and Beni rivers, which unite to form the Madeira. The Mamore, rising on the north-east slopes of the Sierra de Cochabamba, is navigable for about three-fourths of its 600-meter length. The Guapay (or Grande) is another major stream, flowing over 700 meters in a tortuous, non-navigable course before uniting with the Mamore at Junta de los Rios.

## Independence and Early Governance
Bolivia became a distinct and independent nation in August 1825, after fifty-four deputies assembled at the capital, Chuquisaca, decided they would not remain part of the Argentine provinces. General Sucre was initially invested with supreme command. In 1826, Simon Bolivar presented a project for a constitution which proposed a president for life with the power to nominate a successor and no responsibility. This proposal alarmed republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires, while in Peru, Bolivar was accused of attempting to create a confederacy of Bolivia, Peru, and Colombia under his own perpetual dictatorship.

The early years of the republic were marked by instability. In 1827, General Sucre and Colombian troops were driven from La Paz. A new congress in 1828 modified Bolivar's constitution and appointed Marshal Santa Cruz as president. After a period of disorder led by General Blanco, Santa Cruz restored order by 1831, promulgated a code of laws, and established a commerce treaty with Peru. In 1835, Santa Cruz intervened in a Peruvian power struggle, defeating General Gamarra and naming himself protector of Peru to seek a confederation of the two nations. This prompted intervention from Chile. Following a defeat at the battle of Jungay in June 1839, Santa Cruz was exiled.

## Political and Territorial Conflicts
The mid-19th century saw continued volatility. General Ballivian took power after a revolt against General Velasco. In 1841, General Gamarra of Peru attempted to annex the province of La Paz but was killed after his army was routed by Ballivian. Later, in 1871, President Melgarejo was deposed by Colonel Augustin Morales, who was murdered in 1872. His successor, Colonel Adolfo Ballivian, entered into a secret agreement with Peru on February 6, 1873, for mutual defense against exterior aggression, specifically targeting potential Chilean encroachment.

Tensions with Chile were rooted in trade and territory. A 1866 treaty had established the 24th parallel as the boundary and granted Chile half of the customs and free trading/mining rights between the 23rd and 24th parallels. In 1870, Bolivia granted an Anglo-Chilean company rights to nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel for $10,000.

By 1895, Chile and Bolivia signed a treaty at Santiago to strengthen friendship and provide Bolivia free access to the sea. Chile pledged that if it acquired sovereignty over Tacna and Arica, it would transfer them to Bolivia for an indemnity of $5,000,000 silver. If this failed, Chile would cede the roadstead of Vitor or an analogous port and $5,000,000 silver. However, secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina caused Chile to withdraw its conciliatory stance. A final treaty ratified in 1905 required Bolivia to cede maritime provinces occupied by Chile since 1881. In exchange, Chile agreed to pay a cash indemnity, provide pecuniary assistance for railways, and construct a railway from Arica to La Paz.

## Religion
The Bolivian state recognizes and supports the Roman Apostolic Catholic religion, and the public exercise of other worship is prohibited except in colonies or for resident foreigners. According to a 1900 census, the Roman Catholic population was 1,609,365, while other creeds numbered 24,245. The domesticated Indians practice a version of the Catholic faith influenced by ancestral superstitions. The country is organized into one archbishopric (La Plata, with its seat at Sucre) and three bishoprics (La Paz, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz). The state provides an annual appropriation of about L17,150 for the Church, and mission work for Indians is managed by the Propaganda Fide.
