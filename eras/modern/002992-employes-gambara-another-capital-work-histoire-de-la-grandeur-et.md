## Historical and Geographical Works on French Grandeur
The archive contains several records of authors who dedicated their careers to documenting the history and scale of France. Andre du Chesne (1584–1640), often styled as the father of French history, was a geographer and historian who served as the king's geographer and historiographer under the patronage of Cardinal Richelieu. Among his principal works is *Les Antiquites et recherches de la grandeur et majeste des rois de France*, published in Paris in 1609. Du Chesne also produced *Les Antiquites et recherches des villes, chateaux, &c., de toute la France* (1609) and the *Historiae Francorum scriptores* (1636–1649), a project intended to span 24 volumes to encompass all narrative sources for French history during the middle ages, though it remained unfinished.

Other historians focused on the French state include Gabriel Daniel (1649–1728), a French Jesuit historian. His most recognized work is *Histoire de France depuis l'etablissement de la monarchie francaise* (first complete edition 1713), as well as the *Histoire de la milice francaise* (1721). Additionally, the historian Duruy produced several works on France, including *Histoire de France de 1453 a 1815* (1856), *Histoire populaire de la France* (1862–1863), and *Introduction generale a l'histoire de France* (1865).

## Scientific Studies of Terrestrial Grandeur
The archive records the contributions of the Cassini family regarding the physical dimensions and mapping of the earth. Jacques Cassini (1677–1756), who succeeded his father at the Paris observatory in 1712, measured the arc of the meridian from Perpignan to Dunkirk in 1713. He published these findings in 1720 in a volume titled *De la grandeur et de la figure de la terre*. 

His son, Cesar Francois Cassini, also known as Cassini de Thury (1714–1784), continued these hereditary surveying operations. In 1744, Cassini de Thury began the construction of a great topographical map of France. His chief works include *Meridienne de l'observatoire de Paris* (1744), *Description geometrique de la terre* (1775), and *Description geometrique de la France* (1784).

## Diverse Employments and Labor
The archive provides various examples of professional and forced employments across different eras and regions:

**Administrative and Diplomatic Employments:**
Marco Polo served Kúblái in domestic administration and on distant missions. His employments included a three-year term as the government of the city of Yang-chau, as well as missions to the Indian Seas, Champa (or Southern Cochin China), and visits to Kara Korum in Mongolia and Kan-chau in Tangut.

**Public and Private Slave Labor:**
In ancient Sicily, the victorious army of Gelo acquired numerous prisoners who became public property. These slaves were employed in two primary ways: they were either used for public undertakings involving religious solemnity, ornament, and defence, or they were let out to private masters to generate state revenue. At Agrigentum, these public slaves were used for state-works that elevated the city to "signal grandeur" over a seventy-year period. Some private individuals in this region had as many as five hundred slaves in their employment.

**Convict Assignment:**
In a different colonial system, the "assignment" plan was introduced to lend convicts to those who would relieve the authorities of their charge. This system saw convicts employed in various capacities:
* **Agricultural/Pastoral:** Many were utilized on new stations in the interior and wide areas of grazing land.
* **Urban/Skilled:** In towns, skilled convicts were employed as handicraftsmen, book-keepers, and clerks.
The archive notes a disparity in these employments; while unskilled convicts performed heavy manual labour up country, "clever expert rogues" often found profitable and pleasant employment in towns.
