# Rivers

The study of river systems across various global regions reveals a diverse array of drainage patterns, from the high-altitude lacustrine basins of the Andes to the glaciated ranges of North America and the volcanic landscapes of the West Indies.

## Lake Baikal and Associated Streams
Lake Baikal is fed by over 300 streams, the majority of which are short mountain torrents. Its primary inlets include the Selenga to the south-east, the Barguzin to the east, and the Upper Angara, which enters at the north-east extremity. The lake's sole outflow is the lower Angara, which exits via a rocky cleft on the west shore. 

Historically, the Irkut river reached Lake Baikal, but it no longer does so. After approaching the south-west extremity of the lake, the Irkut leaves the broad valley that leads to the water and travels north through a narrow mountain gap to join the Angara at Irkutsk. Additionally, the river Frolikha connects Lake Baikal with the smaller Lake Frolikha, located near the lake's northern extremity.

Geologically, Lake Baikal previously had a larger extension, stretching into the lower valleys of the Barguzin and Upper Angara, as well as the valley of the Irkut.

## Drainage of the Americas
### Central America and the Caribbean
In certain regions, the narrow strip of level ground between mountains and the sea makes the formation of navigable rivers nearly impossible. On the Atlantic seaboard, the San Juan river is a notable stream that drains Lake Nicaragua. It begins within Nicaraguan territory and flows for 95 miles, mostly along the frontier, before reaching its main outfall at the Colorado Mouth in Costa Rica. The San Carlos and Sarapiqui serve as its primary right-hand tributaries. Other significant streams include the Reventazon (or Parismina), which flows from the central plateau to the Caribbean Sea; its volume is considerable due to moist trade-winds near its sources. Lake Nicaragua also receives the Rio Frio and six smaller streams across the northern frontier.

On the Pacific coast, rivers are characterized by rapid flows and a tendency for sudden floods. While three rivers carry the prefix *Rio Grande* ("great river"), none are large. These include the Rio Grande de Pirris and the Rio Grande de Terrabis (or Diquis), which flow into Coronada Bay, and the Rio Grande de Tarcoles, which enters the Gulf of Nicoya. The Rio Grande de Tarcoles rises at the base of Irazu, near the Ochomogo Pass and the sources of the Reventazon. The Tempisque also enters the Gulf of Nicoya, contributing alluvial deposits that silt up the shallow inlet.

In Dominica, the island is drained by the Layou and Pagoua rivers, which flow west and east, respectively, through a narrow central plain. The island's fertility is supported by approximately thirty streams of considerable size and numerous mountain torrents.

### North America
In Illinois, the drainage system consists of more than 275 streams divided into two systems. One system uses the Mississippi as an outlet, receiving three-fourths of the state's waters. The most significant river here is the Illinois, formed by the junction of the Kankakee and Des Plaines in Grundy county; it drains 24,726 square miles and broadens into lake-like expanses at points such as Lake Peoria. Other Mississippi tributaries include the Rock in the north and the Kaskaskia in the south. The second system is tributary to the Ohio or Wabash rivers, with the Saline, Cache, Little Wabash, and Embarrass being important streams. The Chicago river, originally a one-mile stream flowing into Lake Michigan, was diverted in 1900 via the Chicago Drainage Canal to flow ultimately into the Mississippi.

In Idaho, the drainage of the Great Basin region flows southward into the Great Salt Lake via the Bear river. The state also features the Salmon River range and the arid Snake river plains.

### South America
The *alta-planicie*, or great central plateau, is one of the world's most elevated lacustrine basins. While it once drained eastward, it currently has no surface outlet, as drainage is absorbed by evaporation and dry desert soil. Lake Titicaca receives several short streams and discharges into the Desaguadero, a sluggish river that flows south for 184 miles to Lake Poopo (Pampa Aullaguas). The Desaguadero is navigable for small craft and has two or three small western tributaries. Lake Pampa Aullaguas is fed by two small streams and has a small outlet, the Lacahahuira, which flows west for 60 miles to the Cienegas de Coipasa. In the extreme south, the Rio Grande de Lipez is similarly absorbed by the soil.
