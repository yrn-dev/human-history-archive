# Bolivia: History, Geography, and Administration

## War of Independence and Early Republic
The struggle for independence in the region of Upper Peru (modern-day Bolivia) was characterized by nearly continuous warfare from July 1809 until August 1825. The conflict involved royalist Spanish forces and South American patriot forces, with primary action occurring between the shores of Lake Titicaca and the Argentine provinces of Jujuy and Salta. 

Early patriot successes included an invasion by General Balcarce's army from Buenos Aires, which defeated Spanish troops twice and celebrated the first anniversary of independence near Lake Titicaca in May 1811. However, due to negligence and dissolute conduct by leaders, the patriot army was defeated in June 1811 by the Spanish army under Gey fol Goyeneche. Following years of alternating victories, the patriots suffered a total rout in 1815 in a battle between Oruro and Potosi. This was followed by a royalist suppression of an insurrection involving Creoles and Indians from southern Peru. In 1816, the Spanish General Laserna attempted to march on Buenos Aires but was forced to retire by irregular *gaucho* troops. For the next six years, patriots maintained guerrilla warfare from the mountains, particularly in the province of Yungas.

Following independence, the country faced repeated instability. General Sucre and Colombian troops were driven from La Paz in late 1827. A new congress in Chuquisaca in April 1828 modified the constitution given by Bolivar and appointed Marshal Santa Cruz as president. After a period of disorder led by General Blanco, Santa Cruz restored order by 1831, promulgated a code of laws, and established a commerce treaty with Peru.

## Mid-19th Century Conflicts
In 1835, Santa Cruz intervened in a power struggle in Peru, defeating General Gamarra and naming himself protector of Peru in 1836 to create a confederation of the two nations. This prompted intervention from Chile, which supported Gamarra. After three years of fighting, Santa Cruz was defeated at the battle of Jungay in June 1839 and exiled. 

Following the installation of General Ballivian in power, General Gamarra attempted to annex the province of La Paz in August 1841. Gamarra besieged the capital but was killed after his army was totally routed by Ballivian. Ballivian remained president until 1848, succeeded by General Belzu after a military revolution.

## Territorial Changes and the War with Chile
Bolivia's borders have shifted due to conflict and diplomacy. In the late 19th century, a double revolution occurred in Peru and Bolivia; in Bolivia, General Narciso Campero was proclaimed president in June 1880. During the subsequent war with Chile, Bolivia played a minimal role in the struggle. After the surrender of Lima and Callao to Chilean forces, and the eventual peace treaty between Peru and Chile in October 1883, the remaining Bolivian army was stationed at Oruro. On December 11, 1883, Bolivia signed a peace treaty with Chile, ceding its entire sea-coast, including the port of Cobija. Consequently, the railways of the Litoral department passed under Chilean control.

Later, a dispute arose with Brazil over the rubber-producing territory of Acre. This led to a declaration of independence by the state of Acre and the dispatch of Bolivian troops in 1900. To avoid hostilities, President Pando settled the dispute via a 1903 treaty, ceding a portion of the Acre province to Brazil in exchange for a $10,000,000 cash indemnity.

## Administrative Divisions and Urban Centers
As of the 1900 census, the republic was divided into eight departments and one national territory, further subdivided into 54 provinces, 415 cantons, 232 vice-cantons, 18 missions, and one colony. The total area, adjusted for the 1903 loss to Brazil, was 515,156 sq. m.

The national capital has shifted between four cities: Sucre (also known as Chuquisaca), La Paz, Cochabamba, and Oruro. Following a revolution in 1898, the capital became La Paz, which serves as the commercial metropolis. Other notable towns include:
* **Pulacayo:** A silver mining center in the department of Potosi.
* **Uyuni:** A railway junction and highway converging point.
* **Tupiza:** A mining and commercial center near the Argentine frontier.
* **Trinidad:** The capital of the El Beni department, situated on the Mamore river.

## Infrastructure and Education
To overcome its isolation, Bolivia entered treaties with Brazil (1903) and Chile (1904). Brazil agreed to build a railway around the Madeira falls and provided L2,000,000 for internal Bolivian railway construction. Chile agreed to construct a railway from Arica to La Paz. Other connections include an Argentine railway extension to Tupiza and a line from Guaqui on Lake Titicaca to Alto de La Paz, which connects to La Paz via an electric line.

Education is free and compulsory, though progress has been limited. In 1901, there were 733 primary schools with 41,587 pupils. Higher education is provided by universities in Oruro, Santa Cruz, Potosi, Tarija, Cochabamba, La Paz, and Sucre. The universities at La Paz and Sucre are the most well-equipped. Specialized institutions include a military academy and agricultural school in La Paz, and a mining and civil engineering school in Oruro.

## Society and Religion
The population is majority native, speaking languages such as Aymara, Quichua, and Guarani, while Spanish is the language of the dominant minority. The constitution recognizes and supports the Roman Apostolic Catholic religion, prohibiting the public exercise of other worship except in colonies. According to 1900 census data, the Roman Catholic population was 1,609,365. The church is organized into one archbishopric (seated at Sucre) and three bishoprics (seated at La Paz, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz).
