# Mathematical Lectures, read in the public schools of the university of Cambridge

The *Mathematical Lectures*, read in the public schools of the university of Cambridge, are a set of scientific works written in Latin. These lectures were authored by a mathematician who was regarded by his English contemporaries as second only to Newton, though continental writers did not place him as high. This author was noted for his ability to handle the severe geometrical method and for his *Method of Tangents*, which approximated the reasoning Newton later used for the doctrine of ultimate ratios.

While the author's scientific output was extensive, his lectures on elementary principles are noted for not providing significant insight into the difficulties existing between philosophy and mathematics. Additionally, his substantial contributions to the science are described as being of no great importance. In contrast, his *Sermons* were highly regarded as weighty pieces of reasoning, though they were characterized by a ponderous style and elaborate construction.

## Mathematical Instruction at Cambridge
The university of Cambridge has a long history of mathematical instruction involving various roles and methods. George Butler, an English schoolmaster and divine, served as a fellow at Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge, where he held the position of mathematical lecturer before becoming a classical tutor. He was later elected as a public examiner of the university in 1804.

Other figures associated with Cambridge mathematics include Augustus De Morgan, who entered Trinity College at the age of sixteen and a half. De Morgan studied mathematics in part under Sir G. B. Airy and took fourth place in the mathematical tripos in 1827. Although he was later prevented from obtaining a fellowship or an M.A. degree due to his objection to signing theological tests required of fellows and masters of arts, he later influenced the Cambridge school through pupils such as E. J. Routh and Isaac Todhunter.

## Comparative Mathematical Education
Beyond Cambridge, mathematical instruction has taken various forms in other institutions:
* **University of Sofia:** Reorganized in 1904, this university includes a faculty of mathematics. As of 1905, the university's staff consisted of 25 lecturers and 17 professors, with a total student body of 943.
* **University College (University of London):** Augustus De Morgan served as a professor of mathematics here for a third of a century, beginning his first lecture at age twenty-two.
* **Stockholm:** Sophie Kovalevsky, a Russian mathematician, held a post as a lecturer in mathematics after being procured for the position by Gustaf Mittag Leffler. She was later appointed full professor in 1884.
* **Berlin:** In the late 19th century, public lectures in Berlin were not open to women, which led Sophie Kovalevsky to read privately with Karl Weierstrass between 1871 and 1874.
