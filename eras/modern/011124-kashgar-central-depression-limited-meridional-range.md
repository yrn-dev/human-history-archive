# The Central Depression of Asia and the Kashgar Region

The central depression of Asia is a vast lateral division of the continent, situated along the parallel of 40 degrees north. This depression begins on the east of the Gobi desert and extends westwards through Mongolia into Chinese Turkestan. Historically, this area is believed to have once been a vast inland sea.

## Geographical Limits and Topography
To the west of Kashgar, the central depression is limited by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol, of which the Sarikol range serves as the eastern face. The level of this depression, located between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the sources of the Hwang-ho, generally does not exceed 2,000 feet above sea level. Modern research suggests that in the central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, the elevation may actually fall below sea level.

The region is further characterized by a great continental water-parting or divide. This divide stretches from the Black Sea, through the Hindu Kush and Elburz, to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, eventually reaching the Bering Strait. While some parts of this divide, such as the Altai, Khangai, and Tian-shan, reach great heights, a significant portion remains below 5,000 feet. South of this divide, the land drops into the central depression of the Gobi, creating a nearly waterless interior space where drainage is lost to swamps or deserts.

## The Kashgar Plains and the Sarikol Range
The plains of Kashgar are situated at an elevation ranging from 4,000 to 6,000 feet. On the western edge of these plains, the meridional range of Sarikol defines the political boundary between China and Russia. Known in medieval times as Bolor and to the ancients as Taurus, the Sarikol range consists of two parallel chains:
* **The Western Chain:** Serves as the water-divide of the Pamirs.
* **The Eastern Chain:** Also known as the Kandar or Kashgar range, this chain is split by lateral gorges that allow for the passage of main drainage from the eastern slopes of the Pamir.

The Sarikol range acts as a rugged intervening barrier between Chinese Turkestan and the Russian Pamirs, though the actual dividing line has been described as indefinite.

## Regional Boundaries and Exploration
The demarcation of boundaries in this region was a focus of late 19th-century commissions. The Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895 demarcated a line to the glaciers and snowfields overlooking the Chinese border. To the north-east of Kashgar, the southern boundary of Siberia follows an irregular course defined partly by the Alatau and Tian-shan mountains until it reaches the Sayan range west of Irkutsk.

Knowledge of the highlands of the Pamirs and the central depression was limited until the mission of Sir Douglas Forsyth to Yarkand in 1870. Earlier European pioneers, Robert Barkley Shaw and George Hayward, arrived at Yarkand in 1868. Subsequent mapping of Chinese Turkestan was conducted by Henry Trotter. Further geographical data was gathered by the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission (1884-1886), during which Ney Elias identified the "Dragon Lake" (Rangkul) while crossing from China through Badakshan and the Pamirs.

## Related Mountain Systems
The broader region is influenced by several other major ranges. The Kuen-lun ranges run along the northern edge of the Tibetan plateau, separating the Tibetan plateau to the south from the Takla-makan desert to the north. To the north and north-west, the Gobi desert is bounded by the Yablonoi Mountains and their western extension in the Tian-shan. 

The geological configuration of this area is largely the result of folding and faulting. A broad belt of folded Mesozoic and Tertiary deposits forms the principal mountain ranges of central Asia, including the Tian-shan, the Himalayas, and the Hindu Kush. In contrast, the regions of Siberia, northern China, and Mongolia are generally free from important folding of the same age, with their topography often defined by trough-like depressions and intervening mountain ridges.
