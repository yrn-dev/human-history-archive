# Campaign of 1745

The military campaigns of 1745 were characterized by significant shifts in diplomatic alliances, major pitched battles in Flanders and Silesia, and strategic maneuvers in Italy. The year's events were centered on three primary engagements: Fontenoy, Hohenfriedberg, and Kesselsdorf.

## Diplomatic Shifts and the Imperial Election
The year began with a significant diplomatic realignment on January 8, when a Quadruple Alliance was concluded at Warsaw between England, Austria, Holland, and Saxony. This occurred twelve days after the death of Charles VII, an event that opened a new election for the imperial title. Because Charles VII's successor in Bavaria was not a candidate for the title, the Bavarian army found itself in a precarious position. Caught in scattered winter quarters during the action of Amberg on January 7, the Bavarian forces were driven from point to point, forcing the young elector to abandon Munich once more.

On April 22, the peace of Fuessen was established. Under the terms of this agreement, the elector secured his hereditary states on the condition that he support the candidature of the grand-duke Francis, the consort of Maria Theresa. Consequently, the "imperial" army ceased to exist, leaving Frederick isolated.

## The Flanders Campaign and Fontenoy
France focused its efforts in 1745 on the Flanders campaign. On May 10, Louis XV and Saxe besieged Tournay. During this operation, they inflicted a great defeat upon the relieving army led by the duke of Cumberland at the battle of Fontenoy.

## Conflict in Silesia
In Silesia, a "small war" had been ongoing for some time. The concentration of the Prussian army required severe fighting to achieve. By the end of May, Frederick had established a camp at Frankenstein, situated between Neisse and Glatz, with approximately 65,000 men. Opposing him behind the Riesengebirge, near Landshut, Prince Charles commanded 85,000 troops consisting of Saxons and Austrians.

On June 4, the battle of Hohenfriedberg (also known as Striegau) took place. This was the greatest victory of Frederick's career to date, surpassed perhaps only by his later battles at Rossbach and Leuthen. Prince Charles suffered a complete defeat and withdrew through the mountains. Frederick conducted a methodical pursuit, cautious of the barren and difficult terrain and uncertain of the enemy's level of demoralization. The remainder of the summer was occupied by the maneuvers of both leaders on the upper Elbe.

During this period, several officers distinguished themselves. Count Leopold Josef von Daun, an Austrian field marshal, held important commands at both Soor and Hohenfriedberg; he was promoted to the rank of *Feldzeugmeister* in 1745. Additionally, Ernst Gideon von Loudon, an Austrian soldier of Scottish origin, served in the Silesian mountains in 1745 as a leader of light troops, where he greatly distinguished himself.

## Operations in Italy
The campaign in Italy in 1745 moved beyond a mere war of posts. In March, a secret treaty allied the republic of Genoa with Naples, Spain, and France. Following a change in Austrian command, the allies made their first moves: De Gages moved from Modena toward Lucca, while Marshal Maillebois led French and Spanish forces through the Riviera to the Tanaro.

By mid-July, these two armies concentrated between the Tanaro and the Scrivia, totaling 80,000 men. A swift march on Piacenza drew the Austrian commander away, allowing the allies to attack and completely defeat the Sardinians at Bassignano on September 27. This victory led to the capture of Casale, Valenza, and Alessandria.
