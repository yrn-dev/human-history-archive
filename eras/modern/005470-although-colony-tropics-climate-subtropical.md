# Subtropical and Tropical Climates

The study of tropical and subtropical environments encompasses a wide range of geographical regions, biological distributions, and human adaptations. These climates are characterized by varying temperatures, rainfall patterns, and distinct ecological zones.

## Regional Climatic Characteristics
Different colonies and territories exhibit diverse subtropical and tropical profiles. In one specific colony located in the tropics, the climate is classified as subtropical. This region records a highest shade temperature of 98° F and a lowest of 50°. Its weather is influenced by easterly sea-winds that prevail for most of the year. The dry season occurs from mid-February to mid-May, while rain falls intermittently during other months and almost continuously from October through December. Annual rainfall averages 81½ inches, though some districts receive 150 inches or more.

In Costa Rica, the proximity of two oceans and varied surface configuration create striking climatic extremes. The country is divided into three zones: tropical, temperate, and cold, which generally succeed one another as altitude increases. The tropical zone, covering the foothills and coast, has a mean annual temperature between 72° F and 82° F. The temperate zone, specifically the San Jose plateau (3000-5000 ft.), averages 68° F. The wet season on the Pacific slope runs from April to December, interrupted in June by a fortnight of dry weather known as the *Veranillo de San Juan*. Normal rainfall is approximately 80 inches, but cloud-bursts can increase this to 150 inches or more.

In British East Africa, climatic zones run parallel to the coast. The coast is hot with tropical temperatures and annual rainfall of about 35 inches. Inland highlands (above 5000 ft.) are healthier, with average temperatures of 66° F in the cool season and 73° F in the hot season. Regions bordering Victoria Nyanza are typically tropical, with rainfall exceeding 60 inches annually.

## Flora and Biological Distribution
Climate significantly influences the distribution of plant and animal life. In certain mountainous regions, tropical forms dominate the lower slopes. As elevation increases, warm temperate vegetation prevails, including subtropical Rosaceae, oak, *Ilex*, *Symplocos*, and *Pinus longifolia*. In the Himalaya, forests extend to 12,000 ft, with rhododendron scrub appearing up to 14,000 ft. In the west, the climate is drier and forests are less dense; deciduous trees are almost universal between 8,000 and 12,000 ft.

In other regions, such as near Trebizond, vegetation becomes almost subtropical as the coast is approached. In the marine environment, the distribution of Enteropneusta shows climatic preferences: the *Ptychoderidae* and *Spengelidae* are predominantly subtropical and tropical, whereas the *Balanoglossidae* are predominantly temperate and arctic.

## Geological and Evolutionary Context
Geologists have used plant remains to analyze ancient climates. Evidence suggests that the Eocene period began with a temperate climate in northern latitudes, later becoming at least subtropical, as indicated by the Bournemouth beds, Alum Bay, and the London Clay. However, the admixture of tropical species with temperate forms makes determining the exact degree of warmth difficult.

Regarding human evolution, it is generally supposed that man originated in subtropical or tropical latitudes before spreading toward the poles. This migration necessitated the evolution of clothing to protect the body from lower temperatures. However, climate is not the sole influence on dress; for example, Arabs in extremely hot countries are fully clothed, while Fuegians in an antarctic climate use only a skin attached by cords.

## Human Adaptation and Health
Architecture in tropical and subtropical countries is modified to accommodate the heat. British Colonial barracks in these regions are designed with increased room height and floor area to ensure the freest possible circulation of air. Buildings are often raised about 4 ft off the ground to allow air to circulate below the floors, and verandahs are used to protect walls from direct sunlight. Kitchens are typically not attached directly to dwelling-houses in hot countries, and building aspects are arranged to catch prevailing winds.

Health risks vary by region. In some tropical colonies, yellow fever and cholera occur sporadically, though these areas may be healthier than Central American states or the West Indies. In Costa Rica, malaria is common on both coasts, and rheumatism is prevalent on the Atlantic seaboard. In British East Africa, the highlands are considered very healthy, with fever being almost unknown.
