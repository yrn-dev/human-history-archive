# Balanoglossus

*Balanoglossus* is the general name for a group of peculiar, opaque, worm-like marine animals. These organisms lead an obscure existence, burrowing in sand from the regions between tide-marks down to the abyssal depths of the sea, where they are often found living under stones. Physically, they are typically yellow in color with dashes of green, brown, and red, and they frequently emit a pungent odor.

## Classification and Discovery
First discovered in 1825 by J. F. Eschscholtz at the Marshall Islands, *Balanoglossus* was initially described as a worm-like animal belonging to the Holothurians (sea-cucumbers) of the Echinoderm order. This classification changed in 1865 after Kowalevsky discovered that the animal's respiratory organs consist of numerous pairs of gill-slits that lead from the digestive canal through the body-wall to the exterior. Consequently, Gegenbaur placed the animal in a special class of Vermes known as the Enteropneusta. 

Between 1883 and 1886, embryological research conducted by Bateson demonstrated that Enteropneusta possess chordate (vertebrate) affinities. These affinities are evident in the nervous, skeletal, coelomic, and respiratory systems. Specifically, Bateson found that the gill-slits are formed on a plan similar to those of *Amphioxus*, utilizing tongue-bars that depend from the dorsal borders of the slits.

## Anatomy and Locomotion
The animal's name refers to its tongue-shaped muscular proboscis, which it uses to work its way through the sand. Locomotion is achieved through waves of contraction by the proboscis combined with the inflation of the collar (the buccal segment), which succeeds the proboscis. This process can sometimes occur with marvelous rapidity.

The third region of the body is the trunk, which can reach lengths of two feet or more. While the trunk is muscular, its muscles are of subordinate importance for movement; instead, they primarily promote the peristaltic contractions that move food through the gut.

## Feeding and Respiration
The function of alimentation is closely linked to locomotion, similar to the burrowing earthworm. As the animal excavates burrows, sand passes through its body. Any nutrient matter adhering to the sand is extracted in the intestine, and the exhausted sand is ejected through the vent at the burrow's orifice, appearing at low tide as a worm casting. To facilitate this, the mouth remains permanently open, held in place by a sustentacular apparatus (the nuchal skeleton) consisting of a pair of skeletal cornua.

Respiration is managed via gill-slits located in the anterior portion of the trunk, starting immediately behind the collar-trunk septum. There are numerous gill-slits, ranging from forty to over a hundred pairs, with new slits added by fresh perforation at the posterior end of the pharynx throughout the animal's life. 

The gill-slits feature two types of branchial bars: septal bars (between contiguous clefts) and tongue-bars. In *Balanoglossus*, the tongue-bar is the essential organ of the gill-slit, exceeding the septal bars in bulk and containing a large coelomic space. Unlike *Amphioxus*, the tongue-bar in *Balanoglossus* does not fuse with the ventral border of the cleft, resulting in a continuous U-shaped cleft. In most species, these slits do not open directly to the exterior but open into individual atrial chambers or gill-pouches, which then open to the exterior via minute gill-pores located in a longitudinal series within the branchial groove.

## Internal Systems
The vascular system of *Balanoglossus* is unique, consisting of channels and lacunae without endothelium located within the mesenteries, the gut-wall, and the basement-membrane of the body-wall. The blood is a non-corpuscular fluid propelled forward by a contractile dorsal vessel and collected into a central blood-sinus. This sinus lies over the stomochord and is surrounded on three sides by the pericardium (*Herzblase*), a closed vesicle with contractile walls. Pulsations of the pericardial vesicle drive blood into the glomerulus—a vascular complex on either side of the anterior stomochord projecting into the proboscis-coelom—from which it moves through efferent vessels to the ventral (sub-intestinal) vessel in the trunk.

The glomerulus also serves as the primary organ of excretion in existing Enteropneusta, a role previously held by coelomic pore-canals, which have since become vestigial or acquired new functions.

## Distribution
Approximately thirty species of *Balanoglossus* are known, distributed across principal marine provinces from New Zealand to Greenland. Distribution varies by group: *Balanoglossidae* are predominantly temperate and arctic, while *Spengelidae* and *Ptychoderidae* are predominantly subtropical and tropical. Examples include *Ptychodera sarniensis* in the English Channel and *Glandiceps abyssicola* (*Spengelidae*), which was dredged in the Atlantic Ocean off the coast of Africa at a depth of 2500 fathoms. Notably, a species of *Balanoglossus sensu stricto* found off the coast of New Zealand is nearly indistinguishable from one found off Japan.
