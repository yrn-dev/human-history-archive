# Karl Ernst von Baer

Karl Ernst von Baer (1792–1876) was a German biologist whose research and discoveries established him as a pre-eminent figure in the science of organic development. Born on February 29, 1792, in Piep, Esthonia, he was the son of a small landowner.

## Education and Early Influence
Von Baer attended school at Reval, which he left at the age of eighteen to pursue medical studies at Dorpat University. During his time there, he attended the lectures of K. F. Burdach (1776–1847), whose teachings suggested the value of conducting research within the broader field of life-history. Seeking greater encouragement and better facilities for scientific work, von Baer moved to Wuerzburg. There, he studied under J. I. J. Doellinger (1770–1841), a professor of anatomy and the father of a Catholic theologian. In teaching von Baer, Doellinger provided a direction to his studies that secured his future pre-eminence in the science of organic development.

## Research and Career
Von Baer began his research by collaborating with C. H. Pander (1794–1865) on the evolution of the chick. The results of these studies were first published in a treatise on physiology written by Burdach. Following this collaboration, von Baer continued his investigations independently, expanding his focus to the evolution of organisms in general. After spending time in Berlin, he was invited in 1817 by his former teacher, Burdach—who had then become a professor of anatomy at Koenigsberg—to serve as the chief of the new zoological museum and as prosector.

## Major Scientific Discoveries
Von Baer is noted for several landmark contributions to embryology and biology:

*   **The Human Ovum:** In his 1827 work, *Epistola de Ovo Mammalium et Hominis Genesi*, von Baer detailed his discovery of the human ovum. He demonstrated that the Graafian follicles in the ovary are not the actual eggs; rather, they contain a spherical vesicle which is the true ovum. This body, measuring approximately one one-hundred-and-twentieth of an inch in diameter, contains the properties that transmit physical and mental characteristics from parents, grandparents, or more distant ancestors.
*   **Vertebrate Development:** In his *History of the Evolution of Animals* (*Ueber die Entwickelungsgeschichte der Thiere*), published in two parts in 1828 and 1837, he showed that in all vertebrates, the primary stage of cleavage of the fertilized egg is followed by a modification into leaf-like germ layers. These layers—mucous, vascular, muscular, and skin—are the source from which the body's various organs arise through differentiation.
*   **Other Contributions:** Von Baer also discovered a cylindrical, gelatinous cord. Additionally, his work extended to other regions; he authored "Kaspische Studien," published in *Bull. Sci. St-Petersbourg* (1855–1859) and Erman's *Archiv russ.* (1855–1856).

## Legacy and Influence
The embryological teachings of von Baer had a wide-reaching influence on subsequent scientists. His work helped the scientific community recognize the critical importance of understanding the development of animals for zoological and morphological studies. One such influenced scientist was Kölliker, who entered the line of inquiry championed by von Baer, eventually applying newer microscopic techniques—such as staining, section-cutting, and hardening—to the study of mammalian, chick, and amphibian embryos, as well as invertebrates.
