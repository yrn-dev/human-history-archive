# The Great Depression: The Global Economic Collapse of the 1930s

## Overview
The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world. While traditionally dated from the stock market crash of October 1929 to the onset of World War II, the crisis was not a single event but a systemic failure of global finance, agriculture, and trade. 

The collapse was characterized by a catastrophic spiral: a crash in asset prices led to bank failures, which wiped out life savings and constricted credit. This triggered a collapse in consumer spending, leading to mass unemployment—reaching nearly 25% in the United States—and a sharp decline in industrial production. Internationally, the crisis was exacerbated by the gold standard and protectionist policies, such as the U.S. Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, which stifled global trade.

Among historians and economists, there remains a vigorous debate regarding the primary cause. Monetarists, led by Milton Friedman, argue that the Federal Reserve failed by allowing the money supply to contract. Conversely, Keynesian economists argue that the depression was caused by a collapse in aggregate demand and a failure of private investment.

## Key Facts
*   **The Dust Bowl:** Environmental catastrophe struck the American Great Plains, where severe drought and poor farming practices created massive dust storms, forcing thousands of "Okies" to migrate west.
*   **The New Deal:** President Franklin D. Roosevelt implemented a series of programs (the "Three Rs": Relief, Recovery, and Reform) including the Social Security Act and the Works Progress Administration (WPA).
*   **Bank Runs:** Because deposits were not insured at the time, rumors of insolvency led crowds to rush banks to withdraw their money, causing healthy banks to collapse.
*   **Global Reach:** While the U.S. was the epicenter, Germany was hit particularly hard, which historians often link to the political instability that allowed the rise of the Nazi Party.

## Did You Know?
1. **The "Bank Holiday":** Upon taking office in 1933, FDR declared a four-day national "bank holiday" to stop the panic and allow the government to inspect banks before they reopened.
2. **Deflationary Spiral:** Prices didn't just stop rising; they plummeted. This meant that while goods were cheaper, debts became harder to pay because the real value of the debt increased.
3. **The GDP Drop:** Between 1929 and 1933, the U.S. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) fell by approximately 30%.
