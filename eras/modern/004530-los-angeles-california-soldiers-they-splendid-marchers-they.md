# Los Angeles, California

Los Angeles is a city and the county-seat of Los Angeles county, located in southern California, U.S.A. It is situated along the small Los Angeles river in the foothills of the San Gabriel Mountains. The city is connected to its waterfront on the ocean, San Pedro Bay, by a narrow strip of land 18 miles long.

## History and Governance
The Pueblo de Nuestra Señora la Reina de los Angeles was founded in 1781, ten years after the establishment of the Franciscan mission of San Gabriel. By approximately 1827, as the largest pueblo in the territory, Los Angeles became a rival to Monterey for the status of California's capital. During this period, it served as a stronghold for the South California party and a center for conspiracies intended to overthrow Mexican authority.

The Mexican Congress declared Los Angeles a city and its capital in 1835, though the capital designation was not enforced and was later recalled. Between 1836 and 1838, the city served as the headquarters for C. A. Carrillo, a legally-named governor whose jurisdiction was not recognized in the north. The city functioned as the actual capital from 1845 to 1847.

During the war between Mexico and the United States, factional quarrels divided the city until the arrival of General John C. Frémont and Commodore Robert F. Stockton. The defenders of Los Angeles fled, and the American flag was raised over the city on August 13, 1846. Following a revolt by inhabitants in October, a garrison of fifty men was forced to withdraw. The city was not retaken until January 18, 1847, when Commodore Stockton and General Philip Kearny entered. This event marked the only important overt resistance to the new régime in California. Los Angeles was officially chartered in 1850.

## Urban Growth and Infrastructure
The city experienced steady growth, accelerated by railway connections: the Central Pacific and San Francisco in 1876, and the Santa Fé system to the East in 1885. This latter connection triggered land booms and railway wars that stimulated southern California. Population growth became particularly remarkable after 1890. Census data shows the population grew from 11,183 in 1880 to 50,395 in 1890, 102,479 in 1900 (including 19,964 foreign-born residents), and 319,198 by 1910. By 1910, the city covered 85.1 square miles, with over half of that area added since 1890.

Transportation is provided by the Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fé, the Southern Pacific, and the San Pedro, Los Angeles & Salt Lake railways, as well as steamers to San Francisco. The city features five electric railway systems with 300 miles of track within the city and 700 miles within a 30-mile radius. Other transit includes subways extending to the western limits and inclined railways ascending Court Street Hill and Third Street Hill.

The harbor at San Pedro Bay was improved by the Federal government, including a 9,250-foot breakwater begun in 1898 and the deepening of the bar. Improvements to the inner harbor at Wilmington began in 1907.

## Economy and Industry
The economic center of Los Angeles is the culture of fruits, utilizing the fertile surrounding country to produce lemons, oranges, figs, and other semi-tropical fruits. The region between San Bernardino, Santa Clara, and Los Angeles is one of the world's most important artesian well regions. To secure its water supply, the city authorized $23,000,000 in 4% bonds in 1907 to build a 209-mile aqueduct from the Owens river in the Sierra Nevada Mountains, a project begun in 1908.

Manufacturing grew significantly between 1900 and 1905, with factory product values increasing by 130% and employed capital increasing by 180.5%. Leading industries in 1905 included:
* Slaughtering and meat-packing ($4,040,162)
* Foundry and machine shop work ($3,146,914)
* Flour and grist milling ($2,798,740)
* Lumber manufacturing and planing ($2,519,081)
* Printing and publishing ($3,326,180 combined for newspapers, periodicals, and book printing)
* Car construction and repairing ($1,549,836)
* Confectionery ($953,915), furniture ($879,910), and malt liquors ($789,393)

The city also maintains important fruit and vegetable canning industries and a large wholesale trade with Nevada's gold-fields, Arizona, and southern California.

## Geography and Culture
The city is situated between the sea and the mountains, with a regular, level business district and suburbs located on hills. It is noted for its climate; between 1877 and 1908, the mean minimum for January was 44.16° F and for August was 60.1° F.

The city is beautified by semi-tropic vegetation, including rubber trees, tropic palms, yuccas, giant bananas, towering eucalyptus, and pepper trees, as well as jasmine, orange trees, and various flowers. There are 22 parks totaling approximately 3,800 acres, including Griffith (3,015 acres), Elysian (532 acres), Eastlake (57 acres), Echo (38 acres), and Westlake (35 acres).

Building styles have been influenced by Spanish-Moorish mission architecture. Notable structures include the City Hall, the Federal Building, the County Court House, the Woman's Club, the large Auditorium, and a County Hall of Records. The Public Library held about 110,000 volumes in 1908. The Chamber of Commerce Building houses an exhibit on southern California's resources and the Coronel Collection, which contains relics of the Mexican and Spanish eras.

Additionally, the city has been a site for the publication of Basque newspapers.
