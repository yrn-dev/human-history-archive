# Hungarian Government and Legislative Developments (1906–1909)

The political landscape of Hungary during the early 20th century was characterized by a critical struggle between two opposing principles: Magyar nationalism, which sought to maintain ascendancy within an independent Hungary, and Habsburg imperialism, which aimed to preserve the military and economic unity of the Dual Monarchy.

## Political Conflict and the 1907 Agreement
By the early 1900s, the tactical advantage in the conflict between the monarchy and the Magyars lay with the crown. The ascendancy of the Magyars was based on a narrow and artificial franchise, leaving them in a minority within Hungary. This allowed the king-emperor to potentially appeal to the disfranchised majority as a means of pressure. 

A turning point occurred with the introduction of a Universal Suffrage Bill by Joseph Kristoffy, the minister of the interior in the "unconstitutional" cabinet of Baron Fejervary. This legislative move brought the leaders of the Opposition in the Hungarian parliament to terms, facilitating the agreement of 1907. However, this agreement served only as a temporary truce.

## The Wekerle Ministry and Unresolved Issues
The Wekerle ministry, which took office on April 9, 1906, succeeded the Fejervary cabinet but contained elements that prevented a lasting compromise. Several critical issues remained unsettled:
* **The "Magyar word of command"**: This burning question remained unresolved, except for the fixed determination of the king-emperor.
* **The Austro-Hungarian State Bank**: The renewal of the bank's charter was not included in the 1907 agreement.
* **Franchise Reform**: Although the Wekerle ministry was pledged to franchise reform—a change rendered inevitable by the granting of universal suffrage in the Austrian half of the Monarchy—the ministry showed no eagerness to fulfill this pledge.

## The Bank Charter Proposal of 1908
In March 1908, a formal proposal was laid before the Hungarian parliament by Mr. Hallo regarding the charter of the Austro-Hungarian Bank, which was set to expire at the end of 1910. The proposal suggested that the charter should not be renewed. Furthermore, it proposed that if negotiations for a convention between the banks of Austria and Hungary failed, a separate Hungarian Bank should be established. While the Balkan crisis obscured this issue during the winter, the matter became acute again in the spring of 1909, causing sharp divisions of opinion within the Coalition cabinet.

## Historical Context of Magyar Nationalism
The tensions of the 1900s were rooted in a longer history of nationalist sentiment. Earlier, the Army bills of 1889, which sought to increase the peace footing of the joint Austro-Hungarian army, were passed with difficulty. During this period, Count Julius Andrassy the Elder and Koloman Tisza worked to carry the bills. Count Andrassy was forced to solemnly warn the country against the dangers of nationalist Chauvinism and remind the public of the obligations established under the Compact of 1867, as demands for the "Magyarization" of the joint army grew.

Similar nationalist expressions appeared during negotiations for the renewal of the economic *Ausgleich* (Customs and Trade Alliance). In 1902, Premier Szell and the Austrian premier Koerber concluded the Szell-Koerber Compact, which established a joint Austro-Hungarian tariff. Although initially seen as a pledge of peaceful development, the compact was never fully ratified by either parliament and was eventually discarded.
