# Hydrobromic Acid

Hydrobromic acid (HBr) is the only compound formed from hydrogen and bromine. In many respects, it is similar to hydrochloric acid, although it is characterized as being rather less stable. It is classified as one of the "strong" acids, as it is ionized to a very large extent even in concentrated solutions.

## Physical Properties
At ordinary temperatures, hydrobromic acid exists as a colourless gas that fumes strongly when exposed to moist air. It possesses an acid reaction and taste. The gas can be condensed into a liquid with a boiling point of -64.9° C (under a pressure of 738.2 mm.). Further cooling of the liquid produces colourless crystals that melt at -88.5° C.

Hydrobromic acid is readily soluble in water. An aqueous solution saturated at 0° C has a specific gravity of 1.78. When the aqueous acid is boiled, it loses either water or acid until a solution of constant boiling point is reached; this solution boils at 126° C under atmospheric pressure and contains 48% acid. The strength of this constant boiling solution varies if the atmospheric pressure varies.

## Preparation Methods
There are several methods for preparing hydrobromic acid, depending on the desired purity and state:

*   **Gas Preparation:** The gas can be produced by passing bromine vapour and hydrogen gas through a tube containing a heated platinum spiral. A common method for liberating the gas rapidly involves dropping bromine onto a mixture of water and amorphous phosphorus, which triggers a violent reaction.
*   **Aqueous Solution:** An aqueous solution may be created by passing bromine water through sulphuretted hydrogen.
*   **Hydrocarbon Action:** The acid can be obtained through the direct action of bromine on various saturated hydrocarbons, such as paraffin-wax, though the resulting product is somewhat impure.
*   **Pure Hydrobromic Acid:** Alexander Scott (1900) described a method for preparing pure hydrobromic acid. This involves placing bromine in a large flask covered by a layer of water, then passing sulphur dioxide into the water until the mixture becomes pale yellow. This solution is distilled in a slow current of air and further purified by distillation over barium bromide.

It is noted that concentrated sulphuric acid cannot be used to prepare the acid with any degree of purity from bromides, as secondary reactions lead to the formation of sulphur dioxide and the liberation of free bromine.

## Chemical Interactions and Relations
Hydrobromic acid is related to several oxy-acids of bromine, including hypobromous acid (HBrO), bromous acid (HBrO₂), and bromic acid (HBrO₃). Bromic acid is very unstable and decomposes into bromine, oxygen, and water when heated to 100° C. It can be rapidly converted into hydrobromic acid by reducing agents such as sulphur dioxide and sulphuretted hydrogen. Additionally, hydrobromic acid can decompose bromic acid according to the equation: $\text{HBrO}_3 + 5\text{HBr} = 3\text{H}_2\text{O} + 3\text{Br}_2$.

In organic chemistry, hydrobromic acid can influence the character of certain compounds. For example, in the bromination of oxyazobenzenes, if the produced hydrobromic acid remains in the system, a brombenzene-azo-phenol is formed. However, if the mineral acid is removed (such as by adding sodium acetate), bromination occurs in the phenolic nucleus. The presence of the mineral acid gives the azo compound a pseudo-quinonoid character.

## Applications
Hydrobromic acid is used as a diluent in some chemical reactions, though it is used less commonly than solvents like water, ether, chloroform, or acetic acid.
