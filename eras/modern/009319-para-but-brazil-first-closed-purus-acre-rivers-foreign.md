# Brazilian Control of the Purus and Acre Rivers

## Commercial Access and the Port of Puerto Acre
The commercial geography of Bolivia has been significantly impacted by the loss of its maritime department, leaving the nation without ports other than those of Lake Titicaca (specifically Guaqui, or Huaqui, which trades with Puno in Peru) and the affluents of the Paraguay and Madeira rivers. Because these outlets require transhipment through foreign territory, the cost of transport is increased, and neighboring nations are positioned to exclude Bolivia from direct commercial intercourse with other countries.

An exception to this restriction previously existed at Puerto Acre, located on the Acre river. This port was accessible to ocean-going steamers ascending from Para. However, Brazil first closed the Purus and Acre rivers to foreign vessels attempting to reach this port. Subsequently, under a treaty signed in 1903, Brazil acquired possession of the port and the adjacent territory.

## The Acre Dispute and the Treaty of Petropolis
The dispute over the Acre region was driven by the presence of valuable rubber forests in the upper Purus. The conflict was exacerbated by the fact that the majority of individuals engaged in the rubber industry in the district were Brazilians, who resisted the authority of Bolivian officials. This tension led to a declaration of independence by the state of Acre and prompted Bolivia to dispatch troops in 1900 to restore order.

President Colonel Pando, who took office on October 26, 1899, sought to avoid hostilities with Brazil. Through diplomatic means and a spirit of concession, the dispute was settled amicably in November 1903 via the Treaty of Petropolis. Under this agreement, a portion of the Acre province was ceded to Brazil in exchange for a cash indemnity of $10,000,000.

## Boundary Modifications
The boundary between Bolivia and Brazil originated from the treaties of Madrid (1750) and San Ildefonso (1777), with further modifications in 1867 and 1903. The 1867 treaty had shifted the starting point of the frontier to the mouth of the Beni, which granted a large area of territory to Brazil.

The 1903 treaty established a new boundary line that ascends the Abuna river to latitude 10 deg. 20' S., then runs west along that parallel to the Rapirran river. From there, it follows the Rapirran to its principal source, moves west to the Ituxy river and follows it to its source, then to the source of Bahia Creek, and finally follows the Acre (or Aquiry) river to its source. Additionally, the 1903 modification included a recession of approximately 1158 sq. m. to Bolivia to improve commercial facilities on the Paraguay river.

## Current Bolivian Outlets to the Amazon
Following the acquisition of Puerto Acre by Brazil, Bolivia's outlet to the Amazon is restricted to the Madeira river. Navigation on the Madeira is interrupted by a series of falls before reaching Bolivian territory. The Bolivian port of entry for this trade, Villa Bella, is located above these falls at the confluence of the Mamore and Beni rivers. Reaching Villa Bella from the lower river requires a long and costly portage. Furthermore, Villa Bella is shut off from navigable rivers further upstream by the falls of the Mamore and Beni.

To remedy this unfavorable situation and promote the development of north-eastern Bolivia—a region featuring an admirable system of navigable rivers above the falls—Brazil is to build a railway.

## Other River Systems and Ports
Bolivian river systems are divided into the Central Plateau, La Plata, and Amazon regions. The Amazon system, covering more than half of the republic, includes the Beni and Mamore rivers, which unite at latitude 10 deg. 20' S. to form the Madeira. The Mamore is approximately 600 m. long, with three-fourths of its length being navigable from Chimore to the rapids near its mouth.

In the south, Bolivian trade is connected to the upper Paraguay through Puerto Suarez on Lake Caceres and Puerto Pacheco on Bahia Negra. Puerto Quijarro on Lake Gaiba is a projected port intended to be more accessible than others in the region. If trade in southern Bolivia becomes sufficient to justify opening a navigable channel in the Pilcomayo, direct river communication with Montevideo and Buenos Aires will become possible.
