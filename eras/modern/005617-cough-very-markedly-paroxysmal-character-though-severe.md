# Bronchiectasis and Related Bronchial Conditions

## Bronchiectasis: Definition and Causes
Bronchiectasis, derived from the Greek words *bronchia* (bronchial tubes) and *ektasis* (extension), is the dilatation of the bronchi. This condition occurs in connection with various lung diseases. It may follow acute or chronic bronchitis, phthisis, chronic pneumonia, and acute pneumonia or broncho-pneumonia, with the position of the dilatation determined by the primary lesion. Mechanical causes can also lead to bronchiectasis, such as bronchial or tracheal obstruction resulting from new growth or the pressure of an aneurism.

While previously viewed as a disease of middle age, Dr. Walter Carr has demonstrated that it is fairly common in debilitated children following whooping cough or measles. The dilatation is typically cylindrical, though it is more rarely saccular. Generally, the smaller and medium-sized tubes are affected, unless the cause is mechanical. This affection usually occurs in only one lung and is very commonly accompanied by emphysema.

## Symptoms of Bronchiectasis
Although initial symptoms may resemble bronchitis, later symptoms are distinctive. The cough is severe and intermittent, though very markedly paroxysmal in character; patients may be entirely free of the cough for many hours at a time. Posture has a marked effect on symptoms: a patient lying on the affected side may remain free from coughing throughout the night, but bending forward or turning to the sound side triggers the expulsion of large quantities of bronchial secretion.

Expectoration is characterized by its abundance and the manner in which it is expelled. In saccular dilatation, secretions may gush from the mouth with suddenness and in large quantities. Because secretions are retained and decomposed *in situ*, they are very commonly foetid. While haemoptysis and dyspnoea occur occasionally, they are not the rule. The presence of pyrexia is a serious symptom indicating septic absorption in the bronchi and may precede gangrene, which is accompanied by sweating and severe attacks of shivering. In long-term cases, clubbing of the toes and fingers is very common.

## Acute and Chronic Bronchitis
Acute bronchitis is a severe ailment that is particularly dangerous for the aged and infants, as it tends to assume a capillary form (broncho-pneumonia) in these groups, which can be fatal within forty-eight hours. In capillary bronchitis, inflammation affects the minute ramifications of the bronchial tubes near the air-cells, leading to imminent peril due to interrupted aeration of the blood. Symptoms include incessant cough, rapid and laboured respiration, livid lips, and suffocative paroxysms that make a recumbent posture impossible.

In mild cases of acute bronchitis, treatment may involve a light diet, warm diluent drinks, and confinement in bed or a warm room. For more developed cases, medicines to promote perspiration and allay fever are used. To favor expectoration and soothe cough pain, mixtures of tolu with opiates like paregorics may be used, though opium is cautioned against in young children without medical advice. After a few days, scanty viscid or frothy expectoration becomes copious and purulent, after which urgent symptoms usually abate, though the cough may persist for three to four weeks.

Chronic bronchitis may result from repeated acute attacks or exist independently. It is more frequent in older persons and often presents as a "winter cough" that recurs during colder seasons and departs in summer. In advanced cases, the cough is seldom entirely absent and is usually more troublesome in the morning. This form is characterized by free and copious expectoration, which may occasionally be so abundant as to be termed bronchorrhoea.

## Asthma and Paroxysmal Conditions
Asthma is associated with an unstable and excitable respiratory centre and a morbid connection to some part of the nasal apparatus, though Dr. Alexander Francis noted it is rarely caused by mechanical obstruction of the nasal passages. The disease occurs twice as frequently in men as in women and is linked to neurotic inheritance. In infants and young children, paroxysmal bronchitis is a form of this disease. Dr. James Goodhart has noted connections between asthma and skin troubles such as eczema and psoriasis.

Attacks can begin suddenly or be preceded by warnings, such as an overpowering desire for sleep (as noted by Dr. Hyde Salter) or mental excitement. Some patients experience profuse diuresis, flatulence, constipation, or loss of appetite. Attacks often begin during the night, causing the patient to struggle for breath, often assuming a specific attitude to use auxiliary respiratory muscles. Physical signs during an attack include a pale or livid face, bulging eyes, protruding forehead veins, and whistling or wheezing breath.
