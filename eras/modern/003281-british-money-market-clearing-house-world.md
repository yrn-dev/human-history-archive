# The British Money Market

The British money market, described as the clearing-house of the world, is a complex financial system centered in London. At the beginning of the 20th century, its operations were managed by several key working organs. These are divided into two primary groups: the most constantly active organs, which include the Bank of England, joint-stock and private banks (including several large foreign banks), discount houses, and bill-brokers; and those that are intermittently active, consisting of certain members of the Stock Exchange as well as certain great finance houses and merchants.

While many of the great merchant houses are nominally merchants, they are largely involved in finance business, such as issuing capital for companies and making loans to foreign governments. Many of these houses are registered as bankers, although they do not perform the functions typically associated with that term in the United Kingdom.

## Historical Development
In the 18th century, the money market was composed of various merchants and the Bank of England, with the distinction between the two not yet fully established. The modern form of the London money market began to emerge toward the end of that century with the appearance of bill brokers, who acted as dealers in credit. 

The development of the market was further influenced by the reconstitution of the Bank of England via the Act of 1844, as well as the formation of discount companies and joint-stock banks. In the common parlance of the City, the term "the market" is often used in a special sense to refer specifically to discount houses or banks, particularly in newspaper reports regarding loans from the Bank of England.

## Market Vulnerabilities and Fluctuations
Due to the smallness of its reserve, the British money market is more exposed to fluctuations than those of any other country. These fluctuations can be triggered by domestic trade or the requirements of other nations for specie. 

The market faces a tendency for currents to flow outwards, a situation enhanced by the increase in foreign banks maintaining branches in England. Furthermore, foreign nations have gained significant power over the British money market; for example, in 1906, there was a recorded excess of imports over exports totaling £147,000,000. While this difference is eventually balanced by "invisible" exports, it creates a need for sufficient reserves to meet these requirements.

## Management of Large-Scale Loans
The London money market has developed modern methods to handle the disturbances caused by massive financial operations, such as the war loans raised by Japan in 1904, 1905, and 1906. These loans were issued through three banks: a Japanese institution representing the Japanese government, a leading London bank, and the principal British bank operating in China.

To minimize disturbance to the London market, banks employed a system of rapid recycling of cash. "Application money" withdrawn from other banks was lent back into the short loan market, often on the afternoon of the issue date. This required heavy work and the clearing of a vast number of cheques in a short window of time. 

The Japanese government also managed its balances considerately to avoid embarrassing the London market. While the government promised not to withdraw the loans in gold, it had freedom in how it employed the money. Consequently, the government transferred a portion of its deposits to the Bank of England.
