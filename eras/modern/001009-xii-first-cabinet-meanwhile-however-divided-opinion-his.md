# The Cabinet of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman

## Formation and Composition
The government of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman was formed in late November 1905 following the resignation of Mr. Balfour as Prime Minister. This administration was designed to represent both wings of the Liberal Party, which had been divided by the Boer War. 

The composition of the cabinet included "Liberal Imperialists" in several of the most significant offices: Mr. Asquith was appointed to the Exchequer, Sir Edward Grey took the Foreign Office, and Mr. Haldane became the Secretary of State for War. Conversely, the Prime Minister appointed figures who represented the main stream of Liberal opinion, such as Mr. John Morley (Secretary of State for India) and Sir Robert Reid (Lord Chancellor), both of whom had consistently condemned the war in South Africa. The cabinet also included democratic politicians who had taken an even stronger stance against the war, specifically Mr. John Burns and Mr. Lloyd George. The administration's dignity was further enhanced by the inclusion of Sir Henry Fowler, Lord Ripon, and Lord Elgin, the newly returned Viceroy of India.

## Parliamentary Structure
The cabinet of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, formed in 1905, followed the practice of dividing ministerial strength between the two Houses of Parliament. In this specific cabinet, there were thirteen ministers in the House of Commons and six ministers in the House of Lords. This distribution reflects a general trend toward strengthening the ministerial bench in the Commons, particularly for "spending departments" such as the First Lord of the Admiralty and the Secretary of State for War, who are ideally members of the House that votes on supplies.

## The Cabinet Council and Constitutional Principles
The Cabinet Council serves as the government of Great Britain, exercising nearly all powers vested in the sovereign. It operates on two fundamental principles established during the reign of William III: that a ministry must consist of statesmen sharing the same political principles, and that it must maintain a parliamentary basis by commanding a majority of votes in the legislature. 

Members of the cabinet are jointly and severally responsible for all measures. While differences of opinion may exist internally, they remain unknown to the public as long as the cabinet lasts; once such differences are publicly manifested, the cabinet is considered at an end. Consequently, the cabinet is collectively responsible to both the sovereign for executive business and to parliament for its legislative and executive conduct.

## Crisis and Deliberation
Under the leadership of Mr. Asquith, the cabinet faced critical deliberations regarding the European situation, meeting daily or twice daily. During these sessions, events shifted rapidly: the German troops violated the Grand Duchy of Luxemburg, and a German ultimatum was delivered to Belgium. The situation became decisive when the King of the Belgians appealed for the guaranteeing Powers to uphold the Treaty regarding Belgian neutrality. By Monday, a majority of Mr. Asquith’s colleagues viewed war as inevitable, though it appeared that numerous resignations would follow.

Simultaneously, the cabinet struggled with the "Irish problem" following the breakdown of the Buckingham Palace Conference. Discussions were characterized by fierce antagonisms over seemingly petty issues, specifically the boundaries of Tyrone and Fermanagh. The cabinet sought an exit from this deadlock to avoid the possibility of civil war and to achieve a decisive abatement of party strife in Britain, a goal that had been pursued since Mr. Lloyd George first mooted schemes of unity and co-operation in 1910.
