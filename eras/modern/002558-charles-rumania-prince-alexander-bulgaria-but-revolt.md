# Charles of Rumania and Prince Alexander of Bulgaria

## Prince Alexander of Bulgaria
Prince Alexander of Battenberg was elected as the first sovereign of Bulgaria on April 29, 1879, by the assembly at Trnovo. A member of the grand ducal house of Hesse and a nephew of Tsar Alexander II, he arrived in Bulgaria on July 7 of that year at the age of twenty-three. Upon his arrival, he found that all civil and military authority was held by Russia.

The early years of Prince Alexander's reign were characterized by a struggle against the autocratic institutions he attempted to establish under Russian guidance, as well as a strong Bulgarian reaction against Russian tutelage. While the popular party in the Trnovo assembly adopted the slogan "Bulgaria for the Bulgarians," the inexperienced Prince Alexander initially selected a "Conservative" ministry based on the suggestions of the Russian consul-general. This ministry conflicted with the parliamentary majority, eventually forcing the Prince to form a "Liberal" administration under Karaveloff and Tzankoff. The Liberals launched a campaign against foreigners, specifically Russians, by passing an alien law and removing foreigners from lucrative positions.

Following a period of chaos, Prince Alexander obtained the Tsar's consent to change the constitution and assumed absolute authority on May 9, 1881. During his tenure, he founded the Order of St Alexander in 1881, which was later reconstituted in 1888 by Prince Ferdinand.

## The Revolt of Eastern Rumelia
The Treaty of Berlin (July 13, 1878) had divided the Bulgarian nation, creating the principality of Bulgaria and the autonomous province of Eastern Rumelia. This division was viewed as artificial, and the Bulgarian population in Eastern Rumelia continued to protest. Two political parties emerged in the province: the Conservatives (or "Unionists") and the Radicals (known as "Kazioni"). Both sought union with the principality.

In 1885, the Kazioni, led by Zakharia Stoyánoff, organized a conspiracy to overthrow the governor-general, Krstovitch Pasha. After securing the acquiescence of Prince Alexander, the revolutionaries seized the governor-general and proclaimed the union with Bulgaria on September 18. The revolt occurred without bloodshed, and Prince Alexander entered Philippopolis shortly thereafter. This action caused international scandal due to the infraction of the Berlin Treaty; while Great Britain showed sympathy, Russia denounced the union and encouraged the Porte to reconquer the province.

## Balkan Diplomacy and King Charles of Rumania
In the aftermath of the war of 1877, there was a reaction against Russia. During this period, the Servian statesman Ristich advocated for a combination of young Balkan states to resist foreign interference and compromise on national aims. This idea included the possibility of a constitutional Turkey joining the confederation. This project was favorably regarded by Prince Alexander of Bulgaria and King Charles of Rumania.

However, the project was abandoned following the revolt of Eastern Rumelia, the subsequent Servo-Bulgarian War, and the coercion of Greece by the powers, all of which increased rivalry between the races. The idea of a Balkan alliance was revived in 1891 by Tricoupis as an offensive alliance against Turkey to partition the Sultan's European possessions. While Servia favored this, it was frustrated by Stamboloff, who reported the scheme to the Porte.

## Later Territorial Conflicts
The relationship between Bulgaria and Rumania remained strained over territorial disputes, specifically regarding the Dobrudja. Under the Treaty of San Stefano, the Dobrudja was not included in the Bulgarian state, as it was reserved as compensation for Rumania following the Russian annexation of Bessarabia; this was later sanctioned by the Treaty of Berlin.

During World War I, Bulgaria joined the Teutonic powers in 1915. The German alliance offered Bulgaria the chance to reclaim the southern Dobrudja, which had been annexed by Rumania in 1913. Following Rumania's intervention on the Allied side in 1916, Bulgarian troops defeated the Rumanians and overran the entire Dobrudja up to the mouth of the Danube, eventually parading through the streets of Bukharest.

Despite this victory, Germany did not grant Bulgaria the entire peninsula, as Germany wished to control the mouth of the Danube and the main railroad trunk-line from Central Europe to the Black Sea. Consequently, Bulgaria was given only the southern Dobrudja, while the rest was held "in common." When Rumania made peace with the Central powers in the spring of 1918, the Dobrudja prize was ceded to the Central powers rather than to Bulgaria.
