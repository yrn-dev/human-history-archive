# Lombard Street and the System of Deposit-Banking

In the history of English finance, the relationship between the issuance of bank notes and the development of deposit-banking is a central theme. This connection is notably analyzed in the work *Lombard Street*, written by Walter Bagehot and published in 1873.

## The Role of Note Issuance in Early Banking
According to Bagehot in *Lombard Street*, the practice of issuing notes served as the introduction to the system of deposit-banking in England's early days. This mechanism was so fundamental to the viability of financial institutions that a bank lacking the power to issue notes could scarcely exist if it were located outside of London.

## Legal Framework of English Banking
The banking environment in which these systems evolved was strictly regulated by a series of legislative acts. The Bank of England was incorporated by the act of 1694. Subsequent legislation, specifically the acts of 1697 and 1708, restricted the competition. The 1697 act declared that no joint-stock bank was to be established within England for as long as the Bank of England continued. The 1708 act further stipulated that no partnership or company exceeding six persons in England could borrow, owe, or take up money on bills or notes payable on demand, or at any time less than six months from the date of borrowing. These restrictions were confirmed by the act of 1800.

Significant changes to these regulations occurred in 1826. One act of that year prohibited bank notes under £5, while a second Banking Act permitted the establishment of co-partnerships exceeding six persons (which were necessarily joint-stock companies) provided they were located more than 65 miles from London. In 1833, the law was amended to allow joint-stock banks to be established within the 65-mile limit and removed various restrictions on notes for amounts less than £50. However, joint-stock banks within that 65-mile radius were still not permitted the power of issuing notes.

## Walter Bagehot and "Lombard Street"
Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873) is described as a brilliant picture of the city at the time of its writing. Bagehot was known for a conversational, epigrammatic style and a scientific, dispassionate approach to subjects that had not previously been treated as scientific studies. 

Bagehot's insights were informed by his extensive professional and social connections. He was connected to the *Economist* starting in 1858 and married the daughter of its first editor, James Wilson, who served as secretary of the treasury and later as secretary of finance in India. Through these ties, Bagehot became an intimate of political figures such as Gladstone, Robert Lowe, Grant Duff, and Sir George Cornewall Lewis, as well as the permanent heads of great state departments. In the financial sector, he maintained close relationships with the directors and governor of the Bank of England and other leading commercial and banking magnates. He was also a member of the Political Economy Club.

## The Financial Landscape of Lombard Street
Lombard Street has historically been a hub for banking houses. For example, the firm Barclay & Co., Ltd., located on Lombard Street, amalgamated with various other banks, including the Gurney & Co. banks established in the eastern counties at the end of the 18th century. Additionally, Martin & Co. of Lombard Street (operating as Martin's Bank, Ltd.) is noted as being descended from the goldsmiths who kept "running cashes" as early as 1677.
