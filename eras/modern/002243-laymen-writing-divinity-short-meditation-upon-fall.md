# Laymen's Writing in Divinity, with a Short Meditation upon the Fall of Lucifer

*Apologie for Laymen's Writing in Divinity, with a Short Meditation upon the Fall of Lucifer* is a work published in 1641 by Sir Richard Baker, Knight. 

## Author and Context
Sir Richard Baker was a prolific writer who produced a significant portion of his work while imprisoned. His literary output spanned various genres and subjects, including translations and historical chronicles. Among his most notable works is the *Chronicle of the Kings of England from the Time of the Romans' Government unto the Death of King James*, first published in 1643. While this *Chronicle* gained extreme popularity and was translated into Dutch in 1649 (and later continued by Edward Phillips), it is noted for having slight historical value due to numerous inaccuracies.

In addition to his historical writing, Baker authored several religious and moral texts. These include *Meditations on the Lord's Prayer* (1637), *Motives for Prayer upon the seaven dayes of ye weeke* (1642), and various *Meditations* based on the psalms of David, which were later edited and collected by A. B. Grosart in 1882. His other publications include *Cato Variegatus* (or *Catoes Morall Distichs*), published in 1636, a 1638 translation of *New Epistles* by Monsieur D'Balzac, and a 1642 translation of Malvezzi's *Discourses upon Cornelius Tacitus*. Baker also wrote *Theatrum Redivivum, or The Theatre Vindicated* in 1642 as a response to William Prynne's *Histrio-Mastix*.

## Broader Context of Lay Participation in Divinity
The concept of laymen engaging in divinity and religious administration is reflected in various historical movements. In Germany, a "sturdy common-sense religion" emerged, asserting that laymen could hold authority in departments previously reserved for the clergy. This movement saw the supervision of charity shift from the church to laymen, the rise of religious confraternities independent of clerical guidance, and the distribution of anti-clerical, mystical, or socialist tracts by medieval Brethren. Additionally, the *jus episcopale* claimed by Luther for secular authorities had been practiced in Brandenburg and Saxony, where districts and cities established police regulations that overrode ecclesiastical decrees regarding begging and holidays.

Similarly, in thirteenth-century Flanders, the poet Jacob Van Maerlandt—regarded as the father of all Flemish poets—utilized the didactical genre to provide laymen with access to knowledge that had been monopolized by clergymen. Maerlandt's work covered ethics, politics, natural history, and both profane and sacred history.

In the early Christian era, the revelation of the *Logos* led to a shift where individuals regardless of sex, occupation, or age—those least exercised in abstract reasoning—aspired to contemplate the Divine Nature. This era saw the rise of popular discourse and private meditation on theology, with Tertullian boasting that a Christian mechanic could answer questions that had perplexed the wisest Grecian sages.
