# Wheat Production and Export in Sind and Gujarat

## Regional Geography and Agriculture
Sind and Gujarat are two of the five well-demarcated tracts that comprise the level parts of the Bombay Presidency. Sind, located in the lower valley of the Indus, is characterized by a very flat landscape with scanty vegetation; its productiveness is entirely dependent on irrigation. In contrast, Gujarat consists primarily of rich, highly cultivated alluvial plains watered by the Nerbudda and Tapti rivers, though it is not frequently subject to inundation. The plains of eastern Gujarat, like those of Sind, are covered by wind-blown sand and alluvium.

Agriculture in these regions is diverse. In the northern part of the Presidency, specifically within Sind and Gujarat, wheat is grown. While other staple grains such as joar and bajra are primary food sources in Khandesh and the Deccan, and rice is the chief product of the Konkan, wheat serves as a significant crop for the northern territories.

## Export of Wheat to Europe
Wheat grown in Sind and Gujarat is exported to Europe in large quantities. The primary port for these large-scale exports is Karachi. Wheat is also exported from Bombay, although this occurs on a smaller scale.

## Regional Demographics and Culture
The two regions differ significantly in their predominant religions and languages. In Sind, Islam has been the predominant religion since the earliest Arab conquest in the 8th century, and the chief language spoken is Sindhi. 

In Gujarat, Hinduism is the predominant religion, although the province shows the influence of petty Mahommedan kingdoms. The languages spoken in Gujarat include Gujarati and Hindustani, while Cutchi is spoken in Cutch.

## Historical Context
The history of these regions involves various dynasties and conquests. In the 8th century, Arab invaders from Sind overthrew a dynasty in Kathiawar that had been founded by a chief named Bhatarka. Earlier, around 600, the Gurjaras established power over Gujarat and north-eastern Kathiawar. By 712, the Arabs had conquered and established themselves in Sind. Later, the Rashtrakutas established a branch line in Gujarat after overcoming rivals such as the Chalukyas.

## Climate and Environment
The climate of these regions is marked by extreme heat. Upper Sind is particularly arid, resembling the sultry deserts of Africa due to its sandy soil and extreme dryness. In Lower Sind, the mean maximum temperature at Hyderabad during the six hottest months is 98 deg. F. in the shade, and the water of the Indus reaches blood heat. In Upper Sind, temperatures have been recorded as high as 130 deg. in the shade. While the heat in Gujarat and Cutch is less extreme than in Sind, it remains very great. Unlike other parts of the presidency, the monsoon rains exert little influence in Sind.
