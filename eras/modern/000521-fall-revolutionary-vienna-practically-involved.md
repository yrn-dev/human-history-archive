# The Fall of Revolutionary Vienna

## Impact on Regional Revolutions
The fall of revolutionary Vienna had immediate and far-reaching consequences for other revolutionary movements in Central Europe, practically involving the collapse of the revolutions in Pest and Frankfort. This victory for the established order was reinforced by external support; Windischgraetz received congratulations from Radetzky's victorious army in Italy and significant commendations from Emperor Nicholas of Russia.

The definitive moral message of this victory was conveyed to the world through the military execution of Robert Blum. Blum's execution was particularly notable because, as a deputy of the German parliament, his person should have been considered sacrosanct.

## Political Transition and the New Ministry
Following the fall of the revolution, a new ministry was established that aligned with imperial sentiment. This administration was devoted to the traditions of the Habsburg monarchy and was inimical to the German ideals associated with Frankfort. The ministry was led by Prince Felix Schwarzenberg, described as an "army-diplomat" and a statesman characterized as both strong and unscrupulous.

On November 27, a proclamation was issued asserting that maintaining Austria as a united state was a necessity for both Europe and Germany.

## Accession of Francis Joseph
The transition of power was finalized on December 2, when Emperor Ferdinand abdicated. Ferdinand was viewed as an ineffective instrument for the new policy due to his excessive personal obligations to revolutionary parties. He was succeeded by his nephew, Francis Joseph.

The proclamation of the new emperor served as a gage of defiance against German unionists and Magyars. The proclamation stated that the crown was determined to preserve its lustre while remaining prepared to share rights with the representatives of its peoples. The stated goal was to unite all races and countries of the monarchy into one great body politic with the aid of God.

## Background of the 1848 Crisis
The instability leading to these events began in 1848. Following the fall of Metternich, Austria lacked a central government for several months. In Vienna, the city was ruled by a committee of citizens and students who imposed their will on the ministry and claimed a voice in imperial affairs. 

The period was marked by a cycle of insurrection and concession:
* **March 14:** The emperor authorized the establishment of a National Guard.
* **April 25:** A constitution was proclaimed for the monarchy, excluding Lombardo-Venetia and Hungary. This was met with protests from Poles and Czechs, and the indirect election of deputies angered radical elements in Vienna.
* **May 13-15:** A Central Committee was established, followed by a fresh insurrection. The government yielded, recognizing the Central Committee and promising a National Convention based on universal suffrage.
* **May 17-20:** The emperor left Vienna for Innsbruck. He issued a proclamation confirming the concessions of March but ignoring those of May 15.
* **May 26:** After the government attempted to close the university to disperse students, another outbreak occurred. The ministry conceded to all insurgent demands, eventually handing over the public treasury and the responsibility for maintaining order to a newly formed Committee of Public Safety.
