# Imperialist Conflicts and Movements

The term "imperialist" appears across various historical contexts within the archive, referring to the military forces of the Holy Roman Empire, the political struggles of the Japanese throne, and the ideological movements of European powers in the modern era.

## The Imperialists in Early Modern Europe

In the 17th century, Imperialist forces were frequently engaged in conflicts across Central Europe and the Rhine. In 1642, on January 17, the French and Hessians achieved a victory over the Imperialists at Kempen. Later, during the Thirty Years' War, a significant engagement occurred involving Wallenstein's Imperialist army and the Swedes. During this battle, the Imperialists were initially driven back, but the cavalry leader Pappenheim managed to drive the exhausted Swedish first line over the road. However, Pappenheim's death disheartened the Imperialist forces. Ultimately, the Duke Bernhard of Saxe-Weimar utilized his second line and closed reserve to force a victory, causing Wallenstein's army to give way at all points.

In the mid-16th century, a "great victory" for the Imperialists at Muhlberg resulted in the temporary crushing of German Protestantism. This victory placed the Bohemians in a precarious position, as they had offended their sovereign, Ferdinand, without aiding the German Protestants. Ferdinand, who sought to make Bohemia a hereditary Habsburg kingdom and curtail its ancient constitution, arrived in Prague on July 20 with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries. While he treated nobles and knights with forbearance, he dealt severely with the towns, specifically Prague, by revising ancient privileges to confiscate municipal lands and installing royal town-judges and town-captains to control municipal affairs. Additionally, the Bohemian Brethren faced severe persecution, and their bishop, Augusta, was imprisoned for many years.

By 1672 and 1676, Imperialist forces were again involved in European wars of manoeuvre. In the autumn of 1672, Turenne checked the advance of the Imperialists under Montecucculi on the middle Rhine. By 1676, Crequi opposed the Imperialists in Lorraine, though he could not prevent the fall of Philipsburg on September 17. The year ended with a war of manoeuvre on the upper Rhine between the French under Luxemburg and the Imperialists under the duke of Lorraine.

## Imperialists in Japanese History

In Japan, Imperialists fought to support the sovereign Go-Daigo. In one engagement, the Imperialists attacked two positions simultaneously but were shattered by Takauji, who was aided by Kyoto men under Enya Takasada. Following this, the Nitta chieftain retired to Kyoto to unite with Nawa Nagatoshi and the loyal Kusunoki Masashige. The cenobites of Hiei-zan also joined the Imperial cause.

Despite these efforts, the general Kusunoki Masashige advised Go-Daigo that the Imperialists' force of "worn-out warriors" was too petty to oppose the fresh Ashikaga host. Masashige proposed a strategy of allowing the enemy to enter Kyoto while the sovereign took refuge at Hiei-zan. The plan was to muster partisans in the home provinces to wage a war of attrition against the Ashikaga's communications, eventually seeking victory through simultaneous attacks from the front and rear.

## Modern Imperialist Ideologies

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, "imperialism" shifted from a description of specific armies to a political and social movement in Europe.

### Germany
In Germany, a "swaggering imperialism" emerged, characterized by the Kaiser's naval growth and preparations for conflict with France and Russia. This ideology was intensified by the Crown Prince, who was described as more Pan-Germanic and imperialistic than his father, having been raised on imperialist propaganda. By the spring of 1914, the German upper-class youth, influenced by teachers and leaders, felt a sense of imminent conflict and a desire for victory over foreign nations and domestic workers.

### Great Britain
In Great Britain, the imperialist movement lacked the unanimity found in Germany. It was opposed by many writers who rejected the theory of "Anglo-Saxon" supermen and by shipping interests that favored free trade over the fiscal proposals of the new imperialists. However, the movement found strong support among the military class, who sought the same importance as their Prussian counterparts, and within the popular press.

Politically, this manifested in the "Unionist" government that took office in 1895. This government, described as "imperialist" in spirit, sought to override the empire with an arrogant "British" nationalism, contrasting with the English Liberal view of the empire as a confederation of free allies. This conflict was evident in Ireland, where British imperialists desired a subjugated Irish population, while Liberals supported a free, participating Ireland. The Unionist government remained in power for ten years, during which time they conquered South Africa, though they were defeated in 1905 in an attempt to establish a tariff wall based on the Teutonic model.
