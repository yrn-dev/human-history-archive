# Theology and Academic Study in European History

The study of theology has historically served as a foundational academic pursuit across Europe, often intersecting with law, philosophy, and ecclesiastical governance. From the universities of France and Germany to the religious orders of Spain and Italy, the pursuit of theological knowledge has shaped the careers of scholars, poets, and church leaders.

## Theological Education in France

In France, the Sorbonne and various colleges provided critical centers for theological and legal training. Nicolas Boileau-Despreaux (1636–1711), a French poet and critic, was educated at the College de Beauvais before being sent to the Sorbonne to study theology. However, Boileau-Despreaux eventually exchanged theology for the study of law, becoming called to the bar on December 4, 1656.

Other French scholars maintained complex relationships with these institutions. Guillaume Budé (1467–1540), a scholar born in Paris, influenced the French intellectual landscape by persuading Francis I to found the library at Fontainebleau and the Collegium Trilingue (later the Collège de France). Budé also successfully induced the King to refrain from prohibiting printing in France in 1533, despite advice to the contrary from the Sorbonne. In the realm of ecclesiastical education, Adrien Baillet (1649–1706) received a thorough education at a theological seminary through the kindness of the bishop of Beauvais, later serving as a teacher at the college of Beauvais before his ordination as a priest in 1676.

## German Theological Development

Theology in Germany was often characterized by a rigorous progression from university study to professorships and ecclesiastical leadership. Johann Joseph Ignaz von Dollinger (1799–1890) began studying theology in 1818, viewing it as the science that lay beneath all others. After studying at Bamberg and being ordained a priest on April 5, 1822, he became a professor of ecclesiastical history and canon law in Aschaffenburg, and later a professor of theology at Munich in 1826.

Karl Gottlieb Bretschneider (1776–1848) studied theology for four years at the university of Leipzig starting in 1794. He later served as a *Privat-docent* at the university of Wittenberg, lecturing on both philosophy and theology. Bretschneider eventually earned a doctor of theology degree from Wittenberg in August 1812.

Other notable German figures include Friedrich Adolf Krummacher (1767–1845), who studied theology at Lingen and Halle before becoming a professor of theology at Duisburg in 1800. His brother, Gottfried Daniel Krummacher (1774–1837), also studied theology at Duisburg and became a leader of the "pietists" of Wupperthal. Additionally, August (b. 1846) served as a professor of theology and co-director of the theological seminary at Wittenberg.

## The Role of Wittenberg and the Reformation

Wittenberg emerged as a significant center for theological study and preaching. Martin Luther, after being ordained and continuing studies in theology—specifically the "experimental" portions of Gerson, Bernard, and Augustine—was sent to Wittenberg in 1508 to assist the university founded by Frederick the Wise. Following a mission to Rome, Luther was sent to Erfurt by his vicar-general, Staupitz, to complete the requirements for a higher graduation in theology. Upon graduating as Doctor of the Holy Scripture and taking an oath to vigorously defend evangelical truth, Luther succeeded Staupitz as professor of theology.

## International Theological Missions

Theological expertise often led to international appointments. Juan de Mariana (1536–1624), a Spanish historian and member of the Society of Jesus, taught theology in Rome in 1561, where Robert Bellarmine was among his pupils. In 1569, Mariana was sent to Paris, where he delivered expositions on the writings of Thomas Aquinas to large audiences.
