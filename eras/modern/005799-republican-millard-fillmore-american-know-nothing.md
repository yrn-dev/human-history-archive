# The Know-Nothing Party

The Know-Nothing Party, also known as the American Party, was a political organization of significant importance in the United States during the decade preceding 1860. The party was founded on the principle of political proscription of Roman Catholics and naturalized citizens.

## Origins and Ideology
The party emerged from "nativism," defined as hostility toward foreign influence in politics. This sentiment was rooted in colonial sectarianism and a distrust of alien immigrants, who were presumed to have attachments to European institutions. Between 1830 and 1860, these feelings were exacerbated by the increasing preponderance of Irish and Catholic immigration. 

Nativists viewed the Catholic Church as a symbol and strength of foreign influence, regarding it as a transplanted foreign institution with ideas and organization that were un-American. Specifically, the church was criticized for its non-conformity to popular education and American methods of church administration. In immigration centers such as New York, Philadelphia, and Boston, this led to political involvement and anti-Catholic (anti-Irish) riots during the 1830s.

## Organization and Growth
The movement began with local election organizations and the formation of the American-Republican Party (later the Native-American Party). It first received a state organization in Louisiana in 1841. In New York, the party drew its membership primarily from the Order of the Star Spangled Banner (established 1850) and the Order of United Americans (established 1845).

By the time it adopted "American Party" as its official name, its strength in Congress had increased nearly thirty-fold compared to 1852. The party successfully elected legislatures or governors in Maryland, Kentucky, California, and four New England states. In Kentucky, the majority of Whigs became Americans or Know-Nothings after the Whig party was destroyed by the slavery issue; this resulted in the election of Governor Charles S. Morehead from 1855 to 1859.

## Political Impact and Decline
The American Party's influence varied by region. Its firmest hold was in Maryland, where it carried the state in the 1856 election. However, its rule in Baltimore from 1854 to 1860 was characterized by abuses and disgraceful riots. In the 1856 presidential election, the popular vote for the party was above three-sevenths in the South, but under one-seventh in the North.

The party's existence had several effects on the broader political landscape:
* **Anti-Slavery Movement:** It delayed the organization of anti-slavery efforts in the North.
* **Immigration:** It presumably discouraged immigration, which was a source of strength for the North over the South.
* **Republican Party:** Hostility from the Know-Nothings contributed partly to Seward's loss of the Republican nomination.

Some of the party's principles, such as the non-support of denominational schools, gained general acceptance. However, other views, such as the refusal to exclude the Protestant Bible from public schools, were generally rejected, and the taxation of all church property remained a disputed issue.

## Notable Figures
Millard Fillmore, a former U.S. President whose monument is located in Forest Lawn cemetery in Buffalo, ran as the American or "Know-Nothing" candidate in the 1856 presidential election, receiving 8 electoral votes. 

Nathaniel Prentiss Banks, a politician and soldier, was re-elected to the national House of Representatives in 1854 as an American or "Know-Nothing." He later left the party and presided over a Republican convention in Massachusetts in 1855.
