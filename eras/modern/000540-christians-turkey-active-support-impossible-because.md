# Christians in Turkey

## Political Influence and the Austrian Empire
During the period following the outbreak of disturbances in the Balkans, the government of Vienna faced a complex political dilemma regarding the sufferings of Christians in Turkey. The Slav inhabitants of the Austrian empire felt a sympathy for these Christians, which prevented the government from regarding their situation with indifference. However, providing active support to the Christians in Turkey was impossible due to internal political pressures. Specifically, the Hungarians—whose memory of the earlier Turkish conquests had been obliterated by the events of 1848—felt strong sympathy for the Turks.

Furthermore, Austrian policy was guided by the cardinal principle that the erection of new Slav states on its southern frontier could not be permitted. There was also the concern that disturbances were being fomented by Russian agents; any resulting increase in Russian influence, which the Pan-Slav party sought, was viewed as a danger to Austria.

## Diplomatic Interventions and Reforms
In 1875, Andrassy drafted a note, which was accepted by the powers, that required Turkey to institute reforms necessary for the good government of its provinces. While Turkey agreed to these terms, the insurgents demanded a guarantee from the Powers that Turkey would keep these engagements. Because this guarantee could not be given, the rebellion continued and spread into Bulgaria. 

Eventually, Russia took the lead. Despite the outbreak of war, Austria did not oppose Russian measures, having entered into a secret understanding at the beginning of 1877. In this agreement, Russia undertook not to take steps injurious to Austria or annex any territory. However, the Russian army's advance on Constantinople was seen as a menace to Austrian influence, leading Andrassy to demand that peace terms be submitted to a European conference in Vienna.

## Regional Insurrections and Greek Involvement
In the 19th century, Greek political figures also engaged with Turkish conflicts. Alexandros Koumoundouros, a Greek statesman, made active warlike preparations against Turkey during the Cretan insurrection of 1866-68, though he was dismissed by King George, who believed Greece could not act without the support of the Powers. Later, as premier during the January 1878 insurrection in Thessaly, Koumoundouros sent an army of 10,000 men to assist the insurgents against Turkey. These troops were later recalled on the condition that Greece be represented at the Congress of Berlin.

## Strategic and Military Perspectives
In the context of the World War, some diplomatic correspondence suggested that the Allied Powers should align themselves with the "Christian States of the Balkans" rather than attempting to secure a neutrality from Turkey that was often characterized by duplicity and intrigue. It was proposed that Bulgaria should regain Turkish territory lost in the second Balkan War, and that the Allies should support Bulgaria, Roumania, Greece, and Serbia in attacks against Germany and Austria.

By the time of Bulgaria's capitulation, Turkey's military situation was described as hopeless. The armistice allowed Allies free passage and use of railways through Bulgaria, enabling them to move through Turkish Thrace—the sole land bastion protecting Constantinople.

## Demographics and Local Presence
Records from the archive indicate the presence of Christian populations in specific Turkish regions. For example, the town of Baindir (ancient Caystrus), located in the Aidin vilayet in Asiatic Turkey in the valley of the Kuchuk Menderes, had a population of under 10,000, of which nearly half were Christian. The town was known for its trade in tobacco, raisins, figs, and cotton, and was connected to Smyrna via a branch of the Aidin railway.
