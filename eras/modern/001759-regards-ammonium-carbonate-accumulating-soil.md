# Ammonium Carbonate in the Soil

Ammonium carbonate accumulates in the soil through several processes, most notably from the conversion of urea and other nitrogenous sources. This substance serves as a critical intermediary in the nitrogen cycle, providing stores of nitrogen that can be rendered available to plants through bacterial activity.

## Formation from Urea
A significant source of ammonium carbonate is the daily deposition of hundreds of thousands of tons of urea. Because ordinary plants are unable to assimilate urea directly, it must undergo chemical changes. Bacteria—described by Cohn, van Tieghem, and Pasteur—facilitate a simple hydrolysis that converts urea into carbonate of ammonia. This process is driven by an enzyme known as urase, which Lea and Miquel proved is separable from the bacteria involved. This conversion occurs in various environments, including manure heaps, soil, and rivers. On farms, a portion of this volatile ammonia is lost, though such loss is preventible if the facts of the process are understood.

## The Process of Nitrification
Once ammonium carbonate has accumulated in the soil, it undergoes oxidation in two stages through the activity of "nitrifying" bacteria. While it was previously believed that the formation of nitrate (saltpetre) on manure heaps was a result of direct oxidation facilitated by porous bodies, research by Winogradsky and others demonstrated that the process is biological. Nitrification is dependent on moisture, aeration, and temperature; it is stopped by sterilization, although nitre-beds can infect one another.

Winogradsky discovered that two types of bacteria are involved in this cycle. The first, termed *Nitroso-bacteria*, oxidizes ammonia into nitrous acid. These bacteria are capable of building organic matter from purely mineral sources in the dark, assimilating carbon from carbon dioxide and nitrogen from ammonia. They utilize pure mineral salts, such as chlorides, sulphates, and carbonates of magnesium, sodium, and ammonium. The energy required to split the carbon dioxide molecule is liberated during the oxidation of nitrogen. Because the supply of free oxygen is dependent on the activity of green plants, the nitrification process is indirectly linked to them.

## Other Sources of Soil Nitrogen
In addition to the conversion of urea, the soil receives nitrogen from living debris and deposits brought down from the atmosphere via rain. Furthermore, nitrogen-fixing bacteria provide an inexhaustible supply of nitrogen. Certain bacteria, such as *Clostridium pasteurianum*, can force inert free nitrogen to combine with other elements into compounds that plants can assimilate. This occurs when the bacteria are provided with necessary minerals and carbohydrates and are protected from free oxygen by an envelope of fungi or aerobic bacteria. A similar symbiotic relationship exists in the nodules found on the roots of Leguminosae, where atmospheric nitrogen is brought into combination in the soil.

## Chemical Applications and Interactions
Ammonium carbonate also appears in various chemical preparations and natural phenomena:
*   **Barium Carbonate:** Barium carbonate ($\text{BaCO}_3$) can be prepared by adding barium chloride to a hot solution of ammonium carbonate, resulting in a dense white powder.
*   **Volcanic Activity:** In nature, alkaline fumaroles (volcanic vents) that remain above 100°C evolve ammonium chloride along with other vapours.
*   **Industrial Byproducts:** In the carbonization of degreased bones, the evolved vapours are condensed. The lower layer of this condensate is an ammoniacal liquor; when distilled with steam and received in sulphuric acid, it produces ammonium sulphate, which is primarily used as a manure.
