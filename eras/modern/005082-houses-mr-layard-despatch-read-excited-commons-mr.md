# Parliamentary Proceedings and Legislative Reform in the United Kingdom

The House of Commons and the House of Lords have served as the primary arenas for significant domestic and imperial legislative shifts in English history, characterized by frequent tensions between reformist movements and reactionary administrations.

## Reform Agitation and Political Leadership
During the mid-19th century, the House of Commons was a site of intense struggle between a "compact phalanx of reactionary administration" and a growing party of reform. In February 1830, Macaulay entered parliament via a "pocket borough" at Calne, offered the seat by Lord Lansdowne. Macaulay's early contributions included a maiden speech on April 5, 1830, regarding the Bill for the Removal of Jewish Disabilities, and a successful reform speech following the introduction of the Reform Bill on March 1, 1831.

Later in the century, John Bright emerged as a popular hero and a recognized leader of the Liberal party. Bright was returned for Birmingham without opposition in June 1865 and again in the autumn of 1868. His career reached a culminating point during the winter of 1866-1867, when a Reform Bill was carried featuring a clause for minority representation. Bright's political influence extended to advocating for Irish reform in land and church, a programme later adopted when Mr. Gladstone came into power. Upon Gladstone's ascension, Bright accepted the post of president of the Board of Trade and became a member of the privy council.

## Legal Debates and Judicial Appointments
The House of Commons has also been the venue for high-profile legal defenses and political appointments. In May 1858, Sir Hugh Cairns delivered two notable speeches in the Commons. The first defended Lord Ellenborough, the president of the board of control, who had censured Lord Canning (the governor-general of India) and made the censure despatch public. In his second speech, Cairns opposed Lord John Russell's amendment to the motion for the second reading of the government Reform Bill. Cairns later served as attorney-general in 1866 under Lord Derby and was appointed lord chancellor in 1868 during the brief Conservative administration following Lord Derby's resignation.

## Legislation on Marriage and Family Law
A protracted legislative struggle occurred regarding the validity of marriages with a deceased wife's sister. The House of Commons first adopted a bill to render such marriages valid in 1850, but it was rejected by the House of Lords in 1851. Similar bills were brought before the legislature repeatedly between 1855 and 1900. While these measures often passed the Commons, they were frequently rejected by the Lords.

In 1896, a judiciously drafted bill passed the House of Lords with the support of the prince of Wales and the duke of York, though it was opposed by the prime minister and eighteen bishops. However, the promoters failed to bring this specific bill before the House of Commons. 

Eventually, reform was achieved through imperial legislation. In 1898 and 1900, Lord Strathcona introduced bills in the House of Lords to validate such marriages contracted in British colonies. Although the House of Commons took no action in 1898 and 1900, and blocked an imperial bill in 1901 and 1902 due to High Church opposition, the Colonial Marriages (Deceased Wife's Sister) Act was finally adopted in 1906. This act legalized such marriages, including rights to honours, dignities, and real property succession. This led to the Deceased Wife's Sister Marriage Act of 1907, which applied to England. This 1907 act preserved the status of the wife's sister under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857, where adultery with her by the husband is classified as incestuous adultery, and allowed clergymen to refuse to solemnize such marriages.
