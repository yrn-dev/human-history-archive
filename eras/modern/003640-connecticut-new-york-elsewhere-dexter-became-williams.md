# Regional History of Connecticut and New York

The history of Connecticut and New York is characterized by early colonial disputes over territory, the development of diverse religious communities, and the rise of significant industrial and political figures.

## Colonial Settlements and Territorial Disputes
In the 17th century, the region experienced overlapping claims between English and Dutch settlers. In 1640, the first settlers of Greenwich arrived from the New Haven Colony. However, the Dutch claimed the area based on Adrian Blok's 1614 exploration of Long Island Sound. Because the New Haven Colony provided no assistance to these settlers, they agreed to unite with New Netherland in 1642, and Greenwich became a Dutch manor. 

A treaty in 1650 established a boundary between New Netherland and the New Haven Colony, leading the Dutch to relinquish their claim to Greenwich. Despite this, the town's inhabitants refused to submit to the New Haven Colony until October 1656. By 1662, Greenwich became one of the first New Haven Colony towns to submit to Connecticut.

## Religious Development and Baptist Influence
The New Netherlands (New York) became a destination for antipaedobaptists from New England and other regions starting in 1643. Lady Deborah Moody moved from Massachusetts to the New Netherlands in 1643 due to her antipaedobaptist views; during her journey, she stopped at New Haven and converted Mrs. Eaton, the wife of Governor Theophilus Eaton. Lady Moody settled in Gravesend (now part of Brooklyn) after receiving a guarantee of religious liberty from Dutch authorities.

Other Baptist figures also operated in the region. In 1656, Francis Doughty, an English Baptist who had spent time in Rhode Island, baptized converts in the region, an action that resulted in his banishment. That same year, William Wickenden of Providence administered ordinances and evangelized at Flushing, though he was banished and heavily fined. 

In Rhode Island, the Providence church faced a schism in 1652. A majority, including William Wickenden, Chad Brown, and Gregory Dexter, advocated for the laying on of hands as indispensable to fellowship and for general redemption. This group, which eventually became involved in Universalism and Socinianism, exerted influence in New York, Connecticut, and Newport. Gregory Dexter, along with Clarke and Williams, served as a leading statesman in Providence Plantations and Rhode Island.

Later developments included the formation of a Baptist church at Oyster Bay before 1724 and the settlement of Baptists on Block Island around 1663. From 1711, Valentine Wightman of Connecticut, a General Baptist, made missionary visits to New York at the invitation of businessman Nicolas Eyres. Wightman baptized Eyres and others in 1714 to organize a church, though the congregation was nearly destroyed in 1730 by debts from building a meeting-house.

## Industrial and Urban Growth
Connecticut developed specialized industrial centers. Danbury, settled in 1684 by emigrants from Norwalk, became a primary center for the manufacture of felt hats starting in 1780. By 1905, this industry involved approximately thirty factories with a yearly product value of $5,798,107, representing 15.8% of all felt hats produced in the United States. Danbury also produced silver-plated ware, notably through Rogers Bros.

Greenwich evolved into a summer resort for New Yorkers. By 1910, the township's population reached 16,463. Its principal manufactures included iron and gasoline motors, woollens, belting, and tinners' hardware, and the town shipped oysters.

## Notable Figures in Politics and Commerce
New York and Connecticut produced several influential capitalists and statesmen. Erastus Corning (1794–1872), born in Norwich, Connecticut, became a prominent capitalist in Albany, New York. He owned extensive ironworks and was a central figure in the development of the New York Central system, serving as its president from 1853 to 1865. Corning also served as the mayor of Albany (1834–1837), a state senator (1842–1845), and a representative in Congress.

The Astor family exerted significant influence in New York. William Backhouse Astor (1792–1875), known as the "Landlord of New York," increased his fortune through real estate and funded the Astor library. His grandson, William Waldorf Astor (1848–), built the Waldorf and New Netherland hotels and served in the New York assembly and state senate before becoming the U.S. minister to Italy.

In diplomacy and journalism, John Bigelow (1817–), born in Malden, New York, served as the managing editor of the New York *Evening Post* and as the U.S. minister to France. He was also the secretary of state of New York from 1875 to 1877.

## Education and Medicine
The region saw the introduction of various academic and medical movements. Homoeopathy was brought to New York in 1825 by Dr. Hans Birch Gram; it later reached Connecticut in 1837. 

In education and theology, Sereno Edwards Dwight (1786–1850), born in Greenfield Hill, Connecticut, served as the president of Hamilton College in Clinton, New York, from 1833 to 1835. In Greenwich, the Greenwich Academy was established in 1827 as a co-educational institution, and the Brunswick School for boys opened in 1901.
