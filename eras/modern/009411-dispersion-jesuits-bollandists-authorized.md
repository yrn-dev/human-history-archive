# The Bollandists and the Acta Sanctorum

The Bollandists are Belgian Jesuits dedicated to the criticism and publication of the *Acta Sanctorum*, a comprehensive collection of legends and biographies of the saints. The work is organized according to the calendar, arranged by days. Known throughout Europe for their superior culture and scientific methods, the Bollandists have spent centuries compiling these lives from manuscripts and providing sober notes to accompany them.

## Origins and Early Development
The original concept for the *Acta Sanctorum* was conceived by a Jesuit father, Heribert Rosweyde. In 1607, Rosweyde issued a prospectus titled *Fasti sanctorum quorum vitae in Belgicis Bibliothecis manuscriptae*, in which he outlined his intention to publish the lives of the saints in eighteen volumes. Although he collected a vast amount of material before his death in 1629, he did not begin the actual publication.

The project was then handed to Father John Bolland, who was sent to Antwerp. Bolland expanded the scope of the work and began publishing volumes. The first two volumes for January appeared in 1643, followed by three volumes for February in 1658, three for March in 1668, and three for April in 1675. During this period, Bolland collaborated with Godfried Henschen (Henschenius) from 1635 until 1681. From 1659 to 1714, Daniel van Papenbroeck (Papebroch) also collaborated. The era of Papebroch is considered the most brilliant in the history of the *Acta Sanctorum*, though his critical approach earned him many enemies.

## Suppression and Interruption
The work continued in the same spirit until the suppression of the Society of Jesus in 1773. The final volume published before this interruption was volume iii of October, released in 1770. By this time, the collection had spanned 126 years (1644–1770) and consisted of fifty folio volumes, reaching only as far as the 7th day of October.

Following the dispersion of the Jesuits, the Bollandists were authorized to continue their work. They remained in Antwerp until 1778, when they were transferred to the monastery of canons regular of Coudenberg in Brussels. In this location, they published volume iv of October in 1780 and volume v of October in 1786. However, the monastery of Coudenberg was suppressed in 1786, and by 1788, the work of the Bollandists ceased. The Premonstratensians of Tongerloo acquired the remains of the Bollandist library and attempted to continue the project, publishing volume vi of October in 1794.

## Restoration and Expansion
The work was resumed in 1837 following the re-establishment of the Society of Jesus in Belgium. This revival occurred with the support of the Belgian government and at the suggestion of the Academie Royale of Belgium. The Bollandists were installed at the college of St Michael in Brussels. A new series began in 1845 with volume vii of October, eventually reaching volume xiii of October in 1883. Collaborators in this series included Jesuit fathers Remi and Victor de Buck, Joseph van Hecke, Joseph van der Moere, Edu. Carpentier, Ant. Tinnebroeck, and Henr. Matagne. Additionally, Father John Martinov of Theazan edited the *Annus Graeco-Slavicus*, which appeared in 1864 at the beginning of volume xi of October.

## Modern Methods and Scholarly Contributions
In 1882, the Bollandists shifted their activities to align more closely with the progress of historical methods. Jesuit fathers J. de Backer, G. van Hooff, and C. de Smedt established a quarterly review titled *Analecta Bollandiana*. By 1906, this review had reached its 25th volume, edited by P. Peeters, A. Porcelet, H. Delehaye, F. van Ontroy, and de Smedt. The *Analecta Bollandiana* served as a venue for dissertations, inedited texts, and studies intended to remould and continue the *Acta Sanctorum*. Since 1892, it has also included the *Bulletin des publications hagiographiques*, which provides criticisms of recent hagiographic works.

Beyond the review, the Bollandists analyzed hagiographic manuscripts in major libraries. They published separate volumes on Latin manuscripts in the Bibliotheque Royale at Brussels (1886–1889) and the Bibliotheque Nationale at Paris (1889–1896), as well as Greek manuscripts in the Vatican (1899) and Latin manuscripts in Rome (from 1905). They also produced essential inventories of published hagiographic texts, including the *Bibliotheca hagiographica graeca* (1895), the *Bibliotheca hagiographica latina* (1899), and the *Bibliotheca hagiographica Orientalis*.

While these scientific endeavors delayed the publication of the principal collection, they provided a more solid foundation. Subsequent publications included volume i for November in 1887, volume ii in 1894 (preceded by the *Martyrologium Hieronymianum* by Louis Duchesne and J.B. de Rossi), and the *Propylaeum ad Acta Sanctorum Novembris* in 1902, which contained the *Synaxarium ecclesiae Constantinopolitanae*.
