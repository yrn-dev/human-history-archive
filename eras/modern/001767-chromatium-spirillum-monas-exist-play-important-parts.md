# Chromatium, Spirillum, and Monas

## Overview of Schizomycetes
Bacteria, also known as schizomycetes, are minute vegetable organisms characterized by the absence of an ordinary nucleus and ordinary sexual reproduction. They multiply through repeated bipartitions and consist of single cells. These cells may be cylindrical, oblong, or spherical in shape, or they may form filamentous structures or other aggregates. In terms of size, bacterial cells are commonly about 0.001 mm (1 micron) in diameter and typically two to five times that length, though variations exist.

Most bacteria are either parasitic or saprophytic because they lack chlorophyll. While the majority are colourless, a small number secrete colouring matters other than chlorophyll. Fossil records indicate that these organisms have existed since very early periods, with evidence showing large numbers existed during the Devonian and Carboniferous times, and likely earlier.

## Sulphur Bacteria and Environmental Roles
A specific series of sulphur bacteria, including the genera *Chromatium*, *Spirillum*, *Monas*, and *Thiothrix*, play important roles in the circulation of sulphur in nature. These organisms are frequently found in estuaries, sulphur springs, and marshes.

In marsh environments, cellulose bacteria release marsh gas, and the resulting nascent gas reduces sulphates (such as gypsum), liberating hydrogen sulphide ($\text{SH}_2$). Sulphur bacteria thrive under these conditions by oxidizing the $\text{SH}_2$ and storing the sulphur within their own protoplasm. If the supply of $\text{SH}_2$ becomes limited, these bacteria oxidize the stored sulphur again into sulphuric acid. This acid then combines with any available calcium carbonate to form sulphate once more.

## Physiological Activity and Respiration
Certain partially anaerobic sulphur bacteria exist only in specific strata below the level of quiet waters. In these zones, $\text{SH}_2$ is released by the bacterial decomposition of vegetable mud and rises to meet atmospheric oxygen descending from above. The zone of physiological activity for these bacteria fluctuates based on the partial pressure of gases and the rate of $\text{SH}_2$ evolution.

In the deeper sections of this zone, the bacteria absorb $\text{SH}_2$. As they rise, they oxidize it and store the sulphur; upon reaching more highly oxygenated planes, they oxidize the sulphur to $\text{SO}_3$. Consequently, these bacteria use $\text{SH}_2$ as their respiratory substance. Rather than liberating energy as heat through the respiratory combustion of sugars, as higher plants do with carbohydrates, they generate energy by oxidizing hydrogen sulphide.

Conversely, *Spirillum desulphuricans* is a definite anaerobic form that attacks and reduces sulphates, effectively reversing the work performed by the sulphur bacteria.

## Morphology and Classification
Bacteria exhibit various forms. Elongated cells may be straight, curved, or twisted into corkscrew-like spirals. Slight sinuosity is categorized as the *Vibrio* form, while more pronounced, well-marked spiral windings are known as *Spirillum* or *Spirochaete*. These terms may refer to individual cells or to filaments composed of several cells.

Some Schizomycetes form aggregates, such as hollow or solid irregular branched colonies or plates. This occurs due to cell displacements after division or divisions occurring in two or three planes rather than just across the long axis, as seen in *Sarcina*.

Regarding classification, the Schizomycetes and Schizophyceae (blue-green algae or Cyanophyceae) are often united in the class Schizophyta, reflecting the view that most typical bacteria derived from Cyanophyceae. However, some bacteria show different analogies. For example, the formation of endospores and the existence of ciliated micrococci suggest a relationship with flagellate Protozoa, such as *Monas* and *Chromulina*.

## Spore Formation
Spore-formation is observed in various Schizomycetes. In *Spirillum*, cells may contain many spores which are liberated when the parent cells break up. Other forms, such as *Clostridium* and the hay bacillus (*B. subtilis*), exhibit endogenous spores. In the case of *B. subtilis*, the germinal rodlet grows at a right angle to the long axis of the spore, whereas in *Clostridium butyricum*, the axis of growth coincides with the long axis of the spore.
