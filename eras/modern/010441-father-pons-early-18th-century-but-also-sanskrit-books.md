# Father Pons and the Transmission of Sanskrit Books

## The Role of Father Pons
Father Pons was a figure active early in the 18th century who contributed significantly to the availability of Indian texts in Europe. He is credited with bringing a rich collection of Sanskrit manuscripts from India to the Imperial library. These manuscripts served as a primary resource for later scholars of the "sacred language of the Hindus" who had access to the library's holdings.

## Sanskrit Studies in Paris
The manuscripts brought by Father Pons were utilized by philologists in the early 19th century, most notably by the German philologist Franz Bopp. In 1812, Bopp traveled to Paris under the sponsorship of the Bavarian government to devote himself to the study of Sanskrit. While at the Imperial library, Bopp utilized two primary categories of resources: the collection of manuscripts provided by Father Pons and the Sanskrit books that had been produced by the Serampore and Calcutta presses.

During his four years of study in Paris, Bopp was influenced by several eminent men, including S. de Sacy, A.L. Chezy, L.M. Langles, and Alexander Hamilton. Hamilton had acquired a knowledge of Sanskrit while in India and had collaborated with Langles to produce a descriptive catalogue of the Sanskrit manuscripts held at the Imperial library.

## Philological Developments and Publications
The study of these texts led to significant philological research. In 1816, Bopp published *Uber das Conjugationssystem der Sanskritsprache in Vergleichung mit jenem der griechischen, lateinischen, persischen und germanischen Sprache*. This work did not seek to prove the common parentage of Sanskrit with German, Latin, Greek, and Persian—as that fact had already been established—but rather aimed to trace the common origin of their grammatical structures.

Bopp continued his work with Sanskrit texts in London and Berlin. He produced a critical edition of the story of *Nala and Damayanti* in 1819, which is described as the most beautiful episode of the *Mahabharata*. He later published other episodes of the *Mahabharata*, including *Diluvium* and *Indralokagamanam*. These works, along with A.W. Schlegel's 1823 edition of the *Bhagavadgita*, served as essential aids for students learning to read Sanskrit texts. Bopp eventually ceased editing these texts to focus exclusively on grammatical investigations following the publication of the complete *Mahabharata* in Calcutta.

## Other Contributions to Sanskrit Scholarship
Beyond Bopp, other scholars engaged with Sanskrit literature and linguistics. Theodor Benfey, a German philologist, produced a *Manual of Sanskrit* (1852-1854) consisting of a chrestomathy and grammar, a practical Sanskrit grammar (1858), and an edition of the *Pantscha Tantra* (1859). Benfey also authored a comprehensive *Sanskrit-English Dictionary* in 1866.

The *Pantscha Tantra* (Five Sections) is an extensive body of early fables. A related collection, the *Hitopadesa*, became more widely known in Europe. These stories were transmitted through various translations, including a Pahlavi version from the 6th century A.D. and an Arabic version known as the "Book of Kalilah and Dimna," which eventually reached Europe via Greek and Hebrew translations.

## Influence on Regional Languages
The revival of Sanskrit learning also impacted other languages. In the early 19th century, modern literary Bengali emerged in Calcutta under the influence of a college founded by the English in Fort William. This version of the language became increasingly dependent on a large imported Sanskrit vocabulary. In contrast, older Bengali writers, such as the 14th or 15th-century poet Candi Das and 15th-century translators Kasi Ram and Krttibas Ojha, wrote in a "genuine nervous Bengali" that did not rely on such a pseudo-classical Sanskrit element.
