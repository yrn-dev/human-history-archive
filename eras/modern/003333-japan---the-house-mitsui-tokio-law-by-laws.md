# JAPAN

## Governance and the Imperial Constitution
The Empire of Japan is ruled by Emperors of a dynasty that has reigned in an unbroken line of descent for ages past. According to the Constitution established in Tokyo on February 11, 1889, the person of the Emperor is considered sacred and inviolable. As the Head of the Empire, the rights of sovereignty are invested in the Emperor, who exercises them in accordance with the Constitution.

The Emperor's powers include the exercise of legislative power with the consent of the Imperial Diet, as well as the authority to sanction, promulgate, and put laws into force. The Emperor is responsible for convoking, opening, closing, proroguing, and dissolving the House of Representatives. In instances of urgent necessity when the Imperial Diet is not in session, the Emperor may issue Imperial Ordinances to avert public danger or maintain public safety. These ordinances must be presented to the Imperial Diet at the next session; if the Diet disapproves, the Government must declare them invalid.

Succession to the throne is reserved for male descendants of the Imperial House, governed by the Imperial House Law. Amendments to the Constitution require a project submitted by Imperial Order for deliberation by the Imperial Diet. Such debates cannot open unless two-thirds of the members are present, and amendments require a two-thirds majority of those present to pass. Notably, the Imperial House Law does not require deliberation by the Imperial Diet, nor can it be modified during a Regency.

## Legal and Administrative History
Japan's legal framework has evolved through various periods, including the legislation of the Daiho (Taiho) year-period and the Constitution of Shotoku (604 A.D.). In the early history of the state, the first conscription occurred in 689 A.D., though there were partial abolitions in 780 and 792. The administrative structure has included roles such as the Sangi (Court Councillor) and the Administrative Council of Man-dokoro.

During the Tokugawa period, laws were established for Military Houses and Court houses (kuge). By 1871, the distinction between court nobles and territorial nobles was abolished. The legal system also integrated various influences, including Confucianism—which was favored by Tsunayoshi and Ko-Komyo—and Shinto. Confucianists were eligible for civil posts, and the ethics of the Bushi class were influenced by the Primer of Yamaga Soko.

## Foreign Relations and Military Expansion
In the late 19th century, Japan underwent a process of Westernization, which was demonstrated during its war with China in 1894-95. This conflict showcased a sound fleet and an efficient Westernized army. While Britain and the United States treated Japan as a European state, other powers—including Russia, France, and Germany—sought to prevent Japan from gaining fruits from the Chinese war.

Following conflicts with Russia, a treaty was established requiring the two governments to exchange statements regarding expenditures for the care and maintenance of prisoners. This treaty was ratified by the Emperor of Japan and the Emperor of all the Russias, with the French Minister in Tokio and the Ambassador of the United States in Saint Petersburg serving as conduits for the announcements.

Japan's influence extended to Korea between 1895 and 1910. Initially, Japan guaranteed the welfare of the Korean imperial house and directed Korea's external relations via a treaty signed in Seoul on November 17, 1905. A resident-general, the first being the Marquis Ito, represented Japan in Seoul. By July 1907, the resident-general's powers increased, granting him control over the appointment of high officials and the enactment of laws. On August 22, 1910, the emperor of Korea made a "complete and permanent cession" of all rights of sovereignty to the emperor of Japan. The resident-general then became the governor-general, and while consular courts were abolished, Japan guaranteed the existing Korean tariff for ten years.

## Economy and Finance
The archive identifies several key financial and industrial entities in Japan. These include the House of Mitsui in Tokio and the Nippon Kogyo Ginko (The Industrial Bank of Japan), the latter of which had established Law and By-Laws by 1903. 

Historically, Japan's economy dealt with various forms of currency and trade. In the 15th century, trade involved Chinese coins and debased Japanese coins, with copper being used for images of Buddha. Cotton was first planted in Japan in 799, and cloth served as both a tax and a currency.
