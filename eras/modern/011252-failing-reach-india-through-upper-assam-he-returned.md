# Upper Assam and Regional Exploration

## Geographical Exploration and the Journey of Krishna
In the modern period of scientific geographical exploration in Asia, significant expeditions were directed toward the central depression north of the Tibetan table-land. Among the native surveyors attached to the Indian survey, Pundit Krishna conducted a remarkable journey between 1879 and 1882. His travels extended from Lhasa northwards through Tsaidam to Sachu (also known as Saitu) in Mongolia. 

Following this, Krishna passed through eastern Tibet to the town of Darchendo (or Tachienlu), located on the borders of China on the high road between Peking and Lhasa. After failing to reach India through Upper Assam, Krishna returned to the neighbourhood of Lhasa and eventually crossed the Himalayas using a more westerly route.

## Geology and Mineral Resources
Geologically, the Assam hills are situated in the angle between the Burmese ranges and the Himalayas, though they belong to neither. The mass is primarily composed of schists and gneiss. The region's older rocks are similar to those of Bengal, while the newer beds are nearly horizontal on top of the plateau. The border ranges to the south and east consist largely of Tertiary beds and belong to the Burmese system of mountain chains.

The most valuable mineral production in the region is coal. The Tertiary coal seams of Upper Assam are noted for their extraordinary thickness and purity compared to the Gondwana coal found in the Indian peninsula. Specifically, the "Thick Seam" of Margherita in Upper Assam averages 50 feet in thickness, reaching up to 80 feet in certain areas. Analysis of 27 assays of Assam coal showed an average ash percentage of 3.8, significantly lower than the 16.3 percentage found in 17 assays of Raniganj coal. These coal seams are frequently associated with petroleum springs. While gold is present in alluvial deposits, exploration results have not been promising.

## Economy and Trade
The external trade of Assam is conducted via native boat, steamer, and, to a lesser extent, rail. In the Brahmaputra valley, steamers are the primary mode of transport, carrying 94% of imports and 86% of exports. In the Surma valley, native boats handle approximately 43% of both. 

Between 1904 and 1905, imports were valued at 457 lakhs of rupees, consisting primarily of salt, sugar, grain and pulse, tobacco, iron and steel, cotton twist and yarn, brass and copper, cotton piece-goods, and rice not in the husk. Exports for the same period were valued at 726 lakhs of rupees, with chief items including tea, oil-seeds, coal, jute, timber, tea-seed, and rice in the husk. Two-thirds of this total trade is conducted with Calcutta. 

Communication has been aided by the Assam-Bengal railway, specifically the opening of the hill-section which provides direct communication between the port of Chittagong and the districts of Upper Assam.

## Regional Districts and Demographics
The province of Eastern Bengal and Assam includes several distinct districts and tribal areas:

*   **Darrang:** This district lies between the Brahmaputra and the Daphla and Bhutan Hills, with its headquarters at Tezpur. Tezpur is part of Upper Assam and has prospered due to tea cultivation, which attracts large quantities of imported coolies annually. In contrast, the Mangaldai subdivision has stationary or receding population levels because its soil is not favorable for tea. As of 1901, the district population was 337,313.
*   **Lushai Hills:** A mountainous district on the border of Burma and Assam, characterized by dense bamboo jungle and rank undergrowth, though the eastern portion features open grass-covered slopes with pine and oak groves. The Lushais, who invaded the district from the north in 1840, were reduced to order by 1892. By 1901, there were 63,452 Lushais in Assam.
*   **The Dwars:** These tracts were formerly part of Bhutan until the Bhutan War of 1864-65. The Eastern Dwars (Bijni, Sidli, Chirang, Ripu, and Guma) are located in the Goalpara district. The Western Dwars, located in the Jalpaiguri district, serve as an important center for the tea-planting industry.
*   **Tribal Groups:** The Khamtis, a tribe of Shan origin who settled on the north-east frontier of India in the middle of the 18th century, dwell in the hills bordering the Lakhimpur district of Assam. Additionally, the Koch (or Rajbansi), an aboriginal tribe found throughout the Assam valley and northern Bengal, have become largely hinduized.

## Flora and Environment
The region's climate is extremely hot and moist, which affects the durability of timber. The sal (_Shorea robusta_), a very durable wood, is found abundantly along the skirts of the Himalaya from Assam to the Punjab. The area is also characterized by various types of bamboo and dense jungles in the hill districts.
