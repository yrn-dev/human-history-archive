# Flora and Fauna of the Americas

The Americas possess a diverse array of biological species, ranging from specialized forest trees and vascular plants to a wide variety of birds, mammals, and aquatic life. These species are distributed across North and South America, with distinct characteristics defining the different regions.

## Botanical Species of North America

North America is home to several prominent tree species across various genera. Within the genus *Fraxinus* (ash), there are several species present, including the white ash (*Fraxinus americana*), the red ash (*F. pubescens*), and the black ash (*F. sambucifolia*). 

The genus *Fagus* is also represented in eastern North America by *Fagus americana*. This tree is noted for its beauty and wide distribution within the forests of the region. Early European travelers occasionally confused it with *F. sylvatica*, but it is distinguished by its lighter green, more sharply-toothed leaves and paler bark.

Other notable North American trees include the American hornbeam, also known as the blue or water beech (*Carpinus americana* or *C. caroliniana*). Additionally, the species *Ostrya virginiana* is found in America and is commonly referred to as ironwood due to its close-grained, tight, and very hard wood.

## South American Wildlife and Flora

In South America, particularly in regions such as Bolivia, the fauna is characterized by numerous interesting species of insects and birds. The tropical plains and valleys are inhabited by the American ostrich (*Rhea americanus*) and a large species of stork known as the *jaburu* or *bata* (*Mycteria americana*), which can reach a maximum height of 8 feet and a wingspan of 8 feet 6 inches. 

The lowland forests are populated by parrots, toucans, and araras, which are described as having beautiful feathers but harsh voices. These forests, along with the warm valleys, are filled with song-birds and birds of beautiful plumage. Humming-birds are also prevalent, appearing even at high altitudes in the mountains. In the high sierras, the largest sizes of eagles and condors are found, while the common vulture is found throughout the country.

The region has also seen the introduction of European animals, including swine, poultry, goats, sheep, cattle, and horses, which serve as sources of wealth and food. Mules, imported from Argentina, are used extensively as pack animals. In terms of insects, the cochineal insect is found where cactus conditions are favorable, and silkworms have been bred successfully in some departments.

## Aquatic Life and Ichthyology

The fresh-water and marine life of North America has been documented through various scientific works and government reports. J. Richardson's *Fauna Boreali-Americana* (1836) describes species primarily from British possessions in the north, while Dekay's *Zoology of New York* (1842) and reports from the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries and the U.S. Commission of Fish and Fisheries provide further data.

In terms of fish classification, the Holostean ganoids are reduced to a few species, such as *Amia* and *Lepidosteus*, which dwell in the fresh waters of Cuba, Mexico, and North America. Historically, the *Osteoglossidae* occurred in North America during the Eocene. Additionally, the *Ceratodontidae* left remains in the rocks of North America during the Triassic, Rhaetic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous periods.

## Arctic and Greenland Connections

The biological relationship between Greenland and America is evident in its land mammals. Species such as the white polar wolf, the banded lemming (*Cuniculus torquatus*), the musk-ox, the dog, the Eskimo, and probably the reindeer have all arrived from America. 

The fish and whale species of Greenland are also considered more American than European; for example, the bladder-nose seal (*Cystophora cristata*) is described as a Greenland-American species. Among the sixty-one species of birds that breed in Greenland, four are American. The vascular plants of Greenland include about 310 species, of which approximately forty are American.
