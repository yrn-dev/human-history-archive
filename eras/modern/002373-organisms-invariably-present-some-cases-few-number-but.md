# Organisms in Alopecia Areata and Bacterial Study

## Alopecia Areata and Bacterium decalvens
Alopecia areata is a condition characterized by the appearance of round patches that are more or less completely denuded of hair. While these patches most commonly appear on the scalp, they can occur on any part of the body where hair naturally grows. These patches are smooth, rounded, and somewhat depressed, a result of the loss of a large proportion of the follicles. Short broken hairs are typically visible at the margins of these patches.

Clinical evidence suggests that this disease may be transmitted. Organisms are invariably present in cases of alopecia areata; in some instances, these organisms are few in number, while in others they are very abundant and form a continuous sheath around the hair. These organisms were first described by Dr. George Thin, who named them *Bacterium decalvens*.

## Diagnosis and Treatment
It is necessary to distinguish alopecia areata from ringworm, particularly the bald variety. Although clinical distinction can sometimes be difficult, the use of a microscope removes any doubt. 

The prognosis for recovery depends largely on the age of the patient. For those under forty years of age, time alone will generally bring about the desired end, although treatment can hasten the recovery process. For patients over forty, the prognosis becomes less favorable with every additional year of age. Management of the condition requires careful attention to the sufferer's general hygiene and mode of life, and any existing weakness must be suitably treated. A daily lotion consisting of *Acidi lactici* (i dr. to i oz.), *Ol. ricini* (ii dr.), and *Spt. vini* (ad iv oz.) should be applied to the affected areas, starting cautiously and later becoming more vigorous and in a stronger solution.

## General Study of Bacteria and Disease
The study of the relationship between bacteria and disease involves three primary steps: the discovery of a bacterium in affected tissues via a microscope, the acquisition of the bacterium in pure culture, and the production of the disease through inoculation with that pure culture. 

Microscopic examination may reveal more than one organism in the tissues. However, a single organism can usually be identified as the probable cause of the disease based on its special relations to tissue changes and its constant presence. This examination requires the use of the finest lenses and various staining methods. Basic aniline dyes in solution are used almost exclusively due to their affinity for bacterial protoplasm. The goal is to color the bacteria deeply while coloring the tissues faintly. This is sometimes achieved by adding weak alkali, applying heat, or using a mordant to fix the stain. Decolorizing agents, such as alcohol or acids of varying strength, are then used to remove excess stain from the tissues.

Different bacteria react differently to these stains. In some methods, such as Gram's method or those used for the tubercle bacillus, the stain can be entirely removed from the tissues, leaving only the bacteria colored. Gram's method specifically involves treatment with a solution of iodine before decolorization.

## Opsonic Action and Phagocytosis
Opsonic action refers to the effect a serum has on bacteria to make them more susceptible to phagocytosis by the white corpuscles (leucocytes) of the blood. This can be demonstrated outside the body by incubating a mixture of a bacterium suspension, the serum being tested, and leucocytes from a normal person or animal in a thin capillary tube at 37°C for half an hour. The resulting film is stained and examined microscopically to count the average number of bacteria within fifty leucocytes.

The "phagocytic index" is determined by dividing the average number of bacteria in leucocytes from the tested case by the number found using normal serum. Wright and Douglas demonstrated that phagocytosis occurs when a small amount of normal serum is present, but is absent when normal salt solution is used. The opsonin in normal serum is very labile and is rapidly destroyed at 55°C. However, thermostable immune opsonins are developed as a result of immunization against a particular bacterium, and this property is retained even after the serum is heated to 55°C.

## Challenges in Classifying Simple Organisms
The study of minute and simple organisms, such as the Schizomycetes, presents difficulties due to their ubiquity, their few "characters," and the dangers of error during manipulation. It is difficult to recognize a specific stage of development unless all other stages are known, or to trace the full development of a single form. Consequently, evidence regarding the continuity of any two forms should not be accepted unless there is direct and uninterrupted observation. While the "genera" and "species" defined by Cohn must be recast, the strictest rules must be followed before uniting any forms.
