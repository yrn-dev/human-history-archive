# Bajour and the Regional Geography of the North-West Frontier

Bajour (also spelled Bajaur) is a small district located in the North-West Frontier Province of India. Measuring approximately 45 miles in length and 20 miles in width, the district is situated at a high elevation to the east of the Kunar valley. It is separated from the valley by a continuous line of rugged frontier hills that act as a barrier, though this barrier is easily passable at one or two specific points.

## Topography and Drainage
The topography of Bajour is characterized by a gradual downward slope that begins at the Kunar ridge and tilts toward the Panjkora river. The drainage of Bajour flows eastwards, originating from the eastern slopes of the dividing ridge overlooking the Kunar and terminating in the Panjkora river.

A notable topographical feature is a mountain spur extending from the Kunar range that curves eastwards to culminate in the peak of Koh-i-Mor, which can be seen from the Peshawar valley. Historically, the foot of this mountain was the site of the ancient city of Nysa and the Nysaean colony. Koh-i-Mor has been identified as the Meros of Arrian's history, described as a three-peaked mountain.

## Population and Administration
The district is inhabited almost exclusively by Pathan races of Afghan origin, specifically the Tarkani (Tarkalanri) Pathans. These are further sub-divided into the Ismailzai, Isazai, and Mamunds. Together with a few Utmauzais and Mohmands, the population numbers approximately 100,000.

Nawagai serves as the chief town of Bajour. To ensure the safeguarding of the road to Chitral, the khan of Nawagai remains under British protection. To the south of the district lies the wild mountain district of the Mohmands, while to the east, beyond the Panjkora river, are the hills of Swat, which are dominated by another Pathan race.

## Regional Connectivity and Borders
To the north of Bajour is an intervening watershed that separates the district from the small state of Dir. The new road from the Punjab and Malakand runs to Chitral by crossing this watershed and passing through the valley of Dir. Historically, before the adoption of the Khyber Pass as the primary route, the old road from India to Kabul ran across the frontier barrier of Bajour.

The region is part of a broad belt of independent territory—which includes Bajour, Swat, and a portion of Mohmand territory north of the Kabul river—that serves as a neutral buffer between Afghanistan and British India. This boundary was further defined by a 1893 demarcation agreement that fixed the Afghan boundary in Kunar.

## Political History and Conflict
Jandol, one of the northern valleys of Bajour, lost its political importance following the failure of its chief, Umra Khan, to seize control of Dir, Bajour, and a significant portion of the Kunar valley. Umra Khan's ambitions and his active hostility with the amir of Kabul, who claimed sovereignty over those same districts, led to two major events: the 1893 demarcation agreement and Umra Khan's 1895 invasion of Chitral, which resulted in the siege of the Chitral fort.

## Cultural and Historical Context
The region has a complex history of settlement and belief. While Buddhism spread to Swat, Dir, Gilgit, and Chitral—and the Peshawar valley was known as a seat of Buddhist learning—Buddhism apparently never prevailed in the lower Kunar valley or Bajour. In these areas, the Kafirs or Nysaeans maintained their own traditions. Some members of the Kafir community of Kamdesh still claim a Greek origin similar to the Nysaeans and chant hymns to the god who sprang from Gir Nysa.

In the early 16th century, the invader Baber, who wrote extensively about Bajour, fought in the region while on his way to establish the Mogul dynasty in India.

## The Kunar Valley
Adjacent to the Bajour uplands is the Kunar valley, a fertile and well-cultivated region in Afghanistan. The Kunar river is part of a larger system extending from the Hindu Kush to the Kabul river near Jalalabad. The lower reaches of the river are wide and shallow, meandering through a valley inhabited by a mixed population of other Afghan tribes and the Mohmands. 

The valley is bordered to the west by the snow-capped mountains of Kafiristan (Kashmund), which reach altitudes of 14,000 feet. A significant tributary, the Pechdara, flows from the Kafir hills—characterized by forests of deodar, pine, oak, and wild fruit trees—and joins the Kunar river near Chagan Sarai.
