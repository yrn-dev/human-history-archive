# History of Buffalo

## Early Exploration and Settlement
The first white men to visit the site of Buffalo were various Jesuit missionaries and adventurous French trappers. In 1679, La Salle built a ship named the "Griffin" on the east bank of the Niagara river at the mouth of Cayuga Creek. During that same year, he constructed Fort Conti at the mouth of the river, though the fort was burned. Later, in 1687, the marquis de Denonville built a fort at the mouth of the river named in his honor; this structure served as the predecessor to the fortifications on or near that site which were later called Fort Niagara. This neighborhood remained a scene of military operations until the War of Independence concluded.

By 1784, the area became known as the "Buffalo Creek region." This name likely derived from an Indian chief, or alternatively from the bison or buffalo herds that Indian tradition says frequented the salt licks of the creek. The first permanent white resident was Cornelius Winney, an Indian trader who built a cabin near the mouth of the creek possibly between 1788 and 1789.

## Development and Incorporation
The land was originally part of the Phelps-Gorham Purchase and was later acquired around 1793 by the Holland Land Company as part of the Holland Purchase. Joseph Ellicott, the company's agent and a figure referred to as the "Father of Buffalo," laid out a town between 1801 and 1802. He named the town New Amsterdam, a designation that remained on the company's books until approximately 1810. However, the name Buffalo or Buffalo Creek was more popular among the people.

The village was designated as the county-seat of Niagara county in 1808, and the town of Buffalo was officially erected two years later.

## Military History
During the second war with Great Britain, the region around Niagara Falls and Buffalo became a center for active military operations, with the British Fort Erie situated directly across the Niagara river. In October 1812, Lieutenant Jesse D. Elliott (1782-1845) used Buffalo as the starting point for the capture of the "Caledonia" and the "Detroit." Later, on December 30 and 31, 1813, the settlement was captured, sacked, attacked, and almost completely destroyed.

## Geography and Urban Planning
The city occupies a broad, undulating tract of 42 square miles. The land rises gradually from the lake to elevations between 50 and 80 feet, with an average altitude of slightly less than 600 feet above sea-level. Due to its temperate climate, high land, and water-supply and drainage systems, Buffalo is considered one of the healthiest cities in the United States; its death-rate was 14.8 per thousand in 1900 and 15.58 in 1907.

Joseph Ellicott's original plat for the city resembled the plan of Washington, though the plan was not strictly adhered to and was significantly altered. Modern Buffalo features spacious, broad streets, many of which are lined with trees, along with various squares and small parks. The city's municipal park system covers approximately 1030 acres and is connected by driveways and boulevards. This system includes:
* **Delaware Park:** The largest park at about 365 acres, featuring a 46½ acre lake. The northern section was enclosed in the grounds of the 1901 Pan-American Exposition.
* **Other Parks:** Cazenovia Park, Humboldt Park, South Park on the Lake Shore, and "The Front," a bluff overlooking the source of the Niagara river. "The Front" also houses Fort Porter, where the United States government maintains a garrison.

The Forest Lawn cemetery adjoins Delaware Park and contains monuments to President Millard Fillmore and the Seneca chief Red Jacket (1751-1830). Red Jacket, considered by many the greatest orator of his race, was a friend to whites and warned Americans of danger when approached by the Prophet and Tecumseh.

## Commerce and Infrastructure
Buffalo serves as a critical trans-shipment point between rail and lake, and as a distributing center for raw products from the West to the East and manufactured products from the East to the West. It is one of the world's principal flour and grain markets. In 1843, Joseph Dart built the first grain elevator ever constructed. By 1906, annual receipts of grain exceeded 200,000,000 bushels, with a capacity between twenty and thirty million bushels, while flour receipts reached approximately 10,000,000 barrels yearly.

Other major commercial activities include:
* **Livestock:** Over 10,000,000 head of livestock are handled annually in 75 acres of stock-yards at East Buffalo; the city also hosts the largest horse market in America.
* **Other Goods:** Annual receipts include 200,000,000 feet of lumber and 15,000,000 pounds of fish. Coal and iron ore are also handled, partly at Tonawanda.
* **Customs:** As the port of entry for the Buffalo Creek customs district, the city's exports were valued at $26,192,563 and imports at $6,708,919 in 1908.

## Principal Buildings and Institutions
The city is noted for its residential sections featuring detached houses with attractive lawns. Major structures include:
* **Government and Civic:** The Federal building ($2,000,000 cost) and the city and county hall ($1,500,000 cost), which features a 245 ft. clock tower.
* **Commercial and Social:** The Ellicott Square building (one of the world's largest office structures), the chamber of commerce, the builders' exchange, the Masonic temple, and the city convention hall.
* **Culture and Education:** The Albright art gallery and the Buffalo Historical Society building (originally the New York state building for the 1901 Pan-American Exposition), both located in Delaware Park.
* **Hotels and Clubs:** Hotels include the Niagara, Lafayette, Genesee, and Iroquois. Social clubs include the University, Country, Saturn, Park, and Buffalo clubs.
* **Religious Sites:** Notable churches include the Trinity (Protestant Episcopal), the First Presbyterian, the Delaware Avenue Baptist, the Westminster Presbyterian, and the cathedrals of St Paul's (Protestant Episcopal) and St Joseph's (Roman Catholic).
