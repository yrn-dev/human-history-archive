# Lucien Bonaparte

Lucien Bonaparte (1775–1840), the prince of Canino, was a political figure and writer whose life was marked by a volatile relationship with his brother, Napoleon. Born in Ajaccio on May 21, 1775, he was educated at the schools of Brienne and Autun. Although he initially desired a career in the French army, defective sight led him toward the church, and he attended the seminary at Aix in Provence in 1786. However, his disposition was ill-suited for such discipline.

## Early Political Career
Following the outbreak of the Revolution in 1789, Lucien embraced anti-clerical and democratic movements. He became a prominent speaker for the Jacobin club in Ajaccio and urged his brothers to break ties with Paoli, the leader of the conservative party. After the Paolists gained control, the Bonapartes left Corsica for Toulon. In southern France, Lucien continued his Jacobin activities, adopting the name "Brutus" in the Jacobin club of St Maximin.

On May 4, 1794, while still a minor and without family consent, he married Mlle Catherine Boyer. This marriage resulted in a period of penury and disgrace. Following the coup d'etat of Thermidor on July 28, 1794, Lucien fled St Maximin and took a minor post at St Chamans, where he was imprisoned for a time. He was eventually released through Napoleon's influence and served as a commissioner in the French army in Germany.

## Relations with Napoleon
Lucien entered the Council of Five Hundred in Paris in 1798 after refusing an appointment in the army of Egypt. During the consulate (1799–1804), relations between Lucien and Napoleon became strained. Lucien had previously written to his brother Joseph expressing suspicion that Napoleon possessed an ambition that surpassed his love for the general weal.

Though he served as minister of the interior, Lucien was removed from the post due to personal and political differences with the First Consul. In November 1800, Napoleon appointed him ambassador to the court of Madrid. In June 1801, Lucien signed preliminaries of peace at Badajoz with the Spanish minister Godoy, which frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon. Consequently, Lucien resigned and returned to Paris to oppose some of Napoleon's schemes through the Tribunate.

The final breach occurred in 1803. After the death of his first wife in 1800, Lucien became enamored with Mme Jouberthou. Despite Napoleon's express prohibition—and the First Consul's desire for Lucien to marry the widow of the king of Etruria—Lucien secretly married Mme Jouberthou on October 23, 1803. Napoleon ordered him to leave French territory. Lucien departed for Italy with his wife and infant son, publicly giving his wife the name Bonaparte.

## Later Life and Exile
Lucien lived in Italy, primarily in Rome, where he showed marked hostility toward the emperor. He resided at his estate of Canino, taking the papal title of prince of Canino. In December 1807, Napoleon offered him a place as a French prince on the condition that he annul his marriage, which Lucien refused.

While traveling to America, Lucien was captured by a British ship and taken to England, where he remained under surveillance until the peace of 1814. During the Hundred Days in 1815, he returned to Napoleon's side at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris and defended his brother's prerogatives during the second abdication. He spent his remaining years in Italy and died in Rome on June 29, 1840.

## Family and Legacy
Lucien's family included six daughters and four sons. Among his descendants were:
* **Charles Lucien Jules Laurent (1803–1857):** The prince of Canino, who was a scientist rather than a politician. He married his cousin Zenaide Bonaparte in 1822 and published extensive works on zoology and ornithology.
* **Louis Lucien (1813–1891):** Born in England, he later served as a deputy for Corsica and the Seine in France and became a senator. In his later years, he focused on philology and the Basque language.
* **Pierre Napoleon (1815–1881):** A republican who served as a deputy for Corsica. He later led a debauched life and was involved in a violent incident in 1870 in which he killed Victor Noir.

Lucien also left a literary legacy, writing a two-volume epic titled *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree* (1814), as well as *La Verite sur les Cent Jours* and unfinished *Memoirs*. Additionally, he is noted for having assembled a philological library.
