# The Play of the Hand

In the game of Ordinary Bridge, the play of the hand is governed by the specific nature of the declaration made by the dealer or their partner. While the laws are nearly identical to those of whist, Ordinary Bridge differs in that the dealer's partner (the dummy) takes no part in the play, their cards being exposed on the table to be played by the dealer.

## No-Trump Declarations
In a no-trump declaration (*sans atout*), the primary objective is to bring in a long suit. To successfully establish a suit, several favorable conditions are preferred: one hand should possess at least five cards of that suit, and the two hands combined should hold eight cards (unless they possess a sequence of high cards), making it probable that the suit will be established within three rounds. Additionally, the hand containing the strong suit must have sufficient cards of re-entry, and the suit should not contain so many possible tenaces that opening it becomes disadvantageous.

The strategy for playing cards in a suit is to maximize the total number of tricks rather than seeking early wins. Deep finesses are employed when no other method of stealing a trick exists. A player may intentionally give away tricks to create a favorable opening for a finesse. However, if it is unclear which hand should perform the finesse, it is better to delay the action until an adversary fails or a card falls, disclosing the suit. Finessing against a card that must be unguarded is generally considered unsound.

To avoid exhausting a partner's suit from a hand short in re-entry, winning cards should not be led. In some instances, it is correct to give away a trick; for example, if one hand holds seven cards headed by the ace and king while the other holds only two, giving the first trick to adversaries may be the right play.

## Suit Declarations and Leading
When a suit is declared, the dealer's aim is often to discard losing cards from the declaring hand to high cards or established suits in the other hand. This may occur before or after adverse trumps are removed, depending on whether there is time to draw trumps. If one hand has no cards of value in a suit, the lead should come from that hand, though it is preferable to let adversaries open the suit.

The best opening lead for a suit declaration is a singleton, followed by a lead from a strong sequence. Leads from a guarded king or queen, or from a tenace, should be avoided. While some object to it, two small cards may be led; however, a suit of three small cards of no great strength should not be opened. In cases of doubt, preference is given to hearts and, to a lesser extent, diamonds. A valuable rule is to lead up to the dummy's weak suits, though leading through strength must be used with caution (and is not applicable to no-trump declarations).

## Signaling and Information
The dealer should attempt to provide as little information as possible about their own hand, frequently playing false cards and usually playing the highest of a sequence. Conversely, adversaries use signals to convey information. Playing an unnecessarily high card—either to a partner's suit or when discarding in a no-trump declaration—indicates strength in that suit. In a suit declaration, this same method indicates the player holds only two of the suit and wishes to ruff, a tactic best used when a partner has led a king.

## Doubling and Redoubling
After a declaration, an adversary may double, which doubles the value of each trick. The dealer and partner may then redouble, making each trick worth four times the original value. This process can continue, though the value of a trick is capped at 100 points.

A player should generally only double a suit declaration if they feel as strong as the declarer. Minimum strength to justify this is typically five trumps, though it may be six. A player with six trumps and an average hand, or five trumps and a hand of no-trump strength, is likely as strong as the declarer. The position of the doubler matters: if on the declarer's left, the double intimates to the partner to lead trumps through the strong hand. If on the declarer's right, much greater strength is required unless the hand is free from tenaces. Certain declarations require less strength to double, such as a spade declaration made by the dummy or the dealer.
