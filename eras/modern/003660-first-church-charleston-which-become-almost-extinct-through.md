# The First Church, Charleston

The First Church in Charleston, South Carolina, was an early and significant center for the Baptist faith in the American colonies. Its history is characterized by periods of foundational growth, near extinction due to theological shifts, and subsequent recovery and prosperity.

## Origins and Early Development
The congregation was organized around 1684. Its formation was the result of migration from Kittery, Maine, where William Screven (1629–1713) and Humphrey Churchwood—both members of the Boston church—had organized a small congregation in 1682 with the cooperation of their mother church. Following a period of persecution, Screven and several members migrated to South Carolina. There, they collaborated with a number of English Baptists who possessed wealth and position to establish the First Baptist church in Charleston. Through the efforts of Screven, Baptist principles were widely disseminated throughout the region.

## Period of Decline
By the mid-18th century, the church entered a phase of severe religious declension. The congregation was nearly extinguished around 1746. This decline is attributed to several factors: the withdrawal of members who left to establish other churches in the surrounding neighborhood, and the intrusion of Socinianism. Sources describe the church during this time as having been "wrecked by Socinianism" and "almost extinct through Arminianism."

## Recovery and Prosperity
The First Church entered a period of remarkable prosperity in 1749 under the leadership of Oliver Hart (1723–1795), who had previously been associated with the Philadelphia Association. This recovery coincided with broader organizational developments in the region. In 1751, the Charleston Association was formed, modeled after the Philadelphia Association, which provided denominational strength. 

The Charleston Association became a progressive and highly cultivated body that focused on institutional growth, including:
* **Missionary Work:** Raising funds for domestic missionary efforts beginning in 1755.
* **Education:** Establishing a fund for the education of ministers in 1756. This fund benefited individuals such as Samuel Stillman (1737–1807), who later became the pastor of the Boston church. The members of this body also provided significant liberality to Brown University.

## Later Leadership and Regional Growth
In the four decades following the War of Independence, the most prominent leader of the South Carolina Baptists was Richard Furman (1755–1825), who served as the pastor of the First Church, Charleston. 

While the First Church and the Charleston Association maintained influence, particularly in more cultivated communities and cities such as Savannah and Augusta, they were not the primary drivers of the massive numerical growth of Baptists in South Carolina between 1787 and 1812. During this period, membership rose from 1,620 to 11,325, a surge attributed largely to the "Separate" Baptist movement led by Marshall and Stearns, which focused on evangelizing the masses.

Additionally, the Baptist faith spread among negro slaves in Georgia, the Carolinas, and Virginia. While many became members of white Baptist churches, those in cities such as Savannah and Richmond were encouraged to establish their own separate churches.
