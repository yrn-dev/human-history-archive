# The Buccaneers of the West Indies

The buccaneers were a roving community of sailors and adventurers who, for three-quarters of a century, acted as a scourge to the dominions and trade of Spanish America. Composed of English, French, and Dutch sailors, and occasionally complemented by bands of native Indians, they functioned as a mercenary navy. They were available for employment by any nation seeking to oppose Spain, provided they were permitted to share in the resulting plunder. They were characterized by their extraordinary skill in seamanship, as well as their daring and cruelty.

## Origins and Early Settlements
The community's early history was marked by instability and the search for secure refuges. In 1641, the Governor-General for the French West India Islands took possession of Tortuga, expelling the English. Although the Spaniards regained Tortuga from the French in 1654, it fell back into French hands six years later. The buccaneers assisted in the taking and re-taking of the island. 

A more secure refuge was established in 1655 following the conquest of Jamaica by the navy of the English Commonwealth, an operation in which the buccaneers also played a role. Other early activities included the settlement of St George's Cay off the town of Belize in 1638 by a Scottish buccaneer named Wallis (or Wallace) and a party of logwood cutters.

## Major Leaders and Expeditions
The first period of buccaneer activity saw the rise of influential leaders, most notably Mansfield and Henry Morgan. In 1654, the buccaneers conducted their first great land expedition, resulting in the capture and sack of New Segovia on the American mainland. Under the command of a Frenchman named L'Ollonois, the towns of Gibraltar and Maracaibo in the Gulf of Venezuela were plundered; L'Ollonois is said to have executed the entire crew of a Spanish vessel consisting of ninety seamen.

In 1664, Mansfield attempted to establish a permanent settlement on New Providence, a small island in the Bahamas. This rational scheme for settlement failed following Mansfield's untimely death. Leadership then passed to Henry Morgan, a Welshman described as unscrupulous and intrepid. Operating from headquarters in Jamaica, Morgan led triumphant expeditions to the mainland and Cuba, including the surprise and plunder of Porto Bello, one of the best-fortified ports in the West Indies.

## The Panama Expedition and Peak Power
The period between 1671 and 1685 represented the height of buccaneer power, prosperity, and daring. During this time, the buccaneers frequently envisioned the South Sea as a lucrative field for their united power. 

In 1671, under commissions granted by the governor of Jamaica, Thomas Modyford, approximately 2,000 buccaneers attacked the Isthmus of Panama to intercept bullion from South American mines. After seizing Chagres and traveling by canoe and on foot, they routed the defenders of Panama. The city was taken and burnt, and the sack was marked by great barbarities. However, the Spaniards had removed the treasure before the city fell. The resulting division of booty was small, and many buccaneers died of starvation during the return to Jamaica. Morgan was subsequently recalled and imprisoned in the Tower in 1672, though he later returned to Jamaica as lieutenant-governor in 1674. In his later years, Morgan worked to suppress the buccaneers.

By 1685, the buccaneers' strength remained significant; in the Bay of Panama, ten buccaneer vessels were encountered by a Spanish fleet of fourteen sail, though no engagement occurred. During this same year, large numbers of buccaneers under Townley and others crossed the isthmus to join forces in the bay.

## Internal Decline and Dissolution
Despite their power, the buccaneer community possessed inherent instabilities that led to its decline. The primary bonds of union were a hatred of the Spaniard and a desire for gain and adventure. While they rarely turned their savagery upon one another, the desire for gain was an insufficient bond when booty could be obtained through private ventures under separate European national colors.

Furthermore, the dream of equality was never sustained. While the spoil from wealthy cities was divided equally, the subsequent gambling and debauchery often left half of the conquerors in pressing want. This created a rift between those who wished to return home with their wealth and those who demanded renewed attacks. The separation of the French and English buccaneers, who had presented a united front in 1685, marked the beginning of the final epoch of their history.
