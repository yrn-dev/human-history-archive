# Russia in East Asia and the Korean Peninsula

## Territorial Boundaries and Expansion
Russia's eastern boundaries are defined by a complex series of geographical markers and diplomatic commissions. In the west, the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886 established a line between the Hari Rud and the Oxus river. The Oxus serves as the northern boundary of Afghanistan, extending to its source in Victoria Lake of the Great Pamir. Beyond this, the Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895 demarcated a line toward the Chinese border, separated from Chinese Turkestan by the Sarikol range.

The southern boundary of Siberia follows an irregular course northeast, defined partly by the Alatau and Tian-shan mountains, reaching the Sayan range west of Irkutsk. The boundary then moves southeast to the Kerulen affluent of the Amur river, following that river to its junction with the Usuri. From the Usuri, the boundary strikes the Pacific coast at the mouth of the Tumen river, located 100 miles south of the Amur bay, where the Russian port of Vladivostok is situated.

## Strategic Interests in Manchuria and Korea
Russia sought an unobstructed avenue from Vladivostok to the Pacific and an ice-free port in the Far East. While the Amur waterway and ocean routes existed, Russia pursued a railway across Asia. To avoid the long northern detour of the Amur river, Russia sought a shorter, more economical route through Manchuria.

Russia's occupation of Manchuria—an area larger than the United Kingdom and France combined—provided a littoral on the Yellow Sea and the Gulf of Pehchili. However, because the Manchurian coast offered few convenient naval bases outside of Talien and Port Arthur, Russia viewed the harbors of southern Korea as strategically necessary. Without Korea, Russia's East Asian extension was considered strategically defective and economically incomplete.

## Conflict with Japan
Japan viewed Russian expansion with profound anxiety, fearing that the absorption of Manchuria would make the subsequent absorption of Korea inevitable. Japan's concerns were based on several factors:
*   The presence of a powerful, ambitious state within "cannon-shot" of Japanese shores.
*   Japan's status as the sole owner of railways in the Korean peninsula, contrasting with Russia's railway-based influence in Manchuria.
*   Japan's dominant share of Korean oversea trade and its tens of thousands of settlers, compared to Russia's insignificant commerce and fewer than ten settlers.
*   The fear that Russian domination from the Sea of Okhotsk to the Gulf of Pehchili would lead to the absorption of northern China and the closure of the region to other Western nations.

Japan attempted to negotiate a "policy of the open door," proposing that Russia recognize Japan's status in Korea as Japan recognized Russia's status in Manchuria, with both parties respecting the territorial integrity of China and Korea. Russia refused to make appreciable reductions in its demands and remained obdurate regarding Manchuria, while simultaneously commencing aggressions in northern Korea and increasing its military forces in East Asia.

## The Russo-Japanese War
The tension culminated in war. A significant early engagement occurred during the Chemulpo Affair at the port of Seoul. A Japanese squadron under Admiral Uryu demanded that Russian vessels leave the port. The Russian protected cruiser *Variag*, the gunboat *Korietz*, and the transport *Sungari* engaged the Japanese for thirty-five minutes. Badly injured, the Russian crews eventually sank their own vessels, including the *Sungari*.

The conflict involved the transportation of Japanese soldiers to Korea and Port Arthur, while Russian peasants were sent via the Siberian railway. The Russian forces, described as badly led and dishonestly provided, suffered defeats on land and sea, including the total destruction of the Russian Baltic Fleet in the Straits of Tshushima.

## Aftermath and Resolution
The war ended in 1905, prompted in part by a revolutionary movement among the Russian people who were infuriated by the slaughter. Under the terms of the peace, the Tsar evacuated Manchuria, resigned Korea to Japan, and returned the southern half of Saghalien (which had been seized by Russia in 1875). This period marked a decline in European arrogance and the beginning of the retraction of European influence in Asia.
