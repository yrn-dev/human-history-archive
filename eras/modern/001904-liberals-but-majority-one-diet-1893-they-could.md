# Political Transitions in Baden and the Austrian Empire

The political landscapes of Baden and the Austrian Empire during the late 19th century were characterized by intense struggles between Liberalism, Clericalism, and various nationalist movements. These conflicts frequently manifested in the composition and stability of their respective legislative bodies.

## Political Shifts in Baden

In Baden, internal politics were primarily centered on the question of religion. A constitutional struggle began following the signing of a concordat with the Holy See on June 28, 1859, which had facilitated the establishment of religious orders and placed education under clerical oversight. This struggle concluded in 1863 with a victory for Liberal principles, shifting the responsibility for education to the communes, although priests retained a share in management.

Further Liberal constitutional changes occurred in 1867 under the premiership of Julius von Jolly (1823–1891), including the introduction of compulsory education, freedom of the press, and the responsibility of ministers. That same year, a law required all candidates for the priesthood to pass government examinations. This led to resistance from the archbishop of Freiburg; after the archbishop's death in April 1868, the see remained vacant. Tensions escalated further with the 1869 introduction of civil marriage and the 1870 proclamation of the dogma of papal infallibility. This period, known as the "Kulturkampf," saw the government encourage the formation of Old Catholic communities.

Reconciliation with Rome was eventually reached in 1880 after the fall of Jolly's ministry, and the archbishopric of Freiburg was refilled in 1882. By the end of the century, the political trend in Baden mirrored that of Germany. In 1891, the National Liberals held a majority of only one in the diet. By 1893, they could only maintain their position with the assistance of the Conservatives. In 1897, the opposition won a majority in the chamber through a coalition consisting of Radicals (_Freisinnige_), Socialists, Social-democrats, and Ultramontanes.

## Constitutional Struggles in Austria

Austrian history during this era was marked by abrupt policy changes driven by practical necessity and racial tensions. In 1867, the government faced a constitutional crisis regarding the _Ausgleich_, or compact with Hungary. The Hungarians required parliamentary ratification, necessitating a parliament where Germans held a majority to oppose the ambitions of Federalists and Slavs. 

After the dismissal of Belcredi, Beust became minister-president on February 7, 1867, and issued an edict ordering the diets to elect a Reichsrath according to the February constitution. However, diets with Federalist majorities—specifically those in Tirol, Carinthia, Moravia, and Bohemia—refused to elect deputies on the government's terms.

## The Rise and Fall of Liberal Influence in the Reichsrath

The influence of Liberals in the Austrian Reichsrath fluctuated significantly. Following a reform bill, Liberals initially secured an increased majority. The opposition was limited to representatives of other Slav races and approximately 40 Clericals under the leadership of Hohenwart. The Liberal majority, which included 105 members of the Liberal Club under Herbst, used its power to pass several laws concerning ecclesiastical affairs.

However, this dominance was short-lived. By February 1871, the emperor appointed a ministry led by A. E. F. Schaeffle and Count Hohenwart, chosen from Clericals and Federalists rather than Liberals. This ministry sought to grant Federalist demands and ordered new elections in diets where Liberals held majorities. With Clerical support, they succeeded in putting the Liberals in a minority in the Reichsrath.

Later, under Auersperg's eight-year ministry, a vigorous Liberal centralizing policy was pursued. In Bohemia, the government used significant pressure to ensure a German majority in 1872, including the suppression of opposition newspapers and the confiscation of Czech manifestoes. To secure the votes of feudal proprietors, German capitalists in Vienna formed a society (popularly called _Chabrus_) to purchase estates with attached votes. This resulted in a large German majority, with Viennese Jews replacing figures such as the Schwarzenbergs and Thuns.

## Electoral Reform and Language Conflicts

A major shift occurred with the electoral law of 1873, which moved the election of Reichsrath members from the diets to a direct vote by the people. This deprived Federalists of their primary weapon—the ability to have a territory refuse to appoint deputies. Although the law was opposed by a petition of 250,000 names from Bohemia and led to the withdrawal of Poles from the Reichsrath, it passed 120 to 2, increasing the total membership to 353.

By the 1880s, German ascendancy began to wane. In 1880, the _Deutscher Schulverein_ was founded to support German schools, eventually reaching 100,000 members. German Liberal parties in the Reichsrath attempted to make German the official language of state through motions in 1880 and 1886, but these failed. In Bohemia, Germans proposed dividing the country into German-speaking and Czech-speaking parts at the end of 1886. However, since 1882, the Germans had been in a minority in the diet. The proposal was rejected by Prince Karl Schwarzenberg, leading the Germans to leave the diet in protest.
