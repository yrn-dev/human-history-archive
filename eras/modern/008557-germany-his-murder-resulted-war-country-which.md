# Germany and Related Historical Conflicts

## Early Bohemian Relations and Conflict
In the early history of Bohemia, the introduction of Christianity was initially attempted by Germany, though these efforts saw little success due to a natural distrust of the Germans among the Bohemians. The faith was more readily adopted when it arrived from Moravia via the Greek monks Cyrillus and Methodius; Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius around the year 873.

A significant conflict involving Germany occurred during the reign of Wenceslas, surnamed the Holy. Wenceslas had been an ally of Germany, but in 935 he was murdered by his brother, Boleslav. This murder resulted in a war with Germany. Based on the limited records available from that era, Boleslav, who succeeded Wenceslas, was generally successful in this conflict. Under Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its frontiers to include Moravia, a large portion of Silesia, and the part of Poland currently known as the Austrian province of Galicia.

## The Outbreak of the Great War
In 1914, geopolitical tensions rose involving Germany and the Central Powers. While France was distracted by financial scandals—including the murder of the editor of the *Figaro*, M. Calmette, by the wife of the finance minister, M. Caillaux—and Britain faced potential civil war in Ireland, Germany was assured that Britain might not intervene if Germany and Austria attacked Russia and France.

The catalyst for war occurred on June 28, 1914, with the assassination of the Archduke Francis Ferdinand, heir to the Austrian throne, and his wife in Sarajevo, Bosnia. Germany, seeking to diminish the power of Balkan states to facilitate a design of extending its influence eastward through Constantinople to the Persian Gulf, approved of Austria's response. Although Austrian commissioners reported no evidence implicating the Serbian government in the murder, the Austro-Hungarian government used the event as a grievance. 

On July 23, Austria issued an ultimatum to Serbia. Despite Serbia's practical submission and efforts by the British Foreign Secretary, Sir Edward Grey, to organize a conference of powers, Austria declared war on Serbia on July 28. The German Emperor viewed the situation as a "now or never" opportunity to set armies marching.

## Diplomatic Deadlocks and Alliances
Prior to the war, attempts were made to maintain peace. Efforts were pursued to create a friendly spirit and placate Germany's aspirations, including negotiations in the colonial sphere that were nearly concluded in Germany's favor. However, a deadlock was reached regarding the new German Navy Law, which Germany would only modify rather than withdraw.

Despite the shifting groupings of great powers, which were to Germany's disadvantage, there was initially nothing that threatened her with war. France, while dreaming of recovering lost provinces, remained generally pacific due to the perceived might of Germany. Ultimately, Germany is described as having rushed with settled resolve to its own undoing, placing itself "hopelessly in the wrong" as France, Russia, and Great Britain aligned.

## Post-War Reparations and Restoration
Following the war, Germany was subjected to specific requirements for reparations. To effect payment through deliveries in kind, Germany was required to deliver specific amounts of chemical drugs, dyestuffs, coal-tar products, and coal to the Reparations Commission for a limited number of years. Coal deliveries were primarily intended to compensate allied countries for production losses resulting from the war.

Regarding chemical drugs and dyestuffs, including quinine, Germany granted the commission an option for up to 50 percent of its total stock at the time the treaty took effect, and 25 percent of the output every six months until the end of 1924. Additionally, Germany undertook to use its economic resources for the physical restoration of invaded areas. This included the manufacture of reconstruction materials and the replacement of destroyed animals and machinery, provided these requirements did not unduly interfere with Germany's essential domestic industrial needs.

## Commercial and Military Intersections
Historically, Germany's relations with other powers also involved commercial and military maneuvers. In 1743, Lobkowitz joined with reinforcements from Germany to drive enemies back to Rimini during conflicts in Italy. 

In the late 19th century, commercial relations between Germany and Austria-Hungary shifted. Initially, customs tariffs were based on commercial treaties including a "most favoured nation" clause, which nearly eliminated customs duties. However, after 1873, Austrian manufacturers pushed for protective duties to combat competition from English, Belgian, and German goods. By 1877, attempts to reach new agreements with Germany failed due to a change in policy adopted by Bismarck, leading Austria-Hungary to establish tariffs based on its own legislation.
