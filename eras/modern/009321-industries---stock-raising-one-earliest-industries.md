# Industries: Stock-Raising and Related Enterprises

Stock-raising has historically served as a primary industry in various global regions, often developing alongside or following mining and agriculture. The nature of this industry varies by geography, ranging from the breeding of native highland animals to the large-scale export of dressed meats and wool.

## South American Stock-Raising
In certain regions, stock-raising was one of the earliest industries established after mining. In the temperate regions and departments such as La Paz, horses are raised, though they previously struggled in the north following the introduction of a *peste* from Brazil. In the Mojos and Chiquitos llanos, the industry began when Jesuit founders of the Mojos missions introduced cattle while laboring among the Indians. Despite high transportation costs and inaccessibility, these areas provide a steady supply of beef for inland markets. Other cattle-raising districts include the well-watered plains of the Chaco, the llanos of Tarija and Chuquisaca, and small districts in Cochabamba and Potosi. 

To serve home markets, *saladeros* are used to salt and sun-dry beef, while hides are exported. Other livestock in these regions include goats in temperate and warm areas, and sheep for wool in temperate zones. On colder, higher plateaus, breeding focuses on alpacas and llamas, as well as the native fur-bearing chinchilla (*C. laniger*), the exportation of which is prohibited by the Bolivian government to encourage local production.

In other South American provinces, cattle-raising became the principal industry shortly after Spanish settlement. By the end of the 19th century, exports of live-stock, dressed meats, and wool reached enormous proportions. This included the export of live-stock to South Africa, Europe, and neighboring republics, as well as *tasajo* (jerked beef) to Cuba and Brazil. In addition to cattle, these grassy plains support the raising of mules, asses, goats, swine, and horses. Since 1890, cheese and butter-making have become prominent, with butter becoming an export item.

Conversely, in the southern highlands of Maranhão, stock-raising was once an important industry but has since declined. This decline is attributed to the failure to introduce new blood into degenerated herds, as well as the presence of vampire bats and troublesome insects.

## North American and European Livestock
In Idaho, the stock-raising industry is most extensive in the southeastern part of the state. In 1899, live-stock was the most valuable animal product, totaling $3,909,454. Wool followed in value at $2,210,790. By 2007, Idaho was ranked fourth among wool-producing states in number of sheep and fourth in scoured wool. As of January 1908, the state's livestock included 3,575,000 sheep, 344,000 neat cattle (excluding 69,000 milch cows), 150,000 horses, and 130,000 swine. Regional concentrations include:
* **Neat Cattle:** Lemhi, Idaho, Washington, Cassia, and Owyhee counties.
* **Horses:** Washington, Canyon, Fremont, Idaho, and Nez Perce counties.
* **Sheep:** Canyon, Blaine, and Owyhee counties.
* **Swine:** Idaho and Nez Perce counties.

In Europe, stock-raising is a staple interest in regions such as the county of Kinross, where hilly land is particularly suited for the industry. While many cattle are pastured on lowland farms, the cattle are primarily a native breed improved through crossing. The area also maintains a high number of sheep and horses, the latter of which are used for both agricultural work and breeding. Milnathort serves as an important center for livestock sales. In the Marne department, the raising of other stock and mixed merino breed sheep, alongside bee-farming, is considered profitable.

## African and Asian Industrial Contexts
In certain African higher regions, white settlers have focused on raising large herds and flocks, including the introduction of Merino sheep from Australia. These areas have also seen the establishment of ostrich farms, with the introduction of ryegrass, lucerne, and clover to improve fodder.

In Japan, the industrial landscape shifted significantly during the Meiji era. Previously, manufacturing was entirely domestic and agriculture—specifically the intensive cultivation of rice—monopolized attention. However, manufacturing increased rapidly, growing from a domestic character to an industry comprising 11,000 factories employing 700,000 operatives and 9,000 joint-stock companies.

## Urban Industrial Centers
Budapest stands as a primary center of commerce and industry in Hungary. Its principal industries include distilling, steam flour-milling, and the manufacture of railway plant, machinery, cutlery, chemicals, bricks, jute, carriages, and gold and silver wares. The city's trade involves horses, cattle, pigs, wool, hides, corn, flour, spirits, wines, and wood. The growth of Budapest's industrial capacity is reflected in its joint-stock companies, which grew from 28 companies with £2,224,900 in capital in 1873 to 242 companies with £31,378,655 in capital by 1899.
