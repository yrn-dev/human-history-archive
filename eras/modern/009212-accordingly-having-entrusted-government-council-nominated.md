# Nominated Councils in Governance and Administration

Across various historical and geographical contexts, the use of nominated councils has served as a primary mechanism for exercising executive, legislative, and administrative authority. These bodies, whose members are appointed by a higher authority rather than elected by a popular vote, appear in colonial administrations, municipal governments, religious institutions, and transitional political periods.

## Colonial and Imperial Administrations
In the British colonial empire, which comprises over fifty distinct governments, nominated councils are central to the administration of crown colonies. In these territories, the crown maintains entire control of legislation. Except for St Helena and Gibraltar—where the governor may make laws alone—laws are created by the governor with the concurrence of a council nominated by the crown. These councils may be established under local or imperial laws (as seen in Jamaica) or their authority may rest wholly on the crown (as seen in Ceylon).

Other colonial structures utilize mixed systems. In colonies with representative institutions but without responsible government, such as the Bahamas, Barbados, and Bermuda, there are two legislative bodies: one elected and one nominated by the crown. In the Bahamas specifically, the governor is assisted by a legislative council of nine members nominated by the crown, as well as an executive council of nine members who are partly official and partly unofficial. Similarly, in Malta and the Leeward Islands, the single legislative body is partly elected and partly nominated.

In the Nyasaland Protectorate, the governor, acting under the orders of the colonial office, is assisted by an executive council and a nominated legislative council consisting of at least three members.

## Municipal and Local Governance
Nominated members are frequently integrated into city and local administrations to ensure representation from specific professional or social sectors. In Bombay, the port—including its warehouses and docks—is managed by a port trust with members nominated by the government from the commercial community. 

The municipal government of Bombay, framed by an 1888 act of the legislative council, employs a dual structure of a town council and a municipal corporation. The corporation consists of 72 members; while 36 are elected by ratepayers, 16 are nominated by the government. Other members are elected by the senate of the university (2), the chamber of commerce (2), and the justices of the peace (16). The corporation's standing committee, the council, consists of 12 members, four of whom are nominated by the government.

In the historical "Terra di Comune" republic, a system of autonomous parishes existed until the Revolution. Each parish nominated councillors known as "fathers of the commune" to administer justice under a president called a *podesta*. These *podestas* then chose a member for the supreme council, known as the Twelve, which was responsible for making regulations for the Terra di Comune.

## Executive and Transitional Councils
Nominated councils have been used to manage government during periods of transition or instability. In South America, Bolivar entrusted the government to a council he nominated himself, with Santa Cruz serving as its head, before departing Lima in September 1826.

In Japanese history, to avoid violence regarding the succession of Nobunaga, it was agreed that Samboshi would be nominated as the successor. Administrative duties were entrusted to a council consisting of Hideyoshi, Ikeda Nobuteru, Niwa Nagahide, and Shibata Katsuiye. These four barons took turns discharging these functions, with each residing in Kyoto for three months of the year.

## Religious and Educational Administration
Nominated bodies also extend to the governance of faith and education. In Baden, the Evangelical Church is governed by a synod and a nominated council. The synod includes a "prelate," seven nominated lay and clerical members, and 48 elected members. In the Bahamas, elementary free schools are controlled by a nominated board of education, though local supervision is handled by committees that are partly elected.
