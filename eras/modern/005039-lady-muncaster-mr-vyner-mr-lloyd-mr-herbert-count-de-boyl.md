# The Capture of the English Party at Oropos

## Overview of Brigandage in Greece
Brigandage in Greece, as well as in Spain, Corsica, and southern Italy, was a persistent issue with deep roots. These regions provided numerous hiding places within mountains and forests. The persistence of this activity was attributed to poor administration and a legal system where officers were viewed as enemies or dangers, resulting in a lack of native assistance. Additionally, local potentates often protected brigands because it served their own interests.

Under Turkish rule, the situation in Greece was such that individuals were either victims of official extortion or became "klephts." Certain clans, such as the Mainotes, lived by the sword under their own laws to avoid being flayed by the Turks. Following the establishment of independence, Greece continued to suffer from poor administration under a nominal parliamentary government. Politicians during this era frequently utilized brigands for their own purposes.

## The Capture at Oropos
In 1870, an English party was captured at Oropos, located near Marathon. The group consisted of:
* Lord and Lady Muncaster
* Mr Vyner
* Mr Lloyd
* Mr Herbert
* Count de Boyl

Following their capture, the brigands demanded a ransom of £25,000. In an effort to secure the funds, Lord and Lady Muncaster were released to seek the ransom. However, the Greek government responded by sending troops to pursue the brigands. During this military action, the remaining prisoners—Mr Vyner, Mr Lloyd, Mr Herbert, and Count de Boyl—were murdered.

## Aftermath and Resolution
The individuals responsible for the murders were subsequently hunted down, captured, and executed. Following these events, Greece became tolerably free from the reproach of brigandage. This followed a pattern of strong measures previously seen in the Morea, where the peasantry provided active support to government troops once it became clear the government was earnest in its efforts to clear the region. This earlier success in the Morea had been prompted by the misfortunes of S. Soteropoulos, an ex-minister who had been captured by brigands.
