# George Bancroft

George Bancroft (1800–1891) was a prominent American statesman and historian. Born on October 3, 1800, in Worcester, Massachusetts, he was the son of Aaron Bancroft, a clergyman, author, and revolutionary soldier whose family had resided in America since 1632.

## Education and Early Life
Bancroft received an extensive education, attending Phillips Academy in Exeter and Harvard University. He furthered his studies in Europe at the universities of Berlin, Goettingen, and Heidelberg. While at Goettingen, he studied natural science with Blumenbach, New Testament Greek with Eichhorn, and Plato with Heeren. Bancroft was particularly influenced by Heeren, whom he regarded as the greatest living historical critic and a precursor to the modern school; from Heeren, Bancroft adopted a commitment to meticulous and painstaking erudition.

Following his studies, Bancroft undertook a European tour where he met numerous distinguished figures in art, science, and letters, including Hegel, Goethe, Humboldt, Byron, Niebuhr, Savigny, Schleiermacher, Bunsen, Manzoni, Constant, and Cousin.

Upon returning to the United States in 1822, Bancroft initially attempted to follow his father's path into the ministry. However, his early experiences with preaching were unsatisfactory, as he felt the theological instruction of the era had replaced faith with literature and criticism. He subsequently became a tutor at Harvard. During this time, Bancroft found the classical studies and curriculum at Harvard to be pedantic and narrow. His European manner and ardent personality initially drew ridicule from the local society. In 1823, he published a small volume consisting of original pieces and translations, though it did not bring him fame.

## Literary and Historical Contributions
Despite the severe routine of the Round Hill school, Bancroft was a frequent contributor to *Walsh's American Quarterly* and the *North American Review*. He also translated Heeren's work, *The Politics of Ancient Greece*. 

In 1834, Bancroft published the first volume of the *History of the United States*, followed by a second volume in 1837. Other volumes were published as his public life permitted. In 1835, he published an article in the *North American Review* titled "The Documentary History of the Revolution," which served as a supplement to his first volume. This piece introduced the reading public to a new method of history and highlighted the abundance of available materials.

Bancroft's historical work was characterized by a philosophic spirit, solidity, and a sense of proportion. His writing was marked by vivid generalizations, acute insight, and exuberant enthusiasm. He was known for his dedication to style, often rewriting chapters and testing descriptions in as many as eight different forms. While some critics noted that he occasionally lacked representative power or employed artificial tones, he is regarded as the foremost historian of the United States and an American historian who stood equal to his European contemporaries. His work emphasized the epic and heroic value of American history and its connection to the "great central stream," effectively dispelling sentimental and visionary philosophies of the 18th century.

His volumes achieved significant commercial success in the United States and were reprinted in England and translated into French, German, Italian, and Danish.

## Public Life and Later Years
Bancroft's intellectual achievements earned him international recognition. While preparing to leave Berlin, the Royal Academy honored him as a guest at a public dinner—an unprecedented gesture—and the universities of Munich, Heidelberg, and Berlin provided a joint testimonial of regard.

In his political and social life in Washington, Bancroft served as a confidential advisor to statesmen until his death. He was also considered the unofficial dean of the city's best society. Between 1872 and 1875, while serving as the American Ambassador in Berlin, he employed William Milligan Sloane as his secretary.
