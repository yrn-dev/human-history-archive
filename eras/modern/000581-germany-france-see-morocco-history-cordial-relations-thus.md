# Germany and France: The Morocco Disputes

The relations between Germany and France in the early 20th century were characterized by significant tension, primarily centered on competing interests in North Africa. This friction manifested in a series of diplomatic crises regarding Morocco, which influenced broader European alliances and the geopolitical stability of the era.

## The Origins of Conflict
The tension stemmed in part from England's desire to pacify France over Egypt, which led England to connive at the French occupation of Morocco. Germany viewed Morocco as its own share of North Africa, and this perceived loss contributed to a "bristling attitude" from Germany toward France. Simultaneously, this environment led to a revival of the *revanche* idea within France.

By early 1905, a French mission arrived in Fez. Their actions and language suggested an intent to treat Morocco as a French Protectorate, an act that appeared to ignore the international obligations established by the Treaty of Madrid. In response, the Sultan of Morocco appealed to Germany to determine if France was authorized to speak on behalf of Europe.

## The Tangier Crisis and Diplomatic Escalation
Germany utilized the Sultan's appeal to position itself as the champion of international agreements. The underlying German objective was to demonstrate to France that her agreement with Britain did not grant her the freedom to offend Germany. 

On March 31, 1905, the German Emperor traveled to Tangiers. Despite his own better judgment, he delivered an open challenge to France using uncompromising language drafted by his ministers. This speech was widely circulated by the German Foreign Office. Following this, on April 11 and 12, Germany sent threatening despatches to London and Paris, demanding a conference of all powers that had signed the Treaty of Madrid. Germany employed drastic measures to ensure France understood that a refusal to hold the conference would result in war, including the dispatch of a special envoy from Berlin to Paris.

## The Algeciras Conference and Allied Support
The resulting Algeciras Conference took place during a period of shifting European dynamics. While the German Government had initially accepted the 1904 Anglo-French Agreement without protest—with Chancellor Prince Bülow stating there was nothing in it to which Germany could take exception—pressure from the Colonial and Pan-German parties eventually forced a change in attitude.

France received critical support from Great Britain, which helped dispel the immediate danger of complications. During the critical division at the Algeciras Conference on March 3, 1906, Austria-Hungary was the only power to side with Germany. The ultimate settlement between France and Germany was based on a proposal from the Austro-Hungarian plenipotentiary.

## Strategic Maneuvers and Imperialism
Beyond formal diplomacy, Germany attempted to undermine the French position in Algeria and Tunis. This was pursued by providing Moroccans with money and arms to induce attacks on French rule in Western Algeria and through Saharan oases in Southern Tunis. Some perspectives suggest that these German actions forced France to address the "thorny problem" of Morocco to avoid the potential evacuation of Algeria.

The German spirit of imperialism was further emboldened by Russia's disasters in Manchuria in 1905, which reduced the fear of a joint Franco-Russian attack. This confidence allowed the German Emperor to pursue bolder aggressions, including the threat of war that compelled France to dismiss her foreign minister, Delcassé.

## Long-term Consequences and Resolutions
The hostilities over Morocco had wider repercussions for European diplomacy. The German Emperor's aggressions against France and the Slavs, combined with his naval challenge to Britain, pushed Russia, France, and Britain into a defensive understanding. Furthermore, the close relations between Germany and Austria-Hungary—emphasized during the Morocco dispute—encouraged Austria to pursue bolder policies, such as the 1908 annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina.

In a later resolution, Germany was required to renounce all rights, titles, and privileges under the Act of Algeciras and the Franco-German agreements of 1909 and 1911, as well as all arrangements with the Sherifian Empire. Germany accepted the consequences of the French protectorate and undertook not to intervene in negotiations between France and other powers regarding Morocco. Additionally, Germany relinquished interests in the State Bank of Morocco and agreed that Moroccan goods entering Germany would receive the same privileges as French goods.
