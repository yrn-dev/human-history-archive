# The Deromes and French Bookbinding

The Deromes were recognized as one of the great families of binders in France. The most celebrated member of this family was Nicholas Denis, known as "Le Jeune," who was born in 1731. His work is characterized by the use of "dentelle" borders—lace-like gold tooling—which resembled the style used by Padeloup. However, Nicholas Denis distinguished his work by interspersing little birds among the arabesques, a style referred to as "dentelles a l'oiseau."

## French Binding Masters of the 16th and 17th Centuries

Prior to the 18th century, French bookbinding was distinguished by several influential masters. Nicholas Eve is noted for binding the *Livre des Statuts et Ordonnances de l'ordre du Benvist Sainct Esprit* for Henry III. Clovis Eve is credited with inventing the "fanfare" style, which consists of a delicate tracery across the book boards filled with spirals of leafy stems.

Another significant innovator was Le Gascon, who invented dotted work. He achieved this effect by scoring his small gilding tools—specifically his arabesques and curves—across, ensuring that impressions resulted in a dotted line rather than a straight one. This style was continuously followed and had many imitators, the most accurate of whom were the Dutch binders Magnus and Poncyn, who worked in Amsterdam during the 17th century. Florimond Badier worked in a style very similar to Le Gascon; unlike Le Gascon, Badier sometimes signed his work.

## 18th Century French and English Binding

During the 18th century, there were notable improvements in presswork, type, and paper in England and France. While the average technique of binding improved, the best binders of this era were considered inferior to those of the 17th century until the arrival of Roger Payne.

In France, Padeloup le Jeune belonged to a family of excellent binders active during the 17th and 18th centuries. His bindings often featured inlaid work and lace-like gold tooling borders, frequently accompanied by small tickets bearing his name. Another skilled French binder of the 18th century was Jean Charles le Monnier, who was perhaps the most skilled in the use of delicate coloured leather inlays. Le Monnier often signed his work by impressing small capitals in gold near the inlaid sections.

In England, various distinct styles emerged. Samuel Mearne, the royal binder to Charles II who died in 1683, invented the "cottage style" of decoration. This style features an inner rectangle running parallel to the book's edges, with the upper and lower lines breaking outwards to form the outline of a gable roof. This style remained in use into the modern era and was used for the Bible upon which Edward VII took his coronation oath. Additionally, Mearne was known for painting the fore-edges of books so that the artwork only became visible when the pages were fanned out.

Other English styles include the "Harleian style," utilized by Eliot and Chapman when binding the library of Robert Harley, earl of Oxford, in the mid-18th century. These bindings used morocco leather with diamond-shaped centre-pieces and broad, richly gold-tooled borders. Thomas Hollis utilized fine red morocco ornamented with stamps engraved by the medallist Thomas Pingo, featuring figures of Britannia, a figure of liberty, and a cap of liberty.

## Evolution of Materials and Technical Processes

The history of binding saw a shift following the introduction of printing in Europe in the mid-15th century. Early printed books were often cheap and "ugly," bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf and decorated with blind panel stamps. This led to the birth of gold tooling on leather, an art likely brought to Venice from the East. In England, Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, was the first to practice this art, working until approximately 1558 with a style strongly influenced by Italian masters.

In the 18th century, trade bindings moved away from the use of calf and sheepskin, opting instead for paper boards. By the modern era, the industry faced challenges including the prevalence of leather treated with sulphuric acid—which rots the fibre—and paper overladen with wood pulp and clay.

The art has also transitioned from labor-intensive handwork to mechanical processes. Machinery can now efficiently execute the making of outer cases, sewing sheets together on bands, rounding backs, folding sheets, and stamping designs. While these machines save significant time and labor, the older hand-sewing and rounding methods are considered stronger, safer, and less likely to injure the paper. Consequently, while large trade binders use machines, high-class trade binders and private binders generally adhere to older manual methods.
