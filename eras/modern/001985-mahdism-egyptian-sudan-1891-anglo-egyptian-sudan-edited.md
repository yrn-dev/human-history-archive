# Mahdism and the Egyptian Sudan

The history of the Egyptian Sudan during the late 19th century is defined by the rise of the Mahdist movement, the subsequent overthrow of Egyptian authority, and the eventual establishment of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. This period was marked by significant military conflict, the shifting of political capitals, and the reorganization of provincial administration.

## The Mahdist Rising and the Fall of Khartum
The Mahdi, Mahommed Ahmed, was a native of Dongola. During the Mahdist rising in 1884, General Gordon was dispatched to Khartum to organize the evacuation of the Sudan by the Egyptians. While at Khartum, Gordon was besieged by the Mahdists, who had established their headquarters at Omdurman. 

The siege ended on January 26, 1885, when Khartum was captured and General Gordon was killed. This event occurred two days before a small British relief force arrived at the town; upon discovering the city had fallen to the enemy, the force withdrew. Following the capture, the Mahdists destroyed nearly every building in Khartum and abandoned the city.

## The Mahdist State and the Khalifa
Following the fall of Khartum, Omdurman became the headquarters of the Mahdi's successor, the khalifa Abdulla. The khalifa ruled from 1885 to 1898. During this era, the term "Ashraf" (or Shurefa/Sherifs) was used to refer to the family and adherents of the Mahdi, as the Mahdi's family claimed to be Ashraf (a title for descendants of the prophet Mahomet). This differed from the Ashraf of Tokar, a small tribe of African "Arabs" who remained loyal to Egypt during the Sudan troubles.

The Mahdist period also saw military engagements involving various local tribes. For example, the Awadia and Fadnia—two small nomad tribes of pure Arab blood from the Bayuda desert—were noted for their courage on January 17, 1885, at the battle of Abu Klea, where they rode against the British square.

## Transition to Anglo-Egyptian Sudan
The Mahdist state ended in September 1898 when Anglo-Egyptian forces under General (later Lord) Kitchener captured Omdurman. Following this victory, the seat of government was transferred back to Khartum. The city was rebuilt on a finer scale than its original form. Infrastructure improved rapidly after the reconquest: a railway from Wadi Haifa reached Khartum in 1899, and rail communication with the Red Sea was established by 1906.

## Geography and Administration of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan
The Anglo-Egyptian Sudan was organized into several provinces, known as *mudirias*.

### Khartum
Khartum serves as the capital of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. Located on the left bank of the Blue Nile just above its junction with the White Nile, the city's name derives from the local Arabic dialect word for an elephant's trunk, reflecting the shape of the promontory at the confluence of the two rivers. By 1907, the population of the city and its suburbs (excluding Omdurman) was 69,349.

### Dongola
The Dongola *mudiria* is located within the region of Nubia, extending along both banks of the Nile. While the town of Dongola is a thriving agricultural and trading center, the administrative headquarters of the *mudiria* are located at New Merawi. The region is known for its date palms, grain crops such as barley and durra, and a breed of strong, hardy horses.

### Kordofan
Kordofan is a *mudiria* of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan covering approximately 130,000 square miles. It is bounded by Darfur to the west, the Bayuda steppes to the north, the White Nile *mudiria* to the east, and the country of the Shilluks and other negro tribes to the south.

## Exploration of the Region
The region, particularly Darfur, was documented by several European travelers. William George Browne was the first known European visitor to Darfur, staying at Kobbe from 1793 to 1795. Other notable accounts were provided by Gustav Nachtigal in 1873, as well as journeys by Gordon and Slatin. Slatin Pasha's work, *Fire and Sword in the Sudan*, provides a detailed account of the overthrow of Egyptian authority by the Mahdi.
