# Tabular Book-keeping

Tabular book-keeping is a specialized method of accounting designed to modify traditional book-keeping practices. It serves as a device to achieve several specific operational ends: the reduction of clerical work, an increase in the speed at which results become available, and the ability to handle those results with greater certainty and speed.

## Methodology and Application
The primary mechanism of tabular book-keeping is the substitution of the more usual form of accounting books with books ruled with numerous columns. This system is versatile and can be applied to two different types of accounting records: books of first entry and ledgers.

### Books of First Entry
When applied to books of first entry, the tabular system allows a single book to conveniently manage more than one class of transaction. This is particularly useful for businesses divided into several departments. By utilizing a separate column for the sales of each individual department, a business can readily determine separate totals for the aggregate sales of each. This process simplifies the preparation of departmental trading accounts.

### Ledgers
In the application of tabular book-keeping to ledgers, the system enables a single opening of a ledger to record a very large number of personal accounts. This is possible provided that the number of entries required for each individual is few.

## Other Tabular Forms and Devices
While tabular book-keeping is a specific accounting device, the use of tabular forms appears in other educational and analytical contexts within the archive's records.

### Educational Use
In the construction of the horn-book—a primer for children consisting of a sheet of vellum or paper protected by transparent horn and mounted on wood—tabular forms were used to present the combinations of vowels with consonants. These combinations followed the alphabet and preceded the Lord's Prayer and Roman numerals.

### Analytical Use
Tabular forms are also employed for the purpose of demonstrating filiation. For example, in the study of the various texts of Marco Polo’s book, a tabular form is used to show the relationship and derivation between the Geographic Text, the Italian *Crusca*, and the old Latin version.
