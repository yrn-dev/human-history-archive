# Provisions and Definitive Formulations

Across various historical, legal, and mathematical contexts, the establishment of "definite provisions" or "definite values" serves to resolve ambiguity, regulate social behavior, or define theoretical boundaries. These formulations range from maritime law and ancient social codes to the rigorous definitions of mathematical analysis and religious dogma.

## Legal and Regulatory Provisions

In maritime law, the York-Antwerp Rules provide specific guidelines regarding general average (G.A.) sacrifices. While these rules do not significantly alter English common law regarding which acts constitute G.A. sacrifices versus expenditures, they do establish definite provisions for points where previous authority was either doubtful or scanty. One notable departure from common law is found in Rule I concerning the jettison of deck cargo; the rules specify that such jettison is not allowed as a G.A. sacrifice, even if the cargo was carried on deck according to an established custom of that specific trade.

## Social and Domestic Ordinances

Ancient and early medieval societies utilized specific provisions to regulate marriage and civic duty. In the laws of Kent, marriage is represented largely as a matter of purchase. Provisions were made for widows to receive a proportionate share of their husbands' property, and it was customary for the bridegroom to provide a present, known as *morgengifu*, to the bride. In the case of queens, this often included considerable estates and a residence.

Other ancient laws focused on civic engagement and familial succession. One set of laws declared any citizen who remained neutral during a party conflict to be disfranchised, intending to prevent citizens from listlessly protecting their own property while avoiding the dangers of national misfortune. Additionally, a specific provision allowed an heiress with an impotent husband to use the services of the husband's next of kin to produce an heir. To ensure the offspring remained within the family, the heiress was restricted to conversing only with those chosen from her husband's relations. Further ordinances required the bride and bridegroom to eat a quince together in a closed room and mandated that the husband of an heiress approach her at least three times per month as a mark of respect.

## Mathematical and Theoretical Definitions

In the field of mathematical analysis, the "theory of aggregates" (*Mengenlehre*) provides rules for assigning numbers to the domain of a variable. An aggregate is defined as "infinite" if a one-to-one correspondence can be made between all its elements and some of its elements (such as the correspondence between positive integers and even integers). Furthermore, an aggregate of real numbers is said to "extend to infinite values" if, regardless of how great a number $N$ is specified, numbers exceeding $N$ in absolute value can still be found within the aggregate.

Regarding the limits of functions, if a definite finite real value $[f](x, y)$ is attached to every point of a region, it may have a finite upper limit $H$. If no point exists where $[f](x, y) > H$, but points exist where $[f](x, y) > H - [\epsilon]$ (where $[\epsilon]$ is however small), then $H$ is the upper limit. In the study of divergent integrals, a "principal value" is determined provided that $[\epsilon]$ and $[\epsilon]'$ are connected by a definite relation and both tend to limit zero while remaining positive. Cauchy's principal value specifically occurs when $[\epsilon]' = [\epsilon]$.

## Dogmatic and Theological Formulations

In Roman Catholic theology, the transition from a statical to a dynamic conception of dogma is evidenced by the definition of the Immaculate Conception of the Virgin by Pius IX in 1854. While the word "dogma" was not used in the 1854 definition, it was later employed in the Vatican decrees of 1870 to affirm the personal infallibility of the pope as *divinitus revelatum dogma*. 

The church distinguishes between "dogma," which is definitely formulated by authority, and "faith." Faith is defined as being revealed through either Scripture or unwritten tradition, and taught via formulated decrees or the ordinary *magisterium*. By ranking unformulated teachings as no less *de fide* than formulated ones, the church warns against attempting to draw a definite line around dogma.
