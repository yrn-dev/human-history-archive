# The French Revolution

The French Revolution was a period of profound social, political, and economic upheaval characterized by the collapse of the ancient order and the emergence of competing ideological factions. Unlike the American experience, the French National Assembly faced significant obstacles, including a bankrupt national treasury and a king and court determined to create mischief. The revolution caused an unprecedented economic disorganization by altering marketing and land tenure conditions. Furthermore, the French were surrounded by aggressive neighbors and a church organization that was inextricably bound to the old order. The queen maintained correspondence with exiled princes, including the Duke of Bourbon and the Count of Artois, who sought to encourage Austria and Prussia to attack the new nation.

## Political Factions and Key Figures

The revolutionary period saw the rise of various democratic movements, most notably the Jacobins and the Cordeliers. The Cordeliers, who met in an old convent of the same name, represented the popular principle of the revolution carried to its extreme. They were among the first to suspect the court of being hostile to freedom and advocated for root-and-branch measures. George Jacques Danton, a former advocate at the Paris bar, emerged as a natural leader of the Cordeliers due to his energetic and impetuous temperament. Danton served as the president of his district's popular club, commander of a national guard battalion, and administrator of the department of Paris.

Other figures were shaped by the chaos of the era. Babeuf is described as a type of the French revolutionist—warm-hearted and excitable—who propounded socialism as a practical policy. This ideology later influenced the revolutions of 1871 and 1848. In contrast, some individuals remained loyal to the previous order. Georges Cadoudal, a leader of the Chouans, remained true to his Catholic and royalist teaching. From 1793, he organized rebellions in the Morbihan against the revolutionary government and later joined the Vendean army, fighting in the battles of Savenay and Le Mans.

## Military Developments and Napoleon Bonaparte

The revolution provided a catalyst for military advancement. Pierre Antoine Noel Bruno, Count Daru, was called from his literary pursuits to a "sterner occupation" during the outbreak of the revolution. By 1793, he served as a commissary protecting the coasts of Brittany from French royalists and British descents.

Napoleon Bonaparte also rose through the ranks during this period. Initially an industrious student of history and mathematics at the military schools of Brienne and Paris, he entered the artillery in 1785. Influenced by Rousseau, he became an ardent republican and a Jacobin until the fall of Robespierre. Bonaparte gained distinction at Toulon, where he commanded the artillery to force the British and Spanish allies to abandon the port and town.

## Impact on Family and Society

The revolution disrupted the lives of many prominent families. Jerome, born in Ajaccio in 1784, shared his family's fortunes during the early years of the revolution and was educated at Juilly. He later served as a prince of France and was eventually raised to the throne of the kingdom of Westphalia under the treaty of Tilsit (July 7, 1807). In Westphalia, the influence of France led to the introduction of the *Code Napoleon*, the abolition of feudalism, and the freeing of the Israelites from repressive laws.

The royal houses of Europe were also affected. Marie Amélie Thérèse, daughter of the king of Naples, was driven from Naples in 1798. Her later experiences with the revolution of 1830, which made her queen of the French, were viewed with forebodings of disaster based on her earlier experiences with revolutionary instability.

## Aftermath and Legacy

The revolutionary era left a lasting mark on French political thought. Figures such as Philippe Joseph Benjamin Buchez, born during the revolutionary period, later sought to organize armed risings against the government in the 1820s. Buchez eventually developed a theory of "Christian socialism" and a science of history that divided historical development into four great epochs originated by universal revelations.
