# Banking: History, Structure, and Periods of Crisis

Banking is a business that, like all others, experiences periods of difficulty. In the banking sector, the severity of these periods is intensified because they affect a vast number of interests, ranging from world-wide scopes to every home in a country.

## Classification and Historical Development
From the 16th century onward, European banks were divided into two primary classes: "exchange banks" and "banks of deposits." 

Exchange banks, such as the Bank of Amsterdam and the Bank of Hamburg, were established to facilitate trade with other countries and remedy the inconveniences merchants faced due to the uncertain value of foreign currencies. They provided a common standard of value—a "money of account"—which allowed trade to proceed without the delays and expenses of verifying various coins that differed in purity, weight, and intrinsic value. In Hamburg, this took the form of the "Mark Banco," a metallic currency representing an equal value of silver. While these institutions were once of the highest importance, their period of activity has generally passed.

Banks of deposits, which include the Bank of England, the Bank of France, the Bank of Germany, the Bank of Sweden, and the Bank of Venice, receive deposits and make loans, thereby associating themselves with a country's general industries and trade.

## The British Banking System
In the United Kingdom, the banking system evolved through various legislative acts in 1826-1833, 1844-1845, 1862, and 1879. Until 1862, private banks were restricted to six partners; the act of 1862 increased this limit to ten, though this power did not extend to private issuing banks. Legal technicalities and these restrictions made large private banks practically impossible, meaning banking did not assume its modern form until the middle of the 19th century.

The act of 1844 had a significant influence on the system's constitution. While it initially favored small banks, it eventually led to the formation of large, powerful banks in England and Wales. Under this act, no new banks of issue were allowed. Private issuing banks with six or fewer partners could remain or amalgamate, but if a private issuing bank joined a joint-stock bank, its issue lapsed.

Modern transactions in the United Kingdom are primarily settled by cheque via ledger transfers, with the London Clearing House facilitating a large proportion of these. The annual circulation of the London Clearing House exceeds £12,000,000,000. This system operates on a well-founded trust, where the specific gold reserves of the drawing bank are not questioned.

## Regional and International Banking
### Scotland and the Colonies
Scottish banking was characterized by a strong emphasis on note circulation. For example, the Bank of Dundee operated solely on the issue of notes from 1764 until 1792. Private banking had little hold in Scotland; eight private banks existed in 1819, but all had disappeared by 1844. By 1906, ten banks of issue, with 1,180 branches, carried out nearly all the country's business.

British interests also extend globally through joint-stock banks. In the Dominion of Canada, 29 banks operate with capitals of nearly £19,000,000 and deposits of about £140,000,000. Australia and New Zealand have 24 banks with capitals of nearly £18,000,000. In India, approximately 15 banks operate with capitals over £5,000,000, some connecting to Zanzibar, Hong Kong, and Bangkok. Additionally, there are at least 8 banks in South and West Africa. In total, more than 80 joint-stock British banks work in the colonies, with combined deposits of £360,000,000.

### Germany and the United States
In Germany, the Reichsbank is the most powerful institution, managed by a directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire. It assists business through note circulation and a clearing system known as *Giro-Verkehr*.

In the United States, banking has seen periods of extreme volatility. Between 1811 and 1815, a "banking mania" saw the number of banks increase from 88 to 208. However, insufficient specie to support the created credit led to collapses in 1814. Later, from 1836 to 1842, the U.S. experienced another crisis driven by speculation in public lands. In 1836, land sales reached 20,074,870 acres, bringing in $25,167,833; by 1842, receipts fell to $1,417,972. President Jackson attempted to stop this speculation via the "Specie circular" of July 11, 1836, which required land payments to be made in specie or notes of specie value. This led to banks across the Union stopping payment, and banking capital dropped from $358,442,692 in 1840 to $196,894,309 in 1846.

## Periods of Pressure and Panic
In England, the act of 1844 enhanced the stringency of difficult periods because of doubts regarding whether a relaxation of the act would be permitted. Between 1844 and 1890, there were five periods of pressure: 1847, 1857, 1866, 1870, and 1890. Three of these (1847, 1857, and 1866) reached the level of panic.

The crisis of 1847 was caused by speculation in railway enterprises starting in 1845. The bank rate of discount, which was 3% on January 1st, rose sharply throughout the year: to 3-1/2% on January 14th, 4% on January 21st, 5% on April 8th, 5-1/2% on August 5th, 6% on September 30th, and peaking at 8% on October 25th. Following an October 1st announcement that no advances would be made on public securities, general alarm ensued and the bank's reserves were rapidly reduced.
