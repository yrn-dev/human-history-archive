# The Grand-Duchy of Baden

## Early History and Territorial Formation
The history of Baden as a separate entity began in 1112 with Hermann, a son of the margrave of Verona and grandson of Bertold, duke of Carinthia and count of Zaehringen. Hermann adopted the title of margrave, likely due to his family's connection to the margraviate of Verona. By approximately 1200, the territories were divided, establishing the lines of Baden-Baden and Baden-Hochberg; the latter further split into the branches of Baden-Hochberg and Baden-Sausenberg.

The Baden-Baden line successfully expanded its holdings, and by 1391, margrave Bernard I united several possessions, including Baden-Hochberg after that ruling family died out in 1418. In the 15th century, Margrave Charles I lost some territories in a war with the count palatine of the Rhine, though these were recovered by his son, Christopher I. Christopher united the whole of Baden in 1503 following the extinction of the Baden-Sausenberg family, but he divided the land among his three sons before his death in 1527.

## Dynastic Divisions and Conflict
Following the death of one son in 1533, the remaining brothers, Ernest and Bernard, divided the territories in 1535, creating the lines of Baden-Baden and Baden-Durlach (originally Baden-Pforzheim). This division led to prolonged rivalry and open warfare from 1584 to 1622, during which Baden-Baden was held by a prince of Baden-Durlach. These conflicts were further complicated by the Reformation, as different rulers adopted different faiths.

Baden suffered significantly during the Thirty Years' War, with both family branches facing exile. While the treaty of Westphalia in 1648 restored the status quo and ended the family rivalry, the region was later ravaged by French troops during the reign of Louis XIV. Margrave Louis William of Baden-Baden was a prominent figure in resisting these French aggressions. The unification of Baden occurred in 1771 when Augustus George of Baden-Baden died without sons, leaving his territories to Charles Frederick of Baden-Durlach.

## Constitutional Development and Political Unrest
In 1815, Baden joined the Germanic confederation. A dispute arose regarding succession to the grand-duchy between the house of Zaehringen and the king of Bavaria. To secure popular support for the Hochberg heir—the issue of a morganatic marriage between grand-duke Charles Frederick and Luise Geyer von Geyersberg—grand-duke Charles granted a liberal constitution in 1818. This established two chambers whose assent was required for taxation and legislation.

Despite these reforms, the administration under grand-duke Louis was viewed as inefficient and unpopular. Following Louis, grand-duke Leopold (the first of the Hochberg line) succeeded in 1830 and displayed liberal tendencies, overseeing reforms in education, civil and criminal law, and the constitution. Material prosperity increased after 1832 when Baden joined the Prussian *Zollverein*.

By 1847, Radicalism resurfaced. At a demonstration in Offenburg on September 12, demands were made for a progressive income-tax, a fair adjustment between capital and labour, and the conversion of the regular army into a national militia sworn to the constitution.

## The Revolution of 1849 and Prussian Intervention
Serious unrest returned in early 1849 when a new constitution failed to satisfy Radicals, who wanted a constituent assembly. A military mutiny occurred at Rastatt on May 11, followed by the proclamation of a revolution at Offenburg on May 13. A simultaneous mutiny at Karlsruhe forced the grand-duke to flee. Initially, a committee under Lorenz Brentano, representing moderate Radicals, attempted to direct affairs. A provisional government was established on June 1, and a "constituent diet" of advanced politicians assembled on June 10.

The grand-duke of Baden requested military intervention from Prussia, in coordination with Bavaria, on the condition that Baden join the League of the Three Kings. In mid-June, Prussian forces under Prince William invaded. The insurgent forces, led by the Pole Ludwig von Mieroslawski, were completely defeated at Waghaeusel on June 20. Prince William entered Karlsruhe on June 25, and by the end of the month, the provisional government members at Freiburg dispersed.

Following the collapse of the revolution, ex-officers among the insurgent leaders faced military execution, and the army was dispersed among Prussian garrison towns. The grand-duke returned on August 19, dissolved the diet, and passed reactionary laws to strengthen the government.

## Later Political Alignment and Geography
Between 1850 and 1866, Baden generally supported Austria. However, during the war of 1866, popular resentment regarding the Schleswig-Holstein question led the second chamber to petition the grand-duke to end the war and ally with Prussia. Baden subsequently withdrew from the German confederation and signed a peace and alliance treaty with Prussia on August 17. While Bismarck prevented Baden from joining the North German confederation to avoid provoking Napoleon III, Baden's opposition to a South German confederation eventually made the final German union inevitable. In 1870, Baden's troops participated in the war, and the grand-duke was the first to hail the king of Prussia as German emperor at Versailles.

Geographically, Baden is characterized by extremes. The Rhine valley is the warmest district in Germany (mean temperature approximately 50°F), while the Black Forest high table-land is much colder (mean temperature 43°F). The region is known for its numerous celebrated mineral springs, including those at Baden-Baden and Badenweiler. Its mineral wealth includes iron, coal, zinc, lead, and abundant peat, along with smaller quantities of gold, silver, and copper.
