# The Reign and Reforms of Joseph II

The death of Maria Theresa in November 1780 marked a significant transition in the governance of the Habsburg dominions. While Maria Theresa had been the practical founder of the unified Austrian state, her approach to centralization was characterized by a cautious statesmanship. She followed the principle of preserving the form of old institutions while changing their substance, allowing old estates to survive and leaving ancient constitutions, such as that of Hungary, untouched. Her death removed this restraint, leaving her son and successor, Joseph II, free to pursue a "drastic revolution from above."

## Political Philosophy and Administration
Joseph II is described as a paradoxical figure, embodying both the old Habsburg autocracy and a doctrinaire liberalism. A disciple of Rousseau rather than Machiavelli, he viewed his diverse dominions—divided by class and racial prejudices and burdened by traditional institutions—as vacant territory upon which to build an ideal state. Guided by the watchwords of "reason" and "enlightenment," he regarded opposition to his measures as unreasonable or obscurantist.

In his effort to reconstruct the state, Joseph II implemented a uniform system of government, dividing the entire monarchy into thirteen departments. In a departure from local tradition, he refused to be crowned or to take the oath of the local constitutions.

## Ecclesiastical and Social Reforms
Joseph II sought a "reform from above" regarding the church, aiming for the complete subordination of the clergy to the state and the severance of effective ties with Rome. In his correspondence, he referred to the powerful clerical elements of the monarchy as "Fakirs and Ulemas." Among his specific actions was the issuance of an Edict of Tolerance in 1780.

While he took steps to emancipate the peasants, he also introduced conscription. This policy proved detrimental, turning the peasantry against him and contributing to the eventual failure of his plans.

## Governance of the Austrian Netherlands
Joseph II took an active and personal interest in Belgium, appointing his sister, Archduchess Maria Christine, as governor-general. His efforts in the region were divided between external diplomatic struggles and internal administrative changes.

Externally, Joseph II sought to remove impediments to Belgian development by addressing the "Barrier" treaties with Holland. He successfully compelled the Dutch to withdraw their garrisons from Barrier towns and ordered the demolition of remaining Dutch-occupied fortresses. However, his attempts to free the navigation of the Scheldt were unsuccessful. After diplomatic negotiations failed in 1784, he attempted a stratagem by ordering vessels to sail between Antwerp and Ostend; the Dutch fired upon these ships, forcing their withdrawal and leaving the Scheldt closed.

Internally, Joseph II's "reforming zeal" provoked fierce opposition. He attempted to sweep away inherited privileges and change the traditional system of civil government. His religious reforms and the Edict of Tolerance were viewed as infractions of the Joyous Entry. This led to a general uprising: malcontents organized a military force in Holland, defeated the Austrians at Turnhout, and overran the country. On December 11, 1789, the people of Brussels compelled the Austrian garrison to capitulate. By January 11, 1790, the provinces formed an independent state known as the "Belgian United States."

## Decline and Reversal
The aggressive nature of Joseph II's reforms led to widespread instability across his territories. In addition to the revolt in the Netherlands, he faced an actual revolt in Tirol and a threatened revolt in Hungary. These internal crises, combined with disasters during the war with Turkey, forced Joseph II to formally reverse his entire policy of reform before his death in 1790.
