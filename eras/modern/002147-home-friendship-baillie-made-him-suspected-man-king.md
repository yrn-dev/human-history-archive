# Sir Patrick Home and Robert Baillie of Jerviswood

## Political Conflict and Suspicion
Sir Patrick Hume, also known as Home of Polwarth and later the Earl of Marchmont, became a suspected man due to his friendship with Robert Baillie of Jerviswood. Robert Baillie was a Scottish conspirator and the son of George Baillie of St. John's Kirk, Lanarkshire. Because of this association, the king's troops occupied Redbraes Castle, the home of Sir Patrick in Berwickshire. 

Following the occupation of his home, Sir Patrick remained in hiding for a period of time within a churchyard. During this time, he was kept supplied with food by his eldest daughter, Lady Grizel.

## Robert Baillie of Jerviswood
Robert Baillie had previously incurred the resentment of the Scottish government in June 1676 when he rescued his brother-in-law, a Presbyterian minister named Kirkton, who had been illegally confined in a house by an informer named Carstairs. For this action, Baillie was imprisoned for four months and fined L500, though he was liberated after paying half of that fine to Carstairs.

In 1683, driven by despair over the state of his country, Baillie considered emigrating to South Carolina, though this plan failed. That same year, he and several friends traveled to London to communicate with Russell, Monmouth, and their party to seek redress. Following the discovery of the Rye House Plot, Baillie was arrested. Although he was questioned by the king and denied knowledge of the conspiracy, he admitted that he had been consulted regarding a potential insurrection in Scotland.

Despite a lack of evidence connecting him to the plot, Baillie was kept in close confinement and fined L6000. On December 23, 1684, while in a languishing state, he was brought before the high court on charges of treason. He was found guilty the following day and hanged at the market cross in Edinburgh. His treatment is remembered as one of the worst crimes of the Stuart administration in Scotland. Bishop Burnet, a cousin of Baillie, described him as a man of great virtue, piety, and learning in mathematics, law, and languages, who held Presbyterian principles.

## Aftermath and Family
Upon hearing of Robert Baillie's execution in 1684, Sir Patrick Home fled to Holland, where his family joined him shortly thereafter. The family eventually returned to Scotland during the Revolution.

Robert Baillie's son, George, also took refuge in Holland. He later returned with William III and was restored to his estates. In 1692, George Baillie married Lady Grizel, the eldest daughter of Sir Patrick Home. Lady Grizel had a history of supporting the patriot Baillie; at age twelve, she had carried letters from her father to Robert Baillie while the latter was in prison.

## Lady Grizel Baillie
Born on December 25, 1665, at Redbraes Castle, Lady Grizel Baillie (née Home) was a Scottish song-writer. She died on December 6, 1746. She had two daughters: Rachel, Lady Binning, and Grizel, who married Sir Alexander Murray of Stanhope. Lady Murray possessed a manuscript of her mother's prose and verse. 

Lady Grizel's songs were featured in Allan Ramsay's *Tea-Table Miscellany*, and her most famous work, "And werena my heart light I wad dee," first appeared in *Orpheus Caledonius* in 1725.
