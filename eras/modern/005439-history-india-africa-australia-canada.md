# The British Empire: Territorial Extent and Administration

The British Empire was a vast collection of states, acquired through various methods and differing in character, held together under the supreme headship of the crown. At its height, the empire occupied nearly one-quarter of the earth's land surface, covering approximately 12,000,000 square miles.

## Geography and Distribution
The empire's territory was divided almost equally between the northern and southern hemispheres. In the northern hemisphere, India (including native states), Canada, and the United Kingdom covered 5,271,375 square miles. In the southern hemisphere, South Africa and Australasia covered 5,308,506 square miles. This distribution ensured a complete alternation of seasons, allowing summer produce from one region to supply winter requirements in another.

The distribution between the eastern and western hemispheres was less equal. Canada alone occupied 3,653,946 square miles in the western hemisphere, while the United Kingdom, India, South Africa, and Australasia occupied 6,925,975 square miles in the eastern hemisphere. Much of the empire lay within temperate zones suitable for white settlement, with notable exceptions including the northern portion of Australia, New Guinea, British Borneo, the southern half of India and Burma, Central, East, and West Africa, the West Indian colonies, and the Arctic regions of North America.

## Administrative Evolution
The structural basis of the empire's constitution was a generally acknowledged triple principle: self-government, self-support, and self-defence. While this principle was applied more fully in some areas than others, it served as the primary framework. The British Isles were distinguished as the residence of the crown and the place of origin of the British race.

In the home government, the crown received advice on colonial administration from the secretaries of state for the colonies and for India. This replaced the "Council of Trade and Plantations," which was suppressed in 1782. Following a period where the government attempted to treat colonies simply as dependencies, a shift toward representative institutions occurred. In 1791, Pitt granted representative institutions to Canada, though the home government maintained responsible control. Similar institutions were later granted to South Africa and Australia.

## Regional Developments and Governance
By the early 19th century, Canada, Australia, and South Africa had developed into important communities. As representative institutions under home government control became insufficient, these regions reasserted claims for the liberty to manage their own affairs.

Different models of governance emerged across the empire:
*   **Federation:** Canada and Australia became great self-governing groups of federated colonies. In these systems, communities voluntarily created a common government for matters of common interest while retaining control over local matters.
*   **Unification:** In 1910, the self-governing colonies of South Africa were united into a single state known as the Union of South Africa.
*   **Dependencies:** India served as an example of the practical federation of dependencies, with its associated provinces under the control of a central government.

In 1815, the empire's holdings included the coastal river and lake regions of Canada (with the Hudson Bay Company maintaining fur-trading stations in the hinterland), about one-third of the Indian peninsula under the East India Company, and the coast districts of the Cape of Good Hope. Other holdings included trading stations in West Africa, Gibraltar, Malta, Jamaica, British Guiana, and convict dumps in Tasmania and Botany Bay in Australia.

## International Status and the League of Nations
Following the Covenant of April 28, 1919, the League of Nations was established as a league of "states, dominions, or colonies" that were stipulated to be "fully self-governing." Within this framework, the British Empire appeared as a whole, but India and the four dominions—Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa—also appeared as separate sovereign states. 

Critics of this arrangement noted that the term "fully self-governing" lacked a clear definition and that the Indian representative was a British nominee, while the representatives of the dominions were colonial politicians. The inclusion of India in the League also raised logical claims for French Asia and French Africa.

## Military and Strategic Operations
The empire's global reach was evident during major conflicts. During the world crisis, the British military coordinated the transport of troops and supplies to France while simultaneously managing convoys from Canada, Australia, and India. Additionally, the empire conducted six separate expeditions in Togoland, the Cameroons, German East Africa, New Guinea, Samoa, and German South-West Africa, alongside operations in the Persian Gulf and at the Suez Canal.
