# District and Chief Towns

The organization of administrative districts and the identification of chief towns serve as primary methods for recording population distribution and economic activity across various global regions. Records from 1905 and surrounding years provide detailed snapshots of these divisions in Europe, Asia, and the Americas.

## Districts of Baden
In the early 19th century, Baden was a margraviate with a population of 210,000 and an area slightly exceeding 1,300 sq. m. By 1905, through the acquisition of additional territory, its area increased to 5,823 sq. m. and its population grew to 2,009,320. The population density was approximately 330 persons per square mile, with about half the population classified as rural (living in communities of fewer than 2,000 inhabitants).

The religious composition of the population in 1905 consisted of approximately 60% Roman Catholics, 37% Protestants, and 1-1/2% Jews. The region is divided into four districts:
*   **Mannheim:** Chief towns include Mannheim (pop. 162,607) and Heidelberg (pop. 49,439).
*   **Karlsruhe:** Chief towns include Karlsruhe (pop. 111,200) and Pforzheim (pop. 59,307). Karlsruhe also serves as the capital of the duchy.
*   **Freiburg-im-Breisgau:** Chief town is Freiburg (pop. 74,102).
*   **Constance:** Chief town is Constance (pop. 24,818).

Other important towns in the region include Lahr, Bruchsal, Baden-Baden, and Rastatt. Population clusters are most dense in the north and near the Swiss town of Basel.

## Administrative Divisions in Germany and Europe
Various other German regions utilize district classifications for governance and industry:
*   **Saxony:** The town of Bischofswerda is located in the governmental district of Bautzen.
*   **Alsace-Lorraine:** The town of Bischweiler is situated in the district of Lower Alsace.
*   **Westphalia:** Lüdenscheid, a center for hardware manufactures, was part of a district ceded to Brandenburg in the early 17th century from the counts of the Mark.
*   **Greece:** In the province of Corinth (pop. 71,229), the modern town of New Corinth serves as the head of a district. Founded in 1858 after an earthquake destroyed Old Corinth, the town had a population of about 4,300 in 1905.

## Districts in Asia
In British India, the Punjab region includes the Jullundur division, which contains the town and district of Ludhiana. As of 1901, the town of Ludhiana had a population of 48,649 and served as a trade center for grain and various manufactures, including furniture, carriages, turbans, scarves, and shawls. In the United Provinces, the Fatehpur district contains the ancient town of Kora (pop. 2806 in 1901), which previously served as the capital of a Mahommedan province.

In Russia, the government of Tomsk includes a coal-mining district centered around the town of Kuznetsk in West Siberia, which had a population of 3,141 in 1897.

## Regional Centers in Other Territories
Other regions identify chief towns as hubs for specific industries or administration:
*   **South Australia:** Kooringa (also known as Burra) is the center of an agricultural and mining district. It is noted for wheat-growing and the Burra Burra copper mine discovered in 1844.
*   **European Turkey:** In the vilayet of Salonica, the town of Koprülü (pop. 22,000 in 1905) is a center for the silk trade.
*   **Central America:** In a region where the 1905 official population estimate was 500,136, the capital is Tegucigalpa (pop. 35,000). Other important towns include Jutigalpa (18,000) and Comayagua (8,000), while the towns of Santa Rosa, Choluteca, La Esperanza, and Nacaome each have more than 10,000 inhabitants. Seaports include Trujillo (4,000), Amapala (4,000), and Puerto Cortes (2,500).
