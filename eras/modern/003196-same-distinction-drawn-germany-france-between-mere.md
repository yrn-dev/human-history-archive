# Bankruptcy Law and Commercial Relations in Germany and France

## German Bankruptcy Law
Bankruptcy in Germany is governed by a code passed in 1877, which replaced a previously "wholly chaotic" system where each state maintained its own laws. Under this code, Germany draws a distinction between mere commercial failure and bankruptcy. Bankruptcy is further categorized as either simple or fraudulent.

Simple bankruptcy is established through offenses such as extravagance in living, disorderly book-keeping, dealing in "futures," or gambling. Fraudulent bankruptcy involves offenses of a "deeper dye," including the falsifying of books, the manufacture of fictitious debts, the concealment of property, and the giving of illegal preferences. Both forms of bankruptcy are punishable. Fraudulent bankruptcy carries the penalty of penal servitude, though imprisonment for no less than three months may be applied in cases of mitigating circumstances. Accessories to fraudulent bankruptcies, such as a creditor who conspires with a debtor to secure an advantage to the detriment of other debtors, are also liable to penal servitude.

The procedural aspect of German bankruptcy involves calling creditors together within one month of the date of adjudication. At this meeting, creditors may appoint a committee from among their own number to advise the trustee, whose functions are overseen by the court. Estates are liquidated rapidly, typically through the auction of assets to ensure creditors receive dividends as early as possible. While a composition may be accepted by a majority in number and three-fourths in value of the creditors, such an arrangement requires court approval. Fees are described as very moderate; for example, attorney's fees in an ordinary bankruptcy are said not to exceed L5.

## Comparative Commercial Law
In the realm of negotiable instruments, there is general agreement on fundamental principles among commercial nations. However, nations differ in detail. French law was codified in the 17th century by the "Ordonnance de 1673," and the subsequent "Code de Commerce" substantially adopted these provisions. Consequently, the growth of French law was arrested at an early stage. 

In contrast, English law developed gradually through trade custom and judicial decision. While the practice regarding bills of exchange was nearly uniform between France and England around the time of Beawes' *Lex Mercatoria* (published about 1740), English law continued to evolve while French law remained static. This resulted in a divergence where French law rigidly views a bill of exchange as an instrument for transferring a trade debt from one place to another, representing a trade transaction. In England, bills developed into a flexible paper currency.

## Diplomatic and Political Relations
The relationship between Germany, France, and Great Britain has been characterized by shifting alliances and tensions. In the period leading up to the World War, British policy viewed German colonial expansion with indifference, and a strong commercial connection grew between Britain and Germany, with the two nations serving as each other's best customers in Europe. Despite this, Germany and France remained the principal disputants in the balance of power.

During the lead-up to the conflict, the British Cabinet was largely pacific, hoping that France and Germany might mutually neutralize each other without fighting. However, the situation shifted when the King of the Belgians made a direct appeal for French and British aid on August 3.

Following the war, the settlement of the Rhine and Moselle regions involved specific mandates. The Rhine was placed under the Central Commission, composed of four representatives each from France and Germany, and two each from the Netherlands, Switzerland, Belgium, Italy, and Great Britain. France was granted the presidency of this commission. Germany was required to hand over all drafts and designs for this part of the river to France and grant France rights to take water for canals, while Germany agreed not to construct canals on the right bank opposite France.

## Historical and Territorial Context
Historically, the political status of Belgium was shaped by the desire of the Allies at the Congress of Vienna (1814-15) to prevent France from becoming a new menace. To create a bulwark against French extension in the North, the Kingdom of the Netherlands was formed, uniting Belgium and Holland under one sovereign. This established Belgium as a neutral buffer-state located between Germany, France, and England.

In earlier centuries, the nature of nobility differed between the two regions. In France, as the crown overcame the feudal spirit of independence, the title of duke sank from a sovereign title to a mere social distinction without political power. In Germany, where the crown was weaker against forces of disruption, the ducal title eventually came to imply independent sovereignty.
