# Ostitis

Ostitis, derived from the Greek word *osteon* meaning bone, is the inflammation of bone. This condition may present as either acute or chronic.

## Acute Ostitis
Acute ostitis is considered one of the most serious diseases affecting young people. It is caused by the cultivation of virulent germs within the marrow and the delicate growing tissue of the bone. This condition is also referred to as septic osteomyelitis, a term that identifies both the cause and the exact seat of inflammation (*myelos* meaning marrow). 

The inflammation is caused by the micro-organism *Staphylococcus pyogenes aureus*. These germs are characterized by a yellow tinge, their tendency to collect in clusters resembling grapes, and their virulent, pus-producing nature. Typically, these germs enter the blood-stream via the gullet, the membrane lining the mouth, or other parts of the alimentary canal. In the era before antiseptics, germs frequently entered sawn bone during limb amputations, often leading to death or blood-poisoning.

In healthy individuals without injury or strain, staphylococci may circulate harmlessly in the blood until they are consumed by white corpuscles. However, an injured bone provides an attractive focus for these wandering germs. The disease typically begins in the delicate growing tissue located between the cartilaginous end and the diaphysis (the main part of the bone shaft). Consequently, initial pain is often reported just above or below the wrist, elbow, ankle, or knee. In infants, these germs may move into the neighboring joint, resulting in acute arthritis of infants.

Septic ostitis is an infective disease; micro-organisms can travel from damaged bone through the blood-stream to other tissues, potentially causing blood-poisoning. This poses a risk to surgeons who may prick their finger during operation, risking the loss of the finger, hand, or life. While it may be confused with rheumatism or erysipelas, it is distinguished by central tenderness and thickening.

## Chronic Ostitis
Chronic ostitis and periostitis are characterized by increased and long-continued vascular supply, which may result from rheumatism, syphilis, or injury. This form of the disease primarily affects the shafts of the bones, causing tenderness, thickening, and a dull pain that worsens at night. The resulting lump is known as a "hard node," which is clearly visible via X-rays. Treatment involves keeping the affected limb elevated and rested, using fomentations and leeches for pain, and administering iodide of potassium.

## Tuberculous Ostitis and Caries
Chronic inflammation of tuberculous origin affects the spongy ends of long bones, the vertebrae, and the bones of the feet and hands. In this condition, bacilli in the spongy tissue cause colorless corpuscles from the blood to collect around them, forming small greyish-white heaps called tubercles. When these tubercles exist in large numbers and replace living tissue, "rarefying ostitis" is produced.

Over time, these tubercles may break down into tuberculous abscesses that escape to the surface almost painlessly. These should not be allowed to open spontaneously, as they may become infected with pus-producing germs, accelerating destruction. Treatment includes prescribing iron and cod-liver oil, providing fresh air, and placing the affected part on a splint for absolute rest. If the abscess advances toward the surface despite these measures, a surgeon should scrape out the foci and disinfect the area with a strong antiseptic lotion. Tuberculin injections may also be considered.

When tubercles run together and break down the cancellous tissue, the condition is called caries (meaning decay or rottenness). While caries generally refers to tuberculous ostitis, the term has also been used to describe syphilitic ulceration of bone.

## Surgical Intervention and Complications
Upon recognizing the disease, a surgeon should make an incision to the bone, scrape out the affected area, and disinfect it with a solution of corrosive sublimate. In some cases, the shaft of the bone must be opened for much of its length to clear pus and germs. A sub-periosteal abscess may strip a considerable area of bone bare, leading to necrosis.

In severe cases, the diaphysis may be found loose within a large abscess cavity. Immediate amputation may be necessary to save the patient's life, or may be required later due to grave constitutional disturbance and long-continued suppuration. In instances of multiple necrosis, inflammatory engorgement and devastating abscesses may kill several bones simultaneously.
