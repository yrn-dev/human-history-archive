# The Right of the Banker to Summarily Debit Customer Accounts

The relationship between a banker and a customer is defined in law as that of debtor and creditor, rather than a fiduciary relation such as that of trustee and *cestui que trust* or depositee and depositor. Under this framework, when a customer pays money into an account, they are effectively lending that money to the banker. Consequently, the banker is not responsible to the customer for how those funds are utilized.

## The Role of Banking Custom
Bankers may often fortify their legal position by appealing to the "custom of bankers." For such a custom to be valid, it must be general and reasonable. It is presumed that any person entering into business relations with a banker implicitly agrees that business will be conducted according to these customs. 

While there have been suggestions regarding the difficulty of proving such customs without evidence from the general public, it is conceived that outside evidence may be dispensed with, similar to the rules of the Stock Exchange or local customs. This line of reasoning was indicated by the court of appeal in Vagliano's case.

## Summary Debiting of Returned Cheques
A primary example of the application of banking custom is the unquestionable right of a banker to summarily debit a customer's account with a returned cheque. This right persists even in instances where the cheque was unindorsed by the customer and the banker took the instrument under circumstances that made the banker a transferee of that instrument.

## Debiting Accounts in Cases of Fraud and Forgery
The banker's right to debit a customer's account is subject to specific rules when forgery or fraud is involved:

*   **Unauthorized Payments:** A banker cannot charge a customer for payments made based on a forgery of the customer's signature. This holds true regardless of whether the banker is presumed to know the signature or if the payment was unauthorized.
*   **Customer Estoppel:** A customer is estopped from disputing the banker's right to debit them for a loss if the customer accredited the forgery, or if the customer failed to warn the banker despite having knowledge or reasonable grounds to believe a forgery had been committed.
*   **Facilitating Forgery:** If a customer's carelessness in filling out a cheque allows a third party to fraudulently increase the sum, the banker is not entitled to debit the customer for more than the original amount. Such carelessness is not considered a breach of the customer's duty to the banker.

## Other Related Banking Practices
Beyond summary debiting for returned cheques, other customs and legal rights govern the banker's management of accounts:

*   **Stale Cheques:** Bankers commonly refuse payment on "stale" cheques, which are those presented an unreasonable time after their date. However, the validity of this as a general custom is complicated by the fact that different banks use different timeframes (some six months, others twelve).
*   **The Banker's Lien:** Bankers possess an exceptional right known as the banker's lien, which allows them to retain and apply any of the customer's securities coming into their possession (including cheques and bills paid in for collection) to discharge the customer's liability. This is effectively an implied pledge.
*   **Course of Business Limitations:** A banker's strict legal rights, including the exercise of a lien, may be curtailed by the "course of business." If dealings have proceeded on a recognized footing for a reasonable time, the banker cannot suddenly assert strict legal rights to the customer's detriment. For example, a banker may be precluded from using a lien on one account to cover an overdraft on another account if the two had been habitually operated separately.
