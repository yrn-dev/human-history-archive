# Bonin Islands

The Bonin Islands, known in Japanese as Ogasawara-jima, are a chain of twenty small islands belonging to Japan. They stretch nearly due north and south, located a little east of 142 E. and from 26 deg. 35' to 27 deg. 45' N. The chain is situated approximately 500 m. from the mainland of Japan, possessing a total superficies of 28.82 sq. m. and a coast-line of 174.65 m.

## Geography and Nomenclature
While there are twenty islands in total, only ten are of appreciable size. Listed from north to south, these are:
* Muko-shima (Bridegroom Island)
* Nakadachi-shima (Go-between Island)
* Yome-shima (Bride Island)
* Ototo-jima (Younger-brother Island)
* Ani-shima (Elder-brother Island)
* Chichi-jima (Father Island)
* Haha-jima (Mother Island)
* Mei-jima (Niece Island)
* Ani-jima (Elder-sister Island)
* Imoto-jima (Younger-sister Island)

European geographers have historically divided these into three groups: the Parry Islands (northern), the Beechey Islands (central), and the Coffin or Bailey Islands (southern). In 1827, Captain Beechey used different nomenclature for some islands, calling Chichi-jima "Peel Island," Ototo-jima "Stapleton Island," and Ani-jima "Buckland Island." He also named two bays: Fitton Bay on the south of Peel Island and Walker Bay on the south-west of Buckland Island.

The islands are plainly volcanic in nature. Port Lloyd, the chief anchorage located on Peel Island, was observed by Commodore Perry in 1853. Perry believed the port was formerly a volcanic crater that threw up the surrounding hills, with the harbor entrance serving as a fissure where lava once poured into the sea.

## History and Settlement
Japanese annals indicate the islands were discovered toward the close of the 16th century and added to the fief of a Daimyo, Ogasawa Sadayori. The name *Bunin-jima* (which foreigners corrupted to "Bonin") refers to the islands being without (*bu*) inhabitants (*nin*). 

Effective occupation was not maintained early on; communications ceased entirely in 1635 due to the Japanese government's veto on constructing sea-going vessels. Communication was briefly restored in 1728 by a representative of the Ogasawara family, but was interrupted again until 1861.

In 1830, a small party consisting of a Dane, two American citizens, and two British subjects sailed from the Sandwich Islands to Port Lloyd, accompanied by some Hawaiian natives. This group settled on Peel Island and hoisted the British flag. By the time Commodore Perry visited in 1853, the population of Peel Island was thirty-one, including four Americans, four English, one Portuguese, and the remainder being natives of the Ladrones and the Sandwich Islands. 

By 1875, a visit by Mr. Russell Robertson found the colony had grown to sixty-nine people, though only five were "pure whites." Robertson noted that the settlers lived comfortably on cultivated land but lacked laws, religion, education, and a system of government. The settlers spoke English and viewed themselves as a British colony.

## Administration and Modern Status
In 1861, the British government renounced all claims to the islands, recognizing Japan's right of possession. That same year, an attempt to establish a Japanese colony at Port Lloyd was unsuccessful. 

In more recent times, the islands have seen the establishment of regular steam communication and are duly administered. The population has increased to approximately 4,500.
