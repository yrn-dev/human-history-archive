# Principles of Protective Inoculation

Protective inoculation is the practical application of active immunity to prevent disease. The fundamental principle of this process is based on the established fact that recovery from certain infective diseases provides protection against subsequent attacks for varying lengths of time. To prevent disease, a modified attack is produced in the subject.

## Concepts of Immunity
Immunity is defined as non-susceptibility to a specific bacterium, toxin, or disease. This state is relative and depends on existing conditions; for instance, an animal might be susceptible during experimental inoculation but rarely suffer from the disease naturally if the necessary conditions for infection are absent in nature. Susceptibility can be increased by general or local diminished vitality.

There are two primary varieties of artificial immunity:
*   **Active Immunity:** This is produced by a reaction or series of reactions within the body, typically triggered by injections of bacteria or their products.
*   **Passive Immunity:** This occurs through the transference of serum from an actively immunized animal to a fresh animal. Because this process involves no active change in the tissues of the recipient, it is used for serum therapeutics.

## Methods of Producing Active Immunity
Active immunity likely results from substances introduced into the system during a disease rather than the disease itself. Consequently, immunity can be achieved without symptoms if the poison is administered in properly adjusted doses.

One primary method of producing active immunity is inoculation with an "attenuated virus," which consists of bacteria with diminished virulence. Methods for attenuation include:
*   **Environmental Manipulation:** Growing the organism outside the body under unsuitable conditions, such as in the presence of weak antiseptics or at temperatures higher than the optimum.
*   **Artificial Media:** Growing organisms on ordinary artificial media, a process sometimes accelerated by passing a current of air over the growth surface.
*   **Species Passage:** Passing a bacterium through the body of a different animal species to lessen its virulence for the original target animal.

By using vaccines of increasing virulence, immunity can be gradually increased. A very high degree of immunity may be reached by using "supervirulent" cultures, which are attained through the method of passage. A second method of producing active immunity is the injection of bacteria in a dead condition.

## Historical Development and the Anthrax Bacillus
The anthrax bacillus (*Bacillus anthracis*) was central to early attenuation methods, though it is considered exceptional due to the relative stability of its virulence. Around 1880, Pasteur demonstrated that *Bacillus anthracis* cultivated in chicken broth at 42-43°C with plenty of oxygen lost its virulence after a few generations, eventually ceasing to kill mice. Toussaint and Chauveau confirmed these observations. It was discovered that animals inoculated with these attenuated bacilli became resistant to the deadly effects of the non-attenuated wild form.

## Cultivation of Bacteria
To prepare inoculations, bacteria must be cultivated outside the body using sterilized media that mimic the organism's natural habitat, such as the natural fluids of the body. 

### Media and Environment
Media can be fluid or solid. Solid media are created through coagulation or the addition of gelatinizing agents like agar or gelatine. Blood serum solidified at a suitable temperature is highly suitable, as are media based on meat extract with the addition of non-coagulable proteids (commercial "peptone"). The reaction of the media is typically kept neutral or slightly alkaline.

Cultures are created by transferring material via a sterile platinum wire into tubes or flasks plugged with cotton-wool to prevent contamination. These are kept in incubators at temperatures of either 20°C (warm summer temperature) or 37°C (body temperature). "Subcultures" may be made by inoculating fresh tubes to maintain growth indefinitely.

### Anaerobic Cultivation
While the aforementioned methods apply to organisms that grow in the presence of oxygen, anaerobic bacteria require the exclusion of oxygen. This is achieved through "deep cultures," where inoculation is made deep in a solid medium containing a reducing agent like glucose. For fluid cultures or plate methods, air is displaced by an indifferent gas, usually hydrogen.

## Testing and Experimental Inoculation
To test the effects of bacteria, researchers typically employ smaller rodents, including mice, guinea-pigs, and rabbits. However, these animals may not always be susceptible to a given bacterium, or the disease may manifest differently than in humans. For example, monkeys have been used for relapsing fever and Malta fever, but no lower animals have been found susceptible to leprosy.

For inoculation, young vigorous cultures are mixed with a fluid culture or an indifferent fluid. Injections are administered via hypodermic syringe into a vein, the subcutaneous tissue, serous sacs, or other specific body parts. Following injection, the animal is monitored for symptoms. Post-mortem examinations and microscopic analysis of tissues are then used to determine the relationship between the bacteria and structural changes in the organs.
