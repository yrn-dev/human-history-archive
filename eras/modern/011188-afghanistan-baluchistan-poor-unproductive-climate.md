# Afghanistan and Baluchistan

## Geography and Climate
Afghanistan and Baluchistan are part of a great plateau extending from the Mediterranean to the Indus, covering a length of approximately 2500 miles from east to west. This region generally lies at altitudes between 2000 and 8000 feet above sea level. The eastern half of this plateau, which includes Persia, Afghanistan, and Baluchistan, is characterized as poor and unproductive.

The climate in this area is severe, featuring extremely hot summers and very severe winters. Rainfall is very scanty, and running waters are rarely found except among the mountains that form the scarps of the elevated country. This rainless region of south-western Asia shares a continuous flora with the desert flora of northern and eastern Africa, extending from the coast of Senegal to the 75th meridian East. Due to these conditions, the population is sparse and frequently nomadic, with limited progress in civilization and the arts.

## The Territory of Baluchistan
Baluchistan is a country within the borders of British India, extending from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea, and from the borders of Afghanistan and Persia to those of Sind and the Punjab. It is a sparsely populated tract, larger in area than any Indian province except for Bengal, Madras, and Burma.

The region is divided into two primary divisions:
*   **British Baluchistan:** An official province of British India under a chief commissioner. It covers 9,403 square miles and consists mainly of tracts ceded by Afghanistan under the treaty of Gandamak in 1879. This includes the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali.
*   **Foreign Territories:** These areas are under the superintendence of the agent to the governor-general. This category includes native states—such as Las Bela and Kalat (which includes Kharan and Makran)—covering 79,382 square miles, as well as tribal areas possessed by the Bugti and Marri tribes (7,129 square miles). Other directly administered districts, including the Chagai and Zhob political agencies and the Bolan Pass, cover 36,401 square miles.

## Regional Topography
A railway running from the Indus to Quetta, along with its surrounding plains and passes, divides Baluchistan into two distinct sections:

**Northern Baluchistan:** Located between Afghanistan and the Indus plains, this area consists of rough highlands and the central ranges of the Suliman system, the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander. The climate here is clear and bracing without great temperature extremes, and vegetation is often alpine. Drainage in this district flows either north toward the Gomal or east toward the Indus.

**Southern Baluchistan:** This is a square block of territory measuring roughly 300 by 300 miles, primarily the home of the Baluch and Brahui. It contains the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, where drainage cuts deep gorges to reach the plains of Sind. Further south, streams either flow into the Arabian Sea north of Karachi or disappear into inland swamps.

## Frontiers and Political Status
While Afghanistan is regarded as a distinct entity among Asiatic nations, the independence of Baluchistan is qualified. Much of the region is subject to British control, with the agent to the governor-general of India exercising supreme control, similar to the status of native states in the Indian peninsula.

The boundary between Afghanistan and Baluchistan is precisely demarcated. From the Gomal, Baluchistan serves as an intervening state between Afghanistan and British India. The border follows the Kundar stream for 40 miles south-west from Domandi, then diverges north before striking the Kadanai river. It follows the western edge of the Toba plateau (reaching 8000 feet) near New Chaman, then descends to the plains and follows hill ranges south to Nushki. From Nushki, the boundary cuts across the Lora hamun and runs through the desert south of the Gaud-i-Zirreh salt swamps, passing 50 miles south of the Helmund.

To the north-east, Afghan territory extends to just short of 75 degrees East, following the water-divide to the head of the Taghdumbash Pamir and the water-parting of the Hindu Kush. This creates a belt of independent territory—including Bajour, Swat, and parts of Mohmand territory—between Afghanistan and British India.

## Exploration and Trade
Since 1880, a systematic survey of Baluchistan has progressed, aided by the Afghan war of 1878-80 and the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1885. Geographers such as Sir George Robertson and Cockerill explored the Hindu Kush and Kafiristan, while Major Percy M. Sykes, Captain H.B. Vaughan, and General Sir A. Houtum Schindler worked in Persia. These efforts produced maps extending from the Oxus to the Indus and from the Indus to the Euphrates.

The settlement of Afghanistan's southern boundaries and the surveys of Makran and Baluchistan have reopened old trade routes between India and Seistan. A desert route between Seistan and Nushki has been developed with the digging of wells and the establishment of serais, supporting a promising khafila traffic.
