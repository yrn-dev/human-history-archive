# BANK-NOTES

Bank-notes are defined as all paper substitutes for metallic currency, regardless of whether they are issued by governments, banks, or other financial institutes.

## Legal Nature and Characteristics
For ordinary legal purposes, a bank-note may be viewed as a promissory note created by a banker that is payable to the bearer on demand. A promissory note is defined as an unconditional written promise made by one person to another, signed by the maker, who engages to pay a sum certain in money to a specified person or bearer at a fixed, determinable future time, or on demand. 

While a promissory note is a direct promise to pay (unlike a bill of exchange, which is an order to pay), bank-notes are subject to special stamp regulations. Unlike standard promissory notes, a bank-note is not discharged by payment; instead, it may be re-issued repeatedly.

## Regulation and Issue in the United Kingdom
The issue of bank-notes is subject to various statutory restrictions to protect the currency. In England, no bank other than the Bank of England may issue notes unless it already had a lawful note issue in 1844. Notes issued by the Bank of England are considered legal tender, except by the bank itself.

In Scotland and Ireland, legislation from 1844-1845 required banks to hold gold at their offices against any notes issued in excess of an allowed limit. Sir Robert Peel, in speeches delivered in 1844 and 1845, explained that the amount of specie to be held was determined by the average note-issue of the preceding four weeks. The intent was to ensure that bank-notes remained uniformly equivalent to coin and that the circulating medium—composed of both notes and specie—would fluctuate as if it were a purely metallic circulation.

In Scotland, the limitation of note-issuing power to banks that possessed it in 1845 resulted in no important new banks being established in that country since that time. Because notes are so heavily used in ordinary Scottish business, a bank without the power to issue them cannot practically supply its customers' needs or carry on business. Despite this limitation on the number of banks, there is a higher proportion of banking offices relative to the population in Scotland than in Ireland, England, or Wales.

## The United States National Banking System
The national banking system in the United States was characterized by the issuance of circulating notes based on United States bonds. A national bank could deposit U.S. bonds with the United States treasurer—up to an amount not exceeding the bank's capital stock—and receive circulation equal to 90% of the bonds' par-value. 

Initially, any bank established was required to invest one-third of its capital in bonds, though this was reduced in 1874 to 25% (with a maximum mandatory requirement of $50,000). These notes were taxed at an annual rate of 1%. While critics claimed banks earned "double interest" by receiving interest on the bonds held by the treasury while simultaneously lending notes to the public, this profit was reduced by the 1% tax, the fact that notes were not issued at full par-value, and the requirement to maintain reserves in gold or other lawful money for redemption.

Under existing law, national banks are required to keep a 5% reserve in Washington for current redemption. The redemption system has been described as defective because redemptions are not authorized in other locations, meaning notes reach the treasury on average only once every two years. Regarding the retirement of notes, a long-standing prohibition limiting retirement to $3,000,000 monthly was raised to $9,000,000 per month by an act on March 4, 1907.

Following the suspension of specie payments at the end of 1861 and the issuance of government paper-money, gold ceased to be a medium of exchange (except in California). Consequently, national banks redeemed notes in government paper, and the gold-value of bank-notes fluctuated alongside government notes until the national treasury resumed specie payments on January 1, 1879.

## International Note Circulation
In Germany, the average note circulation of the Reichsbank is between £60,000,000 and £70,000,000. This is part of the broader resources of German banks, which include deposits, mortgage bonds, and notes. Additionally, the imperial treasury is obligated to pay state notes in cash upon request, although there is no independent fund of cash set aside for this specific purpose.

## Historical Trends in Circulation
In the United Kingdom, note circulation was a vital part of banking business during the late 18th and early 19th centuries. However, from that period onward, deposits became far more important. By 1906, total resources in the United Kingdom—including capitals, reserves, and a note circulation of approximately £177,500,000—were recorded. In the modern era, the circulating medium of the United Kingdom consists mainly of cheques, a shift that differs from the specie-based fluctuations contemplated by Sir Robert Peel.
