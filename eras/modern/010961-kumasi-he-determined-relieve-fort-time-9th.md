# Kumasi

Kumasi, also referred to as Coomassie, is the capital of Ashanti in British West Africa. Located at 6° 34´ 50´´ N., 2° 12´ W., the town is situated on a low rocky eminence extending across a valley to an opposite hill. It lies within a clearing of the dense forest that covers most of Ashanti, occupying an area approximately 1½ miles in length and over 3 miles in circumference. The name Kum-asi (more correctly Kum-ase) means "under the okum tree," named for the abundance of such trees in its streets. As of 1906, the population was 6,280, increasing to over 12,000 when including the suburbs.

## Geography and Infrastructure
The land immediately surrounding Kumasi was once marshy but has since been drained. The small river Dah, a headstream of the Prah, is located to the north-west. The town is connected to the coast via a 141-mile wide road from Cape Coast and a railway line from Sekondi (168 miles away). While the wide road exists, the usual means of communication remain tortuous forest paths too narrow for wheeled vehicles, and travel in the bush is often conducted via palanquins borne by four carriers. The town is further connected to the Northern Territories and coast towns by telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service.

## Architecture and Urban Layout
The most imposing structure in Kumasi is the fort, built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner and can house several hundred men. The town also contains officers' quarters, cantonments, European and native hospitals, and stations for the Wesleyan and Basel missions. Native houses are constructed from red clay in the universal Ashanti style, often richly ornamented and arranged in compounds for the better class. Leading mercantile houses are located near the railway station, while principal Ashanti chiefs own large European-style houses which they lease to strangers.

## Early History and British Relations
The power of Ashanti was founded or greatly extended by Osai Tutu, who subdued the Mahommedan countries of Banna and Gaman, as well as the state of Denkera in 1719. Later, under King Osai Tutu Quamina (who became king in 1800), the Ashanti invaded Fantiland, resulting in the destruction of a town near Anamabo and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants.

British relations were characterized by periodic conflict and diplomacy. In 1817, a mission including Frederick James, T.E. Bowdich, and W. Hutchinson visited Kumasi, where a treaty was eventually concluded after the king expressed rage over unpaid quit rents for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort. In 1820, Mr. Joseph Dupuis arrived as consul and drew up a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory, though this was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith. In 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy at Essamako; the governor was killed, and his skull was used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. Peace was eventually purchased in 1831 by the king from George Maclean for 600 oz. of gold.

## 19th Century Conflicts
In 1869, an Ashanti force captured several Europeans, including members of the Basel mission (Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife, and son) and a French trader, taking them to Kumasi. Tensions escalated following the 1872 transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British, as the Ashanti viewed the Elmina as their subjects. This led to an invasion of the British protectorate on January 22, 1873, prompting Sir Garnet Wolseley to lead an expedition to march on Kumasi.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Following the deportation of Prempeh, Kumasi was administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart. A rebellion broke out in 1900 involving the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes, who commanded a force reported at 40,000. The conflict was triggered by the governor, Sir F. Hodgson, who demanded 4,000 oz. of gold yearly and the surrender of the "golden stool," which the Kumasi had hidden since 1896.

Kumasi warriors attacked the fort, forcing Sir F. and Lady Hodgson and Basel missionaries to take refuge inside. The garrison grew to 700 men through reinforcements of Gold Coast and Lagos constabulary. By July, the garrison was in dire straits. Colonel James Willcocks organized a relief expedition from Cape Coast, overcoming impenetrable bush and deep mire. After a feint toward Kokofu to draw away enemy forces, Willcocks's column—consisting of 1,000 West African fighting men, 60 white officers/NCOs, and various guns (Maxims, seven-pounders, and 75-millimetre guns)—reached the main rebel stockade on July 15. The stockade was carried by Yoruba troops, and Kumasi was entered that evening.

The rebellion was eventually crushed after the Kumasi were beaten at Obassa on September 30. Peace was restored by 1901, with total campaign casualties reaching 1,007.
