# Oesterreichisch-ungarische Monarchie

The *Oesterreichisch-ungarische Monarchie* (also known as the *Oesterreichisch-ungarisches Reich*), commonly referred to as Austria-Hungary or the Dual Monarchy, was a country located in central Europe. As of 1905, it occupied approximately one-sixteenth of the total area of Europe, covering 239,977 square miles.

## Geography and Borders
The monarchy was bounded to the north by Russia and the German Empire. To the east, it bordered Russia and Rumania, while its southern borders were shared with Rumania, Servia, Montenegro, and Turkey. To the west, the country was bounded by the Adriatic Sea, Italy, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, and the German Empire.

## Political Structure and Administration
The monarchy consisted of two independent states:
*   **Cisleithania:** Unofficially called Austria, this state comprised the kingdoms and lands represented in the council of the empire, known as the *Reichsrat*.
*   **Transleithania:** Unofficially called Hungary, this state consisted of the "lands of St Stephen's Crown."

The official name of the monarchy was established by a diploma issued by Emperor Francis Joseph I on November 14, 1868, replacing the previous designation of the Austrian Empire. The original archduchy, which included Upper Austria, served as the nucleus of the empire and was the oldest possession of the house of Habsburg within the current dominions.

## Population and Territorial Changes
In 1901, the population of the monarchy was 45,405,267 inhabitants, representing about one-eighth of the total population of Europe. 

The monarchy's territory expanded through the Berlin Treaty of 1878, which placed the principalities of Bosnia and Herzegovina under Austro-Hungarian administration. These principalities, which owned Turkey as suzerain, had an area of 19,702 square miles and a population of 1,591,036 as of 1895. Their annexation in 1908 was subsequently recognized by the Powers in 1909, fully integrating them into the dominions of the monarchy.

## Internal Governance and History
Historical records indicate periods of restricted political activity within the monarchy's constituent parts; for example, the diet in Hungary was not summoned between 1811 and 1825, and the diet in Transylvania was not summoned between 1811 and 1834. In certain periods, imperial decrees were required to settle specific matters, such as claims for conscription exemptions not recognized by precedent, while the war department handled the general number of recruits.
