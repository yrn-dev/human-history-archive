# German Political Obstruction in the Austro-Hungarian Empire

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the political landscape of the Austro-Hungarian Empire was characterized by intense national jealousy and race hatred. This environment frequently led to legislative paralysis, particularly within the Reichsrath, where various nationalities utilized obstruction to prevent the conduct of government business.

## The Badeni Ordinances and German Resistance
A significant period of obstruction began following the appointment of Badeni. Seeking support from other nationalities to stabilize his government, Badeni attempted to reach agreements with the Poles and the Young Czechs. The Young Czechs, desiring more influence over the administration, requested further concessions regarding language in Bohemia.

In May 1897, Badeni published ordinances that mandated two primary changes:
1. All correspondence and documents brought before government officials in Bohemia were to be conducted in the language in which they were first introduced, effectively introducing Czech into government offices throughout the kingdom.
2. After 1903, any individual appointed to a government post in Bohemia was required to pass an examination in Czech.

These ordinances were viewed by German Nationalists and Radicals as a fulfillment of their worst fears. In response, these German parties declared that no business should be conducted until the ordinances were repealed and Badeni was dismissed. They resorted to obstruction and filed repeated motions to impeach ministers. Consequently, although no business of any kind was transacted, parliament had to be prorogued in June. During the subsequent recess, the Germans refused to enter into discussions unless the ordinances were withdrawn. This agitation spread across the country, leading to serious disturbances and large meetings in Aussig and Eger, which were attended by Germans from across the frontier.

## The Conflict over Paragraph 14
Following the tenure of Badeni, the Reichsrath was summoned again, but German parties continued to prevent any business from being conducted. The focus of their obstruction shifted to Paragraph 14 of the Constitutional law of 1867. This paragraph allowed the emperor to proclaim orders—signed by the whole ministry—in cases of pressing necessity if the Reichsrath was not in session. Such orders remained valid unless they were laid before the Reichsrath within four months or failed to receive approval from both Houses.

The Germans contended that applying this clause to the *Ausgleich* (the general settlement and bargain between Austria and Hungary) was invalid and demanded its repeal. This political pressure led to the retirement of Thun in September 1899. His successor, Count Clary, attempted to restore peace by withdrawing the controversial language ordinances. However, the German parties were not sufficiently united to support a minister who granted the relief they had sought for two years. Simultaneously, the Czechs entered into opposition and utilized their own forms of obstruction.

Count Clary gave a formal pledge not to use Paragraph 14, but when the Czechs blocked a necessary excise law related to agreements with Hungary, Clary found it impossible to govern without breaking his word. He resigned after less than three months in office. Subsequently, the emperor appointed a ministry of officials who were not bound by Clary's pledge and utilized Paragraph 14 for state purposes.

## The Administration of Dr. von Koerber
Under the ministry of Herr v. Koerber, which began around 1900, initial periods of peace were followed by renewed conflict. The Czechs demanded the restoration of their language in the internal service of Bohemia, and by June 8, they used noise and disturbance to force the president to suspend proceedings.

Dr. von Koerber found himself unable to induce parliament to vote for the budgets of 1903, 1904, or 1905. He was forced to revert to the practice of his predecessors by sanctioning estimates via imperial ordinance under Paragraph 14. In December 1902 and January 1903, Koerber attempted to broker a compromise between Germans and Czechs by proposing a division of Bohemia into ten districts: five Czech, three German, and two mixed. He further proposed that of the 234 district tribunals, 133 be Czech, 94 German, and seven mixed.

The Czechs rejected this, demanding that German and Czech languages be placed on an equal footing for all official purposes throughout Bohemia. The Germans refused this demand because it required recognizing Czech as a language of internal service. Until his fall on December 31, 1904, Koerber governed practically without parliament. The Chamber was summoned primarily as a pretext to justify the subsequent use of Paragraph 14. During this time, the Czechs blocked business using "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction, including one sitting that lasted 57 hours without interruption. In response to this aggressiveness, several German parties—including the German Populists, German Progressists, Christian Socialists, and Constitutional Landed Proprietors—formed a supreme committee and a joint executive committee to protect German racial interests.
