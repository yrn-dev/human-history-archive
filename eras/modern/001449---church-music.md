# CHURCH MUSIC

Church music encompasses a wide array of vocal and instrumental compositions designed for liturgical use, private devotion within a church setting, and the formal organization of ecclesiastical choirs and singing schools. The history of the medium is marked by the contributions of various composers, the development of specific forms such as the cantata and anthem, and ongoing efforts to preserve and restore sacred musical traditions.

## Liturgical Foundations and Early Traditions
The origins and development of church music are often linked to ecclesiastical tradition and the organization of liturgy. In the Roman tradition, tradition ascribes to Gregory the foundation of the Roman *schola cantorum*, the compilation of an Antiphonary, and the rearrangement of the system of church music. However, historical evidence regarding these claims is disputed. While it is doubted that Gregory invented or revived the *cantus planus* or founded the singing school, he is credited with specific liturgical changes. These include the chanting of the Allelulia after the Gradual (at times other than Easter), the recitation of the Pater Noster at the end of the Canon before the fraction of the bread, and the addition of words to the prayer *Hanc igitur*. Additionally, he forbade subdeacons from wearing chasubles and restricted deacons from performing musical portions of the service, with the exception of chanting the gospel.

## Notable Composers of Church Music
Various composers across different eras have specialized in ecclesiastical works, ranging from the severe style of the Catholic Church to the choral traditions of the Lutheran and Anglican faiths.

### European Composers
In Italy, Giuseppe Baini (1775–1844), a priest and musical director to the choir of the pontifical chapel, composed works in the severe ecclesiastical style. His ten-part *Miserere*, written in 1821 by order of Pope Pius VII, remains a permanent part of the Sistine chapel's services during Passion Week. Earlier, Adriano Banchieri (c. 1557–1634) of Bologna served as a composer for both the church and the stage.

In England, Maurice Greene (1695–1755) was a prolific composer of church music. After serving as a chorister and later the organist of St Paul's cathedral, he became the organist and composer to the chapel royal. He is chiefly remembered for his church music, specifically his collection *Forty Select Anthems*, which became a standard work. His compositions also included a "Te Deum" and various occasional pieces for the king's birthday. Additionally, the English madrigalist John Bennett contributed to various collections of church music.

### The Works of J.S. Bach
Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) produced a vast body of church music, particularly during his final period from 1733 to 1750, when his choral works became more numerous and terse. His church music with orchestra includes:
* **Church Cantatas:** 190 in total. Of these, 40 are for solo voices and approximately 60 are founded more or less on chorales. The remainder are practically short oratorios, often titled as such by Bach, and almost always contain a chorale for congregational singing.
* **Other Works:** Three wedding cantatas, an Easter oratorio, and a Christmas oratorio consisting of six connected cantatas designed for performance on six separate days.

The *Neue Bachgesellschaft* has worked to make the results of the first *Bachgesellschaft* accessible through cheap editions and festivals, supporting a movement to restore Bach's vocal music to its place in the Lutheran Church.

## American Contributions
In the United States, Dudley Buck (1839–1909) became a well-known composer of church music. After studying in Paris, Dresden, and Leipzig, he served as an organist in Hartford, Chicago, and Boston. From 1877 to 1903, he held the position of organist at Holy Trinity church in New York. In addition to his church music, Buck composed several cantatas, including *Columbus* (1876), *Golden Legend* (1880), and *Light of Asia* (1885).

## Performance and Practice
The practice of church music varies between formal service roles and private activity. For instance, the scholar Spitta noted that certain musical activities in church referred to making music privately rather than singing in a choir during a formal service. The technical structure of these works has also been a subject of academic debate, such as the conjectures regarding the "displacing" of structure in the *kyrie* of the G minor Mass.
