# Magyar Political and Military Conflicts in the Austro-Hungarian Empire

The political history of the Magyars within the Austro-Hungarian Empire was characterized by recurring tensions regarding national identity, linguistic rights in the military, and the balance of power between the Hungarian crown and the Austrian monarchy. These conflicts often manifested as disputes over the interpretation of fundamental laws and the preservation of Magyar supremacy.

## Military Language and Constitutional Disputes
A significant point of contention between the Hungarian Opposition and the crown concerned the language of command within the joint army. A section of the independence party demanded that German words of command be replaced by Magyar words in regiments recruited from Hungary. The crown viewed this demand as an encroachment on royal military prerogatives as defined by the Hungarian Fundamental Law XII. of 1867.

Specifically, Clause 11 of this law recognized that everything relating to the inner organization, leadership, and unitary direction of the whole army—including the Hungarian army as a complementary part—was subject to the monarch's disposal. The crown argued that implementing Magyar words of command would effectively force the monarch to differentiate the Hungarian part of the army from the Austrian part, making it impossible for non-Magyar officers to command Hungarian regiments, even though less than half of those regiments had a majority of Magyar recruits.

Conversely, partisans of the Magyar words of command cited Clause 12 of the same Fundamental Law. This clause reserved the country's right to fix the conditions under which recruits were granted. They argued that the abolition of German commands could be made a condition for granting recruits, despite the restrictions listed in Clause 11.

## The Crisis of 1902 and the Independence Party
In the autumn of 1902, the Austrian and Hungarian governments proposed bills to increase annual recruit contingents by 21,000 men (approximately 6,500 from Hungary and 14,500 from Austria). Tension arose when military authorities detained several thousand supernumerary recruits in barracks before the bills were passed by parliament. This step was intended to avoid the delay and expense of summoning them again later, but it was resented by the Hungarian Chamber.

The Opposition, led by Francis Kossuth (the eldest son of Louis Kossuth), utilized this grievance to obstruct ministerial measures. Even after the supernumeraries were discharged at the end of 1902, the Opposition continued to demand that the Army bills be withdrawn or counterbalanced by concessions to Magyar nationalist feeling. These requested concessions were aimed at promoting the use of the Magyar language and making Hungarian regiments more Magyar in character.

## The Coalition and Universal Suffrage
Between January 1905 and April 1906, a deadlock occurred between the crown and Hungary. The Coalition, which held the majority in the Chamber, refused to take office unless the crown granted demands including customs separation from Austria and the use of Magyar words of command. The crown rejected these, fearing they would wreck the dual system established in 1867.

This led to a non-parliamentary administration under General Baron Fejervary. To resolve the dispute, Fejervary and Minister of the Interior Kristoffy proposed subjecting the conflict to a test of universal suffrage via a radical reform of the Hungarian franchise. This proposal alarmed the Coalition, as they feared universal suffrage would destroy the political hegemony of the Magyar gentry and nobility. Furthermore, they believed that granting political equality to non-Magyars would undermine the supremacy of the Magyar race. The Coalition eventually came to terms with the crown in February 1906, after the Chamber had been dissolved by military force, in order to ensure that any introduction of suffrage would safeguard Magyar supremacy.

## Relations with Slav Nationalities
The Magyars faced internal pressure from growing Slav separatist movements. In the south, the "Illyrian" movement sought a revival of the "Triune Kingdom" (comprising Dalmatia, Slavonia, and Croatia) independent of the Hungarian crown. Jellachich, the ban of Croatia, played the nationalism of the Illyrians against the radicalism of the Magyars and Germans to preserve his province for the monarchy.

During a critical period involving a campaign in Italy, the Hungarian government attempted to alter the army's relationship with the monarchy by establishing a National Guard in May and inviting line soldiers to join for higher pay. On June 1, the garrison of Pest took an oath to the Constitution. However, Jellachich issued a proclamation on June 10 bidding Croatian regiments to remain and fight for the emperor, cementing an alliance between the southern Slavs and the imperial army.

## External Relations and the Balkans
The political climate also affected foreign policy. While the government in Vienna felt sympathy for Christians in Turkey during insurrections in Bosnia and Herzegovina, active support was impossible because the Hungarians were full of sympathy for the Turks. This sentiment existed because the events of 1848 had obliterated the memory of earlier Turkish conquests among the Magyars.
