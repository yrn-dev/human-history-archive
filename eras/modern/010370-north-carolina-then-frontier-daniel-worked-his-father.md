# North Carolina

North Carolina, particularly its frontier and interior regions, served as a significant site for religious expansion, military conflict, and early American pioneering during the 18th and early 19th centuries.

## Frontier Settlement and Pioneering
The frontier of North Carolina was a site of early settlement for figures such as Daniel Boone. Born in Pennsylvania in 1734, Boone moved with his father, Squire Boone, to the Yadkin Valley in what is now Davie County, North Carolina, around 1751. While living on the frontier, Boone worked on his father's farm and spent a significant amount of time trapping and hunting. 

Boone's experiences in North Carolina preceded his later explorations of the Kentucky region, which he first visited in 1767. His subsequent activities included serving as a blacksmith and wagoner during Braddock's expedition in 1755 and later leading settlers to found Boonesborough in 1775.

## Religious Development
The religious landscape of North Carolina was shaped by the "awakening" and the spread of Baptist principles. Initially, the Baptist cause in North Carolina and Virginia was considered feeble, but it was strengthened by missionaries from the Philadelphia Association, including individuals such as John Gano, Benjamin Griffith, Samuel Eaton, Benjamin Miller, John Thomas, David Thomas, and John Garrard.

A significant development occurred when "New Light" evangelists and Baptists, specifically Shubael Stearns and his brother-in-law Daniel Marshall, settled at Sandy Creek, North Carolina. Within a few years, they established a church with over six hundred members. Marshall further expanded this influence by organizing a church at Abbott's Creek, located approximately 30 miles away. These centers allowed "Separate" Baptist influence to permeate Virginia and extend into Tennessee and Kentucky. The resulting Sandy Creek Association eventually encompassed churches across several colonies.

## Political and Social Tensions
During the War of Independence, North Carolina experienced deep divisions between Loyalists (Tories) and those seeking independence. In the southern colonies, the division did not strictly follow religious lines, as Whig leaders were often members of the established church. However, the Tories in the upper country found their primary support among non-Anglican Scotch and Germans.

Settlers on the frontier of North Carolina and South Carolina faced social and political marginalization by the low-country aristocracy. They were denied adequate representation in the colonial assembly and lacked courts of justice. Because these frontier settlers were not engaged in commerce, they had little use for legal documents or stamps and did not drink tea, making the political disputes over taxation and stamps less materially relevant to them.

## Military Conflicts and the Revolutionary War
North Carolina was a central theater for military operations during the American Revolution. General Nathanael Greene took command of the Southern army at Charlotte, North Carolina, on December 2, 1780. Greene employed a strategy of dividing his troops to force the division of the superior British forces under Cornwallis. This strategic approach led to the battle at Guilford Court House, North Carolina, on March 15, 1781. Although Greene was defeated in this engagement, the cost to the British was so high that it was described as "the pledge of ultimate defeat," leading Cornwallis to withdraw toward Wilmington three days later.

Additionally, the region's geography played a role in key engagements. King's Mountain, a ridge with its highest point and great escarpment in North Carolina, is located in Gaston County. While the Battle of King's Mountain on October 7, 1780, took place just south of the state line, the victory of approximately 900 American backwoodsmen over Loyalist militia contributed significantly to the success of General Greene's campaign.

## Territorial Boundaries
The definition of North Carolina's borders involved long-term surveying and treaties. Between 1779 and 1780, commissioners from North Carolina and Virginia surveyed the southern boundary to the Tennessee River. Although intended to run along the parallel of latitude 36° 30´, the line was mistakenly run north of that parallel. This boundary was later formally adopted by commissioners representing Tennessee and Kentucky in 1820.
