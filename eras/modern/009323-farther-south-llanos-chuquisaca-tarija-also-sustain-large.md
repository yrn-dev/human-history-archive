# The Llanos of Chuquisaca and Tarija

The llanos, or open grassy plains, of Chuquisaca and Tarija are significant geographical and economic regions located in the southern portion of Bolivia. These areas are characterized by their ability to sustain large herds of cattle, particularly within the well-watered plains of the Chaco and the more elevated districts.

## Geography and Hydrography
The southern region of Bolivia contains various low-lying terrains. In the extreme south are the llanos of Manzo and the Bolivian Chaco. Above these, in southern Santa Cruz and eastern Chuquisaca, there are extensive low-lying plains and swamps. While some of these areas are of little value for pastoral or agricultural use due to periodical inundations and defective drainage—caused by streams with currents too sluggish to remove water during the rainy season—there are considerable areas that remain above the flood levels and provide rich grazing lands.

The hydrography of the region is part of the south-east drainage basin, which discharges into the Paraguay. A key feature of this system is the Pilcomayo river, which flows through the sierra region to the Bolivian Chaco. Additionally, the Tarija (or Rio Grande) serves as a large tributary from the Bolivian uplands to the Bermejo, an Argentine river, draining a small district situated south-east of the Santa Victoria sierra.

## Stock-Raising and Industry
Stock-raising was one of the earliest industries established in the country following mining. In the llanos of Chuquisaca and Tarija, the industry is centered on cattle. This follows a broader national pattern of cattle-raising that was successfully established in other regions, such as the llanos of Mojos and Chiquitos, by Jesuit founders who introduced cattle when entering those regions to work among the Indians.

Beyond the large herds in the south, cattle are also raised in small districts within Cochabamba, Potosi, and La Paz. The industry serves several purposes:
* **Consumption:** Cattle are driven into mining districts for food.
* **Processing:** *Saladeros* are used to prepare beef for home markets, typically through salting and sun-drying.
* **Export:** Hides from the cattle are exported.

Other livestock industries in the country include the raising of goats in temperate and warm regions, and sheep for wool in temperate areas. On colder, higher plateaus, breeding focuses on alpacas and llamas, as well as the native fur-bearing chinchilla (*C. laniger*), the exportation of which is prohibited by the Bolivian government to encourage local production.

## Administrative and Religious Context
Chuquisaca and Tarija are two of the eight departments that divide the republic. According to the 1900 census:
* **Chuquisaca:** Has a capital at Sucre, an area of 26,418 square miles, and a population of 204,434.
* **Tarija:** Has a capital at Tarija, an area of 33,036 square miles, and a population of 102,887.

Religiously, both departments fall under the archbishopric of La Plata, which has its seat at Sucre. This archbishopric also includes the departments of Oruro, Potosi, and the Chilean province of Antofagasta. The state recognizes and supports the Roman Apostolic Catholic religion, and the domesticated Indians in these regions profess this faith, though it is influenced by ancestral superstitions.

## Education and Higher Learning
Educational infrastructure exists in both departments. Both Sucre (in Chuquisaca) and Tarija have universities that provide instruction in law. The university at Sucre is noted for dating back to colonial times and is one of the few institutions sufficiently equipped to merit the title of university; it also provides instruction in theology and medicine. In addition to these, there are commercial schools located at Sucre.
