# Germany and Austria

## Diplomatic Relations and European Conflict
The relationship between Germany and Austria was characterized by close strategic alignment and mutual support during periods of European tension. In the early 20th century, Austria, supported by Germany, refused demands from Russia and Great Britain for a conference regarding the annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina. This diplomatic standoff continued until April 1909, when the German Chancellor, Prince von Bülow, intervened. He insisted that Russia should advise Serbia to accept the annexation without compensation or a conference. Bülow asserted that if Russia did not consent, Austria would declare war on Serbia with the "full and complete support of Germany." This pressure led to a Russian collapse, resulting in a "Teutonic triumph" that left England as an isolated defender of international law and treaties.

Earlier, during the Algeciras events, Germany found itself isolated as Russia, Spain, and other signatory Powers aligned with France and England. While a compromise suggested by Austria allowed Germany to withdraw without a loss of dignity, the experience led Germany to feel the necessity of binding Austria more closely to her.

## Political Unification and the Role of Prussia
The political map of Germany underwent significant shifts in the mid-19th century. In 1848, all of Germany, including German Austria, was briefly united under the Frankfort parliament. However, this union was opposed by foreign offices and German courts, who preferred unification through regal and diplomatic action rather than the will of the people.

During this period, the German parliament insisted that the largely German provinces of Schleswig-Holstein belong to Germany and ordered the Prussian army to occupy them. The King of Prussia refused to take orders from the parliament, leading to the body's downfall. Later, Von Bismarck, a minister of the seventeenth-century type, utilized a campaign of annoyance by the King of Denmark, Christian IX, against these duchies to champion German nationality. Bismarck persuaded Austria to join Prussia in a military intervention, which successfully forced Denmark to relinquish the duchies. Subsequently, Bismarck instigated a "fratricidal war of Germans" by picking a quarrel with Austria over the possession of these states to ensure the ascendancy of the Hohenzollern dynasty and the glory of Prussia.

## Wartime Pressures and Internal Stability
During the World War, both the Austrian and German empires faced significant internal pressure caused by privations and hunger. In Austria, the situation was more severe than in Germany. The necessity for peace in Austria was indicated by a letter from the Austrian Emperor to his brother-in-law, Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma. 

This desperation created suspicion within the alliance; Germany doubted the loyalty of Austria, recognizing that the Austrian empire might find a separate peace more welcome than no peace at all, regardless of the sacrifices required. The relationship was described as one between "thieves," characterized by hypocrisy and suspicion.

## Economic and Material Restrictions
By August 13, 1914, the British government established a committee to monitor and hamper the flow of food and raw materials to Germany and Austria. The committee's duties included reporting all importations and exportations and recommending financial, commercial, diplomatic, and military methods to restrict these supplies. Specific focus was placed on neutral ports, particularly Rotterdam in Holland, as well as potential overland routes through Italy, the Adriatic, Norway, Denmark, or through Sweden and the Baltic.

## Musical Instrumentation
In the field of musical instruments, specifically the bombardon, Germany and Austria are noted for the use of the "cylinder action." In these models, the standard piston or pump is replaced by a four-way brass cock, which is operated by a series of cranks and a key.
