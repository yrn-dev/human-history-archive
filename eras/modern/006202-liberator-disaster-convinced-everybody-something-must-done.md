# The Liberator Disaster and the Building Societies Act of 1894

The Liberator disaster served as a catalyst for systemic change in the regulation of building societies, convincing contemporary observers that immediate action was necessary to prevent similar calamities from occurring in the future. This event led to a period of legislative activity involving both the government and private members of the legislature.

## Legislative Response and the Select Committee
Following the disaster, the government of the day introduced a bill aimed at avoiding future calamities. Simultaneously, several private members prepared their own measures, many of which were more stringent than the government's proposal. To resolve these various approaches, all the bills were referred to a select committee. This committee was chaired by Mr. Herbert Gladstone.

The deliberations of this committee ultimately resulted in the passage of the Building Societies Act of 1894.

## The Role of W.L. Jackson
During the committee's proceedings, the Rt. Hon. W.L. Jackson (who later became Lord Allerton) moved for an address to the crown. He requested a return of the property held in possession by building societies. This request marked the first time such a return had been called for.

The managers of the building societies reacted with resentment to this request. Because there were no existing means of enforcing the return, many large societies failed to provide the information, despite receiving frequent applications from the registrar.

## Provisions of the Building Societies Act of 1894
The Act established new requirements for all incorporated societies, mandating that they furnish returns in a prescribed form. These returns were required to include specific schedules detailing:
* Mortgages for amounts that exceeded £5000.
* Properties that the societies had taken possession of for more than twelve months due to the default of the mortgagors.
* Mortgages that were in arrear of repayment subscription for more than twelve months.

## Implementation and Financial Impact
The Building Societies Act of 1894 did not come into operation until January 1, 1895. Consequently, the first complete return under the new regulations was not due until 1896. 

The 1896 returns revealed the scale of the issue that W.L. Jackson had previously highlighted; it appeared that the properties in possession at the time of Jackson's original request must have been valued at at least seven and a half millions in the societies' assets.

In the years following the passage of the Act, building societies significantly reduced their properties in possession. These holdings dropped from 14% of all mortgages to 5%, representing a reduction from 7½ millions to 2½ millions—essentially one-third of the original amount.

While this process required some sacrifice for many societies, the overall balance of profit increased rather than diminished. Despite the initial alarm felt by the managers of the societies, the provisions of the Act proved to be a blessing in disguise.
