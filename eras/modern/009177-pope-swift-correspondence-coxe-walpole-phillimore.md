# Pope's and Swift's Correspondence; W. Coxe's Walpole; Phillimore's

The study of the life and works of Bolingbroke relies upon a diverse array of primary and secondary sources, including personal correspondence, official state papers, and biographical accounts. Among the key references for his letters are the *Correspondence* of Pope and Swift, W. Coxe's *Walpole*, and Phillimore's *Life of Lyttelton*.

## Bolingbroke's Literary and Political Works
Bolingbroke produced a significant body of philosophical and political writing. His chief political works and philosophical essays—including *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge*, *On the Folly and Presumption of Philosophers*, *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*, and *On Authority in Matters of Religion*—were first published in 1754 in an edition by Mallet. This edition was denounced by Johnson as "the blunderbuss charged against religion and morality." Subsequent publications of these works occurred in 1778, 1809, and 1841. Additionally, *A Collection of Political Tracts* was published in 1748.

Bolingbroke's letters were published by G. Parke in 1798 and by Grimoard in 1808 (*Lettres historiques, politiques, philosophiques, &c.*). Other essential sources for his correspondence include the *Hardwicke State Papers* (vol. ii), the *Marchmont Papers* (edited by Sir G.H. Rose, 1831), and letters to Lord Chancellor Hardwicke found in the *Add. MSS. Brit. Museum*, many of which were transcribed by W. Sichel. Further collections are held in the British Museum, specifically letters relating to the treaty of Utrecht, and within the *Hist. MSS. Comm.* (MSS. of Marquis of Bath and Duke of Portland at Welbeck). For those researching his attempts at verse, Walpole's *Royal and Noble Authors* (1806) serves as a reference.

## Biographical Accounts of Bolingbroke
Several biographies of Bolingbroke were produced during and after his lifetime. An *Authentic Memoirs* appeared around 1740 (currently in the Grenville Library, Brit. Mus.), which detailed his escapades. Other contemporary accounts were published in 1752 and 1754, followed by a biography by Goldsmith in 1770.

Modern biographical treatments include:
* Sir Leslie Stephen in the *Dict. of Nat. Biog.* (1897).
* C. de Remusat in *L'Angleterre au 18me siecle* (1856).
* T. Macknight (1863).
* J. Churton Collins (1886).
* A. Hassall (1889).
* Walter Sichel (1901-1902), whose work is described as brilliant and elaborate, though unduly eulogistic.

## Political Career and Relations with Walpole
Bolingbroke's relationship with the English government was marked by exile and eventual pardon. In March 1716, he abandoned the Pretender and promised to help secure the withdrawal of his friends, though he refused to betray individuals or secrets. During his exile, he wrote *Reflexions upon Exile* and a letter to Sir W. Windham (1717), the latter of which was not published until 1753.

He received a pardon in 1723 through the duchess of Kendal, the king's mistress. Upon returning to London, he offered his services to Walpole, but Walpole received his offers to bring Tories into the administration coldly. Despite a 1725 act allowing him to hold real estate (passed by a peremptory order of the king against Walpole's wishes), Walpole successfully kept Bolingbroke excluded from the House of Lords.

By 1726, Bolingbroke sought to organize an opposition to Walpole in conjunction with Windham and Pulteney. In 1727, he began writing a series of letters to the *Craftsman* attacking the Walpoles under the pseudonym "Occasional Writer."

## Intellectual and Social Circle
While residing at his estate in Dawley, Bolingbroke renewed his intimacy with Voltaire, Pope, and Swift. He participated in the literary disputes of Pope and contributed the philosophy for the *Essay on Man*. His correspondence with Swift includes a letter dated June 24, 1727, in which Bolingbroke remarked that "to hanker after a court is below either you or me."
