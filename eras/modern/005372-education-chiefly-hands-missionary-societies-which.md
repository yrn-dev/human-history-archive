# Missionary Societies and Education

Missionary societies have played a significant role in the establishment and administration of education across various regions and denominations, often filling voids in formal schooling or raising the standards of professional training for clergy.

## Educational Initiatives in East Africa and Bagdad
In certain regions, missionary societies serve as the primary providers of education. In British East Africa, missionary societies maintain numerous schools that provide instruction in ordinary branches of elementary education as well as handicrafts. This exists alongside other specific educational institutions, such as Arab schools in Mombasa and government schools for Indians and Europeans in Nairobi.

In Bagdad, the educational landscape is more restricted. While the English Church Missionary Society and the Carmelites maintain missions in the city, the only part of the population provided with schools are the Jews. These include an apprentice school for industrial training, a school for boys established by the *Alliance Israelite* in 1865, and a school for girls established in 1899.

## The Society of Jesus and Catholic Education
The Society of Jesus, or the Jesuits, was founded by Inigo Lopez de Recalde (St. Ignatius of Loyola), a Spanish soldier and priest, as an effort to apply military discipline to religious service. The Jesuits became one of the world's greatest missionary and teaching societies, carrying Christianity to America, China, and India. The society is credited with raising the level of Catholic intelligence and the standard of education throughout the Catholic world, which in turn stimulated competitive educational efforts across Protestant Europe.

## Baptist Missionary and Educational Development
In the United States, Baptist interest in foreign missions grew in the early 19th century, initially supporting the work of English missionary William Carey in India. Following the conversion of Luther Rice and Adoniram Judson in 1812, missionary societies were organized to support work among the heathen. This led to the formation of "The Baptist Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in India and other Foreign Parts" in Boston in 1813, and later the "General Missionary Convention of the Baptist Denomination in the United States of America for Foreign Missions" (the Triennial Convention) in 1814.

As the need for educated ministers for both home and mission work became apparent, the Triennial Convention established Columbian College in Washington (now George Washington University) in 1821, including a theological department intended as a national Baptist institution.

## Other Missionary and Theological Efforts
Educational advancement has also been a focus within Wesleyanism. Jabez Bunting, who served as secretary to the Wesleyan Missionary Society for eighteen years, was appointed president of the first Wesleyan theological college at Hoxton in 1835, where he succeeded in raising the educational standards for Wesleyan ministers.

Additionally, missionary societies have been central to the translation and distribution of the Bible. The Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge (founded 1698) supported Danish Lutheran missionaries in south India to produce the first versions of the Bible in Telugu and Tamil. The Baptist Missionary Society, established in 1792, was driven by William Carey, whose primary life work was translating the Bible into various dialects and languages of India.

## Contrasts in European Education
While missionary societies expanded education globally, some historical perspectives suggest a decline in the "missionary spirit" regarding education within Europe. In the Protestant countries, princes who seized national churches viewed education as a means to capture clever young people for the service of their superiors, often regarding education for the poor as mischievous. In Britain, universities like Oxford were seen as part of the machinery of the aristocracy, serving as nurseries for a race of owner-rulers rather than a broader society.
