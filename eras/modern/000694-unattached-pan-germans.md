# Unattached Pan-Germans

## The Pan-German Movement
The broader Pan-German movement is characterized as a drive for the expansion of a "great German brotherhood." This movement claimed that several groups—specifically the Scandinavians, the Dutch, the Flemish Belgians, and the German Swiss—were members of this brotherhood. From the perspective of a "hungry young empire" seeking growth, these populations were viewed as "good assimilable stuff."

The movement received support from the German monarchy. The sovereign of Germany promoted the cause of consolidating the nation and supported the Pan-German movement as part of a broader vision of Germany as a "world power." This vision included the belief that the future of the nation lay "upon the water," leading to an increased interest in the development of a great navy.

## Political Influence and International Relations
The Pan-German movement, alongside the Colonial parties, exerted significant pressure on the German Government. An example of this influence occurred during the Algeciras Conference. While the German Chancellor, Prince Bülow, had initially stated in 1904 that Germany had no objection to the Anglo-French Agreement on Egypt and Morocco from the standpoint of German interest, the "serious agitation" caused by the Pan-German and Colonial parties forced a change in the government's attitude. Consequently, Germany openly challenged the Agreement and sought to assert its claims in Morocco.

## Geopolitical Ambitions
The ambitions of the Pan-German movement extended toward world dominion. This drive is associated with the "Berlin-Bagdad" dream, which became a "hard reality" alongside the emergence of Mitteleuropa. These goals were pursued through strategic alignments; for instance, Bulgaria joined the Teutonic powers in 1914, striking Serbia from the rear after Russia had been weakened by Hindenburg's "hammer-strokes" in the summer of 1915.

## Cultural and Intellectual Context
Pan-Germanism was also linked to cultural preservation and promotion. The German sovereign used his influence to maintain the "distinctive and blinding German blackletter" type over the Roman type used in the rest of western Europe. Additionally, the ideology was promoted through public lectures; however, some observers noted that such efforts were redundant in centers of German culture, such as Heidelberg, which was described as the "ancient cradle of German culture."
