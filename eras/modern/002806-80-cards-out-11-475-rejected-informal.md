# Voting Cards and Ballot Systems

The implementation of the ballot system in the 19th century involved various procedural adaptations across different colonies and regions to ensure security and efficiency.

## Australian Ballot Systems
The ballot was introduced in Australia via the Constitution Act of South Australia in 1856, and was adopted by other colonies at the same time. Different colonies employed distinct methods for handling voting cards:

*   **South Australia:** Under the Electoral Act of 1858, the returning-officer initialed the voting card. Voters were required to fold the card so the officer could not see the vote, which was indicated by a cross; failure to do so resulted in nullity.
*   **Victoria:** The Electoral Act of 1865 required the officer to add a number to his initials that corresponded to the voter's number on the register.
*   **Tasmania:** To prevent double-voting, the use of non-official cards, or voters carrying away their cards, the voter did not place the card in the box directly. Instead, the card was handed to the officer, who then placed it in the box.

## English Test-Ballots
In 1869, test-ballots based on the Victorian system were conducted in Stafford and Manchester, England. In these tests, voting cards listed all candidates' names printed in different colors to assist illiterate voters. Voters scored out the names of candidates they did not support and placed the card in the box inside an official envelope.

In Manchester, the system was found to be less expensive and more rapid than the previous system. Data showed that only 80 cards out of 11,475 were rejected as informal. The risk of personation was decreased because the public was barred from receiving information on how voters voted, and representatives of candidates were present to check identity statements. Because cards in Manchester were not numbered consecutively, no scrutiny could detect which individuals cast particular votes, provided officials were not corrupt.

In Stafford, the returning-officer used a stamp on each card, with the stamp's die finished only on the morning of the election. This was intended to exclude "the Tasmanian Dodge," a practice where a corrupt voter placed a blank or fraudulent card in the box or gave it to the returning-officer.

## Other Card-Based Systems
Beyond electoral ballots, cards are utilized in various games and administrative contexts:

*   **Lotto (Keno):** In this gambling game, players use large boards or cards divided into three horizontal rows of nine spaces. Each row has five spaces marked with numbers up to 90 and four blank spaces. Each card has a general number and is registered or "pegged" on a table upon purchase.
*   **Calabresella:** An Italian card game for three players using a modified pack where all eights, nines, and tens are removed.
*   **Administrative Cards:** In the context of the U.S. Department of Justice and FBI, "Information Cards" are used to track Victim Identification Numbers (VIN) and Personal Identification Numbers (PIN) for case notifications and investigative status.
