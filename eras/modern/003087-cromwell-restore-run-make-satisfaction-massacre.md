# Cromwell, the Restoration of Run, and the Amboyna Massacre

## Conflict in the Banda Islands
The Banda Islands were first annexed by the Portuguese Antonio D'Abreu in 1512, though the Dutch expelled the Portuguese at the start of the 17th century. British interests in the region began in 1608 with the construction of a factory on Wai, which the Dutch demolished following the departure of the English vessel. Despite this, the British later gained Banda Neira and Lontor through resignation by the natives, and by 1620, they added Wai and Run to their dominions. Although treaties were established, the Dutch subsequently attacked and expelled their British rivals.

## The Intervention of Oliver Cromwell
In 1654, the Dutch were compelled by Oliver Cromwell to restore the island of Run to the British. As part of this resolution, the Dutch were also required to make satisfaction for the massacre of Amboyna. However, the English settlers lacked adequate support from home, leading to the island being retaken by the Dutch in 1664. The Dutch maintained undisturbed possession of the islands until 1796, when they were captured by the British. The islands were later restored to the Dutch via the treaty of Amiens in 1800, captured once more, and finally restored by the treaty of Paris in 1814.

## Cromwell's Naval and Diplomatic Strategy
Cromwell's efforts to secure English interests extended beyond the Banda Islands. He turned his attention to Holland after the country seized upon English internal divisions to injure a trade rival. Through a series of obstinate sea fights, the English drove the Dutch from British seas, establishing England as the ascendant naval power to the point that Dutch and French ships were required to dip their flags.

Cromwell's diplomatic and military reach also extended to the Mediterranean, where the first English naval force to enter those waters addressed grievances with Malta and Tuscany and destroyed the pirate fleet of Algiers. Additionally, Cromwell intervened to protect Protestants in the south of France who were being hunted to death by the Duke of Savoy. In 1655, Sir George Downing was sent to France to remonstrate regarding the massacre of the Protestant Vaudois. By 1657, Downing was appointed resident at The Hague to defend English traders against the Dutch, mediate between various European powers (including Sweden, Denmark, Portugal, and Holland), and monitor exiled royalists.

## Other Military Campaigns and Massacres
Cromwell's leadership was characterized by the discipline and strength of the army he created. In Ireland, he suppressed an insurrection with great vigour. This was a response to a ferocious and barbaric massacre of Protestant English in 1641 perpetrated by Irish Catholics, which spared neither women nor children. Because these atrocities were fresh in his mind, Cromwell gave no quarter in battle to men with arms in their hands—a severity that remains a source of bitterness for Irish Catholics.

In Scotland, Cromwell shattered a Royalist army at the Battle of Dunbar on September 3 (13), 1650. Facing David Leslie, Cromwell commanded under 11,000 effective men on unfavorable terrain against odds of two to one. Despite being enfeebled by sickness, Cromwell held his ground and successfully maneuvered his forces across the Brocksburn (or Spott Burn) to surprise the Scots in their bivouacs.
