# Chinese Turkestan and the Central Asian Highlands

The region of Chinese Turkestan, also referred to as Eastern Turkestan, forms a critical part of the vast central Asian landscape. In its widest sense, the term "China" includes this region along with Tibet, Manchuria, Mongolia, and the Eighteen Provinces. Geographically, Chinese Turkestan is situated within the great desert of central Asia known as the Gobi, specifically forming the western portion of this desert.

## Geography and Orography
The region is defined by significant mountain systems and high-altitude plateaus. To the south lies the northern face of the Tibetan plateau, while the north and northwest are bounded by the Tian-shan and the Yablonoi Mountains. The Kuen-lun mountains extend from the frontiers of Kashmir, north of Leh, almost due east to the Chinese province of Kansu. While once considered the southern limit of the Turkestan depression, the Kuen-lun is now recognized as the northern limit of the Chang Tang, the elevated highland steppes of Tibet.

Between the Kuen-lun and the Thien Shan lies the Tarim Basin, which corresponds roughly to Eastern Turkestan. This basin is characterized by rivers that do not reach the sea, ending instead in intermittent lakes and swamps. Historically, this basin was more fertile than it is currently. To the northeast of the Kuen-lun, a minor system branches off at approximately 83 deg. E. longitude, culminating in the Altyn Tagh and extending to the Nan Shan mountains north of the Koko Nor basin. This creates a system of elevated valleys known as Tsaidam, situated 8,000 to 9,000 feet above sea level, serving as an intermediate steppe between the highest regions and the low-lying sands of the Gobi east of Lop Nor.

The elevation of the region is considerable; no part of the central Asiatic desert is believed to be less than 2,000 feet above sea level. The plains around Yarkand and Kashgar range from 4,000 to 6,000 feet.

## Historical Movements and Imperial Expansion
The region has been shaped by the expansion of the Han Empire (B.C. 206 – A.D. 220). The Han pushed strongly from north China into Mongolia, with Chinese farmers following imperial frontier guards to plough the grasslands. This expansion forced Hunnish peoples to either settle as Chinese tax-payers or migrate. Some nomads drifted eastward and northeastward through mountain passes into western Turkestan.

Between 200 B.C. and 200 A.D., the Chinese Empire maintained a resolute advancing front against nomadism, causing a steady westward drift of nomads. A second major Chinese thrust began around 75 A.D. By 102 A.D., the Chinese general Pan Chau sent explorers from an advanced camp—located by some authorities at the Persian Gulf and others at the Caspian—to investigate the Roman power.

Later, the Moslem Empire expanded into Western Turkestan and pushed eastward until it encountered the Chinese.

## Trade and Connectivity
The Tarim Basin serves as a natural meeting place for Mongolian and Aryan peoples. While the mountain barrier to the west of the basin is high, it is not forbidding. Practicable routes exist along the Tarim valley or the northern foothills of the Kuen-lun, leading from China to Kashgar. From Kashgar, travelers could cross the mountains to reach Bokhara, Samarkand, and Kokand.

The geography of the region explains why the great trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed so far to the north. The area between South Russia, Turkestan, and Manchuria is noted for exceptional climatic insecurity, characterized by cycles of humidity and killing droughts.

## Scientific Exploration and Mapping
Recent geographical research has sought to define the highland plateau stretching from the Himalaya northwards to Chinese Turkestan and from the frontier of Kashmir eastwards to China. Key obligatory points for modern map construction include Sachu in Mongolia (with a revised longitude of 94 deg. 54' E. given by Prjevalsky) and Darchendo (Tachienlu) on the extreme east (adopted by the Royal Geographical Society as 102 deg. 12").

Early European pioneers in the dominion of Kashgar included Robert Barkley Shaw and George Hayward, who arrived at Yarkand in 1868. In 1870, Sir Douglas Forsyth led a mission to Yarkand, during which Henry Trotter produced the first maps of Chinese Turkestan. Further knowledge was gained via the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission (1884-1886), during which Ney Elias identified the "Dragon Lake" (Rangkul) while crossing from China through Badakshan and the Pamirs. In 1886, Sir Francis Younghusband crossed the Muztagh, the mountain barrier separating Kashmir and China.
