# Agricultural and Climatic Conditions North of the Tropic

## Indian Agricultural Zones
In the Indian region, agriculture is characterized by a combination of harvests from both temperate and tropical zones. In the areas located north of the tropic, the winter cold is sufficient to allow for the cultivation of nearly all vegetables and cereals found in Europe. In this specific region, wheat is typically sown in November and reaped in early April.

Conversely, the summer months in this same region are characterized by rain and heat, creating a thoroughly tropical climate. This allows for the free raising of tropical cereals, such as rice, which are generally sown in early July and reaped in September or October. This differs significantly from southern India and other similar Asian islands, where the variation between the summer and winter half-years is not sufficient to permit the proper cultivation of barley or wheat. In those southern areas, other cereals may appear in all stages of progress throughout the year only where artificial irrigation is used; otherwise, agriculture is generally limited to the rainy months when water is available.

## Flora and Regional Variations in India
The flora of eastern India's wetter and hotter tropical regions is continuous with that of the islands and the Malayan peninsula. This vegetation extends along the lower ranges of the Himalaya, though it becomes less marked and rises to lower elevations as one moves westward, where winter cold increases and rainfall diminishes.

The higher ranges of the Himalaya, being cooler and less rainy, exhibit a more uniform character and a closer approach to European forms. At the highest elevations, many alpine plants are found that are believed to be identical to species from the north Arctic regions. On the Tibetan plateau, the flora takes on features of the Siberian type due to increased dryness, including many Siberian genera and species. Some of these Siberian forms extend into the plains of upper India and the rainy parts of the mountains. Additionally, marine plants are frequently found growing on the Tibetan plateau at elevations of 15,000 to 14,000 ft., particularly near salt lakes.

## Comparative Global Climatic Zones
Different regions of the world exhibit varied climatic zoning based on altitude and latitude:

*   **Bolivia:** The natives identify several zones. The *yungas* (up to 5,000 ft.) are tropical and humid with no known winter. The *valle* (5,000 to 9,500 ft.) is sub-tropical and described as a region of perpetual summer. The *cabezera de valle* (9,500 to 11,000 ft.) is temperate and favorable for vegetables and cereals. The *puna* (11,000 to 12,500 ft.) has a cold, dry air and only two seasons (winter and a cold summer or autumn), limiting production to barley and potatoes. The *puna brava* (12,500 to 17,500 ft.) is a bleak territory for miners and shepherds, above which lies an Arctic zone of eternal snow.
*   **Costa Rica:** This region is divided into tropical, temperate, and cold zones that generally succeed one another as altitude increases. The tropical zone includes the foothills and coast. The temperate zone includes the San Jose plateau (3,000-5,000 ft.). Above 7,500 ft., snow rarely falls, but frosts are frequent.
*   **Denmark:** The climate is similar to Great Britain at the same latitude, though the winter is colder and the summer slightly warmer. The mean average temperature for the country is 45.14°. Winter is marked by frost (averaging twenty days per month from December to March) and ice that can make the Great Belt and the Sound impassable, sometimes allowing sledges to pass between Malmö and Copenhagen.

## Historical and Theoretical Perspectives on Zones
Historically, the concept of *climata* based on the length of the longest day was introduced by Hippocrates around 400 B.C. Additionally, the Pythagoreans introduced "zones" similar to those of the celestial sphere. Parmenides of Elea (544-430 B.C.) distinguished five specific zones: two frigid zones and two intermediate temperate zones, with a torrid zone between the tropics of summer and winter, which he considered uninhabitable due to heat.
