# Retirement and Strict Discipline in Historical Context

Across various historical eras and cultures, the concepts of retirement and the exercise of strict discipline—whether domestic, social, or professional—have manifested in diverse forms, ranging from genuine seclusion to strategic political maneuvers.

## Retirement as Political and Personal Transition

Retirement has frequently served as a transition for figures of power, though the reality of such withdrawals varied. In Japan, Tokugawa Ieyasu retired to the castle of Sumpu in 1605 after conveying his desire to the Imperial Court to be relieved of military functions in favor of his son, Hidetada. While his income was reduced to 120,000 koku annually from estates in Omi, Ise, and Mino, this retirement was formal rather than factual. Ieyasu continued to manage all administrative affairs from Sumpu, while the shogun in Yedo exercised merely the power of sanction. Ieyasu maintained this influence through frequent journeys to Yedo, ostensibly for hawking but actually for government purposes.

In contrast, Matsudaira Sadanobu resigned his office in 1793. While the exact cause of his retirement is uncertain, it is attributed to enemies created by his uncompromising zeal for reform and resentment from the sovereign and loyalists regarding his influence in a dispute between the Courts of Yedo and Kyoto. Unlike a disgraced official, Sadanobu was promoted to general of the Left and invited to the castle for State occasions. He chose a life of retirement focused on literary pursuits—writing under the pen-name Rakuo—and the administration of his own domain until his death in 1829.

In the Roman era, the poet Horace adopted a more meditative and retired life following the publication of his first book of *Epistles*. This transition involved a growing fondness for study and the country. During this period, he framed a personal scheme of life and sought to inculcate it in others, while continuing to maintain friendships with younger men interested in literary ambition or public affairs.

## Domestic and Social Strictness

Strictness in personal conduct and domestic management has often been linked to early life experiences or cultural expectations. Letizia, known by the title *Madame Mere*, lived a life characterized by a strict domestic economy. This habit was a "second nature" developed from her early privations. However, this strictness rendered her unpopular in France and displeased Napoleon. Following the downfall of Waterloo, she resided in Rome under the protection of Pope Pius VII. Her later years were marked by mournful seclusion following the deaths of her daughters, Elisa and Pauline, as well as several grandchildren. By 1829, she was crippled by a fall and became nearly blind before her death in 1836.

In ancient Athens, strictness was a defining feature of the lives of free citizen women. They lived in a state of "almost oriental recluseness," whether single or married. Their rights, happiness, and lives were managed entirely by male relatives, and they were generally destitute of mental culture. This stood in sharp contrast to the *hetæræ* (courtezans), such as Aspasia, who lived free lives, managed their own affairs, and exercised mental ascendency or inspired passion.

## Discipline and Propriety

The exercise of strict discipline also extended to social decorum and legal application. In the context of Japanese social decorum, the Ogasawara school of etiquette emphasized that the end of all etiquette is to cultivate the mind so that one remains secure even when quietly seated. This strict observance of propriety was viewed as moral training, using correct manners to bring the body's faculties into harmony and express the mastery of spirit over flesh.

In the realm of English criminal law regarding homicide, the archive notes that the consequences of too strict an adherence to technical definitions are avoided through specific mechanisms. These include the jury's power to convict of manslaughter instead of murder, the judge's report on the circumstances of a case, and the Home Office's examination of evidence to determine if the prerogative of mercy should be exercised.

## The Risks of Retiring Views

The tension between retirement and imperial necessity is highlighted in the history of the Peloponnesian War. It was argued that those who are "retiring and unambitious" are unable to secure themselves without vigorous protectors. In the context of an imperial city, such qualities were deemed useless, as the burdens of empire—including the animosities incurred in its exercise—cannot be declined while still expecting to share in its honors. From this perspective, adopting an unambitious or retiring part was seen as a danger that could ruin a state.
