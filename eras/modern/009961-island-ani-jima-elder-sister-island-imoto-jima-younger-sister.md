# Ani-jima and Imoto-jima

Ani-jima (Elder-sister Island) and Imoto-jima (Younger-sister Island) are two of the islands that comprise the Bonin Islands, a chain of small islands belonging to Japan. 

## Geography and Nomenclature
The Bonin Islands, known in Japanese as Ogasawara-jima, consist of twenty islands according to Japanese investigations. The chain stretches nearly due north and south, located a little east of 142 E. and between 26 deg. 35' and 27 deg. 45' N. The group is situated approximately 500 miles from the mainland of Japan, possessing a total superficies of 28.82 sq. m. and a coast-line of 174.65 m.

While there are twenty islands in total, only ten are considered to have any appreciable size. These ten are named, from north to south:
* Muko-shima (Bridegroom Island)
* Nakadachi-shima (Go-between Island)
* Yome-shima (Bride Island)
* Ototo-jima (Younger-brother Island)
* Ani-shima (Elder-brother Island)
* Chichi-jima (Father Island)
* Haha-jima (Mother Island)
* Mei-jima (Niece Island)
* Ani-jima (Elder-sister Island)
* Imoto-jima (Younger-sister Island)

European geographers have historically divided these islands into three groups: the Parry Islands in the north, the Beechey Islands in the center, and the Coffin or Bailey Islands in the south.

## Geological Nature
The islands are plainly volcanic in nature. This is exemplified by Port Lloyd, the chief anchorage located on Chichi-jima (also known as Peel Island). Commodore Perry, who visited in 1853, observed that Port Lloyd appeared to have formerly been the crater of a volcano. He noted that the surrounding hills were thrown up from this crater and that the harbor's entrance was a fissure through which lava once poured into the sea.

## History and Administration
The ownership of the islands was doubtful for a period of time, as evidenced by the diversity of their nomenclature. Japanese annals state that the islands were discovered toward the end of the 16th century and were added to the fief of a Daimyo named Ogasawa Sadayori, which provided the name Ogasawara-jima. They were also referred to as Bunin-jima—a term corrupted by foreigners into "Bonin"—because they were without inhabitants (*bu* meaning without, *nin* meaning inhabitants).

Effective occupation was not maintained; communications with the islands ceased entirely in 1635 due to a Japanese government veto on the construction of sea-going vessels. Communication was restored fitfully in 1728 by a representative of the Ogasawara family, but was interrupted again until 1861, when a Japanese colony was unsuccessfully attempted at Port Lloyd.

During the intervening years, Captain Beechey visited the islands aboard the "Blossom," publishing descriptions and assigning European names to several islands. He named Chichi-jima "Peel Island," Ototo-jima "Stapleton Island," and Ani-shima (Elder-brother Island) "Buckland Island." He also named Walker Bay on the south-west of Buckland Island and Fitton Bay on the south of Peel Island.

In 1830, a small party including two American citizens, two British subjects, and a Dane sailed from the Sandwich Islands to Port Lloyd, accompanied by Hawaiian natives. This group settled on Peel Island (Chichi-jima) and hoisted the British flag. By 1853, when Commodore Perry visited, the population of Peel Island was thirty-one, consisting of four Americans, four English, one Portuguese, and the remainder being natives of the Ladrones and the Sandwich Islands. By 1875, visitor Russell Robertson found the colony had grown to sixty-nine people, though only five were "pure whites." Robertson described the settlers as living comfortably on cultivated land, though they lacked laws, religion, education, and a system of government. The settlers spoke English and viewed themselves as a British colony.

In 1861, the British government renounced all claims to the islands, recognizing Japan's right of possession. Following this, the islands became duly administered, regular steam communication was established, and the population grew to approximately 4,500.
