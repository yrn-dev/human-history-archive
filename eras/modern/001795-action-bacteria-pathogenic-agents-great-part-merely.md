# Pathogenic Bacteria

The action of bacteria as pathogenic agents is largely an instance of their general capacity to produce chemical changes. While the vast majority of these organisms are harmless or indispensable to natural operations, certain groups have acquired the ability to invade body tissues and produce disease.

## Nature and Evolution of Pathogenicity
Pathogenic bacteria are not organisms with unique features; rather, each is a member of a group possessing closely allied characters. From an evolutionary perspective, it is supposed that certain races within a bacterial group gradually acquired the power to produce disease. While some original characters may change during the acquisition of pathogenic properties, these changes are often slight and not always permanent.

Because the characters of bacteria can be modified by slight changes in their environment, it is often difficult to establish a sharp line between pathogenic and non-pathogenic Schizomycetes. Similarly, a distinction cannot be drawn sharply between parasites and saprophytes, as "facultative parasites" can function as either depending on the circumstances.

## Cultivation and Identification
To study bacteria outside the body, researchers use media that mimic the organism's natural habitat; for pathogenic bacteria, this means using fluids similar to those found in the body. These media are sterilized by heat and may be fluid or solid. Solid media are often preferred because bacterial growth appears as a discrete mass, making accidental contamination easier to recognize. Common solidifying agents include gelatin, agar, or the use of blood serum solidified at a suitable temperature. Meat extract and non-coagulable proteids (commercial "peptone") are also used.

The reaction of the media is carefully managed, typically remaining neutral or slightly alkaline. Cultures are created by transferring material via a sterile platinum wire into tubes or flasks plugged with cotton-wool for protection. These are kept in incubators at specific temperatures, usually 20°C (warm summer temperature) or 37°C (body temperature).

A full description of a bacterium requires an account of its microscopical characters, biological properties, effects produced in animals by inoculation, and growth characters in various media. Specific tests include:
* **Fermentative action:** Adding sugars such as glucose, lactose, or saccharose.
* **Reaction changes:** Adding litmus.
* **Indol formation:** Using peptone solution.
* **Curdling:** Using sterilized milk.

## History of Bacteriological Research
The demonstration by Pasteur that bacteria could produce definite diseases stimulated research into infective conditions. A major advancement occurred in 1881 when Koch introduced the "plate-method" and solid culture media, which simplified the separation and cultivation of bacteria. By 1876, Koch had obtained the anthrax bacillus in pure culture.

Between 1880 and 1890, the organisms responsible for cholera, tetanus, typhoid fever, diphtheria, glanders, tubercle, and suppuration were identified. Later discoveries in the final decade of the 19th century included the bacilli of influenza (1892), plague (1894), and dysentery (1898). Research into immunity began around 1880, with pioneers including Pasteur, Toussaint, and Chauveau in France, and Duguid, J. S. Burdon-Sanderson, and W. S. Greenfield in Great Britain. Additionally, Metchnikoff's theory of phagocytosis, dating from approximately 1884, provided significant stimulus to the field.

## Environmental Effects and Resistance
Radiant energy has a documented bactericidal effect. While early observations by Downes and Blunt in 1877 were uncertain, later work by Arloing, Buchner, and Chmelewski proved that light rays alone can kill bacteria. Marshall Ward's experiments in 1892-1893 demonstrated that blue-violet and ultra-violet rays could kill the spores of *B. anthracis* even at temperatures below those favorable for growth, and that this occurred regardless of the presence of food materials. This process destroys enormous quantities of germs in the atmosphere and rivers, particularly pathogenic bacteria like typhoid and anthrax.

Bacteria also exhibit extreme temperature resistance. Some bacilli can survive seven days in liquid air (-192°C to -183°C) and ten hours in liquid hydrogen (-252°C). This resistance is likely linked to the fact that completely dry albumin does not lose its coagulability when heated to 110°C for several hours.

## Bacterial Action in Plants
Bacterial diseases in higher plants are characterized primarily by the wilting of shoots due to the stoppage of vessels. In such cases, transverse sections of shoots often appear brown and choked with a dark yellowish slime containing bacteria; this is seen in potatoes, cucumbers, and cabbages.

However, some attributed plant diseases are doubtful. In cases like the slime fluxes of oaks or Sereh disease of sugar-cane, bacteria are likely saprophytic forms that entered rotting tissues already injured by other agents, such as fungi or insects. While it was once argued that plant acidity provided immunity, it is now known that many bacteria flourish in acid media. Furthermore, while some bacteria cannot penetrate cell-walls, certain anaerobic forms can ferment cellulose, and others, such as those in Leguminosae described by Marshall Ward in 1887, can penetrate the cell-walls of living cells.
