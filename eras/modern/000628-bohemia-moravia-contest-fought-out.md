# Bohemia and Moravia: Political and Cultural Contests

Bohemia is a kingdom and crownland of Austria, serving as the principal province of the Austrian empire. It is situated in the geographical center of the European continent, bounded to the northeast by Prussian Silesia, to the southeast by Moravia and Lower Austria, to the south by Upper Austria, to the southwest by Bavaria, and to the northwest by Saxony. Covering an area of 20,060 square miles, it acts as a borderland between the Slavonic and German worlds.

## Early History and Christianization
The introduction of Christianity into Bohemia initially met with little success when attempted from Germany, due to an innate distrust of the Germans. However, the faith was more readily adopted when it arrived from Moravia, taught by Cyrillus and Methodius, Greek monks from Thessalonica. Around 873, Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius. 

Early rulers included Wenceslas, surnamed the Holy, who was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized by the Church of Rome. Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, expanded the frontiers of Bohemia to include Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and the region now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. This empire did not endure; Boleslav III lost the foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland, and subsequent internal civil wars between Boleslav III and his brothers Jaromir and Ulrick led to the Bohemians dethroning him in favor of the Polish prince Vladivoj.

## Medieval Sovereignty and Expansion
By 1197, Premysl Ottakar became the undisputed ruler of Bohemia, and the royal title of the Bohemian sovereigns continued uninterruptedly from 1198. One of the most significant rulers was Premysl Ottakar II, who obtained the archduchies of Austria and the recognition of the nobility of Styria. His power reached its peak after defeating King Bela of Hungary at the battle of Kressenbrunn in 1260, eventually extending his possessions from the Giant Mountains to the Adriatic. Known as "the man of gold" for his wealth and "the man of iron" for his military power, Ottakar encouraged German immigration to create a counterpoise to the power of the Bohemian nobility.

## Religious and Political Strife
Bohemia was the site of significant religious conflict during the reign of King George, the only Hussite king of Bohemia. Initially allied with Matthias Corvinus of Hungary, George later entered a protracted struggle with the papal see. In 1452, George declared his commitment to the communion in both kinds. This led to the formation of a confederacy against him at Zelena Hora in 1465, supported by Pope Paul II, who excommunicated George. King Matthias of Hungary eventually invaded Moravia and took possession of Brunn, and on May 3, 1469, Matthias was proclaimed king of Bohemia at a meeting of Catholic nobles in Olmutz.

Later conflicts arose between Rudolph and his brother Matthias. The rupture was formally caused by Rudolph's refusal to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey. Matthias's forces entered Moravia, where the estates renounced allegiance to Rudolph. Although Matthias invaded Bohemia, the Bohemians declined his overtures at Caslav. In June 1608, an understanding was reached: Rudolph ceded the government of Hungary, Moravia, and Upper and Lower Austria to Matthias, while Rudolph retained his rights as king of Bohemia.

In 1619, the Bohemian estates deposed Ferdinand and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as king. While Thurn occupied Moravia, the imperial forces—aided by Spain, Poland, Italy, and the duke of Bavaria—reconquered the region. On November 8, 1620, the Bohemians were defeated at the "Bila Hora" (White Hill), leading to the occupation of Prague and the flight of King Frederick.

## Nationalist Contests and Language
In the 19th century, Bohemia became the center of a vehement contest between Czech and German populations. Over three-fifths of the population were Czech, and a party led by Palacky and Rieger demanded the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy, including Silesia and Moravia. They insisted the emperor be crowned in Prague and that Bohemia receive a position similar to Hungary's. This movement was supported by some German-descent nobles, such as Count Leo Thun and Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg, who sought to preserve their power against German Liberals. Conversely, Germans in Bohemia advocated for centralization to avoid Czech control.

The struggle manifested in language and education. An 1880 ordinance mandated that government business in Bohemia and Moravia be handled in the language in which it was introduced, granting Czech a position in government service. In 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Prague and Bruenn. The establishment of Czech elementary and middle schools, and the introduction of Czech classes at the university of Prague in 1882, were viewed by Germans as a desecration of the oldest German university.

## Constitutional Struggles
In 1868, Czech leaders presented a "declaration" to the diet at Prague demanding the full restoration of the Bohemian kingdom and a separate settlement with Austria similar to that of Hungary. This led to serious riots and the decree of exceptional laws. In 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the empire, and the sovereign recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. However, this failed when a government with a German tendency took office in 1872. 

Under Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe in 1879, Bohemian representatives returned to the parliament of Vienna, though they maintained that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state. In recognition, Taaffe's government removed anomalies in the electoral system that favored the German minority and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.
