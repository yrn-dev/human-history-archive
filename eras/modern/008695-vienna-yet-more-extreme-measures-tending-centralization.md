# Vienna

Vienna served as a primary center of administration, political power, and measurement for the Habsburg empire, though its role evolved through periods of revolution, constitutional experimentation, and centralization.

## Political Administration and Centralization
Vienna was the seat of the Austrian central administration. During the 19th century, it became the site of various attempts to balance imperial unity with the rights of the countries under Habsburg rule. Following the Italian campaign of 1859, the empire attempted to move away from absolutism. While a decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, a subsequent decree on February 26, 1861, established a central parliament at Vienna. This parliament possessed extensive powers and utilized an electoral system that was grossly partial to the Germans.

The central parliament in Vienna was a point of contention for the Bohemians, who sent representatives to the city but withdrew in 1863, claiming the assembly had encroached upon the constitutional power of the diet of Prague. The central parliament was suspended two years later. Following the Austro-Prussian war of 1866 and the resulting treaty signed at Prague on August 23, the house of Habsburg's lands were excluded from Germany. A new constitution in 1867, applying to the Slavic and German parts of the empire, maintained a system of centralization.

Later, under the ministry of Auersperg, which held office for eight years, a vigorous Liberal centralizing policy was pursued. This period saw the use of government influence to secure German majorities in the diets. In Vienna, German capitalists formed a society, popularly known as *Chabrus*, to acquire estates with attached votes through real or fictitious purchases. This effort resulted in a large German majority, with individuals from Vienna taking seats previously held by the Schwarzenbergs and the Thuns.

## Civil Unrest and the 1848 Revolution
The city experienced significant instability during 1848. Following the fall of Metternich, Austria lacked a central government for months. On March 14, the emperor authorized the establishment of a National Guard in Vienna. The city was subsequently ruled by a committee of students and citizens who imposed their will on the ministry and claimed a voice in imperial affairs.

Tensions escalated throughout May. On May 13, a Central Committee was established, and a fresh insurrection broke out on May 15. The government yielded by recognizing the Central Committee and admitting the National Guard's right to participate in politics. However, after the emperor left Vienna for Innsbruck on May 17 and issued a proclamation on May 20 that ignored the concessions of May 15, a revulsion of feeling occurred in the city. When the government attempted to close the university to disperse students on May 26, another outbreak occurred. The ministry eventually conceded to the insurgents' demands, handing over the public treasury and the responsibility for maintaining order to a newly formed Committee of Public Safety.

## Relationship with Budapest
In the later years of the Austro-Hungarian arrangement, Vienna shared certain functions with Budapest. The two cities alternately served as the meeting-place for Austro-Hungarian delegations. In 1892, Budapest was elected as a royal residence on an equality with Vienna. While Budapest became the center of Hungarian political, social, and intellectual life, Vienna remained the center of the Austrian administration. Budapest was viewed as a "favoured rival" of Vienna after 1867, possessing the advantage of having no competitors within its own sphere similar to the rivals Vienna faced in other Austrian provincial capitals.

## Technical Standards
In the field of cartography and measurement, the "Vienna inch" was utilized as a standard. According to archival map scales, 2 Vienna inches are equivalent to 1 Austrian mile, which consists of 288,000 inches.
