# Harley and the Tory Administration

## Rise to Power
Harley and St John were introduced into the ministry in May 1704 as younger adherents of the Tory cause. During the early part of Queen Anne's reign, the ministry under Sidney Godolphin had been chiefly composed of Tories, though more ardent members of the party had previously withdrawn. 

St John, who had been returned to parliament in 1701 for the family borough of Wootton Bassett in Wiltshire, declared himself a Tory and attached himself to Harley, who was then the speaker. St John referred to Harley as "dear master" and gained a significant ascendancy over the House of Commons through his eloquence in debate. In 1704, St John took office as secretary at war alongside Harley, which brought him into a relationship with Marlborough, who treated him with paternal partiality.

## Conflict with the Marlboroughs
The political position of Marlborough was eventually undermined by party intrigues. Harley and St John, with the assistance of Abigail Hill, worked to widen a breach between Queen Anne and the duchess of Marlborough. This breach had been initiated by the imperious manner of the duchess, who had moved toward the Whig cause partly due to the influence of her son-in-law, the earl of Sunderland, and partly because of Tory opposition to the French war.

As the friendship between the queen and the duchess changed into hate, Harley and St John sought every opportunity to humiliate the Marlborough family. This resulted in the fall of Sunderland and Godolphin in July and August of 1710, followed a few months later by the dismissal of the duchess from her offices. Marlborough's own fall was delayed until the final day of 1711.

## The Tory Ministry and the Peace with France
In 1710, Harley formed a new ministry in which St John served as secretary of state and privy councillor. A primary objective of this government was to establish peace with France. The ministry, supported by the parliament, the people, and the queen, sought to terminate hostilities because the Whigs had previously refused to grant terms in 1706 and 1709, suggesting the war was not being continued for English national interests.

The peacemaking process was characterized by a series of underhand conspiracies with the enemy rather than open conferences with allies, and it involved the violation of solemn international assurances. This process, along with the abandonment of the Catalans, left a lasting stain on national credit and honour; Bolingbroke was held primarily responsible for these actions. 

On July 7, St John was created Baron St John of Lydiard Tregoze and Viscount Bolingbroke. He traveled to France to conduct negotiations, signing a four-month armistice on the 19th of August. The Treaty of Utrecht was eventually signed on March 31, 1713, by all allies except the emperor. While the terms granted to England were perhaps not subject to much criticism, the peace was met with indignation by the Whigs.

## Internal Dissolution
The Tory administration was based on the friendship between Harley and Bolingbroke (St John), but this relationship gradually dissolved. Tensions rose in July 1711 when St John was disappointed to receive only a viscountcy and be passed over for the Garter, while Harley obtained the earldom of Oxford and was appointed lord treasurer in May. Additionally, in March 1711, Harley received a wound intended for St John during an attempt on the latter's life by Guiscard, for which Harley received all the credit.

By September 1713, Swift attempted to reconcile the two men, but the effort was vain. A further cause of difference emerged as Queen Anne's health declined. The Tory ministers feared their own downfall upon the accession of the elector of Hanover. Consequently, both Bolingbroke and Oxford maintained secret communications with James.
