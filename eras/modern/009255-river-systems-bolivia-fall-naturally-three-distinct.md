# The River-Systems of Bolivia

The river-systems of Bolivia are naturally divided into three distinct regions: the Amazon, La Plata, and the Central Plateau. These systems define the drainage basins of the republic and significantly influence its commercial access and geographical layout.

## The Amazon System
The Amazon system is the largest of the three, covering more than one-half of the republic. It consists of rivers that flow either directly or indirectly into the Madeira, which is one of the great tributaries of the Amazon. This system also includes some small tributaries of the Purus and Acre rivers in the north.

The two principal rivers within this system are the Beni and the Mamore, which unite at 10 deg. 20' S. latitude to form the Madeira.

### The Mamore River
The Mamore rises on the north-east slopes of the Sierra de Cochabamba, south of the 17th parallel, where its upper section is known as the Chimore. It follows a serpentine northerly course between the 65th and 66th meridians for approximately 600 m. Three-fourths of this length, from Chimore (925 ft. above sea level) to the rapids near its mouth, is navigable. 

The Mamore has several large tributaries rising on the north-east flanks of the Andes, including the Secure, Chapare, Manique (or Apere), and Yacuma. The Yacuma drains a region of swamps and lakes north of the Sierra Chamaya. Another significant tributary is the Guapay (or Grande), which is longer and larger than the Mamore above their confluence. The Guapay rises in the Cordillera Oriental east of Lake Pampa Aullaguas, flows east to the Sierra de Misiones, and then turns north around the south-east extremity of the Sierra de Cochabamba, skirting the Llanos de Chiquitos. It unites with the Mamore at Junta de los Rios (15 deg. 20' S. lat. and 64 deg. 40' W. long.) after a tortuous, non-navigable course of over 700 m. Tributaries of the Guapay include the Yapacani, Piray (or Sara), and Mizque.

### The Beni River
The Beni rises in the elevated Cordilleras near La Paz, where it is initially called the Rio de La Paz. It flows east and north-east, with half of its length passing through the mountainous districts of central Bolivia as a raging torrent fed by snowclad peaks. Below Reyes, the river passes through open plains and forest-covered hills. The lower Beni is navigable for 217 m. from Reyes to the Esperanza rapids, located 18 m. above the confluence with the Mamore. Navigation is obstructed here by a fall of 20 ft. over 330 yards.

The most important affluent of the Beni is the Madre de Dios (or Mayu-tata). Rising in the eastern Cordilleras about 35 m. east of Cuzco, the Madre de Dios flows through northern Bolivia to join the Beni 120 m. above its mouth. It is a more important river than the Beni in terms of size and tributaries—including the Paucartambo, Inambari, Tambopata, Marcapata, and Chandless—and is navigable during the wet season to the foot of the Andes.

## The La Plata System and Southern Drainage
The southern regions of Bolivia include the Bolivian Chaco and the llanos of Manzo. In eastern Santa Cruz and southern Chuquisaca, the land consists of low-lying plains and extensive swamps subject to periodical inundations. This region suffers from defective drainage due to the sluggish current of its streams during the rainy season.

Commercial access to the La Plata system is connected via the upper Paraguay river. Key ports include Puerto Suarez on Lake Caceres (approximately 1600 m. from Buenos Aires by river), which handles the bulk of trade in that direction, Puerto Pacheco on Bahia Negra, and the projected port of Puerto Quijarro on Lake Gaiba. Additionally, direct river communication with Montevideo and Buenos Aires would be possible if a navigable channel were opened in the Pilcomayo.

## Commercial Access and Navigation Challenges
Bolivia's lack of a maritime department leaves it dependent on the ports of Lake Titicaca (specifically Guaqui, which trades with Puno in Peru) and the rivers of the Madeira and Paraguay systems. Because these outlets require transhipment in foreign territory, transport costs are increased.

Access to the Amazon via the Madeira is interrupted by a series of falls. The Bolivian port of entry, Villa Bella, is situated above these falls at the confluence of the Mamore and Beni. Reaching Villa Bella from the lower river requires a costly and long portage. Furthermore, Villa Bella is shut off from navigable rivers further upstream by the falls of the Mamore and Beni. To remedy this, Brazil agreed in a 1903 treaty to construct a railway approximately 180 m. long around the falls of the Madeira to provide north-eastern Bolivia with better access to the Amazon.

Historically, Bolivia had an exception at Puerto Acre on the Acre river, where ocean-going steamers could ascend from Para. However, Brazil closed the Acre and Purus rivers to foreign vessels and acquired the port and adjacent territory under a 1903 treaty.
