# Schizomycetes and Bacterial Spore-Formation

## Overview of Schizomycetes
The schizomycetes, commonly known as bacteria, are minute vegetable organisms. They are characterized by a lack of chlorophyll and an absence of both an ordinary nucleus and ordinary sexual reproduction. These organisms consist of single cells that may appear in various shapes, including cylindrical, oblong, or spherical, and can also exist as other aggregates of cells or in filamentous forms. They multiply through a process of repeated bipartitions.

Due to their manner of division and the absence of a nucleus and sexual reproduction, bacteria resemble the Schizophyceae (blue-green algae or Cyanophyceae). Consequently, these two groups are typically united under the class Schizophyta, reflecting the widely held view that most typical bacteria are derived from the Cyanophyceae.

## Spore-Formation and Types
A characteristic method of reproduction for many bacterial forms is the formation of spores, which serve as reproductive bodies representing a resting stage of the organism. Historically, two types of spores were described: arthrospores and endospores.

An arthrospore is not considered a true spore; rather, it is an ordinary vegetative cell that separates and enters a state of rest. This can occur in species that do not form endospores, as well as in those that do, such as *B. subtilis*.

The true spore, or endospore, begins as a minute granule appearing in the protoplasm of a vegetative cell. Over a few hours, this granule enlarges, absorbing all the protoplasm and secreting a resistive, thin envelope. The resulting ripe ovoid spore is smaller than the mother-cell and lies loosely within it. In certain Schizomycetes, such as *Clostridium* and *Plectridium*, the sporiferous cells enlarge. In others, such as *B. ventriculus* and *Bacillus inflatus*, it is suggested that more than one spore may form within a single cell.

Conversely, some of the simplest and most minute Schizomycetes, such as *Micrococcus*, have no discovered definite spores. In these "asporous" forms, any vegetative micrococcus may begin a new series of cells through growth and division.

## Phylogenetic Relationships and Analogies
The phylogeny of bacteria is considered very doubtful because they appear to be a heterogeneous group with various biological analogies:

*   **Fungi and Yeasts:** There are similarities between bacterial endospores and spore-formations in Saccharomycetes. Some extremely minute yeasts can be easily mistaken for Micrococci, and yeasts occasionally form only one spore per cell. Furthermore, the discovery of frequent chlamydospores and minute oidia among fungi increases the probability that some bacteria—particularly branching forms—are actually reduced fungi. Meyer views certain forms as chlamydospores, while Klebs identifies them as "carpospores" or chlamydospores similar to those of yeast.
*   **Algae:** The one-sided growth of species that form stalks and sheaths, such as *B. pediculatum* and *Bacterium vermiforme*, can be matched by Diatoms and Algae such as *Hydrurus* and *Oocardium*. Additionally, the form "Sarcina" has algal analogues in the Palmellaceae (green algae).
*   **Other Organisms:** The formation of endospores and the existence of ciliated micrococci—features not found in Cyanophyceae—are reminiscent of flagellate Protozoa, such as *Chromulina* and *Monas*. Additionally, Thaxter's group of Myxobacteriaceae suggests a relationship with the Myxomycetes.

## Comparative Cytology of Yeast-Cells
In the study of yeast-cells, the nucleus can be difficult to identify due to the presence of metachromatic granules, which are deeply staining and often mistaken for nuclei. During budding, the nucleus appears to divide through direct division. During spore formation, the nucleus divides and the protoplasm collects around the nuclei to form spores via free-cell formation; the remaining protoplasm, known as epiplasm, becomes disorganized.

While fusion of nuclei has been described by Leblanc and Jansens, it was not observed by Guillermond or Wager. However, in *Zygosaccharomyces* and *Schizosaccharomyces*, nuclear fusion occurs during the conjugation of cells that precedes the formation of a sporangium. This has led to the theory that ordinary forms may have evolved from sexual forms like these through a loss of sexuality, with the sporangium then forming parthenogenetically.
