# The Cabinet of Count Khuen-Hedervary and the Independence Party

The political tenure of Count Khuen-Hedervary was marked by a period of significant instability characterized by conflicts between the government and the Hungarian independence party. The crisis surrounding his administration was exacerbated by disputes over military organization and allegations of political corruption.

## The Independence Party and Francis Kossuth
The independence party, also known as the obstructionists, was led by Francis Kossuth, the eldest son of the Hungarian patriot Louis Kossuth. Francis Kossuth, who had been educated at the London University and the Paris Polytechnic, spent thirty-three years in Italy working as a civil engineer before settling in Hungary in 1895. He entered active politics after being returned for Tapolca in 1895 and Cegléd in 1896. By the autumn of 1898, he had assumed leadership of the independence party, opposing several administrations, including those of Szell, Khuen-Hedervary, Szápáry, and Stephen Tisza.

## The Army Bill Crisis
The tensions leading to the instability of the Khuen-Hedervary era began in the autumn of 1902. At the instance of the crown, the Austrian and Hungarian governments proposed bills to increase annual recruit contingents by 21,000 men. Of this total, 16,700 were intended for the joint army, with the remainder for the national defence troops (the *honved* and Landwehr). Hungary's contribution was to be approximately 6,500 men, while Austria's was to be some 14,500.

A conflict arose when military authorities detained several thousand supernumerary recruits in barracks before the bills had received parliamentary sanction. This was done to avoid the delay and expense of summoning them again later. The Hungarian Chamber, sensitive to its prerogatives, resented this action. Francis Kossuth and the independence party utilized this grievance to obstruct ministerial measures. Although Kossuth allowed the Szell cabinet a vote on account for the first four months of 1903, the government remained at the mercy of the Opposition. Even after the supernumeraries were discharged at the end of 1902, the Opposition continued to demand the withdrawal of the Army bills or concessions to promote the use of the Magyar language and the Magyar character of Hungarian regiments.

## The Fall of the Khuen-Hedervary Cabinet
When Count Khuen-Hedervary took office, Francis Kossuth had relinquished the leadership of the independence party. Initially, it appeared that the crisis would subside as only a few extreme nationalists continued to obstruct estimates. However, an attempt to accelerate this process through bribery backfired, provoking an outburst of feeling against Khuen-Hedervary. Although he was personally innocent of the bribery, his position was shaken.

The situation deteriorated further due to two primary events:
1. An army order issued during cavalry manoeuvres at Chlopy in Galicia, in which the monarch stated he would "hold fast to the existing and well-tried organization of the army" and would not "relinquish the rights and privileges guaranteed to its highest war-lord."
2. A provocative statement made in the Reichsrath by Koerber, the Austrian premier.

These events led to the overthrow of the Khuen-Hedervary cabinet on September 30 by an immense majority. The cabinet fell following a motion of censure brought forward by Francis Kossuth, who had used the bribery incident as an opportunity to resume the leadership of his party.
