## Financial and Legal Regulations
In the context of commercial law and the maturity of bills of exchange, the 1st and 4th of February serve as an example of the application of "days of grace." A bill of exchange is payable on demand if expressed as such, or at sight, or upon presentation. However, for bills payable at a future time, three days known as days of grace must be added to the nominal due date. 

For instance, if a bill is accepted on the 1st of January and is payable one month after sight, its nominal due date would be the 1st of February. However, due to the addition of the three days of grace, the bill is actually payable on the 4th of February. While these days of grace have generally been abolished on the continent because they are viewed as misleading, they have been maintained in England to avoid curtailing the credit of small traders who rely on bills drawn at fixed periods of currency.

## Astronomical and Calendar Calculations
The 1st of February is also referenced in the use of epacts to determine the age of the moon and the days of the new moon. Epacts are placed in the calendar in retrograde order alongside dominical letters and days of the month. In this system, the asterisk corresponds to the 31st of January, and the number 29 corresponds to the 1st of February. This distribution allows for the calculation of the new moon's occurrence; for example, if a lunation ends on the 2nd of December, the new moon falls on the 3rd, making the epact of the following year twenty-nine. This epact of twenty-nine then indicates that the new moon for the following year falls on the 2nd of January.
