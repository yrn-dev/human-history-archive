# Nationalists and the Transference of Quarrels from the Viennese

## Political Shifts in Vienna
The political landscape of Vienna underwent a significant transformation characterized by the disappearance of the Liberals. In 1879, the Liberals held 33 of the 37 seats returned in Lower Austria; however, they were later largely replaced by the Socialists. This shift made it difficult to maintain a strong party of moderate constitutionalists for the government to rely upon, as such a party required a large nucleus from Lower Austria.

During this period, the influence of Lueger became a source of embarrassment. Lueger was elected burgomaster after securing a two-thirds majority in the town council. Although the emperor initially refused to confirm this election and personally appealed to Lueger to withdraw—a request Lueger consented to—the situation changed following the election of 1897. Because that election provided Lueger with many followers in the Reichsrath, Badeni advised that his election as burgomaster be confirmed.

## Transference of Quarrels to the Reichsrath
A violent antipathy existed between the German Nationalists and the Christian Socialists. The conflict between these two groups was not confined to the Viennese Council Chamber; rather, their quarrels were transferred from the Council Chamber to the Reichsrath. This transference was considered very detrimental to the orderly conduct of debate within the legislative body.

## Nationalist Movements in the Empire
Nationalist sentiments were also prevalent in other regions of the monarchy, particularly among the Magyars. By the late 19th century, signs of the decay of dualism and the growth of an extreme nationalist Magyar spirit became visible. This was evident in the difficulty of passing the Army bills of 1889, despite the efforts of Count Julius Andrassy the Elder and Koloman Tisza. Public opinion echoed demands for the Magyarization of the joint army, leading Count Andrassy to warn the country about the obligations of the Compact of 1867 and the dangers of nationalist Chauvinism.

Nationalist feeling also manifested during negotiations for the renewal of the economic *Ausgleich* (Customs and Trade Alliance) between various Austrian premiers and the Hungarian premiers Banffy and Szell. While the Szell-Koerber Compact of 1902 was initially seen as a pledge of peaceful development and an end to economic strife, these hopes proved baseless.

## Magyar Nationalist Demands and the Crown
In October 1903, Count Stephen Tisza accepted a mission to form a cabinet. He presented a programme—known as the "programme of the nine" because it was drafted by a committee of nine members—which included several concessions to Magyar nationalist feeling. These concessions concerned:
* Military badges and penal procedure.
* The establishment of military scholarships for Magyar youths.
* The introduction of a two-year service system.
* The transfer of officers of Hungarian origin from Austrian to Hungarian regiments.

The programme declared that the cabinet's responsibility extended to the military prerogatives of the crown and that legal influence of parliament existed regarding constitutional rights. However, for "weighty political reasons affecting great interests of the nation," the question of military language was excluded. An addendum sanctioned by the crown maintained that the king held the right to fix the language of command and service in the Hungarian army based on his constitutional prerogatives.

## Revolutionary Nationalism and the Fall of Metternich
Earlier historical instances of nationalist unrest also impacted Vienna. On March 11, 1848, "young Czechs" in Prague drew up a petition for liberal and nationalist demands. Simultaneously, the diet of Lower Austria petitioned the crown to organize the Austrian finances. 

The atmosphere in Vienna became volatile; a mob of students and workmen invaded the hall of the diet. They adopted the proposals of Kossuth's speech as a popular programme and forced members of the diet to lead a tumultuous procession to the Hofburg to demand government assent to a petition based on the catch-words of the Revolution. This escalated into riots and a revolution involving the middle classes, during which soldiers intervened and blood was shed. Consequently, on the evening of March 13, Metternich escaped from the Hofburg and went into exile in England.

During this era, Kossuth and his followers aimed for the complete separation of Hungary from Austria, maintaining sympathy or alliance with German radicals in Frankfort and Vienna. While the imperial government procrastinated under pressure from Magyar nationalists to renounce Jellachich, the military party within the government began envisioning a centralized state to replace the old empire's loose federalism.
