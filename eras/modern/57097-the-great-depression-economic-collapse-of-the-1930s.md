# The Great Depression: The Global Economic Collapse of the 1930s

The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world. Lasting roughly from 1929 to 1939, it was not a single event but a systemic collapse that triggered mass unemployment, bank failures, and a drastic decline in global trade. While it is often associated with the United States, the crisis rippled across the globe, destabilizing governments and contributing to the geopolitical tensions that led to World War II.

The crisis began with the dramatic stock market crash of October 1929, but the roots of the Depression ran deeper. A combination of agricultural overproduction, unsustainable consumer debt, and a fragile banking system created a "perfect storm" that turned a market correction into a decade-long catastrophe.

## The Spiral of Collapse
Following the "Black Tuesday" crash, a vicious cycle of deflation and unemployment took hold. As stock values plummeted, consumers stopped spending, leading businesses to cut production and lay off workers. This created a feedback loop: unemployed workers could not buy goods, which led to further business failures.

The crisis was exacerbated by the collapse of the banking system. In the U.S., thousands of banks failed as panicked depositors attempted to withdraw their savings simultaneously—a phenomenon known as a "bank run." Because banks had invested depositor funds into the stock market or risky loans, they lacked the liquidity to meet these demands, wiping out the life savings of millions.

## Policy Responses and Scholarly Debate
Governments initially responded with protectionist measures. The U.S. passed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, intending to protect domestic industries. However, this prompted retaliatory tariffs from other nations, causing international trade to plummet and deepening the global slump.

In the U.S., President Franklin D. Roosevelt introduced the "New Deal," a series of programs aimed at relief, recovery, and reform. These included the creation of the Social Security system and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC). 

Among historians and economists, there is ongoing debate regarding the cause and cure of the Depression. Monetarists, such as Milton Friedman, argue that the Federal Reserve worsened the crisis by failing to maintain the money supply. Conversely, Keynesian economists argue that the collapse was caused by a drop in aggregate demand, suggesting that massive government deficit spending was the only way to stimulate recovery.

## Key Facts
* **Peak Unemployment:** In the United States, unemployment reached approximately 25% by 1933.
* **GDP Decline:** The U.S. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) fell by nearly 30% between 1929 and 1933.
* **The Dust Bowl:** A severe drought in the Great Plains compounded the economic crisis, forcing thousands of "Okies" to migrate west.
* **Bank Failures:** Roughly 9,000 banks failed in the U.S. during the 1930s.
* **End of the Era:** Most historians agree that the massive industrial mobilization for World War II finally ended the Depression.

## Did You Know?
* **Hoovervilles:** Shanty towns built by homeless people during the crisis were named "Hoovervilles" after President Herbert Hoover, whom many blamed for the crisis.
* **Gold Standard:** Many economists believe that countries that abandoned the Gold Standard earlier in the decade recovered more quickly than those that clung to it.
* **The "Forgotten" Recovery:** While the New Deal provided essential relief, it did not fully end the Depression; unemployment remained high until the start of wartime production in 1940.
