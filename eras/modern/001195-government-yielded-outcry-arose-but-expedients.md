# Government Responses to Public Outcry and Crisis

Throughout history, governments have frequently faced periods of intense public outcry resulting from economic instability, political shifts, or diplomatic decisions. The methods employed to mitigate these crises—ranging from administrative expedients and concessions to the implementation of authoritarian measures—have often determined the stability or downfall of the ruling order.

## Economic Crisis and the French Directory
In the late 18th century, the French Directory attempted to address a severe economic crisis. The government had pledged to end a system where Paris was fed at the expense of the rest of France, setting February 20, 1796, as the date for ceasing the distribution of meat and bread at nominal prices. This announcement caused widespread consternation among various social groups, including government officials, *rentiers* whose incomes were paid in *assignats* at arbitrarily fixed scales, workmen, and idlers.

Although the government yielded to the resulting outcry, the expedients it used to mitigate the situation—specifically the division of those entitled to relief into different classes—only served to increase discontent and alarm. This universal misery provided a platform for Babeuf, who gathered a circle of followers known as the *Societe des Egaux*. This group eventually merged with the rump of the Jacobins at the Pantheon. By November 1795, police reports indicated that Babeuf was openly preaching revolt, insurrection, and the constitution of 1793.

## Diplomatic Tension in Japan
In Japan, the appointment of Ii Kamon no Kami as prime minister in Yedo was a strategic move by the shogun's entourage to avoid the abdication of Iesada and the succession of Keiki, who was viewed as a potential advocate for liberal policies rather than national seclusion. Ii Kamon no Kami subsequently nominated the young daimyo of Kii as the successor to the shogunate and signed the Harris treaty.

This action triggered a vehement outcry. Opponents, led by the Mito baron, argued that the treaty was signed without the sovereign's sanction, thereby ignoring the will of the Imperial Court. They maintained that unconditional yielding to foreign demands was intolerable. While the Mito baron did not insist on absolute seclusion, he and his followers demanded a delay to allow for definite preparation, opposing the premier's desire for the immediate opening of the country.

## Feudal Obligations in the Austrian Empire
The Austrian government faced a serious dilemma in Galicia regarding the Ruthenians. Following a victory, the Ruthenians refused to return to work and demanded the abolition of all feudal obligations. The government feared that refusing the claim would prolong the crisis, while conceding it would encourage the peasantry across the entire empire to make similar demands, potentially leading to a general rising.

On April 13, 1846, an imperial decree was issued to abolish some of the most burdensome feudal obligations. However, this concession was met with a fierce outcry, as it was viewed as an authoritative endorsement of atrocities. The decree was subsequently revoked, and Count Franz von Stadion was dispatched to restore order. This sequence of events led the peasants to conclude that redress was only possible through a change of government, linking agrarian and nationalist movements and making the downfall of the Austrian system inevitable.

## Ecclesiastical and Political Transitions
In other contexts, the transition of power or the management of authority has been driven by the perceived failure of existing systems. In Media, Dejoces used emissaries to suggest that the republic's face must be entirely changed to prevent the country from becoming uninhabitable. The proposed expedient to remedy public evils was the election of a king with the authority to make laws and restrain violence. This was approved by the assembly, and Dejoces was elected king by common consent.

Similarly, in the Roman Empire, the "stupendous fabric" of the state eventually yielded to its own weight due to immoderate greatness. To maintain public peace and personal safety, emperors utilized the base expedient of corrupting military discipline. This relaxation of military government, combined with the institutions of Constantine, eventually left the Roman world vulnerable to a deluge of Barbarians.

In the religious sphere, the fathers of Basel clashed with Pope Eugenius IV. Despite the pope's attempts to yield on certain points and seek compromise, the fathers relied on the decrees of the council of Constance to assert the superiority of the council over the pope. They proceeded to impose laws on the sovereign pontiff and interfere in the government of the papal countship of Venaissin, creating an impassable barrier between the two parties.

## Modern Administrative Challenges
In more recent political history, such as the era described by Winston Churchill, governments have faced "storms of angry outcry" and accusations of being reactionary. In one instance, Mr. Chamberlain, facing pressure from younger and moderate Conservatives for conciliatory processes, chose a "desperate remedy" to quell indignation through violence and the "flag of Protection."

On a global scale, it has been argued that the fragmentary administration of world affairs is increasingly wasteful and dangerous. This perspective suggests that infectious diseases, population migrations, and financial and monetary expedients have become world-wide interests that require a common world-wide control and authorities of a greater range than any government that has previously existed.
