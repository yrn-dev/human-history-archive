# The Comédie Humaine

The *Comédie humaine* (Human Comedy) represents the culmination of Honoré de Balzac's literary efforts to organize his vast production into a single, unified structure. This project, which Balzac carried out in 1842, sought to build his various works into one immense structure divided into sections, including *Etudes philosophiques* and *Scenes de la vie parisienne*.

## Intentions and Conceptualization
The selection of the title "Human Comedy" has been a subject of significant analysis. As a description for studies of life, the term is presented as something that either explains itself immediately or cannot be explained at all. The primary justification for the "Comedy" syllabus is the belief that Balzac's entire production existed in his head together as a single entity. 

This conceptual unity led to a process of constant re-connecting, re-shaping, and altering of the different parts of his work. Balzac frequently corrected small printed originals into long novels during the proofing stage. Because of the scale and the interconnected nature of the work, it is noted that even a lifespan as long as that of Methuselah, combined with the powers of Shakespeare, would not have sufficed to truly finish the *Comédie*.

## Composition and Work Ethic
Balzac approached his vocation with a "furious energy" that exceeded that of writers such as Scott. His working habits were extreme; he typically began writing at midnight and would continue into the following day, sometimes for stretches of sixteen hours, a process he maintained for weeks and days at a time. This devotion to work was his primary focus, often taking precedence over business, love, and friendship. He occasionally resisted summons from Mme. Hanska or shortened his visits to her to serve his work, which he viewed as an absorbing mistress.

## Scope and Content
The *Comédie humaine* is characterized by a blending of "observation and imagination," a description provided by Victor Hugo in his funeral discourse on the author. This inextricable mixture of the two qualities is what defines Balzac's unique position in literature. 

While the *Comédie* is the central focus, Balzac's output was varied. He wrote political pamphlets and miscellaneous articles (found in the *Edition definitive*), as well as dramas. His plays include *Vautrin*, *Les Ressources de Quinola*, *Pamela Giraud*, *La Maratre*, and *Mercadet le faiseur*. The latter was a posthumous comedy prepared for the stage by Adolphe Dennery and presented at the Gymnase theatre in 1851.

Some planned works for the *Comédie* never reached print. This includes *La Bataille*, a book devoted to the battle of Essling for which Balzac visited the actual ground; despite being referred to as "in progress" in early letters to Madame Hanska, the work has never been found.

## Bibliographic History
The publication history of the *Comédie humaine* is extraordinarily complicated. The earliest version appeared between 1842 and 1846, spanning sixteen volumes. For many years, the most well-known edition consisted of fifty-five small 24mos published by Michel (Calmann) Levy, though this version did not follow the author's final disposition and omitted miscellaneous works.

The *Edition definitive* (published from 1869 onwards) provided a more complete record, including the miscellaneous works in four volumes and a volume of correspondence. In 1893, the *Repertoire des oeuvres de Balzac* was added, which meticulously tracks the history of the various personages throughout the *Comédie*.
