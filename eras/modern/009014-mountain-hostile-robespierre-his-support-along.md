# The Fall of Robespierre and the Role of the Mountain

The political decline and eventual execution of Robespierre in 1794 were the result of shifting alliances within the French Convention, the loss of key allies, and a growing hostility from various political factions, including the Mountain.

## Political Alliances and the Marais
During the period of the Terror, Robespierre maintained support from various sectors of the Convention. Among these were deputies of the centre, known as "le Marais." One such figure was Francois Antoine de Boissy d'Anglas, a French statesman and former advocate to the parlement who had been elected as a deputy to the states-general in 1789. While Boissy d'Anglas initially supported Robespierre during the Terror, he was eventually gained over by members of the Mountain who were hostile to Robespierre. The shift in support from Boissy d'Anglas and other leaders of the Marais was a critical factor that made the events of the 9th Thermidor possible.

## Conflict with the Committees and the Mountain
Robespierre's position became precarious as the committees began to quarrel with his pretensions. His influence had been significantly weakened by his assent to the execution of Danton; this act deprived him of the only great force that might have supported him against the committee. 

While Robespierre attempted to work against the severity of the revolutionary tribunal in Paris and the iniquities of "bad men" in the provinces—such as Collot d'Herbois and Carrier—these efforts were unsuccessful. He also faced opposition from the commune of Paris, which was composed of fanatics like Chaumette and Hebert. Although the revolutionary government achieved a victory over this extreme insurrectionary party by executing the Hebertists on March 24, 1794, the committees remained unwilling to adopt the Dantonian policy of clemency.

## The Final Days and the 9th Thermidor
In the summer of 1794, Robespierre displayed signs of mental disorder and became deeply preoccupied with religion. He led the Convention to decree a belief in a Supreme Being and the immortality of the soul, culminating in a great festival at the Champ de Mars where he delivered the chief discourse. Following this, he withdrew from affairs and avoided the Convention for a month.

Robespierre reappeared in July and delivered a speech to the Convention that foreshadowed new prosecutions, claiming a duty to die in the defense of humanity. The Convention responded with a resentful uproar and refused to allow the speech to be printed. After Robespierre reread the speech to his supporters at his club, the Convention turned against him the following morning. During the confrontation, he was shouted down, refused the right to speak, and was accused and arrested along with his chief supporters.

## Arrest and Death
Following his arrest, the Jacobin-led Hôtel de Ville rose against the Convention and rescued Robespierre and his companions. However, by three in the morning, the forces of the Convention faced the forces of the Commune. The soldiers of the Commune eventually defected to the Government. Robespierre and his remaining companions were betrayed and trapped. Some of his associates attempted suicide or threw themselves from a window. Robespierre was shot in the lower jaw by a gendarme and was found with a bloodied face.
