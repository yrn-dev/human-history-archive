# The British Parliamentary System and the Crown

The British system of government is characterized as a "crowned republic," a structure that emerged from a prolonged struggle during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This conflict involved three primary factors: the crown, the private property owners, and the common people. The outcome was a complete triumph of the private property owner over the ambitions of Machiavellian absolutism.

## The Nature of Parliamentary Government
With the arrival of the Hanoverian Dynasty, England adopted a new method of government known as Parliamentary government. This system, which utilizes a restricted representative method, has been described as recalling the Popular Assembly and Senate of Rome. The assembly at Westminster is regarded as the "Mother of Parliaments" globally.

In this arrangement, the British Parliament maintains a relationship with the crown similar to that of the mayor of the palace to the Merovingian kings. The king is viewed as a living symbol of the imperial and royal system, conceived as irresponsible and ceremonial. However, the crown retains latent power through prestige and tradition. Sovereigns—including the four Hanoverian Georges, William IV (1830), Victoria (1837), Edward VII (1901), and George V (1910)—have exercised an indefinable but important influence over the foreign office, the military and naval organizations, and the affairs of the church.

## Governance of the United Kingdom and Empire
The central component of the British system is the "crowned republic" of the United British Kingdoms, which includes Ireland, despite the opposition of a considerable portion of the Irish people. The British Parliament is composed of three united parliaments: those of England and Wales, Scotland, and Ireland. 

The majority of this Parliament determines the policy, quality, and headship of the ministry, primarily based on considerations of British domestic politics. This ministry serves as the effective supreme government, possessing the powers of war and peace over the entire empire.

## Imperial Administration and the Crown
The British Empire consists of various constituents with differing political statuses:

*   **Crowned Republics:** Australia, Canada, New Zealand, South Africa, and Newfoundland (the oldest possession, dating to 1583) are practically independent, self-governing states in alliance with Great Britain. Each has a representative of the Crown appointed by the government in office.
*   **The Indian Empire:** Originally an extension of the empire of the Great Mogul, this region includes Aden and protected states from Burma to Baluchistan. Following a serious mutiny of native troops in 1859, the empire of the East India Company was annexed to the British Crown via *An Act for the Better Government of India*. This act replaced the Company with a Secretary of State for India who is responsible to the British Parliament, and the Governor-General became a Viceroy representing the Sovereign. In 1877, Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India. Consequently, India is described as an autocracy without an autocrat, where the Emperor is a "golden symbol" and grievances must be addressed through the British House of Commons or pamphlets in England.
*   **Egypt:** This possession is described as ambiguous; while it retains its own monarch (the Khedive) and is nominally part of the Turkish Empire, it is under almost despotic British official rule.

Theoretically, the principal sections of the empire enjoy equal rights under the crown, and no section is subordinate to another. While the imperial parliament's intervention in colonial affairs is theoretically admitted only when the crown's constitutional advisers require parliamentary support, there have been movements to bring the supreme headship of the empire more in line with modern ideas.

## Historical Conflicts and the Crown's Power
The balance of power between the crown and Parliament has historically been a point of contention. In the American colonies, inhabitants were in conflict with the commercial selfishness of the oligarchy dominating the British Parliament and the claims of the British crown. 

Following the peace of 1763, the British Parliament resolved to impose taxation on the American colonies. During this period, King George III (who began his reign in 1760) sought to expand the authority of the crown. He believed the crown could claim resources and powers in overseas possessions that were denied to it by the jealous aristocracy in Britain. This ambition led some Whig noblemen to sympathize with the colonists, as they opposed any strengthening of the crown that might make it independent of the aristocracy.

## Comparative Political Structures
The British "crowned republic" is contrasted with other historical systems. For example, the French monarchy in 1789 called together the States General—a gathering of clergy, nobles, and commons roughly equivalent to the earlier British Parliament. When the Commons (the Third Estate) sought to control the Assembly, the States General became a National Assembly resolved to keep the crown in order, mirroring the way the British Parliament managed the British crown.

Additionally, the British system is contrasted with Poland. While Poland was also a crowned aristocratic republic, it lacked a central government and a cohesive "Society" like that found in London. The King of Poland had very little power; he could not alter laws, levy taxes, or make war without the consent of the Diet. Furthermore, any single member of the Polish Diet could use the *liberum veto* to stop any proposal or dissolve the Diet entirely, rendering Poland a "paralyzed" republic.
