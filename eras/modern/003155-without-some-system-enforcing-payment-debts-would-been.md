# Systems of Debt Enforcement and Bankruptcy

The ability to enforce the payment of debts is a fundamental requirement for global commerce. Because modern commerce relies heavily on credit, the power to enforce financial contracts is essential; without such a system, world commerce could not have reached its current proportions.

## Principles of Bankruptcy Law
Bankruptcy laws are designed to balance the interests of creditors and debtors. The primary objectives of these laws are to create a system that is just to creditors without being unduly harsh on debtors. Specifically, these laws aim to distinguish between a debtor's willful neglect or refusal to pay and an involuntary inability to meet obligations. Furthermore, bankruptcy systems seek to ensure that available assets are shared equitably among creditors.

More recent legislation has also focused on protecting the broader trading community from the negative effects of unnatural trade competition and the reckless abuse of credit, thereby fostering a higher standard of commercial morality. Implementing these systems is difficult because the interests of debtors and creditors often conflict, and there has been a lack of consistency and uniformity regarding how a debtor's property should be handled.

## Legal Mechanisms and Trusteeship
In bankruptcy proceedings, a trustee is appointed to take immediate possession of the bankrupt's property, including accounts, books, and deeds. The trustee possesses the powers of a receiver in the High Court to enforce the delivery of these items. After administration costs are paid, the trustee's duty is to distribute the estate in dividends as quickly as possible.

With the sanction of the Board of Trade or a committee, a trustee may:
* Employ a solicitor or institute and defend legal proceedings.
* Compromise claims and debts.
* Sell property on credit or raise money via mortgage.
* Carry on the business if it is necessary for a beneficial winding-up.
* Sell property, including book debts and business goodwill, via private treaty or public auction.

## Historical and International Variations
### Primitive Roman Law
Early Roman law distinguished between debts arising from contracts, sales, or delicts, and those arising from money lent (*pecunia certa credita*). For the latter, creditors had a rapid process to gain power over the debtor's person and property. If a debt was not paid within thirty days of being proved or confessed, the creditor could seize the debtor (*manûs injectio*). If the debtor could not pay or provide a surety (*vindex*), the creditor could keep the debtor in chains for two months. If the debt remained unpaid, the creditor was empowered to sell the debtor as a slave, put the debtor to death, or subject them to forced work and ill usage. The magistrate also authorized the seizure of the debtor's property to satisfy the payment.

### United States National Bankruptcy Act
The National Bankruptcy Act of 1898 (amended in 1903) defines specific "acts of bankruptcy." These include transferring property to hinder creditors, preferring one creditor over others while insolvent, making a general assignment for creditors, or admitting in writing a willingness to be adjudicated a bankrupt due to an inability to pay. Notably, the mere non-payment of debts is not considered an act of bankruptcy; a debtor can only be adjudicated as such on the grounds of indebtedness with their own written consent.

### Other Systems of Security and Debt
Various methods have been used to secure debts:
* **Pledges:** Debtors have pledged crops, houses, or fields. In some codes, the debtor retained the crop but paid the creditor from its proceeds; if the crop failed, interest was not charged and payment was deferred.
* **Antichretic Pledges:** These occurred when the profit from a pledge served as a set-off against the debt's interest.
* **Guarantees:** Personal guarantees were used so that a guarantor would become liable if the debtor failed to repay.
* **Permanent Plans:** In certain societies, a "permanent" plan allowed members to receive advances on mortgage security and pay them off over various terms of years.

## State and International Debt Management
### State Debt and Liquidation
States have faced significant debt through failed financial policies. For example, a state that chartered a bank on state credit in 1819 saw that bank fail, leading the state to borrow money in 1831 to redeem depreciated notes. A second bank chartered in 1835 suspended payment in 1837 and was liquidated in 1843. Additionally, unsuccessful public improvement projects, such as railroads and the Illinois and Michigan canal, led to declined credit and heavy debt. In one instance, a plan was adopted to reduce the entire debt without excessive taxation, and the debt was practically extinguished by 1880. In Louisiana, by 1908, approximately half of all revenues were used for government expenses and the payment of interest on a bonded state debt of $11,108,300.

### International War Debts
Following war, systems of clearing houses were created in Germany and other allied states to handle pre-war debts and contracts suspended by war. Participating states assumed responsibility for debts owed by their nationals to enemy nationals, except in cases of pre-war insolvency. The sale of private enemy property was used to pay debts owed to nationals of that state, with direct communication between debtor and creditor prohibited. Disputes were settled via the mixed arbitral tribunal or courts of the debtor country.
