# Foreign Trade and Economic Structure of the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy

## Customs and Commercial Framework
The economic relationship between Austria and Hungary was governed by a customs and commercial agreement first concluded in 1867 and renewable every ten years. Under this arrangement, several affairs were treated according to the same principles in both states, including customs legislation, commercial affairs, and legislation regarding duties connected to industrial production—specifically for mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer. The agreement also covered the principles regulating the Austro-Hungarian Bank, the determination of coinage and legal tender, and ordinances for railways affecting the interests of both states.

By the renewal of the compact in 1899, the monarchy functioned as a single identical commercial and customs territory, which included the principality of Liechtenstein as well as Bosnia and Herzegovina.

## Foreign Trade Statistics and Partners
The German Empire served as the most significant partner for Austro-Hungarian trade, accounting for approximately 60% of exports and 40% of imports. Other major trading partners, in descending order of importance, included Great Britain, India, Italy, the United States of America, Russia, France, Switzerland, Rumania, the Balkan states, and South America.

The primary articles imported by the monarchy were metals, tobacco, coffee, silk and silk goods, wool and woollen goods, and cotton and cotton goods. Principal exports included leather goods, earthenware and pottery, fancy goods, millinery, cereals, eggs, iron and ironware, glass and glassware, cattle, sugar, and wood.

## Regional Industrial Centers: Budapest
Budapest stood as the most important town in Hungary for industry and commerce, second only to Vienna within the monarchy. The city's commercial importance was driven by its location on the Danube, where the introduction of steamboats in 1830 stimulated the corn trade. Budapest became one of the world's greatest milling centers, utilizing local inventions in machinery. 

Other principal industries in Budapest included distilling, steam flour-milling, the manufacture of railway plant, machinery, carriages, chemicals, jute, bricks, cutlery, and gold and silver wares. The city's trade focused on spirits, wines, pigs, horses, cattle, flour, corn, wood, wool, and hides. The growth of joint-stock companies in the city indicated significant industrial progress: there were 28 such companies in 1873 (total capital £2,224,900), 75 in 1890 (capital £9,352,000), and 242 by 1899 (capital £31,378,655).

## Financial and Fiscal Policy
The Austro-Hungarian Bank, founded in 1816 as the Austrian National Bank and renamed in 1878, held the exclusive legal right to issue bank-notes. Under a 1899 statute, it operated as a joint-stock company with a stock of L8,780,000. Bank-notes were required to be covered by legal specie (current silver and gold) to the extent of two-fifths. The bank was managed and supervised on a footing of equality between the two states.

Financial records show that at the end of 1900, the bank had a capital of L8,750,000, a reserve fund of L428,250, cash of L50,754,000, and a note circulation of L62,251,000. By 1907, the reserve fund grew to L548,041, cash to L60,036,625, and note circulation to L84,501,000.

Regarding specific excise taxes, the Austro-Hungarian Empire levied a tax on wort for beer amounting to 6s. 8d. per English barrel.

## Economic and Political Tensions
The "dualist" system faced significant challenges between 1903 and 1907. In Hungary, the growth of an extreme nationalist Magyar spirit led to demands for economic and political separation from Austria. This was evident in the 1905 deadlock where a motion was adopted to authorize an autonomous Hungarian tariff. 

The Szell-Koerber Compact, concluded at the end of 1902, attempted to establish a joint Austro-Hungarian tariff to facilitate commercial treaties with Italy, Germany, and other states. However, this compact was never fully ratified by either parliament and was eventually discarded. By 1906, the Coalition government was obliged to maintain a dualist policy despite the majority of its members belonging to the Independence or Kossuth party. By July 1906, the Austrian premier Baron Beck declared that the remaining economic ties between the two countries were the joint state bank, the management of excise, commercial treaties with foreign countries, and freedom of trade.

## Strategic Economic Objectives
In recent years, the government's primary goals were to maintain influence and trade within the Balkan states. This was pursued through the opening of the Danube for navigation, the building of railways, and the establishment of commercial treaties with Bulgaria, Servia, and Rumania.
