# Doubling

In the game of bridge, doubling is a tactical action taken by an adversary following a trump or no-trump declaration. The primary effect of doubling is to increase the value of each trick to twice its original point value, though the scores for slam, chicane, and honours remain unaltered. If a declaration is doubled, the dealer and their partner possess the right to redouble, which increases the value of each trick to four times the original amount. The declarer holds the first option for redoubling, and the opposing side may redouble again, although the value of a trick is capped at 100 points.

## Doubling No-Trump Declarations
In no-trump declarations, the leader typically only doubles under specific conditions regarding suit strength and re-entry. A sound double is justified if the leader holds a suit of seven cards that is probably established, or a suit that can be established with the loss of one trick, provided they have good cards of re-entry. Specifically, seven cards of a suit including the queen, king, and ace constitute a sound double regardless of other cards. Alternatively, a player may double with six cards including the knave, queen, and king if they also hold two aces in other suits.

When the third hand doubles, it is universally understood as an indication that the player possesses a very strong suit they can establish. The partner of the doubler responds based on established conventions:
*   **Short Suit Convention:** The partner leads their own shortest suit, assuming it is the third player's strongest. In this convention, the doubler should not double with less than six cards of a suit headed by the ace, king, and queen.
*   **Heart Convention:** The partner leads a heart. Under this convention, the doubler does not require as much strength; six hearts and good re-entry cards are sufficient to compensate for the doubled value.

## Doubling Suit Declarations
Before doubling a suit declaration, a player should feel almost certain they are as strong as the declarer. Generally, the minimum strength to justify such a move is five trumps, though a declaration may have been made on six. A player is likely as strong as the declarer if they hold five trumps with a hand of no-trump strength, or six trumps with an average hand regarding card rank.

The position of the doubler relative to the declarer influences the decision:
*   **On the declarer's left:** The player should double with the strengths mentioned above, as the act intimates to their partner to lead trumps through the strong hand.
*   **On the declarer's right:** The player should possess much greater strength unless their hand is free from tenaces.

Certain declarations allow for more flexible doubling. If a spade declaration is made by the dummy, the doubler needs one fewer trump and does not need to be on the declarer's left. A spade declaration by the dealer can be doubled with even less strength. Additionally, a player may double more freely if a single undoubled trick would take the dealer out, though they must remain cautious about informing the dealer of a strong opposing hand.

## Variations of Bridge
The rules and applications of doubling vary across different versions of the game:

**Dummy Bridge**
In this version, the player who cuts the lowest card takes dummy. If the dealer deals for dummy, the player on the dealer's left may double, but their partner can only double without seeing their hand. If the player of dummy deals for themselves, the player on their right may look at dummy's hand if the declaration is passed, and the restrictions for the partners are reversed. In other instances, the partner of dummy may look at dummy's hand to decide whether to double, but cannot look at their own hand until dummy leads a card.

**Three-handed Bridge**
In three-handed bridge, the dealer declares without seeing the dummy's cards. If the dealer declares trumps, both adversaries may look at their hands, and doubling and redoubling proceed as in ordinary bridge. If the dealer passes the declaration to dummy, the right-hand adversary examines dummy's hand and makes a forced declaration. In this scenario, the third player can only double before seeing their own cards.

**Auction Bridge**
Introduced around 1907 and settled by a joint committee of the Portland and Bath clubs in 1908, auction bridge allows any player to make a declaration. Players may pass, double, redouble, or overcall. In this variety, doubling and redoubling affect only the score and not the declaration itself. For example, a call of two diamonds overcalls one no-trump even if the latter has been doubled. If a player fails a doubled declaration, the opponents score 100 points above the line for each under-trick.
