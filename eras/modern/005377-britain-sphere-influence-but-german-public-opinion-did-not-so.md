# Great Britain's Sphere of Influence in Uganda

## Diplomatic Agreements and Public Opinion
In July 1887, Great Britain and Germany engaged in diplomatic correspondence regarding the "hinterland doctrine." Under this accepted doctrine, Uganda was designated to fall within Great Britain's "sphere of influence." However, this diplomatic agreement was not reflected in German public opinion. German maps of the period assigned the territory to Germany, while public opinion in England strongly expected British influence to be paramount in the region.

## Territorial Disputes and Expeditions
Tensions over the territory escalated in 1889 when Karl Peters, a German official, led what was described as a raiding expedition into Uganda after running a blockade of the ports. During the same year, a company expedition led by F.J. Jackson had been sent to Victoria Nyanza; although Jackson was initially instructed to avoid Uganda, he decided to enter the country following representations from Uganda and reports of Peters's activities.

Upon Jackson's approach, Peters retired, though he claimed to have established "effective occupation" through treaties. Peters's treaty was dated March 1, 1890, while Jackson concluded a separate treaty in April of that year. These competing claims were eventually resolved through negotiations in Europe, resulting in the Anglo-German agreement of July 1, 1890, which formally assigned Uganda to the British sphere.

## Consolidation of British Authority
To consolidate the British position and counter the influence of French missionaries, who were hostile to Great Britain, the company dispatched Captain F.D. Lugard. Lugard arrived at the capital, Mengo, in December 1890, where he established the authority of the company despite intrigues by the French.

## Humanitarian and Infrastructure Goals
In July 1890, representatives of the powers met at Brussels and agreed to cooperate in the suppression of the slave trade. The company's interference in Uganda was viewed as a material step toward achieving this objective. Simultaneously, there were efforts to open the country via the construction of a railway extending from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza.

Because the company lacked the necessary resources for such a project, they sought imperial aid. Lord Salisbury, the prime minister at the time, expressed high tribute for the company's work, and a preliminary grant for a survey was nearly agreed upon. However, the scheme was ultimately wrecked in parliament. The railway was eventually constructed at a later date, funded entirely by the government.
