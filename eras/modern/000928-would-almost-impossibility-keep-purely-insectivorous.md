# Maintenance of Insectivorous Species

## Aviculture and Insectivorous Birds
The practice of aviculture allows for the study of living birds in a manner that would otherwise be impossible, particularly for species that are rare, shy, or inhabit native haunts that are difficult to visit. In large, suitably designed aviaries, observers can accurately record nesting habits, courtship, incubation, moult, and display.

Within aviculture, there is a large group of fruit-eating and insectivorous birds suitable for keeping, though they require considerable care regarding food preparation. Birds in this category vary by their dietary needs: some are partially insectivorous, consuming insects when plentiful and seeds at other times; others feed their young insects during early life despite eating grain as adults; and some are exclusively insect-eaters throughout their lives.

## Dietary Management of Insectivorous Birds
Keeping purely insectivorous species would be almost an impossibility if they could not be gradually accustomed to "insectile" or "insectivorous" food. This composition typically consists of the following principal ingredients:
* Dried flies
* Dried ants' cocoons
* Preserved egg yolk
* Dried powdered meat
* Bread or biscuit crumbs

This mixture is typically mixed with mashed boiled potato or moistened with water. While this serves as a good staple diet, purely insectivorous species often necessitate the addition of grubs, caterpillars, spiders, and mealworms. 

Different species require specific dietary supplements to their staple food. Fruit-eating species from the New World, such as sugar-birds and tanagers, require an abundance of ripe fruit. Birds that feed largely on earth-worms benefit from the addition of shredded raw meat. Waders require a similar diet to the staple insectile mix but with the addition of mealworms, chopped raw meat, and any available insects. Birds of prey require a meat diet consisting of small birds and mammals that are freshly killed; the feathers and fur should remain on the prey to assist in digestion. Ready-made food or the individual ingredients can be purchased at most bird-fancier's shops.

## Insectivorous Plants
The term insectivorous also applies to certain botanical species, such as the Butterwort (*Pinguicula vulgaris*). This small herb grows in boggy, wet land and is characterized by a rosette of pale, fleshy, oblong leaves (1 to 3 inches long) that are appressed to the ground.

The Butterwort captures insects using a sticky surface created by a viscid excretion. This excretion contains an enzyme or digestive ferment that makes the nitrogenous substances of the insect's body soluble, allowing the leaf to absorb them as food. The leaves possess two sets of glands: smaller, almost sessile glands and larger glands borne on usually unicellular pedicels. When a fly is captured, the excretion becomes strongly acid and the leaf margins curve further inward to ensure complete contact between the leaf surface and the insect. The Butterwort is distributed across the arctic zone and the north temperate zone.
