# The Constitutional History of Bohemia

The constitutional status of Bohemia has been characterized by a long-standing tension between the desire for national autonomy and the centralizing efforts of the house of Habsburg. Throughout several centuries, the Bohemians sought to maintain or re-establish an ancient constitution that recognized the kingdom's separate identity and rights.

## The Habsburg Suppression of the Ancient Constitution
Following the battle of the White Hill, the house of Habsburg moved to dismantle Bohemian autonomy. In 1627, Ferdinand published a decree that formally suppressed the ancient free constitution of Bohemia. While a semblance of representative government remained, the new constitution established the Bohemian crown as hereditary within the house of Habsburg. 

Under this 1627 system, the sovereign held exclusive legislative power, and the diet was restricted to discussing matters presented by the king's representatives. Although the estates retained the right to vote on taxes, they were forbidden from attaching conditions to these grants. Additionally, a new "estate" of the clergy—consisting of the Roman archbishop of Prague and ecclesiastics with landed estates—was added and given precedence over the three existing estates. To accommodate the influx of foreigners who had acquired land through wholesale confiscations, the German language was granted equal right with Bohemian in law-courts and government offices.

## 19th Century National Revivals and Absolutism
National aspirations revived after 1815, initially through literature. By 1845, the estates of Bohemia began opposing the government in Vienna, asserting their right to vote on taxes based on the 1627 constitution. In 1847, they proposed increasing town representation in the diet, tightening financial control, and introducing the Bohemian language into higher schools.

The revolutionary events of 1848 led to a petition demanding equal rights for the Bohemian language in government offices and the convocation of a general diet elected via extensive suffrage. Emperor Ferdinand initially responded favorably, promising equality of rights and a new meeting of the estates. However, by the end of 1849, all constitutional government in Bohemia ceased. A period of severe reaction followed, during which the national language was targeted for extermination, Bohemian newspapers were suppressed, and German became the exclusive language of schools and government offices.

## Mid-19th Century Constitutional Experiments
Following the Italian campaign of 1859, the Austrian empire attempted to move away from absolutism. A decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers modeled on ancient Bohemian diets. This hope was short-lived; a February 1861 decree established a central parliament in Vienna with extensive powers and an electoral system that favored Germans. Bohemian representatives left this parliament in 1863, arguing that it encroached upon the constitutional power of the diet of Prague.

The constitutional position shifted again after the Austro-Prussian war of 1866. The subsequent constitution of 1867, which applied to the German and Slavic parts of the empire, maintained centralization and sought to preserve German influence. The Bohemians energetically opposed this constitution.

## The Hohenwart Attempt and Later Developments
In 1871, a brief period of optimism emerged under Austrian prime minister Count Karl Hohenwart. Hohenwart intended to reach an agreement that would satisfy Bohemian claims while keeping them connected to the Habsburg empire. On September 14, 1871, an imperial message to the diet of Prague recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia, citing its former constitutional position and the fidelity of its population; the sovereign expressed willingness to confirm this by taking the coronation oath.

This attempt failed due to various influences. In 1872, a government with a pronounced German tendency took office, leading Bohemians to once again refuse to attend parliamentary assemblies in Vienna and Prague. 

Stability returned partially under Count Eduard Taaffe, who became prime minister in 1879. He persuaded Bohemian representatives to return to the parliament in Vienna. The Bohemians agreed, provided it was understood that they still viewed Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia as a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. In exchange, Taaffe's government removed some of the grossest anomalies in the electoral system that had favored the German minority and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.
