# The Upper Avon

The Upper Avon, also referred to as the Warwickshire or sometimes the "Shakespeare" Avon due to its associations with the town of Stratford, is an eastern tributary of the Severn. The river rises near Naseby in Northamptonshire and follows a course of approximately 100 miles before joining the Severn immediately below Tewkesbury in Gloucestershire.

## Course and Geography
The river initially flows in a south-westerly direction toward Rugby. From there, it continues west and south-west to Warwick, where it receives the Leam from the east. Maintaining a general south-westerly direction, the river flows past Stratford-on-Avon, receiving the Arrow on the north and the Stour on the south. It then continues past Pershore and Evesham before reaching Tewkesbury.

The valley of the Upper Avon is consistently broad. The scenery is described as very beautiful, particularly from Warwick downward through the Vale of Evesham. This rich valley is bordered by the wooded slopes of the Arden district of Warwickshire to the north and the bold Cotteswold Hills to the south. Notable locations along the river include the admirable positions of Evesham and Stratford, as well as Warwick Castle, which rises from the river's wooded banks.

## Navigation and Hydrology
The total fall of the Upper Avon is approximately 500 feet. Specifically, the fall is about 230 feet from Rugby and 120 feet from Warwick. 

The river is locked and supports a small trade up to Evesham, which is located 28 miles from Tewkesbury. While the locks between Evesham and Stratford (a distance of 17 miles) are decayed, the river remains accessible to pleasure boats in many reaches thanks to mill-dams and weirs located further upstream. Additionally, the river is noted for abounding in coarse fish.

## Regional Associations
The river's association with the poet William Shakespeare is linked to the town of Stratford on its banks. Local tradition, as noted by Nicholas Rowe in 1710 and corroborated by Archdeacon Davies of Sapperton, suggests a conflict between Shakespeare and Sir Thomas Lucy, a Warwickshire squire. According to the tale, Lucy prosecuted Shakespeare in 1585 for stealing deer from Charlecote Park, and Shakespeare responded by writing a ballad about his prosecutor. This incident is said to have driven Shakespeare from Stratford to London.
