# Kumasi

Kumasi, also known as Coomassie, is the capital of Ashanti in British West Africa. It is situated on a low rocky eminence that extends across a valley to an opposite hill, located within a clearing of the dense forest that covers much of Ashanti. The town occupies an area approximately 1½ miles in length and over 3 miles in circumference. Its name, more correctly rendered as Kum-ase, means "under the okum tree," referring to the abundance of such trees in its streets. Geographically, it is located 168 miles by rail north of Sekondi and 120 miles by road north-northwest of Cape Coast.

## History and Urban Development
During the height of its prosperity from the middle of the 18th to the middle of the 19th century, Kumasi was a city of high culture with broad, regular streets; the main avenue measured 70 yards in width. The city featured a large market-place to the southeast and a "Spirit House" in a nearby grove of trees, which served as the place of execution. The king's palace was constructed of red sandstone and believed to be modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina.

The city suffered significant destruction during British military campaigns. On February 6, 1874, forces under Sir Garnet Wolseley blew up the red sandstone palace, leaving only scanty vestiges. Following the withdrawal of British troops, the town was only partially rebuilt. A new royal palace was later constructed, though it was made of clay rather than brick. Further damage occurred in 1896, when fire destroyed several ancient and large houses in the priestly and royal suburb of Bantama, and the city was injured again during the revolt of 1900.

## Administration and Military Conflict
The government of Ashanti was historically a mixture of military aristocracy and monarchy. While various tribes—including the Adansi, Kokofu, and Kumasi—had their own kings, the king of Kumasi was recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti from the beginning of the 18th century, succeeding to the "golden stool" as a symbol of authority.

Tensions with Britain escalated in the 1870s, partly due to the 1872 transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British, which the Ashanti viewed as their territory. Other causes included the captivity of Europeans, such as members of the Basel mission and a French trader, who were taken to Kumasi. 

Following the deportation of Prempeh in 1896, no successor was appointed to the throne. British administration was established under a resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart, and the affairs of the Kumasi were managed by chiefs under British guidance. A fort was built in Kumasi in 1896 to house the chief commissioner and a garrison of several hundred men. In 1900, a rebellion broke out involving the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes. The conflict was intensified when Governor Sir F. Hodgson demanded the "golden stool," which the Kumasi had hidden since 1896, and ordered the chiefs to pay 4,000 oz. of gold annually.

## Trade and Economy
Kumasi serves as the distributing centre for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti. This role was solidified by 1906, when Kumasi supplanted the coast towns in commercial importance. The town's expansion was accelerated by the completion of the railway from Sekondi in September 1903, which passes through the gold-bearing regions of Tarkwa and Obuassi. This infrastructure encouraged merchants from Gold Coast ports to open branches in Kumasi.

The broader economy of the region relies on several key products:
*   **Agriculture:** The rubber tree (_Funtumia elastica_) is commercially important. Kola nuts are in great demand, and cocoa plantations were established in eastern districts starting in 1905. Tobacco is grown in the north, and gum copal is exported.
*   **Mining:** Gold is found in the western districts. The Obuassi mines provided most of the gold exported from the Gold Coast after 1902. In 1905, Ashanti's gold output was 68,259 oz., valued at L254,790.
*   **Crafts:** The Ashanti are skilled in pottery, gold work, and weaving cotton. In 1874, highly artistic beaten gold masks and silver-plate were discovered in the king's palace.

## Demographics and Culture
As of 1906, the population of Kumasi was 6,280, increasing to over 12,000 when including the suburbs. The inhabitants are of the pure negro type. While there are some converts to Christianity via Wesleyan and Basel missionaries, and a few Mahommedan traders, the majority of the people are spirit-worshippers.

The town's architecture consists of native houses built with red clay, often richly ornamented and enclosed in compounds. Some principal Ashanti chiefs own European-style houses which they lease to strangers. Modern facilities in the town include European and native hospitals, mission stations, and mercantile houses located near the railway station.
