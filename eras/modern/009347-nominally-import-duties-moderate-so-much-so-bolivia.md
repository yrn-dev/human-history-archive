# Bolivia: Finance, Commerce, and Geography

## Finance and Taxation
The financial administration of Bolivia is characterized by a lack of published itemized returns regarding expenditures and receipts. Information is primarily drawn from estimates presented to congress by cabinet ministers. Taxation is generally not considered heavy, and expenditures are not large. For the years 1904 and 1905, at a rate of 21 pence per boliviano, estimated revenues were L632,773:15s. and L693,763:17:6, respectively. Expenditures for the same period were L748,571:10s. in 1904 and L828,937:19:9 in 1905.

Revenues are derived from several sources, including:
* Duties and fees on imports.
* Excise taxes on sugar, tobacco, wines, and spirits.
* General and mining taxes.
* Export duties on coca, rubber, and minerals (with the exception of silver).
* Taxes on stock company profits.
* Stamp taxes, postal and telegraph revenues, and fees for patents and licences.

Expenditures are primarily allocated to public instruction, justice, military expenses, official salaries, church and mission support, subsidies, and public works, as well as interest on the public debt. In 1905, appropriations were distributed as follows: government and fomento (2,021,428 bolivianos), war (2,081,119 bolivianos), justice and public instruction (1,878,941 bolivianos), and finance and industry (1,462,259 bolivianos).

## Import Duties and Trade Status
Nominally, import duties in Bolivia are moderate, leading to the country sometimes being called a "free-trade country." However, this is considered a misnomer. In addition to schedule rates of 10 to 40% *ad valorem* on imports, the system includes several other charges:
* A consumption tax of 10 centavos per quintal (46 kilogrammes).
* A consular fee of 1-1/2% for the registration of invoices that exceed 200 bolivianos.
* Fees for viseing certificates for merchandise in transit.
* Import and consumption taxes levied by municipalities and departments.
* Special "octroi" taxes on merchandise controlled by monopolies, such as tobacco and spirits.

## Commerce and Foreign Trade
Bolivian foreign trade is comparatively unimportant, though statistical returns are unsatisfactory and incomplete. In 1904, exports were valued at L1,851,758 and imports at L1,734,551. Imports consist of machinery, hardware, provisions, live-stock, woollens, cottons, spirits, wines, and clothing. 

The principal exports, based on 1903 figures, include:
* Tin and its ores (L1,039,298)
* Silver and its ores (L636,743)
* Rubber (L260,559)
* Copper ores (L157,609)
* Coca (L28,907)
* Other minerals (L20,948)
* Bismuth (L16,354)
* Cinchona (L9,197)

Trade is restricted by the poverty of the population and the country's geographical position. Due to the tariffs of neighboring countries and the cost of transportation, exports are limited to pastoral, forest, and mineral products. Furthermore, trade passing through Mollendo, Arica, and Antofagasta is often credited to Peru and Chile rather than Bolivia.

## Geography and Climate
Bolivia possesses a wide range of temperatures across the same latitude, divided into several climatic zones:
* **Yungas:** Lowlands and mountain valleys up to 5,000 ft. This tropical region is exceedingly humid with no winter and a mean temperature of 70 deg. F. Malaria and fevers are common here.
* **Valle:** Deep valleys from 5,000 to 9,500 ft. This sub-tropical region is described as a region of perpetual summer with a warm climate.
* **Cabezera de valle:** The heads of deep valleys from 9,500 to 11,000 ft. This temperate region has regular seasons and is suitable for vegetables and cereals.
* **Puna:** The great central plateau between 11,000 and 12,500 ft. The air is cold and dry with a mean temperature of 54 deg. F.; only barley and potatoes can be produced here.
* **Puna brava:** Bleak territory from 12,500 ft. to the snow limit (approximately 17,500 ft.), inhabited only by miners and shepherds.

Above the *puna brava* lies the region of eternal snow. While the temperate and sub-tropical regions are healthy, there is a high mortality rate among natives due to diet and unsanitary habits.

## Communications and Infrastructure
Bolivia is an isolated region. The loss of its maritime department left it without ports except those of the Paraguay and Madeira rivers, their affluents, and Lake Titicaca (specifically Guaqui). Because these require transhipment in foreign territory, transport costs are increased. Access to the Amazon via the Madeira river is interrupted by falls; the port of entry, Villa Bella, requires a costly portage.

To improve communication, treaties were made with Brazil (1903) and Chile (1904). Brazil agreed to pay L2,000,000 for railway construction and to build a 180 m. railway around the falls of the Madeira. Chile agreed to build a 295 m. railway from La Paz to Arica. Other connections include a line from Guaqui to Alto de La Paz (54 m.), connected to La Paz by a 5 m. electric line, providing access to the Peruvian port of Mollendo.
