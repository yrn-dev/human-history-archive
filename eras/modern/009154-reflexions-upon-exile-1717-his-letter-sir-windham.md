# Reflexions upon Exile and the Letter to Sir W. Windham

The *Reflexions upon Exile* and the 1717 letter to Sir W. Windham are literary and political compositions authored by Bolingbroke during a period of exile and political transition. These works reflect his efforts to reconcile with the English government and clarify his political standing following his association with the Jacobite cause.

## Context and Composition
In March 1716, Bolingbroke sought a pardon from the English government. As part of this process, he declared his final abandonment of the Pretender. While he promised to utilize his influence to ensure his friends withdrew, he maintained a refusal to betray any individuals or secrets. During this period, he wrote *Reflexions upon Exile* and, in 1717, composed a letter to Sir W. Windham. This letter served as an explanation of his position and is generally regarded as one of his finest compositions. Despite its quality, the letter was not published until 1753, following Bolingbroke's death.

## Political Implications and the Jacobite Connection
The relationship between Bolingbroke and Sir William Windham was situated within the context of Jacobite politics. Sir William Windham, a Jacobite, had been appointed chancellor of the exchequer during a time when a Jacobite restoration appeared inevitable and a new ministry of Jacobites was being projected. 

The contents of Bolingbroke's correspondence with Windham have been used to analyze his political sincerity. In his letter to Windham, Bolingbroke stated that "none of us had any very settled resolution." This statement is contrasted with his claims in *The Patriot King*, where he asserted there were no designs to place the crown on the head of the Pretender—a claim described as a "palpable falsehood." Evidence suggests Bolingbroke's primary objective was the acquisition and maintenance of supreme power, regardless of the betrayals required. This is highlighted by his actions surrounding the death of the queen on August 1, which ruined his position; on the day before her death, he had ordered overtures to be made to the elector, and on the day the earl of Oxford was removed, he hosted a dinner for Whig leaders.

## Life in Exile and Return to England
Following these writings, Bolingbroke resided at the estate of La Source near Orleans. During this time, he studied philosophy and criticized the chronology of the Bible. He was visited by Voltaire, who expressed unbounded admiration for his politeness and learning. In 1720, he married Marie Claire Deschamps de Marcilly, the widow of the marquis de Villette, after the 1718 death of Lady Bolingbroke.

Bolingbroke eventually received a pardon in 1723 through the duchess of Kendal, the king's mistress. He returned to London in June or July of that year and offered his services to Walpole. Although Walpole received his offers to bring Tories into the administration coldly, Bolingbroke remained active, gaining information for the government in France during the following winter.

## Later Literary and Political Activities
After an act was passed in 1725 allowing him to hold real estate (though not alienate it), Bolingbroke purchased an estate at Dawley near Uxbridge. There, he renewed his intellectual connections with Voltaire, Swift, and Pope. He contributed the philosophy for the *Essay on Man* and participated in the literary disputes of Pope.

Bolingbroke returned to active political opposition in 1726 following Pulteney's rupture with Walpole. In conjunction with Windham and Pulteney, he attempted to organize an opposition. Starting in 1727, he wrote a celebrated series of letters to the *Craftsman* attacking the Walpoles, using the pseudonym "Occasional Writer." To further his influence, he provided a bribe of £11,000 to the duchess of Kendal, funded by his wife's estates.
