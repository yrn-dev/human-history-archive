# Influence of Boehme, Hume, Hartley, and Godwin on Franz Xaver von Baader

## Franz Xaver von Baader
Franz Xaver von Baader (1765–1841) was a German theologian and philosopher born in Munich on March 27, 1765. He was the third son of F. P. Baader, who served as the court physician to the elector of Bavaria. Baader's brothers were also distinguished, with Joseph serving as an engineer and Clemens as an author. 

Baader's early professional pursuits were varied; he studied medicine in Vienna and Ingolstadt and briefly assisted his father in medical practice. Finding this path uncongenial, he transitioned to mining engineering, studying under Abraham Gottlob Werner at Freiberg and traveling through various mining districts in north Germany.

## English Intellectual Influence
Between 1792 and 1796, Baader resided in England. During this period, he encountered several influential thinkers and texts. He became acquainted with the works of Jakob Boehme, as well as the ideas of Godwin, Hartley, and Hume. Baader found the ideas of Hume, Hartley, and Godwin to be "extremely distasteful." In contrast, he found his own mode of thought to be more in harmony with the mystical speculations of Saint Martin, Meister Eckhart, and especially those of Boehme.

## The Ideas of Hume and Hartley
The thinkers Baader found distasteful were central to English psychology and philosophy. David Hume is noted for his work on the principles of association, which he reduced to three: Cause and (or) Effect, Contiguity in time and place, and Resemblance. Hume's skepticism regarding knowledge of the self or object was later addressed by Kant, who sought to demonstrate that consciousness of ideas and impressions is impossible without a permanent, external world of objects. Additionally, Hume and Berkeley contended that matter was an abstraction, leading to the conclusion that the mind must also be considered an abstraction.

David Hartley, a physician, published *Observations on Man* in 1749. Hartley sought to combine a theory of mental association with a physical hypothesis regarding the nervous system, specifically a vibratory motion within the nerves suggested by Newton in the *Principia*. While his physical hypothesis was eventually dropped by followers such as F. Priestley, Hartley's doctrine of mental association remained significant. He viewed mental states as something to be scientifically analyzed and sought a principle of synthesis to explain complex reasonings, emotions, and objective perception. Like Hume, Hartley viewed the principle of association as having the same importance for the science of mind that gravitation had for the science of matter.

## Godwin and the Radical Tradition
William Godwin is identified as one of the more formidable adversaries to the apologists of the church and state, such as Paley and Butler. Along with figures like Tom Paine, Price, and Mackintosh, Godwin represented a school of "free thought" and radicalism that challenged the continuity and conservation of the church and state championed by figures such as Edmund Burke.

## Later Life and Philosophical Transitions
Upon returning from England in 1796, Baader settled in Hamburg, where he formed a long-term friendship with F. H. Jacobi. During this time, he became acquainted with the works of Schelling. While Baader's published works from this period show a manifest influence from Schelling, he was not a disciple of the philosopher. Their friendship ended around 1822 after Baader wrote a letter to Emperor Alexander I. of Russia denouncing modern philosophy, an act that entirely alienated Schelling.
