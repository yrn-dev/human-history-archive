# Protection and Regulation of Merchant Commerce

The protection and regulation of merchant shipping and trade have historically involved a combination of diplomatic treaties, naval strategies, and legal frameworks to ensure the movement of goods and the security of traders.

## Diplomatic and Territorial Protections
In certain territorial agreements, the protection of merchants was formalized through treaties. One such arrangement required a leader to protect all merchants passing through his territory. Under this agreement, he was permitted only to collect a transit duty, the amount of which was fixed by a schedule attached to the treaty. In exchange for the faithful performance of these duties, the British government provided an annual subsidy of Rs.50,000 (L5000).

In other regions, the experience of merchants was shaped by the geography and local environment. For example, travelers moving from a specific city toward the kingdom of Kerman would ride for seven days over great plains. Along this route, merchants found diversion in hunting and hawking for game such as quails and partridges in the fine woods.

## Naval Strategies for Commerce Protection
The role of navies in protecting merchant shipping has evolved through various conflicts. During the mid-17th century, the Dutch realized that imposing the direct protection of a large flock of merchant ships upon their fleets was not an effectual way to protect commerce, as it hampered their fleets. Consequently, commanders like Tromp later deployed unhampered fleets of warships specifically to engage the English fleet.

In the mid-18th century, the British government adopted a more energetic naval policy to intercept French communications and convoys. During a conflict in October 1748, a French convoy protected by a strong squadron was intercepted in the Bay of Biscay. While the warships of the two nations engaged, the merchant vessels attempted to continue to the West Indies with the limited protection the French admiral, Desherbiers de l'Etenduere, could spare. However, most of these merchant vessels were eventually intercepted and captured.

In the early 20th century, concerns were raised regarding the "paralysis of seaborne trade" and the possibility that German raiders would hunt British merchant ships from the seas. These fears led to debates over whether scores of additional British cruisers would be required for commerce protection. Ultimately, these "gloomy prophecies" did not materialize, and the danger of paralyzed seaborne trade was surmounted.

## Naval Identification and Merchant Service
The British navy utilized specific flags, known as ensigns, to distinguish different services. The red ensign served as the badge for the merchant service, while the blue ensign was used by the naval reserve and certain privileged companies. The white ensign was reserved for the royal yacht squadron and the royal navy.

## Legal and Reparation Frameworks
The regulation of merchants also extended to legal status and post-war reparations. In Italy, the Commercial Code of 1883 stipulated that only merchants—defined as those who engage in commercial business as a habitual profession, including merchant companies—could pass through the bankruptcy court. Bankruptcy was declared only in cases of insolvency, and the process involved a trustee realizing the bankrupt's property under the supervision of a judge and a commission of creditors.

Following a major war, the German Government recognized the right of the Allies to replace all fishing boats and merchant ships lost or damaged during the conflict on a class-for-class and ton-for-ton basis. As part of this agreement, Germany ceded all merchant ships of 1,600 tons gross and upward, half of those between 1,000 and 1,600 tons gross, and one-quarter of other fishing boats and steam trawlers. Additionally, Germany agreed to build merchant ships for the Allies, not exceeding 200,000 tons gross annually for five years.
