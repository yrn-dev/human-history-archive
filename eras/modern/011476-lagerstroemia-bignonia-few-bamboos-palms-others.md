# Lagerstroemia, Bignonia, Bamboos, and Palms

## Distribution and Environmental Adaptations
In the perennially humid regions of the western archipelago and the Malayan peninsula, dense forests are common. These areas are frequently covered by the thorny cane, a palm of the genus *Calamus*, which reaches its greatest development in this part of Asia. This forest flora extends along the mountains of eastern India to the Himalaya, ascending to elevations of 3,000 to 4,000 feet in the west and 6,000 to 7,000 feet in the east.

Certain plant species are better equipped to resist the excessive drought and high temperatures of the northern Indian hot months (April to June) than those requiring equatorial heat. These include *Lagerstroemia*, *Bignonia*, *Bauhinia*, *Acacia*, *Butea*, *Dalbergia*, *Bombax*, *Skorea*, and *Nauclea*. Along with a few palms and bamboos, these species extend beyond the tropic, providing a tropical aspect to the forests at the extreme northern border of the Indian plain.

## Bamboos: Varieties and Cultivation
Bamboos are found in all parts of India and are also present on the Kikuyu escarpment. In the British Isles, several species are hardy under cultivation, primarily those native to Japan and China belonging to the genera *Phyllostachys*, *Bambusa*, and *Arundinaria* (including some Himalayan species). These are described as "hungry plants" that flourish in rich, non-stiff loam when well-watered and mulched.

Specific varieties include:
*   ***Arundinaria simoni***: A plant that can reach 18 feet in height.
*   ***Arundinaria japonica***: A tall plant often grown as *Bambusa metake*.
*   ***Arundinaria nitida***: Noted as remarkably hardy and attractive.
*   ***Bambusa palmata***: Features leaves three inches broad and a foot or more long.
*   ***Bambusa quadrangularis***: Distinguished by its square stems.
*   ***Phyllostachys mitis***: Can grow to 60 feet in China and Japan.
*   ***Phyllostachys nigra***: Characterized by a black stem.

## Industrial and Domestic Uses of Bamboo
Bamboo is described as the "staff of life" among Indo-Chinese nations due to its versatility. In construction, it is used for posts, walls, rafters, floor, thatch, and the withes used to bind them. In some parts of Transgangetic India and the Indian Archipelago, semi-cylinders of bamboo are used for roofing, laid similarly to Roman tiles.

Other applications include:
*   **Tools and Infrastructure**: Scaffolding, ladders, landing-jetties, irrigation scoops and wheels, oars, masts, yards, and conduits.
*   **Household and Art**: Paper, mats, clothes-boxes, dinner-trays, cooking-pots, water-stoups, oil-cans, and musical instruments.
*   **Weaponry**: Bows, bowstrings, quivers, spears, and arrows.
*   **Crafts**: In China and Japan, bamboo splits are used for furniture and baskets of high finish. The Japanese specifically use bamboo wicker-work to encase delicate egg-shell porcelain. In Korea, bamboo is used for mats, hats, and fine split blinds.

## Palms and Other Tropical Flora
Palms are abundant in the Malayan peninsula and the Indian subcontinent. The Palmyra palm (*Borassus flabelliformis*) is used in India to manufacture "Cajan" baskets, a practice that has also been established in Germany's Black Forest. In the Seychelles, the fronds of *Lodoicea sechellarum* are used for delicate basket-work. Other coastal characteristic trees include the coco-nut palm and the mangrove.

In the district of Backergunje in Eastern Bengal and Assam, the landscape is characterized by clusters of bamboos and betel-nut palms, which often wall round the villages. In other arable tracts, palmyras and date-palms may appear in straight lines between fields or dot the expanse.

## Regional Forest Compositions
The forests of the west coasts of the Indian peninsula and Burma, where rainfall is heaviest, feature teak (*Tectona grandis*) mixed with bamboos. Teak is considered the finest timber, though many Indian woods lack durability due to the moist, hot climate. The sal (*Shorea robusta*) is abundant in central India and along the Himalaya from the Punjab to Assam.

In the Malayan peninsula, chief trees include those of the orders Meliaceae, Sapindaceae, Dipterocarpaceae, Leguminosae, Clusiaceae, Ternstroemiaceae, and Terebinthaceae, as well as figs, oaks, laurels, nutmegs, Sapotaceae, and Dilleniaceae. In Africa, near Victoria Nyanza, the flora includes bamboo on the Kikuyu escarpment and ebony in the scrub-jungle.
