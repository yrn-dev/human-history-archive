# Tagdumbash Pamir and the Pamir Boundary

The Tagdumbash Pamir, also referred to as the Tagh-dum-bash Pamir or the "Supreme Head of the Mountains," is a significant geographical feature within the Pamir region. It serves as a critical point in the watershed of the area; while most waters in the Pamir plateau collect in the Oxus, the eastern Tagh-dum-bash stream flows into the Yarkand river. Consequently, the watershed is considered to extend from the Tagdumbash Pamir, continuing down the range that divides it from the Little Pamir and along the Neza Tash mountains to the Kizil Art Pass.

## Regional Geography and Boundaries
The Tagdumbash Pamir is situated in a complex network of high plateaux and mountain ranges. In the province of Badakshan, located on the north-east frontier of Afghanistan and adjoining Russian territory, the boundary skirts around the head of the Tagdumbash Pamir before merging into the Pamir boundary and turning westwards. From this point, the boundary follows the course of the Oxus river to the junction of the Khanabad (Kunduz) and the Oxus.

Badakshan is described as reaching an arm into the Pamirs that is bottle-shaped; it is narrow at the neck, represented by the northern slopes of the Hindu Kush, and swells eastwards to include portions of the Great and Little Pamirs. The northern boundary of Afghanistan is defined by the main channel of the Oxus river, which separates it from Russia and continues to the river's source in Victoria Lake of the Great Pamir.

## The Pamir Plateau and its Divisions
The Pamir plateau is characterized as a broad, rounded ridge extending north and south, intersected by thick mountain chains. These chains create elevated valleys that slope gently toward the east but are narrow and confined with a rapid fall toward the west. Rather than a single steppe, the region consists of a variety of "Pamirs"—lofty valleys between hill ranges with flat floors, typically no more than five or six miles in width, providing luxuriant summer pasture.

Specific divisions of the Pamirs include:
*   Pamir Alichur
*   Pamir Khurd (Little Pamir)
*   Pamir Kalan (Great Pamir)
*   Pamir Khargosi ("of the hare")
*   Pamir Sares
*   Pamir Rang-kul

The Little Pamir, located at Bozai-Gumbaz, joins the Pamir-i-Wakhan at the Wakhijrui Pass. The route from Wakhan to the Little Pamir can be traversed via three routes up the Sarhadd: one during winter via the frozen river, and two during spring and summer along the snowy chain that serves as the dividing ridge between the Sarhadd and the Aksu.

## Political Demarcation and Exploration
The boundaries of this region have been the subject of various international agreements and commissions. The Anglo-Russian agreement of 1873 acknowledged Badakshan and its dependent district, Wakhan, as belonging to the amir of Kabul. Later, the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886 fixed a boundary between the Hari Rud and the Oxus. In 1895, an Anglo-Russian Commission demarcated a line extending to the snowfields and glaciers overlooking the Chinese border.

The Sarikol range, known in medieval times as Bolor and to the ancients as Taurus, defines the political boundary between Russia and China on the western edge of the Kashgar plains. This range consists of two parallel chains: the western chain acts as the water-divide of the Pamirs, while the eastern chain (the Kandar or Kashgar range) is split by lateral gorges that allow drainage from the eastern Pamir slopes.

Exploration of the region was conducted by various figures, including George N. (Lord) Curzon and the Swedish Professor Sven Hedin, who investigated the glacial regions of the Sarikol mountains between 1894 and 1896. Additionally, Captain H. Deasy continued the Pamir triangulation into the plains of Kashgar and the sources of the Zarafshan, following work carried across the Hindu Kush by Colonels R.A. Wahab and Sir T.H. Holdich during the 1895 Pamir Boundary Commission.

## Etymology and Nomenclature
The term "Pamir" is suggested to mean "wilderness"—a place that is waste, abandoned, or depopulated, yet remains capable of habitation. This explanation, provided by guides on the Great Pamir and confirmed at Yangi-Hissar, indicates that the word is of Khokandi-Turki origin. Historically, the valleys were known by their specific village names; the generic term "Pamir" was applied once the areas became deserted. In Persian, the Pamir is known as *Bam-i-dunya*, meaning the "Roof of the World."
