# The Buffs and the Ross-shire Buffs

The "Buffs" and the "Ross-shire Buffs" are designations for two specific units within the British army: the old 3rd Foot regiment of the line and the old 78th Foot, respectively.

## Etymology and Nomenclature
The term "buff" is derived from the French word *buffle*, meaning buffalo. Historically, buff referred to a leather produced from the skins of buffaloes, though it was later produced using the skins of other animals. This leather is characterized by a dull pale yellow colour and was utilized in the creation of jerkins or buffcoats, which served as leathern military coats.

The 3rd Foot and the 78th Foot earned their titles as the "Buffs" and the "Ross-shire Buffs" because of the buff or yellow colour of their facings. In contemporary usage, the term "buff" is commonly applied to the colour itself.

## The East Kent Regiment
The old 3rd Foot regiment of the line is now known as the East Kent Regiment. This unit possesses a specific ceremonial privilege shared with the Royal London Militia, the 3rd Battalion Grenadier Guards, and the Royal Marine Forces. Due to a common original association with the London train bands, the East Kent Regiment is permitted to march through the city of London with their bands playing, their bayonets fixed, and their colours flying.

## The Ross-shire Buffs
The old 78th Foot is now designated as the 2nd battalion of the Seaforth Highlanders. Like the East Kent Regiment, it is identified by the "Buffs" nomenclature due to the specific yellow colour of its uniform facings.
