# The Indus and Kabul River Systems

The Indus and its Kabul tributary form a critical hydrological and geographical network in Asia, draining the highlands of Afghanistan and the surrounding regions southward into the Arabian Sea. This system serves as a primary drainage mechanism for the extensive plateau located west of British India, which encompasses regions such as Afghanistan, Baluchistan, Iran (Persia), Armenia, and Asia Minor.

## Geography and Course
The headwaters of the Indus and its Kabul tributary are separated from the headwaters of the Oxus—which flow toward the Aral Sea—by a high-altitude mountain range in the north-eastern portion of the Afghan table-land. The Indus and Kabul rivers unite below Peshawar, after which they are discharged southward into the Arabian Sea.

The Kabul River is defined by the Safed Koh, a great central range of rugged peaks that acts as the southern wall and water-divide of the Kabul River basin. The river breaks through a northern spur of the Safed Koh, where it is forced into a curved channel or trough characterized by deep sinks, precipices, and terrific cliffs. To the south of the Peshawar plains, the mountain barrier consists of a series of flexures folded parallel to the river, creating gorges and clefts where plateau drainage breaks through in transverse lines.

In other regions, such as the district of Bannu, the Indus is joined by other streams including the Kurram and its tributary, the Gambila. Within the Bannu district, the Indus is navigable for native boats over a course of 76 miles.

## Strategic and Historical Significance
The region surrounding these rivers has been of immense strategic importance for over 2,000 years. The Khyber Pass, a narrow defile of shale and limestone cliffs ranging from 600 to 1,000 feet high, serves as the primary gateway from Afghanistan into the plains of India. This pass has been traversed by numerous conquerors and warrior chiefs, including Alexander the Great, Mahmud of Ghazni, Jenghiz Khan, Timur, Baber, Nadir Shah, and Ahmed Shah.

Ancient empires utilized these waterways for exploration and administration. The Persian ruler Darius sent an expedition led by the Carian captain Scylax of Caryanda down the Kabul and Indus rivers to explore the Indian Ocean from the mouth of the Indus to Suez. Later, the empire of Alexander the Great included Indian provinces such as the Paropanisidae (the Kabul valley) and a province on the Indus assigned to Pithon, the son of Agenor. Alexander himself undertook a voyage down the Indus and its tributaries to the sea-coast, a journey that lasted seven months. Upon reaching the ocean, he explored the coast and sailed to an island known as Psiltukis (or Skillustis) before returning.

## Regional Administration and Exploration
In more recent history, the Indus valley was a focus of British exploration and political treaty-making. Sir Alexander Burnes, a British traveller and explorer, conducted extensive investigations in the region during the 1830s. In 1835, he was appointed to the court of Sind specifically to secure a treaty for the navigation of the Indus.

Political control of the region also involved local dynasties. The Talpur family, who rose after the fall of the Kalhora dynasty of Sind, ruled the state of Khairpur. In 1832, a treaty with the British government recognized the individuality of the Khairpur state and secured the use of the roads of Sind and the river Indus.

## Surrounding Terrain and Tribes
The eastern flank of the high table-land follows a line of hills a short distance from the Indus between the river's mouth and the Himalaya, generally staying between 4,000 and 5,000 feet, though some summits exceed 10,000 feet. The southern and south-western face of this region follows the coast of the Persian Gulf from the mouth of the Indus.

The lands surrounding the Indus valley are inhabited by various groups. The Durani (or Durrani) are the dominant Afghan race, with the ruling family at Kabul belonging to this group. The term "Bar Durani" is applied to independent Pathan tribes inhabiting the Indus valley, the Salt Range, the range of Suliman, and the hill districts south of the Hindu Kush. These include the Yusafzai, Utman Khel, Tarkanis, Mohmands, Afridis, Orakzais, and Shinwaris, as well as Pathan tribes in the plains of Peshawar. In the district of Bannu, the population includes the Waziri Pathans, the Marwats, and the Bannuchis.
