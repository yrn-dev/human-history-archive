# Budapest

Budapest serves as the seat of the Hungarian government, the parliament, and all highest official authorities, including financial, judicial, military, and civil branches. It is the intellectual and artistic center of Hungary, as well as the location of its centralized trade and industry. The city is administered by an elected municipal council comprising 400 members and is the see of a Roman Catholic archbishop. In 1892, it was elected as a royal residence, achieving equality with Vienna. Additionally, it serves as a meeting-place for Austro-Hungarian delegations, alternating this role with Vienna.

## Urban Layout and Architecture

The city features several prominent thoroughfares and public spaces. The left bank of the Danube is characterized by a line of broad quays extending 2½ miles from the custom-house to the Margaret bridge. Among these, the 1-mile-long Franz Joseph Quai is a favorite promenade and contains the most fashionable hotels and cafés.

The inner town is encircled by the Innere Ring-Strasse, a circle of wide boulevards built on the site of the old wall. This inner ring is connected to the Grosse Ring-Strasse via the Váczi Körut. The Grosse Ring-Strasse consists of a succession of boulevards forming a semicircle that begins at the Margaret bridge and ends at the Boráros Platz near the custom-house quay. Other wide, tree-shaded streets that demarcate different districts include the Üllöi Ut, the Kerrepesi Ut, and the Király Utcza.

One of the city's most beautiful streets is the Andrássy Ut. Spanning 1½ miles, it connects the Városliget (the favorite public park) with the Váczi Körut. The first half of this busy thoroughfare is lined with magnificent new buildings, while the second half reaches a width of 150 feet and is lined with handsome villas in their own gardens, creating the atmosphere of a fashionable summer resort. The city also possesses numerous squares, which are generally decorated with monuments of prominent Hungarians created by Hungarian artists.

## Population and Demographics

Budapest experienced rapid growth during the 19th century, a transformation particularly evident since 1867. In 1799, the combined population of Buda and Pest was 54,179 (24,306 in Buda and 29,870 in Pest), marking the first time Pest's population exceeded that of Buda. By 1869, the joint population reached 270,685, with 200,000 residing in Pest. The civil population continued to rise: 360,551 in 1880, 491,938 in 1890, and 716,476 in 1900. Including a garrison of 15,846 men, the total population in 1900 was 732,322.

In terms of growth, Budapest was only slightly surpassed in Europe by Berlin; between the start of the century and 1890, Berlin saw an increase of 818% while Budapest saw an increase of 809%. This far exceeded the growth of London (340%) and Paris (343%) in the same period. A notable trend in the city's progress was the decline of the death rate, which dropped from 43.4 per thousand in 1874 to 20.6 per thousand in 1900.

The population is heterogeneous, reflecting the elements of the Habsburg dominions. As of 1900, the total population included:
*   Magyars: 578,458
*   Germans: 104,520
*   Slovaks: 25,168
*   Others: Croatians, Russians, Rumanians, Greeks, Armenians, Servians, and Gypsies.

Religious demographics include 445,023 Roman Catholics, 168,985 Jews, 67,319 Protestants of the Helvetic Confession, 38,811 Protestants of the Augsburg Confession, 5,806 Greek Catholics, and 4,422 Greek Orthodox.

## Social and Political Development

The progress of the city is attributed to the restoration of the constitution and the revival of the Hungarian national spirit during the first half of the 19th century. Count Stephan Széchényi, a great reformer of the pre-Revolutionary period, provided a powerful impetus for the ideal of a brilliant Magyar capital intended to unite the intellectual flower and nobles of the country in service of the Fatherland.

Since 1848, when it became the seat of the legislature, Budapest has been the center of Hungary's social, intellectual, and political life. Following 1867, it became a rival to Vienna, benefiting from a lack of internal competitors within its own sphere. A strong movement toward "Magyarizing" institutions has developed in the city, resulting in the removal of all German names from streets and buildings.
