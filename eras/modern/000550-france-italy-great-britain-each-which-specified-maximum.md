# Commercial and Political Relations of France, Italy, and Great Britain

## Trade and Customs Agreements
In the 19th century, the commercial policy of the dual state of Austria-Hungary was initially shaped by a series of commercial treaties with several nations, including Great Britain, France, Italy, and Germany. These treaties were designed to specify the maximum duties that could be levied on particular articles and included "most favoured nation" clauses. The practical effect of these agreements was a system that closely resembled an absence of customs duties. Because these treaties were in place, the tariff could not be revised through legislation during the period they remained active.

By 1873, Austrian manufacturers, facing competition from English, German, and Belgian goods, began pushing for protective duties. This coincided with a broader shift in economic thought reacting against free trade doctrines. While Hungary continued to favor free trade to ensure a market for its agricultural produce, the commercial treaties expired after 1875. Although a new treaty was established in 1877—which introduced low duties on corn and textile goods and raised excises on brandy and sugar—the tariff treaties with France and Great Britain were not renewed. Additionally, attempts to reach an agreement with Germany failed due to the policy changes adopted by Bismarck. Consequently, the system of commercial treaties ended, allowing Austria-Hungary to determine its tariffs via legislation.

## Imperial Expansion and Colonial Interests
During the era of imperial expansion, Great Britain, France, and Italy, along with Russia, Germany, and Spain, entangled themselves in the affairs of non-European states. This expansion involved populations that were often resentful and unassimilable. 

*   **Great Britain:** Maintained interests in India, Burmah, and Egypt (though Egypt was not yet formally annexed), as well as minor areas such as Shanghai and Malta.
*   **France:** Expanded its holdings to include Algiers and Tunis, as well as Tonkin and Annam.
*   **Italy:** Encountered difficulties in Tripoli and faced a significant setback in Abyssinia at the battle of Adowa in 1896, where over 4,000 Italians were taken prisoner and more than 3,000 were killed.

German policy during this period encouraged France to develop its colonial possessions as a means of diverting its attention from Europe, which served to create friction and rivalry between France and Great Britain.

## Military and Strategic Alliances
In the lead-up to the First World War, strategic planning by Great Britain accounted for various political contingencies involving France and Italy. War orders issued in August 1913 considered scenarios such as Great Britain being at war with Germany alone, or with Germany and Austria, or with Germany, Austria, and Italy. If Great Britain and France were allied, British squadrons were to join the French for a general battle, with concentration at Malta. However, if Britain faced the Triple Alliance single-handedly, the strategy required a temporary abandonment of the Mediterranean to concentrate forces at Gibraltar.

The Triple Alliance itself was influenced by Italy's reluctance to enter a conflict with Great Britain. Italy required a specific statement in the original secret text of the Triple Alliance ensuring it would not be committed to a war against Great Britain.

## Post-War Commissions and Reparations
Following the war, France, Italy, and Great Britain participated in several international commissions to manage European territories and reparations.

*   **Waterways:** The European Danube Commission reassumed its pre-war powers with representatives from France, Italy, Great Britain, and Rumania. For the Elbe, a commission was formed consisting of four German representatives, two from Czecho-Slovakia, and one each from Italy, France, Great Britain, and Belgium.
*   **The Rhine:** The Rhine was placed under a Central Commission meeting at Strassbourg. This body included four representatives from France (who also selected the President), four from Germany, and two each from Italy, Great Britain, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Switzerland.
*   **Reparations:** The Austrian section of the Reparation Commission included representatives from Italy, France, Great Britain, the United States, Poland, Rumania, Czecho-Slovakia, and Greece. The first four nations each appointed a delegate with two votes.

## Historical and Demographic Context
Historically, the regions now known as France and Great Britain have been sites of significant Roman expansion. Julius Caesar established his reputation as a military leader in Gaul (modern France and Belgium) and crossed the Straits of Dover into Britain in 55 and 54 B.C., though he achieved no permanent conquest there.

Demographic records from the archive provide varying estimates for these nations. One source lists the populations of France at 20 million, Italy (including islands) at 10 million, and Great Britain and Ireland at 8 million. Another authority, the Almanach de Gotha, provides higher figures: France at 32,897,521, Italy at 20,548,616, and Great Britain and Ireland at 24,062,947.
