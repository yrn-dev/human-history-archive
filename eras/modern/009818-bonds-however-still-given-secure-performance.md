# Bonds

In English law, a bond is defined as an obligation by deed. Its primary purpose is to ensure that the obligor (the person providing the bond) either pays a specific sum of money or performs—or refrains from performing—a particular act. To achieve this, the obligor binds themselves to the obligee through a penalty. A condition, known as the defeasance, is added to the agreement; if the obligor fulfills the specified act or pays the secured sum (which is typically half of the penalty), the bond becomes void. If these conditions are not met, the bond remains in full force.

## Types and Applications of Bonds
While often associated with the payment of money at a fixed date, bonds are used to secure a wide variety of other matters. These include:
*   **Guarantees of Fidelity:** Bonds may be used to guarantee the honesty of a rent collector, a clerk, or an individual holding an office of public trust.
*   **Contractual and Business Obligations:** They can secure the completion of a building contract or ensure that an obligor does not operate a rival business except within specific limits of time and space.
*   **Personal Obligations:** Bonds may be used to secure that a husband settles a sum on his wife should she survive him.

Specialized forms of bonds include:
*   **Post Obit Bonds:** Given by a legatee or expectant heir, these are payable on or after the death of the person from whom the obligor expects an inheritance. Such bonds may be set aside if the obligee has exacted unconscionable terms.
*   **Bottomry Bonds:** A contract of hypothecation where a ship's owner or master borrows money for ship emergencies (such as necessary repairs) by pledging the ship's keel or bottom as security. If the voyage is completed safely, the obligee is repaid with interest; if the ship is totally lost, the obligee loses the investment.
*   **Lloyd's Bonds:** Instruments under a railway company's seal that admit indebtedness for goods supplied or work done, allowing companies to increase debt without technically violating their charter.
*   **Debenture Bonds:** Bonds secured solely by the company's covenant, without a fixed or floating charge on assets.
*   **Heritable Bonds:** A term in Scots law referring to a bond for money joined with a conveyance of land held by a creditor as security.

## Legal Procedures and Remedies
If an obligor fails to perform the condition or pay by the stipulated day, the bond becomes absolute or forfeit at law, charging the obligor and their estate. Historically, the entire penalty was recoverable, and payment made after the due date (*post diem*) could not be pleaded. However, the court of chancery intervened to prevent oppression, viewing the penalty as a pledge to secure the bona fide repayment of principal, interest, and expenses. This was codified in an act of 1705, which allowed the payment of the lesser sum with costs and interest to satisfy a common money bond.

For bonds with special conditions, an act of 1696 (preserved by the Judicature Act) prescribes the remedy. The obligee assigns the breaches, damages are assessed, and a stay of execution is entered once the obligor pays those damages into court. A recurring legal difficulty is determining whether the sum in a special condition bond is a "true penalty" or "liquidated damages." Courts generally will not interfere if the parties have agreed upon the damages for a breach, a principle highlighted in the case of *Kemble v. Farren* (1829).

## Limitations and Prohibitions
Bonds are void if they secure actions contrary to the policy of the law. Examples include marriage brocage bonds (used to procure a marriage) or bonds given to a woman for future cohabitation.

## Financial and State Applications
Bonds have played a significant role in national banking and state revenue:
*   **National Banking:** Under the gold standard law of March 14, 1900, banks were authorized to issue circulation based on the full par-value of deposited bonds. This led to a significant increase in notes outstanding secured by bonds, rising from $207,920,774 on October 31, 1899, to $562,727,614 by 1907.
*   **Bonded Warehouses:** These are state or private warehouses where goods are stored until customs duties are paid. Originally, importers had to provide bonds for future payment. This system, proposed by Sir Robert Walpole in 1733 and adopted in 1803, was intended to prevent the need for immediate sales of goods during market depressions and to curb revenue fraud. The Customs Consolidation Act 1853 eventually dispensed with the requirement of giving bonds.

## Other Contexts
The term "bond" also appears in other disciplines. In Babylonian law, bonds are among the thousands of contracts, deeds, and receipts used to study ancient legal customs. In medicine, the term refers to the "fibrous bond of union" that forms between fragments of a fractured patella (knee-cap) during the healing process.
