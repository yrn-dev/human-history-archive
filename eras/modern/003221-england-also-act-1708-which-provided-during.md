# England

## Banking and Financial Legislation
The Bank of England was incorporated by the act of 1694. Following this, the acts of 1697 and 1708 defined the status of other banks, declaring that no joint-stock bank was to be established in England while the Bank of England continued. The act of 1708 further stipulated that no partnership or company exceeding six persons could borrow, owe, or take up money on bills or notes payable on demand or in less than six months from the time of borrowing. These restrictions were confirmed by the act of 1800.

Historically, banking in England was closely tied to the issuance of notes, which originated from goldsmiths' notes. Goldsmiths provided receipts or notes payable on demand for money deposits. Because the power to issue notes was seen as the primary feature of banking, prohibitions on such issuance were viewed as effective bars against the formation of banks. Consequently, the act of 6 Anne, c. 50 (1707) and the Bank of England Act 1708, which prohibited more than six persons from issuing promissory notes, effectively prevented the formation of joint-stock banks. London bankers continued providing deposit-receipts or notes until approximately 1781, when they began providing books of cheques instead.

Significant changes to banking laws occurred in 1826, when one act prohibited bank notes under £5 and a second Banking Act allowed the establishment of joint-stock companies (co-partnerships of more than six persons) provided they were located beyond 65 miles from London. The act of 1833 subsequently permitted joint-stock banks within the 65-mile limit and removed various restrictions on notes for less than £50, though joint-stock banks within that 65-mile radius were still not allowed to issue notes.

## Judicial System
The English legal system includes Lords Justices of Appeal and Lords of Appeal in Ordinary. The Lords Justices of Appeal serve as ordinary judges of the appellate division of the High Court of Justice. Their style was established by the Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1877, and their number was fixed at five by the Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1881, s. 3. They receive an annual salary of £5000.

Lords of Appeal in Ordinary are limited to four individuals who have practiced at the bar for at least fifteen years or held high judicial office. They sit as members of the House of Lords to adjudicate cases in a legal capacity and assist the judicial committee of the Privy Council with appeals. Their annual salary is £6000. Appointed under the Appellate Jurisdiction Act 1876, they are life peers and are nominated and appointed by the style of Baron. They are entitled to a writ of summons to sit and vote in the House of Lords during their tenure. Typically, one is appointed from the Scottish bar or bench and one from the Irish.

## Criminal Punishments and Law
England historically employed various severe punishments. Boiling to death was once common, frequently used for coining and later for poisoning. An act passed in 1531 during the reign of Henry VIII specifically provided that convicted poisoners be boiled to death. Examples include Richard Roose (or Coke), a cook who poisoned food intended for the poor of Lambeth and the household of the bishop of Rochester, and a maid-servant in King's Lynn. In 1542, a servant named Margaret Davy was boiled at Smithfield for poisoning her employer. This punishment was repealed in 1547 during the reign of Edward VI.

Regarding heresy, the English common law did not recognize ecclesiastical offences, and church courts lacked the power to condemn to death. While isolated burnings occurred in the 12th and 13th centuries, the burning of heretics was legally established by the statute *de haeretico comburendo* in 1400. This was repealed in 1533, though the Six Articles Act of 1539 revived it for those denying transubstantiation. The acts of Henry IV and Henry V were revived under Queen Mary and finally abolished in 1558 upon the accession of Elizabeth. Despite this, Elizabeth, Edward VI, and James I continued to burn heretics under the supposed right of issuing writs. The last recorded burnings in England occurred in 1610, involving two Arians, Edward Wightman at Lichfield and Bartholomew Legate at Smithfield. Unlike other European countries, England hanged witches rather than burning them.

## Military Billeting
Billeting involves providing board and lodgings for soldiers. In England, this system was historically superintended by civil magistrates who often abused their power to favor friends over political opponents. Due to these abuses, the Petition of Right 1628 and an act of 1679 declared billeting illegal. However, during the reign of James II, orders for billeting were frequently issued, leading to the inclusion of the quartering of soldiers contrary to law as a grievance in the Bill of Rights.

Following the revolution, the Mutiny Act 1689 authorized billeting among victuallers and innkeepers because barrack accommodation was insufficient, sufficing for only 5000 men. This statute was renewed annually until 1879, when it was consolidated into the Army Discipline Act. This was later replaced by the Army Act 1881. By the modern era, extensive barrack accommodation and the use of sea or rail transport have largely removed the necessity for billeting. The Army (Annual) Act 1909 provided for the billeting of Territorial forces during national emergencies at the discretion of the chief officer of police.

## Land Improvement and Burial Laws
To encourage land improvement, England passed several parliamentary acts to assist tenants with limited interests. The Public Money Drainage Acts 1846-1856 allowed landowners to use public money for irrigation, drainage, clearing, erecting buildings, planting for shelter, and making permanent roads. Subsequent special improvement acts allowed private capital to be used, and the Land Improvement Act 1864 (amended in 1899) allowed borrowing by charging costs as a rent-charge upon the land's inheritance.

Burial regulations are matters of ecclesiastical cognizance. Before 1880, burial in consecrated ground required the service of the Church, which was refused for unbaptized persons, those with a verdict of *felo de se*, and those excommunicated for notorious crimes. The Burial Laws Amendment Act 1880 changed this, allowing bodies to be buried in parochial burial grounds without the Church of England service, provided proper notice was given. Burials could then be conducted without religious service or with a Christian and orderly service led by an invited person.
