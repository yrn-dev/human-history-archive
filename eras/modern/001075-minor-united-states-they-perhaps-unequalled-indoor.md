## Azaleas
The azalea is a genus of greenhouse or hardy plants within the heath order (Ericaceae). Botanically, they are scarcely separable from *Rhododendron*. The varieties currently in cultivation were bred from a few original plants native to the United States, Asia Minor, Japan, and the hilly regions of China. These plants are considered perhaps unequalled as indoor decorative plants.

To maintain and grow azaleas, they require a rich, fibrous peat soil mixed with sand to prevent water-logging. The soil must be made quite solid to avoid retaining excessive water. The ideal timing for potting is three to four weeks after blooming has concluded. Young plants must be stopped as required to produce handsome specimens, and "leggy" specimens may be cut back just before growth begins.

Propagation is usually achieved by grafting half-ripened shoots onto stronger-growing kinds, ensuring both the stock and grafts are in a similarly half-ripened state and placed in moist heat of 65 degrees. This method allows for the creation of large specimens in a short time by grafting choicer sorts over large plants of inferior, healthy kinds. Temperature requirements vary by season: during winter, the lowest temperature is approximately 35 degrees; during the growth season, temperatures should be between 55 and 65 degrees at night and 75 degrees by day, with an atmosphere well charged with moisture. Azaleas are susceptible to red spider and thrips, which can cause great mischief if not promptly destroyed.

## Political Power and "Bosses"
In various cities, individuals with exceptional energy have secured irregular power within the state by utilizing class grievances or social conflicts. In the United States of America, men who exercise this type of informal power are referred to as "bosses." This differs from the Greek "tyrants," who were recognized as monarchs and claimed monarchical authority. While the modern boss hides behind legal forms they have acquired for their own ends, tyrants were distinguished from kings, who claimed a right to rule based on family priority. 

Tyrants were sometimes supported by the poorer classes, such as Peisistratus, the tyrant of Athens (between 560 and 527 B.C.), who was supported by poverty-struck hillmen. In other instances, such as in Greek Sicily, tyrants represented the rich against the poor. Additionally, when Persians began to subjugate Greek cities in Asia Minor, they established pro-Persian tyrants.
