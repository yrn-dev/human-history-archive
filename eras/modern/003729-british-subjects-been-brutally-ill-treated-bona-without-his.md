# Suppression of Barbary Piracy and the Bombardment of Algiers

## Context of Mediterranean Piracy
Following the general pacification of 1815, the suppression of African piracy was regarded as a universal necessity. This urgency was heightened by the actions of a Tunisian squadron that sacked Palma in the island of Sardinia, carrying off 158 inhabitants and causing widespread indignation. During this period, Great Britain had acquired the Ionian Islands and Malta, resulting in a significant number of Mediterranean subjects.

Beyond the immediate threat of piracy, Great Britain was actively pressing other European powers to collaborate in the suppression of the slave trade. This trade was practiced on a large scale by the Barbary states, often at the expense of Europe. Consequently, the suppression of this trade became one of the primary objectives of the Congress of Vienna.

## Diplomatic Efforts and the Incident at Bona
Great Britain was called upon to act on behalf of Europe to address these issues. In 1816, Lord Exmouth was dispatched to Algiers and Tunis to obtain treaties. His initial visit resulted in promises and the production of diplomatic documents, after which he sailed for England.

However, while these negotiations were taking place, a number of British subjects were brutally ill-treated at Bona. This occurred without Lord Exmouth's knowledge. In response to these events, the British government ordered Lord Exmouth to return to the region to secure reparation for the mistreated subjects.

## The Bombardment of Algiers
On August 27, 1816, Lord Exmouth returned and, in combination with a Dutch squadron led by Admiral Van de Capellen, conducted a smashing bombardment of Algiers. The military action terrified the pirates of both Tunis and Algiers, leading them to release over 3,000 prisoners and offer fresh promises of reform.

Despite these concessions, the pirates were not reformed, nor were they capable of reformation. Algiers resumed its practices of slave-taking and piracy, although it did so on a smaller scale. These ongoing issues were later discussed at the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818.

## Later Actions and Conquest
The instability of the region required further British military intervention. In 1824, another British fleet, commanded by Admiral Sir Harry Neal, was forced to bombard Algiers once again. The city of Algiers was not thoroughly tamed until it was eventually conquered by France in 1830.
