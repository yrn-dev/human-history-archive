# Ununited Fracture

An ununited fracture is a medical condition characterized by a failure of the body to consolidate broken bone parts after a period of many weeks or months. This condition represents a failure of nature to attempt the consolidation of the fractured sections.

## Causes of Ununited Fractures
Several factors may contribute to the development of an ununited fracture. These include:
* **Improper Positioning:** The ends of the broken bone may not have been brought close enough together to allow for healing.
* **Instability:** The site of the fracture may have been subjected to constant disturbance.
* **Physical Obstructions:** Muscle or tendon may become interposed between the broken ends of the bone, preventing union.
* **Patient Health:** The condition may be the result of a constitutional defect in the patient.

## Treatment and Management
Except in cases where the cause is a constitutional defect, the treatment for an ununited fracture involves surgical intervention. The surgeon cuts down to the broken ends of the bone and "freshens" them by sawing off a thin slice. The bone ends are then adjusted and fixed in place using a screw or a wire. 

In children, ununited fractures of the leg-bones are described as a most rebellious and unsatisfactory condition to manage.

## Related Bone Injuries and Healing Processes
The archive distinguishes ununited fractures from other types of healing failures and bone injuries:

### Delayed Union
Delayed union occurs when consolidation is taking place, but does so very slowly or barely at all. This may be caused by constitutional or local factors. If the bones are maintained in a good position, treatment requires patience, general health-measures, and massage.

### Diastasis
In young people and children, the long bones consist of a shaft with cartilaginous ends where bone develops. An injury may cause the end of the bone to become detached, a specific variety of fracture known as diastasis. Regardless of the quality of treatment, this can lead to stiffness in the neighboring joint or an arrest of the bone's growth.

### General Fracture Classifications
Fractures may be categorized by the cause of the break:
* **Direct Violence:** A break occurring at the point where the bone is struck, generally resulting in a transverse fracture.
* **Indirect Violence:** A break resulting from a strain applied to the bone, which is more or less oblique.
* **Muscular Action:** A break caused by muscle movement, such as a rib breaking due to a violent cough.

Furthermore, fractures are classified as simple or compound. A simple fracture is similar to a subcutaneous laceration in soft parts. A compound fracture involves an open wound in the soft parts, creating a communication between the injured bone and external air. This increases the risk of suppuration and septic inflammation, which can lead to osteomyelitis, blood-poisoning, necrosis (death of large pieces of bone), or delayed union.

In young people, soft bones may simply bend rather than break across; this is referred to as a willow fracture or green stick fracture.

## Clinical Indicators and Recovery
A common indicator of a fracture is crepitus, the feeling produced when two rough osseous surfaces rub together. Crepitus is absent in "impacted fractures," where broken extremities are driven into one another, and in cases where the bone is merely bent.

To achieve firm bony union, accurate apposition of fragments is required. This process of putting the ends together is called "setting the fracture," and is supported by the use of splints, which typically fix the joint above or below the fracture site. 

Modern improvements in treatment include the use of X-rays to ensure fractured surfaces are in close apposition. If ordinary methods cannot assure this adjustment, surgeons may cut down to the broken ends and fix them with a permanent strong wire suture. In compound fractures where easy adjustment is impossible, immediate wiring is considered a necessary part of primary treatment. Additionally, the use of discreet movements, exercises, and massage—advocated by the French surgeon Just Lucas-Championniere—helps ensure more rapid recovery and reduces the frequency of stiff hands and feet.
