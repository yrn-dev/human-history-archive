# Twenty-two Volumes of Official Documents Bearing on Bolivar's Career

## Publication and Content
Between 1826 and 1833, twenty-two volumes of official documents pertaining to the career of Bolivar were officially published in Caracas. These documents serve as a record of the professional life and administrative actions of the leader who achieved the independence of three states: Bolivia, Peru, and Colombia.

## Career and Political Achievements
Bolivar is credited with calling forth a new spirit in the southern portion of the New World. His efforts were central to the independence of three nations, a result achieved in great measure through his own exertions. In his capacity as a leader, he fostered national interests, encouraged the arts and sciences, and purified the administration of justice. He also worked to induce other countries to recognize the independence of these states.

His political trajectory included serving as the president of the Republic of Colombia. On December 17, 1819, a fundamental law was enacted that united the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar as president. This was further solidified on August 30, 1821, when the constitution of Colombia was adopted with general approbation, maintaining Bolivar as president and Santander as vice-president.

## Military Campaigns and Leadership
Bolivar's career was marked by significant military endeavors. In 1816, after receiving a small force from President Petion in Aux Cayes, Haiti, he organized an expedition that landed on the mainland in May. Following a failure, he obtained reinforcements at Aux Cayes and landed in Barcelona and Margarita in December. By February 16, 1817, his forces engaged in a desperate conflict that ended in the defeat of the royalists under Morillo.

By February 15, 1819, Bolivar submitted an exposition of his views on government to the congress at Angostura. Although he initially surrendered his authority to the congress, he was required to resume power until independence was fully established. He then led his troops across the Cordilleras to join General Santander in New Granada. This campaign resulted in the entry into Tunja in July 1819 and a victory at Boyaca on August 7, which granted him possession of Bogota and all of New Granada.

Later, Bolivar concluded a six-month armistice at Truxillo on November 25, 1820. When the contest resumed, he achieved a decisive victory at Carabobo, routing the Spaniards under General Torre and effectively ending the war in Venezuela.

## Later Governance and Retirement
In September 1826, Bolivar departed Lima, having entrusted the government to a council led by Santa Cruz. He arrived in Bogota on November 14, assuming extraordinary powers authorized by the constitution for cases of rebellion. He subsequently traveled to Venezuela to stop the effusion of blood, reaching Puerto Cabello on December 31 and issuing a general amnesty the following day.

In February 1827, Bolivar formally resigned the presidency of the republic. He expressed a desire to retire to his patrimonial estate to refute accusations of ambition. However, Santander and a resolution of congress urged him to resume his functions, arguing that only his personal influence and authority could appease the country's agitations. Before returning to Bogota to take the oaths of office, Bolivar issued three decrees: one establishing constitutional order throughout Colombia, one convoking a national convention at Ocana, and one granting a general amnesty.

## Legacy and Commemorations
Bolivar died without any public money in his possession, having spent nine-tenths of a splendid patrimony in service to his country, despite having had unlimited control over the revenues of Peru, Bolivia, and Colombia for a considerable period.

His memory has been honored with several monuments:
*   **Caracas:** His remains were moved here in 1842, and a monument was erected.
*   **Bogota:** A statue was erected in 1846.
*   **Lima:** An equestrian statue of the liberator was erected by the Peruvians in 1858.
*   **New York:** A statue was placed in Central Park in 1884.

Additional biographical records include lives written by Ducoudray-Holstein (Paris, 1831), Larrazabal (New York, 1866), and Rojas (Madrid, 1883), as well as two volumes of his correspondence published in New York in 1866.
