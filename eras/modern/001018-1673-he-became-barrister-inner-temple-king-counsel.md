# The Legal Profession of Barristers

In England and Ireland, a barrister is a member of the highest class of lawyers, possessing exclusive audience in all superior courts. The term is derived from the "bar" found in law courts. To practice as a barrister in England, an individual must be a member of one of the four ancient societies known as the Inns of Court: Gray's Inn, Lincoln's Inn, the Middle Temple, and the Inner Temple. In Ireland, the corresponding society is the King's Inns.

## History and Education
The English Inns of Court originated as schools of law, with roots tracing back to the 13th century. Their development is attributed to a clause in the Magna Carta that fixed the Common Pleas at Westminster rather than following the king's court, which centralized law professors in London. Lawyers formed associations and acquired their own houses to educate students in common law. Within these institutions, the degrees of sergeant (equivalent to a doctor) and barrister (equivalent to a bachelor or apprentice) were conferred.

## Roles and Responsibilities
The primary business of a barrister is the advocacy of causes in open court. However, their duties extend to other legal functions. Barristers serve as the chief conveyancers and are responsible for drafting pleadings—the counter statements made by parties before joining an issue—in most cases. While conveyancing and special pleading were once handled by a separate class of licensed practitioners, these tasks are now part of the ordinary work of a junior barrister.

A barrister's work is based on a "brief," a written statement provided by a solicitor. The solicitor performs preliminary work, such as marshalling evidence and serving papers, while the brief provides the barrister with a concise summary of the case, material facts in chronological order, witness names, and "proofs" regarding the evidence each witness can provide. The delivery of a brief grants the barrister authority to act for the client in all litigation matters.

## Professional Hierarchy and Ranks
The profession is divided into several ranks and specializations:

*   **King's or Queen's Counsel:** This is the higher rank of barrister. Appointed by letters patent via the lord chancellor, they are designated as "one of His Majesty's counsel learned in the law." They lead in court and provide opinions on submitted cases, but they do not admit pupils to their chambers or accept pleading and conveyancing work. Historically, the first appointment was Sir Francis Bacon under Queen Elizabeth. Until 1831, these counsel received a payment of £40 a year.
*   **Utter Barristers:** These are the ordinary practitioners.
*   **Solicitor-General and Attorney-General:** These officials rank as the heads of the profession and hold office at the will of the government.
*   **Benchers:** The governing bodies of the Inns of Court. Benchers have the power to reject membership applicants, refuse to call a student to the bar, or "dis-bar" (expel) barristers and fellow benchers for professional or personal misconduct.

Precedence among outer barristers and king's counsel is determined by seniority. While all barristers may practice in any English court, they often specialize. A primary distinction exists between those practicing in the courts of common law and those in chancery. Common law counsel typically attach themselves to specific circuits.

## Legal Status and Etiquette
Barristers hold the rank of esquires and enjoy certain privileges, including exemption from serving on juries while in active practice and immunity from arrest while on circuit or attending superior courts. They cannot enter into payment contracts with clients for litigation, nor can they maintain actions for their fees, which are viewed as gratuities. Except in criminal cases—where a fee may be given in open court—barristers cannot undertake a case without a solicitor's intervention.

Professional etiquette is reflected in the use of the brief-bag. Blue bags are used by barristers when first called and should not be visible in court. Red bags are given by leading counsel to juniors who have been useful in important cases and are the only bags permitted on desks.

## Heneage Finch, 1st Earl of Aylesford
A notable example of a career within this system was Heneage Finch (c. 1640–1719). Educated at Westminster school and Christ Church, Oxford, Finch became a barrister of the Inner Temple in 1673. He rose to become a bencher and king's counsel in 1677, and was appointed solicitor-general in 1679. 

Finch served as a crown lawyer, representing the crown in the prosecution of Lord Russell and the attack on the corporation of London. In 1684, during the trial of Algernon Sidney, he supported the view that an unpublished treatise constituted an overt act. Despite his Tory leanings, Finch was a staunch churchman; he was dismissed by James in 1686 after refusing to defend the royal dispensing power. In 1688, he served as leading counsel for the seven bishops, though his tactics were noted as mistaken.
