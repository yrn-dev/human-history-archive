# Venosta, I Fratelli Bandiera and Carlo Tivaroni

The historical record of the Italian patriots Attilio and Emilio Bandiera is documented in several key works, including *I Fratelli Bandiera* (Milan, 1863) by F. Venosta and *L'Italia durante il dominio austriaco* (Turin, 1894) by Carlo Tivaroni. These texts, along with other biographical accounts, detail the lives and revolutionary activities of the Bandiera brothers.

## The Bandiera Brothers
Attilio Bandiera (1811–1844) and Emilio Bandiera (1819–1844) were the sons of Baron Bandiera, an admiral in the Austrian navy. Both brothers served in the same naval service. Despite their professional ties to Austria, they were won over at an early age to the ideals of Italian unity and freedom. This commitment led them to correspond with other members of *Giovane Italia* (Young Italy), a secret revolutionary and patriotic society, as well as with Giuseppe Mazzini.

## Revolutionary Activities and the 1844 Expedition
During 1843, a period marked by various conspiracies and attempts to rise against Italian despots, the Bandieras engaged in propaganda efforts targeting the men and officers of the Austrian navy, the majority of whom were Italian. Their initial plan involved the seizure of a warship for the purpose of bombarding Messina. However, after being betrayed, the brothers fled to Corfu in early 1844.

While in Corfu, the brothers received reports of agitation within the Neapolitan kingdom, suggesting that the population was prepared to rise *en masse* if a leader appeared. Encouraged by Mazzini, the Bandieras organized a band of approximately twenty men dedicated to their cause. On June 12, 1844, they set sail for a raid on the Calabrian coast.

The group landed near Cotrone four days later. Their objective was to reach Cosenza, where they intended to issue proclamations and liberate political prisoners. They did not find the insurgent band they had been led to expect. Furthermore, they were betrayed by some peasants, who mistook them for Turkish pirates, and by a member of their own party, a Corsican named Boccheciampe. Following a brief fight with a detachment of volunteers and gendarmes, the entire band was captured and taken to Cosenza.

## Trial and Execution
In Cosenza, the raiders were held alongside Calabrians who had been arrested during a previous rising. The Calabrians were tried first by court-martial, resulting in many sentences of the galleys or death. When the Bandiera party was tried, the traitor Boccheciampe was spared, while the rest were condemned to be shot. For eight members of the party, the death sentence was commuted to the galleys.

On July 23, 1844, the two Bandiera brothers and nine of their companions were executed. As they fell, they cried "*Viva l'Italia!*"

## Historical and Moral Impact
While the material results of the Bandieras' attempt were nil and the Neapolitan government was considered within its rights to execute them, the moral impact across Italy was enormous. The actions of the authorities were universally condemned, and the martyrdom of the brothers is credited with bearing fruit in subsequent revolutions.

The event also caused a significant impression in England. There were beliefs that the British foreign office had forwarded information regarding the expedition to the Neapolitan and Austrian governments and that the brothers' correspondence with Mazzini had been tampered with. However, more recent publications, specifically the biography of Sir James Graham, tend to exculpate the British government.
