# January and the Ashanti Conflicts

The history of the Ashanti kingdom in British West Africa is marked by several significant military and diplomatic events occurring in the month of January, ranging from early 19th-century colonial incursions to the suppression of rebellions in the early 20th century.

## Early 19th Century Engagements
In January 1819, Mr. Joseph Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast as a consul sent by the British government to cultivate relations with the Ashanti kingdom. Following his arrival, Dupuis traveled to Kumasi, where he eventually drew up a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory. This treaty was later disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and the British government in London.

Hostilities escalated under the first crown-appointed governor, Sir Charles M'Carthy, who supported the Fanti. On January 21, 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles in a battle at Essamako, a location beyond the Prah. The Ashanti force consisted of 10,000 men, vastly outnumbering Sir Charles's 500. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed during the engagement; the governor's skull was subsequently used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. On the same day as this defeat, Osai Okoto succeeded Osai Tutu Quamina as king.

## The War of 1873–1874
The conflict of 1873–1874 was triggered by two primary causes: the capture of four Europeans (Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife, infant son, and Mr. J. Kuhne, along with French trader Monsieur M.J. Bonnat) and the British transference of Elmina fort from the Dutch on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti viewed the Elmina as their subjects and resented the British occupation of what they considered their territory.

On January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force crossed the Prah and invaded the British protectorate. During this invasion, they defeated the Fanti, incited disputes at Elmina, and established a camp at Mampon near Cape Coast. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition led by Sir Garnet Wolseley. By the time Wolseley landed at Cape Coast on October 2, the Ashanti forces had been decimated by fever and smallpox and were preparing to return home.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Following the deportation of King Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III), the British installed Captain Donald W. Stewart as resident at Kumasi. While the Kumasi chiefs appeared submissive, they remained unreconciled to British rule. In January 1900, a secret attempt to seize the "golden stool"—the symbol of Ashanti sovereignty, which had been hidden since 1896—failed.

This tension culminated in a serious rebellion involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes. On March 28, 1900, Governor Sir F. Hodgson demanded that the chiefs pay 4,000 oz. of gold annually and reproached them for hiding the golden stool. Three days later, Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa who had been sent to discover the stool. The rebels, numbering approximately 40,000, threatened the Kumasi fort, forcing Sir F. and Lady Hodgson and Basel missionaries to take refuge inside.

## Suppression of the Opposition
The rebellion was eventually crushed through a series of military reinforcements and campaigns. After the garrison at Kumasi was relieved, Colonel Willcocks led a force comprising 134 British officers, 35 non-commissioned officers, and 3,368 natives (including Sikhs from Central Africa and Yaos), supported by Ashanti levies.

The Ashanti opposition was decisively weakened on September 30, 1900, when the Kumasi were completely beaten at Obassa. Following this defeat, many rebel chiefs surrendered, and the final two remaining in the field were captured on December 28. Peace was officially restored at the opening of 1901. The campaign resulted in 1,007 total casualties, including those who died of disease; among these, nine British officers were killed in action.
