# BASHI-BAZOUK

The Bashi-bazouks were a species of irregular mounted troops employed by the Turks. While they were maintained and armed by the government, they did not receive pay, nor did they wear distinctive badges or uniforms.

## Operational Characteristics and Role
Bashi-bazouks were capable of fighting both mounted and dismounted, though they primarily fought on foot. Due to a lack of discipline, they were considered incapable of undertaking serious military work. Their temperaments were described as uncertain, which occasionally necessitated that Turkish regular troops disarm them by force. Despite these limitations, they were frequently utilized for outpost duty and reconnaissance.

In times of peace, the term Bashi-bazouk also referred to mounted forces existing in various provinces of the Turkish empire that performed gendarmerie duties.

## Composition and Origins
A significant portion of the Bashi-bazouks was formed from Circassians who emigrated to Turkey. Following the Russian conquest in 1861, Circassians (also known as Adyghe) who occupied mountain valleys were forced to either settle on flat land or emigrate. Those who refused to move voluntarily were driven across the mountains to the Black Sea coast; nearly 200,000 of these individuals emigrated to Turkey and formed the Bashi-bazouks.

## Conduct and Historical Actions
The Bashi-bazouks were generally accused of the maltreatment and robbery of civil populations. In their value, fighting methods, and behavior, they were compared to the Pandours, Tolpatches, and Croats of 18th-century European armies.

The troops were involved in several violent suppressions of insurrections:

*   **The Revolt of 1876:** During a general revolt in Bulgaria, the Turkish authorities let loose bands of Bashi-bazouks throughout the district of Philippopolis. Along with Circassian colonists and Pomaks (Moslem Bulgarians), they were involved in a succession of horrors and massacres in towns such as Batak, Bratzigovo, Perushtitza, and Panagurishté.
*   **Monastir Viláyet Insurrection:** Following a general insurrection on August 2nd (the feast of St Elias) and subsequent revolts, Bashi-bazouks and other troops overcame the resistance of insurgents by the end of September. During this period, they burned more than 100 villages and destroyed 8,400 houses, leaving 60,000 peasants homeless in the mountains.
