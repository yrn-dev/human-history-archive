# Respiration and Pulse

In medical and historical contexts, the terms "respiration" and "pulse" refer to the physiological functions of breathing and the rhythmic beating of the heart, as well as various pathological conditions that affect these systems.

## Respiratory Disorders and Conditions
Respiration is the process of air entering the lungs, and its arrestment or hindrance is referred to in medicine as asphyxia. Several conditions can impair this function:

*   **Asthma:** This is a disorder of respiration characterized by severe paroxysms of difficult breathing, known as dyspnoea. These attacks occur suddenly and are marked by extreme difficulty in both inspiration and expiration, particularly the latter, which leaves the diaphragm immobile and the chest distended. While "pure" or "idiopathic" asthma shows no signs of inflammation, secondary forms may be renal, cardiac, peptic, thymic, or bronchitic. Patients may experience warning signs such as intense drowsiness or mental excitement. During an attack, the face may appear pale, anxious, or livid, the eyes may bulge, and the breath often sounds whistling or wheezing.
*   **Bronchitis:** In acute bronchitis, inflammation of the bronchial mucous membrane causes swelling and tough secretions that diminish the calibre of the tubes. This results in "dry sounds" (snoring, cooing, or whistling) during respiration. As the disease progresses, these are replaced by "moist sounds" or râles caused by more abundant, less glutinous discharge.
*   **Broncho-pneumonia (Capillary Bronchitis):** This occurs when inflammation affects the minute ramifications of the bronchial tubes near the air-cells. This condition is life-threatening due to the interruption of air entering the lungs and the resulting lack of blood aeration. Symptoms include incessant coughing, extremely rapid and laboured respiration, and dilating nostrils. If the chest is not cleared, the patient may suffer from delirium and somnolence before death.

## Chemical and Drug Influences on Vital Signs
Certain substances can significantly alter the rate of the pulse and the nature of respiration:

**Physostigmine**
Physostigmine is a drug that stimulates nearly all non-striped muscles in the body. Its effects on the heart and lungs are complex:
*   **Heart and Pulse:** Shortly after absorption, the drug causes a great rise in blood pressure. However, it stimulates the terminals of the vagus nerve, which causes the heart to beat more slowly. In later stages, it depresses intra-cardiac motor ganglia, leading to the prolongation of diastole and eventually the arrest of the heart in dilatation.
*   **Respiration:** A dose of physostigmine initially accelerates respiration due to the stimulation of vagus terminals in the lung. Subsequently, respiration is slowed and ultimately arrested. This final arrest is caused by the paralysis of the respiratory centre in the medulla oblongata, which is further hastened by "water-logging" of the lungs (increased bronchial secretion) and a quasi-asthmatic contraction of the non-striped muscular tissue in the bronchial tubes.

## Other Contexts of Pulse and Respiration
The term "pulse" is also used in non-medical contexts. In a historical account of the Admiralty, the term was used metaphorically to describe the strong and regular operational rhythm of the office under a specific working routine. Additionally, in Athenian tradition, the "boiling of pulse" (referring to the legume) is a customary anniversary practice performed on the seventh day of the month Pyanepsion to commemorate the rescued youths.

Regarding environmental factors, observations in the high passes of north-eastern Tibet noted that sufferings in respiration were attributed by some to pernicious exhalations.

## Balneotherapeutics
While not a direct disorder of the pulse or respiration, balneotherapeutics involves the medical treatment of disease using mineral waters. These waters contain constituents such as sodium, magnesium, calcium, and iron, as well as gases like oxygen, nitrogen, carbonic acid, and sulphuretted hydrogen. Some spas use "medicated" or "aromatic" baths—such as those using pine-needle decoctions or aromatic herbs like thyme and camomile—to stimulate the peripheral nerves and cutaneous circulation.
