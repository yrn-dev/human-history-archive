# The Great Depression: The Global Economic Collapse of the 1930s

## Overview
The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world. While often associated primarily with the United States, it was a global phenomenon that fundamentally reshaped the relationship between governments and their economies. 

The crisis began with the stock market crash of October 1929, but historians and economists argue that the "crash" was a symptom rather than the sole cause. Underlying vulnerabilities included agricultural overproduction, unsustainable consumer debt, and a fragile international banking system tied to WWI reparations. The crisis was exacerbated by policy failures, such as the Federal Reserve's decision to tighten the money supply and the passage of the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, which stifled international trade.

Recovery was a slow, uneven process. In the U.S., Franklin D. Roosevelt’s "New Deal" introduced sweeping social safety nets and regulatory frameworks. However, a central debate persists among historians: did the New Deal actually end the Depression, or did it merely mitigate the suffering until the massive industrial mobilization for World War II provided the necessary stimulus for full recovery?

## Key Facts
*   **Bank Failures:** Thousands of banks collapsed as panicked depositors attempted to withdraw their savings simultaneously (bank runs), wiping out the life savings of millions.
*   **The Dust Bowl:** An environmental disaster struck the American Great Plains, where severe drought and poor farming practices led to massive soil erosion, forcing "Okies" to migrate west.
*   **Deflationary Spiral:** Prices for goods and services plummeted, which sounds positive but actually discouraged spending and investment, as people waited for prices to drop further.
*   **Global Impact:** Germany, already struggling with war debts, saw its economy collapse, creating the political instability that contributed to the rise of the Nazi Party.

## Did You Know?
1. **The "Hoovervilles":** Shanty towns built by homeless people were named "Hoovervilles" after President Herbert Hoover, whom many blamed for the crisis.
2. **Gold Standard Rigidity:** Many economists, including Milton Friedman, argued that the adherence to the Gold Standard prevented governments from expanding the money supply to fight the depression.
3. **The Bonus Army:** In 1932, WWI veterans marched on Washington D.C. to demand early payment of their service bonuses; they were forcibly removed by the U.S. Army.
