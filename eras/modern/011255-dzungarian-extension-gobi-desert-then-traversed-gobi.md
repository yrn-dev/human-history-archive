# The Gobi Desert

The Gobi is a great desert of central Asia, forming a vast interior, almost waterless space. It is situated within a great central depression of the continent that extends from the foot of the Pamir plateau in the west, through the Tarim desert, to Lop Nor and the Gobi itself. This depression begins on the east of the Gobi and extends westwards through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan.

## Geography and Boundaries
The Gobi is bounded to the north and north-west by the Yablonoi Mountains and their western extension in the Tian-shan. To the south, it is bordered by the northern face of the Tibetan plateau, and to the east by the Khingan range. The eastern portion of the desert is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia, while its western portion forms eastern or Chinese Turkestan.

The region is characterized by significant elevation. It is likely that no part of the Gobi is less than 2,000 feet above sea level. Specifically, the route across the desert between Kiakhta and the Chinese frontier never drops below 3,000 feet. While the precise positions of the mountain ridges traversing this central area are not fully known, their elevations are considerable, with some points exceeding 10,000 or 12,000 feet. In the central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, modern research suggests the land may actually be below sea level.

## Geological History and Environment
The Gobi was not always a desert; it is described as having once been a system of seas and lakes. This ancient system, along with great mountain chains, served to split Mongolian races into the Ural-Altaic and Chinese language groups. 

The desert is part of a larger hydrographic system. To the west, waters from the Pamir run past Kashgar toward the central Gobi, where they unite with streams from the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau that pass through Khotan and Yarkand. The Gobi serves as a region where local drainage is lost in swamps or deserts.

## Exploration and Mapping
The Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky conducted extensive journeys through this region. Between 1871 and 1873, he crossed the Gobi from the north to Kansu in western China. In 1876, he visited Lop Nor and discovered the Altyn Tagh range. During a later expedition in 1879, Prjevalsky crossed and named the Dzungarian extension of the Gobi desert, subsequently traversing the Gobi itself from Sachu to Hami. Sachu served as a junction point between Prjevalsky's travels and those of Krishna. 

Other explorers have contributed to the understanding of the region. Sven Hedin revealed the periodic shifting of the Lop Nor system, and both Hedin and Dr. M.A. Stein demonstrated that the sand-strewn wastes of the Turkestan desert once housed highly civilized centers of industry and Buddhist art.

## Historical Traversals
The Gobi has been crossed by various travelers throughout history. In the 13th century, three Venetians (including Marco Polo) traveled from the Plateau of Pamir, descending upon Kashgar and proceeding through Khotan and the vicinity of Lake Lob before crossing the Great Gobi Desert to reach Tangut in the extreme northwest of China.

Other historical routes involved crossing the Gobi via a northern route that passed along the southern slopes of the Thien Shan and skirted Lake Issik Kul toward Samarkand and Tashkend.

## Cultural Beliefs
Among the nations of Central Asia, there is a belief that the earth, its interior, and the atmosphere are filled with Spiritual Beings who influence nature. Deserts and wild, uninhabited tracts where nature is displayed on a terrible scale are regarded as the primary rendezvous for evil Spirits. Consequently, the great sandy Desert of Gobi has been viewed since antiquity as a dwelling-place for malignant beings.
