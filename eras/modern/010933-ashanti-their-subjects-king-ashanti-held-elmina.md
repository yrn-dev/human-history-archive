# The Ashanti Empire

The Ashanti were a powerful and conquering nation of the pure negro type, believed to be of the same race and language as the Fanti people of the coast. According to tradition, they originated from bands of fugitives driven south in the 16th or 17th century by Moslem tribes migrating from the Senegal and Niger countries. After settling in a region of impenetrable forest, the Ashanti developed a national character defined by valor.

## Social and Political Structure
The Ashanti were divided into numerous tribes, twelve of which are distinguished: the Adansi, Adjisu, Bekwai, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Juabin, Kokofu, Kumasi, Mampon, Nkwanta, Nsuta, and Ofinsu. While each tribe maintained its own king, the king of Kumasi was recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti from the beginning of the 18th century. The symbol of this paramount authority was the "golden stool."

The government was a blend of military aristocracy and monarchy. The state's social fabric was based on six great divisions or clans originally organized for war. The King's Council consisted of the chiefs of these clans and a few sub-chiefs with hereditary rights. Unless the king possessed an exceptionally strong character, the council often exercised more power than the monarch. Land was held in common by the tribes, with unallotted "stool lands" attached to the king or head chief. Socially, polygamy was practiced by those who could afford it.

## Economy and Culture
The capital, Kumasi, served as a center of culture and administration. Before its partial destruction by the British in 1874, the city featured broad, regular streets—the main avenue being 70 yards wide—and a large market-place. The king's palace was built of red sandstone, believed to be modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina. The city also contained a "Spirit House," which served as the place of execution. While Kumasi was the political center, Kintampo served as the commercial capital and a meeting point for caravan routes from the east and north.

The Ashanti demonstrated high skill in pottery, gold work, and weaving cotton. In 1874, masks of beaten gold and elaborate goldsmith's work showing Moorish influence were found in the royal palace. Economically, the region relied on gold mining—particularly in Obuassi—and the export of rubber, gum copal, and kola nuts. Cocoa plantations were established in the eastern districts after 1905, and tobacco was grown in the north.

The majority of the population were spirit-worshippers, generally showing a repugnance to Islam, though some Mahommedan traders lived in the region and some converts to Christianity were gained through Wesleyan and Basel missionaries.

## Conflict with the British and Coast Tribes
The Ashanti rose to power under Osai Tutu, who extended the empire through conquests of Banna, Gaman, and Denkera. Later, under Osai Tutu Quamina (king c. 1800), the Ashanti invaded Fantiland after the Fanti refused to surrender fugitive Assin chiefs. This led to the destruction of a town near Anamabo and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants. Following this, Colonel G. Torrane agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

Relations remained volatile throughout the 19th century. In 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy at Essamako, killing the governor and eight Europeans; the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. However, a defeat at Dodowah in 1826 caused Ashanti power over coast tribes to wane. By 1831, the king was forced to pay 600 oz. of gold to George Maclean to purchase peace.

## The War of 1873-74 and Subsequent Decline
The war of 1873-74 was triggered by two main events: the captivity of four Europeans (including members of the Basel mission and a French trader) and the transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti regarded the Elmina as their subjects and the king of Ashanti held the "custom-note," receiving an annual payment from the Dutch as ground rent. The British occupation of this territory was greatly resented.

On January 22, 1873, Ashanti forces invaded the British protectorate, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition led by Sir Garnet Wolseley. Wolseley marched to Kumasi and blew up the royal palace on February 6, 1874.

Following the war, the Ashanti deposed King Kofi Karikari in favor of his brother Mensa, whose rule was characterized by tyranny and human sacrifice. After a civil war, Prince Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III) became king in 1888. However, after Prempeh declined to accept a British protectorate and a resident at Kumasi, he was deported in 1896.

## Later Resistance and Integration
After Prempeh's deportation, the British installed Captain Donald W. Stewart as resident at Kumasi. In 1900, a rebellion broke out involving the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes, sparked in part by Governor Sir F. Hodgson's demand for the golden stool and an annual payment of 4,000 oz. of gold. The rebels attacked a party of Hausa and later the British fort in Kumasi, but were repulsed by reinforcements from the Gold Coast and Lagos constabulary.

By 1903, a railway reached Kumasi from Sekondi, passing through the gold-fields. This led to a trade revival, and by 1906, Kumasi had become the primary distributing center for the entire Ashanti region.
