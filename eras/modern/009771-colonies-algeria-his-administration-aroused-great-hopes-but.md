# Colonies and Algeria

The administration of colonial territories across various empires has historically involved a complex interplay between home governments and local governance, ranging from direct imperial control to the establishment of representative institutions.

## French Administration in Algeria
The subjugation of Algeria involved significant military and administrative efforts. Bugeaud, who initially disapproved of the conquest, eventually advocated for the swift and lasting subjugation of the region. After serving in a subordinate capacity and initiating a war of flying columns, Bugeaud was nominated governor-general of Algeria in 1840. He implemented his system of flying columns in early 1841, which successfully drove back the forces of Abd-el-Kader. 

During his tenure, Bugeaud focused on securing French positions by constructing roads in 1842 and studying the agricultural characteristics of the land. Under his régime, the population of French colonists increased from 17,000 to 100,000. Bugeaud retired from Algeria in July 1846 following differences with the home government regarding the future government of the province.

In a later period, Prince Napoleon (Napoleon Joseph Charles Paul) was appointed minister for the Colonies and Algeria in 1858. While his administration initially aroused great hopes, his focus was diverted in January 1859 due to his marriage to Princess Marie Clotilde of Savoy.

## British Colonial Governance and Evolution
In the British Empire, the crown received advice on colonial administration from the secretaries of state for India and the colonies. In 1782, the "Council of Trade and Plantations" was suppressed, leading to a period where colonies were treated simply as dependencies. However, a shift toward representative institutions occurred, beginning with Canada in 1791 under Pitt, and later extending to South Africa and Australia. Despite these institutions, these communities eventually reasserted claims for the liberty to manage their own affairs.

Specific colonial administrators played pivotal roles in regional stability. Sir Benjamin D'Urban served as governor of Antigua in 1820 and later governed Demerara and Essequibo. In 1831, he amalgamated Berbice with other counties to form the colony of British Guiana. Later, as governor of Cape Colony starting in January 1834, D'Urban oversaw the abolition of slavery and the establishment of municipal and legislative councils. He extended the colony's boundary to the Kei river to protect inhabitants from Xosa Kaffir invasions, though he was later instructed by Lord Glenelg, the secretary for the Colonies, to surrender the annexed territory.

## Regional Administrative Structures
Administrative methods varied by region and the nature of the territory:

*   **French West Africa:** Colonies were administered by a lieutenant-governor and a council of official and unofficial members. Territories were categorized as protected, annexed, or "territories of political action," and further divided into provinces or "circles," each led by an administrator with extensive powers. While native laws and customs were generally retained under French supervision, natives who chose to place themselves under French law were known as "Assimiles." Revenue was primarily derived from the capitation tax and customs.
*   **Nyasaland and Rhodesia:** The Nyasaland Protectorate was administered directly by the imperial government via a governor, assisted by an executive council and a nominated legislative council of at least three members. To the west, North Eastern and North Western Rhodesia were governed by administrators of the British South Africa Chartered Company.
*   **Other British Territories:** In Dominica, local affairs were managed by an administrator and an executive council; by 1898, the constitution was changed to a legislative council of twelve nominated members. In Cyprus, which was handed to Great Britain in 1878 while nominally remaining part of the Turkish empire, administration was handled by a high commissioner.

## Global Colonial Reach and Cooperation
The British Empire maintained a vast array of dependencies. In the Pacific, these included the Cook Islands, Howland Island, and the Phoenix Group, among others. In the Indian Ocean, holdings included the Chagos Islands, the Maldive Islands, and Rodriguez. In some regions, such as Zanzibar and parts of the Far East, native rulers retained considerable powers or land was held on lease.

During the Great War, these diverse entities—including the "great free nations" of New Zealand, Australia, Canada, and South Africa—contributed troops, money, and supplies. India provided two infantry divisions and one cavalry brigade, with native princes and chiefs placing their resources at the call of the King-Emperor.
