# Beringheld; Le Vicaire des Ardennes; Le Tartare, ou Le Retour de l'exile

*Le Centenaire, ou Les Deux Beringheld*, *Le Vicaire des Ardennes*, and *Le Tartare, ou Le Retour de l'exile* are early literary works written by the author Balzac. These novels were produced during a period of his youth and are categorized under the title *Oeuvres de jeunesse*.

## Composition and Publication
The author began his "garret-period" between 1820 and 1822, which was followed by a period of equal length spent at home. During this time, specifically in 1821, he first entered the world of print. The works *Le Centenaire, ou Les Deux Beringheld*, *Le Vicaire des Ardennes*, and *Le Tartare, ou Le Retour de l'exile* were published in 1822. They were part of a group of six novels released that year, following his initial *incunabula*—*Les Deux Hector, ou Les Deux Families bretonnes* and *Charles Pointel, ou Mon Cousin de la main gauche*.

These early novels were originally published across approximately two score volumes. To bring them to print, the author utilized several pseudonyms, including "Horace de Saint Aubin" and "Lord R'hoone" (the latter being an anagram of "Honore"). Additionally, some of these works were produced in actual collaboration with two or three other writers.

## Literary Context and Development
The archive notes that these early productions represent the "faintest dawn" of the true Balzac. Critically, none of these novels are considered good as a whole, and only a few specific parts are deemed deserving of that description, and even then only with significant qualification. Despite their lack of overall quality, they are considered essential for study; the archive suggests that it is difficult to fully apprehend the true Balzac without having studied these early works.

The *Le Vicaire des Ardennes* was later expanded upon in 1824 with the publication of a continuation titled *Annette et le criminel*.

## Cessation of Early Writing
The production of these youth works eventually ceased. This stop was not prompted by the author's own conviction regarding their poor quality—though he did not hold any serious delusions about their merit—nor was it due to a lack of financial success, as they had achieved a certain return in money. Instead, the author ceased writing these novels because he began engaging in business ventures. These activities, which proved to be the most prolonged and disastrous of his "dabblings," involved working as a printer, a type-founder, and, to some extent, a publisher. These business failures resulted in him being hampered by a load of debt.
