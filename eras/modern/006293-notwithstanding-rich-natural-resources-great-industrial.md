# Industrial and Resource Development in Global History

## The Industrial Revolution in Great Britain
The transition from traditional patch agriculture and common pasture to scientific agriculture in Great Britain coincided with a radical shift in the manufacture of commodities. During the eighteenth century, Great Britain led the world in these changes. Historically, industries, building, and manufactures were managed by small masters and craftsmen organized into guilds who worked from their own homes. While some capitalists existed who supplied materials or let out looms, there were no rich manufacturers; wealth was instead concentrated among merchants, money-lenders, and great landowners.

The Industrial Revolution marked a fundamental change in human economy as workers were collected into factories to produce goods in larger quantities through a systematic division of labour. This shift was accelerated by mechanical inventions and the introduction of power sources, beginning with water power and later steam. A pivotal moment occurred in 1765 with the construction of Watt’s steam engine. 

The cotton and woollen industries were among the first to adopt factory production. Simultaneously, the iron industry expanded by switching from charcoal to coke made from coal, causing the industry to shift from the wooded areas of Surrey and Sussex to coal districts. By 1800, the transition to large-scale production under big employers was well underway. This evolution forced the traditional craftsman to either become an employer and gain wealth or sink to the level of a mere labourer. This revolution began in Great Britain and spread globally during the nineteenth century.

## The British Empire and Global Resources
The expansion of the British Empire during the 19th century was aided by revolutions in industrial science, specifically in communication and transport. The development of railways removed the previous economic law that civilization was confined to sea-shores and river valleys. Steam and electricity allowed industry to penetrate the interiors of continents.

The empire's tropical and temperate possessions provided every field of production required for human use, including all main staples of human food and all materials for the textile industry. The empire's scale of production was significant: it accounted for one-third of the world's coal supply, one-sixth of the wheat supply, and nearly two-thirds of the gold supply. Additionally, more than one-third of the world's mining and quarrying workforce was employed within the empire.

## Industrial and Financial Trends in Germany
In Germany, a significant proportion of bank resources has been employed in industrial concerns, including some located outside the empire's boundaries. This practice, aimed at obtaining higher interest rates than those available through domestic loans or discounting bills, has been criticized by figures such as Caesar Strauss and Dr. R. Koch, the president of the Reichsbank. Dr. Koch emphasized the necessity for powerful German banks that are not connected to this type of speculative business, noting that such investments oppose traditional banking rules. Furthermore, a movement toward concentration among banks has resulted in a business model that is more industrial in character than the banking practiced in the United Kingdom.

## Regional Case Study: Bohemia
In 1900, Bohemia had a population of 6,318,280, with a density of 315 inhabitants per square mile. The population was approximately 65% Czech and 35% German. Geographically, Czechs occupied the south, south-east, and middle of the country, while Germans were concentrated in the large towns and near the borders in the west and north. There were also German-speaking enclaves in Czech districts, while Czechs tended to move into German manufacturing and mining districts.

Despite its great industrial development and rich natural resources, Bohemia experienced a steady flow of emigrants. These individuals settled in Russia, Germany, or other provinces of the monarchy, while others emigrated across the Atlantic to America.
