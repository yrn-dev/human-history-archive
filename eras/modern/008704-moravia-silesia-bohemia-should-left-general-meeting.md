# The Reunion of Moravia and Silesia to Bohemia

## Geography and Early History
Bohemia is a kingdom and crownland of Austria, serving as the principal province of the Austrian empire. It is bounded by Saxony to the northwest, Prussian Silesia to the northeast, Moravia and Lower Austria to the southeast, Upper Austria to the south, and Bavaria to the southwest. Enclosed by high mountains and situated in the center of the European continent, it acts as a borderland between the Slavonic and German worlds.

Historically, Bohemia's frontiers expanded under Boleslav II, who established rule over Bohemia, Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and the area of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. However, this empire did not endure; Boleslav III lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great, king of Poland. Later, in 1446, a general meeting of the estates of Bohemia, Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia—referred to as the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—took place, an event of exceptional importance for the country's constitutional history.

## Political Aspirations and National Movements
Following the re-establishment of European peace in 1815, national aspirations in Bohemia revived, initially through literature. By 1845, the estates of Bohemia began opposing the absolutist government of Prince Metternich in Vienna, asserting their right to vote on taxes based on the constitution of 1627. By 1847, they sought to increase town representation in the diet, tighten financial control, and introduce the Bohemian language into higher schools.

In 1848, the February revolution in Paris triggered great excitement in Prague. On March 11, a public meeting petitioned the government in Vienna for several reforms: the equal right of the Bohemian language with German in government offices, the introduction of liberal reforms, and the convocation of a general diet comprising all Bohemian lands, elected via extensive suffrage.

## The Question of Reunion
The issue of reuniting Moravia and Silesia with Bohemia became a central point of political contention. A major party, led by Rieger and Palacky, demanded the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy in its fullest extent, which specifically included Moravia and Silesia. This party insisted that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia in Prague, as previous predecessors had been, and that Bohemia hold a position in the monarchy similar to that of Hungary.

This movement was supported by all Czechs and several great nobles of German descent, including Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg (cardinal archbishop of Prague), Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz, and Count Leo Thun. These nobles hoped a self-governing kingdom would preserve their power against German Liberals. While Czechs in Moravia and some nobles led by Count Belcredi supported these claims, opposition was strong in other areas. In Bruenn, there was a strong German party, and in Silesia, Germans held a considerable majority. Because of this, and the presence of a large Polish element that did not support the Czechs, the diet refused to recognize the Bohemians' claims.

## Imperial Response and Later Developments
On April 8, 1848, Emperor Ferdinand responded to a second deputation by declaring that equality of rights would be secured for both nationalities in Bohemia. Regarding the specific territorial claims, he declared that the question of the reunion of Moravia and Silesia to Bohemia should be left to a general meeting of representatives of all parts of Austria. He also promised to convoke a new meeting of the estates of Bohemia that would include representatives from the principal towns.

Later, in 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reach an agreement between Bohemia and the Habsburg empire. An imperial message on September 14, 1871, recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. Although this attempt failed due to a subsequent government with a pronounced German tendency in 1872, the issue remained relevant. In 1879, under Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe, Bohemian representatives returned to the parliament of Vienna. They did so while explicitly stating that their participation was without prejudice to their view that Bohemia, together with Moravia and Silesia, constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary.
