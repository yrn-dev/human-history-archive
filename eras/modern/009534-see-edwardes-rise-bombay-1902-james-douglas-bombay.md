# Bombay

## Early History and Acquisition
The English presence in the Bombay presidency began in 1618 with the establishment of a factory at Surat by the East India Company, which operated under a charter from Emperor Jahangir. Efforts to acquire the island of Bombay began in 1626 through an unsuccessful joint attempt by the English and Dutch. Later, in 1653, proposals were made to purchase the island from the Portuguese. 

Bombay was eventually ceded to the English crown in 1661 as part of the dower for the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. Due to the low value placed on the acquisition by England and the unsuccessful administration of crown officers, the island was transferred to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10. This transfer included powers for the administration of justice and defense. A European regiment was enrolled, and fortifications were built, which successfully deterred a Dutch attack in 1673.

## Administrative Evolution and Conflict
In 1687, Bombay was designated as the head of all East India Company possessions in India. However, this status changed in 1753 when the government of Bombay became subordinate to that of Calcutta. 

The region experienced significant conflict with the Mahratta power. The first collision occurred in 1774, leading to the treaty of Salbai in 1782, which resulted in the cession of Salsette to the British and the handover of Broach to Sindhia. The second Mahratta war, ending in 1803, brought further expansions; following the annexation of Surat in 1800, the East India Company received the districts of Kaira and Broach.

## Urban Development and Economic Growth
The annexation of the Deccan and the British victory over the Mahrattas initiated a period of unrestricted development. The city's growth was further fostered by Mountstuart Elphinstone in 1819. Population figures reflect this rapid expansion: in 1838, the population was 236,000, rising to 821,000 by 1891. This growth was driven by the foundation of cotton factories, the opening of the Suez Canal, and the development of railways, with the first line completed in 1853.

The province became well-supplied with railways, most of which concentrated at Bombay City. Key systems included the Indian Midland, Great Indian Peninsula, Rajputana-Malwa, Southern Mahratta, and Bombay, Baroda & Central India lines. By 1905, the total length of railway open for traffic under the Bombay government was 7,980 miles (excluding the system in Sind).

Prosperity was briefly interrupted in 1866-1867 by a financial crisis caused by falling cotton prices following the end of the American war. Despite this, the city recovered and reached a peak of prosperity by 1891.

## Public Health and Natural Disasters
The late 19th and early 20th centuries were marked by plague and famine. Bubonic plague first appeared in Bombay City in September 1896. Between that date and the end of October 1902, over 531,000 deaths occurred. The disease continued to cause heavy mortality; in 1903-1904, there were 426,387 cases and 316,523 deaths, followed by 285,897 cases and 212,948 deaths in 1904-1905. 

Government efforts to fight the disease included inoculation and segregation. Segregation was often misunderstood by the public, leading to outbreaks of violence. In March 1898, a riot started by Mahommedan weavers in Bombay resulted in several fatal injuries to Europeans.

Additionally, the region suffered two famines within three years. A failure of the monsoon in 1896 affected 7 million people across 46,000 square miles in the Deccan. Famine relief expenditure totaled Rs. 1,28,000,000, with 301,056 persons on relief in September 1897. A second monsoon failure occurred in Gujarat in 1899.

## Notable Figures and Institutions
Several prominent individuals are associated with the city. Sir George Christopher Molesworth Birdwood (1832- ), born in Belgaum, served in the Bombay Medical Service starting in 1854. He held numerous roles, including sheriff of Bombay, registrar of the university, curator of the museum, and professor at Grant Medical College. He also edited the Bombay *Saturday Review* and helped convert the *Standard* into the *Times of India*.

The city was also a center for linguistic study. J.T. Molesworth published a dictionary of Marathi in Bombay in 1857, and Navalkar published a grammar in 1894. Monsenhor S.R. Dalgado authored a *Konkan-Portuguese Dictionary* in Bombay in 1893.
