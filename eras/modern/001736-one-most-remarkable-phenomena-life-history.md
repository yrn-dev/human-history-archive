# Phenomena in Life-History and Natural Science

The study of phenomena across biological, historical, and philosophical contexts reveals a wide array of complex processes, ranging from the microscopic life-cycles of organisms to the broad movements of human civilization and the theoretical frameworks used to explain them.

## Biological and Physiological Phenomena

In the biological sciences, which deal with phenomena manifested by living matter, a sharp distinction is made between biological and abiological phenomena. While living matter possesses properties that distinguish it absolutely from not-living matter, the phenomena of life—when considered apart from consciousness—depend on the same physical and chemical forces active in the rest of the world. A mass of living protoplasm is described as a molecular machine of great complexity; its vital phenomena are determined by its construction and the energy supplied to it.

Within specific biological groups, certain remarkable phenomena occur. In the life-history of the Schizomycetes, the formation of the zoogloea stage is particularly notable. This stage, which corresponds to the "palmella" condition of lower Algae, involves the creation of a gelatinous matrix—composed of either cellulose or a proteid substance—that encloses innumerable bacteria, cocci, or other elements. This matrix is formed by the active division of mother-cells whose progeny secrete cell-wall substance that absorbs water. The zoogloea can appear as membranes on a medium's surface or as submerged branched masses and irregular clumps. Depending on the species and conditions, these can take various forms, including lamellar, filamentous, ovoid, reticular, spherical, or fruiticose. If iron is present in the medium, older zoogloeae may precipitate oxide of iron within the matrix.

Similarly, fungi exhibit specialized biological phenomena due to their dependence on organic supplies of nitrogen and carbon. While their physiology is not fundamentally different from ordinary plant-cells (requiring water, oxygen, and salts of phosphorus, sulphur, magnesium, and potassium), their life-actions are restricted and specialized.

## Electric Phenomena in Living Beings

The study of electric phenomena in living beings has seen significant advancement through the work of Du Bois-Reymond. He proposed a theory that living tissue, such as muscle, is composed of electric molecules with specific properties, and that the overall electric behavior of the muscle results from the behavior of these native molecules. This theory was challenged by Ludimar Hermann, who argued that untouched living tissue at rest is isoelectric in substance and does not contain electric currents. Hermann maintained that electric phenomena are instead caused by internal molecular changes linked to injury or activity.

## Historical and Social Phenomena

In the context of human history, phenomena are often viewed as the intersection of legendary associations and political actions. In ancient Greece, for example, the stability and security of the Athenian public were connected to the domiciliation of gods in the soil, a belief that explained their terror during the Peloponnesian war when the Hermæ statues were mutilated. Similarly, the Spartan king's habit of performing public sacrifices immediately before sunrise was rooted in the Homeric conception of Zeus awakening from the side of "white-armed Hêrê."

Other historical phenomena are marked by significant political shifts. The demolition of the Dionysian Bastile by Timoleon and the subsequent erection of a building for the administration of justice is cited as one of the most impressive phenomena in Grecian history, serving as a proof of prudence and disinterested patriotism. Additionally, the poems of Solon are noted for containing records of public and social phenomena that existed prior to his time.

## Philosophical Interpretations of Phenomena

Philosophers have sought to categorize and give meaning to these various observations. Lotze argued that the meaning of all phenomena is a philosophical problem. He proposed that the world consists of three regions: facts, laws, and standards of value. In his view, the world of facts is the field where higher moral and aesthetical values are realized through the means of laws, a union made intelligible through a personal Deity.

Lucretius also contemplated all phenomena—both material and moral—as part of one great organic whole termed "Natura daedala rerum." He applied speculative ideas to interpret human life and nature, uniting the concepts of law, order, and ever-changing life within this framework.
