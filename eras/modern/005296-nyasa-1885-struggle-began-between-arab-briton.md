# Nyasa

The region surrounding Lake Nyasa in South Central Africa is characterized by a history of European exploration, linguistic diversity, and a period of conflict between British and Arab interests during the late 19th century.

## Geography and Natural Resources
The territory is primarily composed of high plateau land with an average altitude of 3,500 feet. Lake Nyasa itself sits at an elevation of 1,700 feet, measuring approximately 350 miles in length and between 15 and 40 miles in breadth. The highest point in the protectorate is Mount Mlanje, located in the extreme south-eastern corner, which reaches 9,683 feet. Other significant elevations include the Shiré Highlands, Angoniland, the Nyika Plateau, and the Nyasa-Tanganyika Plateau.

The region is rich in minerals. Iron ore and mica are found almost universally. Gold is present in the mountainous region west of Lake Nyasa, the Shiré Highlands, and the Nyasa-Zambezi waterparting. Silver and lead are also found in the Nyasa-Zambezi waterparting. Other resources include graphite in the western basin of Lake Nyasa, copper in the west Nyasa region, limestone in the Shiré basin, and malachite in North Western Rhodesia and south-west Angoniland. Coal is located in the west and north Nyasa districts, particularly in the Rukuru valley's Karroo sandstones.

## Inhabitants and Languages
The indigenous population is of the Negro type, specifically belonging to the Bantu linguistic family. While the general population is black or chocolate-coloured, the physique of the native Awankonde at the north end of Lake Nyasa recalls that of the Nilotic negro. There are legends of unmixed Bushman tribes in mountain districts such as Mount Mlanje, and Bushman stone implements have been discovered at the south end of Lake Tanganyika. Arabs from Zanzibar began settling in the country starting in the beginning of the 19th century.

The region exhibits a complex array of Bantu dialects:
*   **North Nyasa (Nkonde) group:** Includes dialects such as *Ici-wandia* and *Iki-nyikiusa* on the north-west and north coasts of the lake.
*   **Yao genus:** Stretches from the north-east shores of Lake Nyasa to the south-east corner and the region east of the Shire river.
*   **Tumbuka section:** Occupies the area between the Upper Luangwa river and the western shores of Lake Nyasa, including the *Henga* and *A-tonga* peoples.
*   **Other groups:** The *Kinga* speech is found in the Livingstone mountains, and the *Bemba* (or *Emba*) dialects occupy the ground between Lake Mweru, the Upper Chambezi river, and the south-west and south coasts of Tanganyika.

## European Exploration and Colonial Conflict
European presence began with vague Portuguese wanderings in the 16th and 17th centuries, followed by the exploration of Dr. F.J.M. de Lacerda e Almeida. However, the modern history of the region is tied to David Livingstone, who discovered Lake Nyasa in 1859. Livingstone's journeys to Lake Mweru, Lake Bangweulu, and the south end of Tanganyika established British interests in the area.

Following Livingstone's death in 1873, missionary societies and Scottish settlers entered the region to begin evangelization and oppose Portuguese attempts to extend their influence from the Zambezi and Moçambique provinces. These missionary societies eventually formed the African Lakes Trading Corporation.

This corporation entered into a conflict with Arabs established at the north end of Lake Nyasa. A struggle for the possession of the country between Britons and Arabs began around 1885 and lasted until 1896. During this "unofficial war," the African Lakes Corporation utilized volunteers, including Mr. Alfred Sharpe and Captain F.D. Lugard, both of whom were wounded in operations that did not achieve complete success. In 1889, Mr. H.H. Johnston was dispatched to arrange a settlement between the Arabs and the Corporation and to protect friendly native chiefs from Portuguese aggression. These efforts resulted in the creation of a British sphere of influence and protectorate north of the Zambezi.

## Infrastructure and Settlements
The government capital of the protectorate is Zomba. The principal European town is Blantyre, founded in 1876 by the Church of Scotland mission and named after the birthplace of David Livingstone. Other notable settlements include Port Herald, Chiromo, Fort Anderson, Fort Johnston, and Karonga.

Transportation is facilitated by light-draught steamers from the Chinde mouth of the Zambezi to Port Herald, though barges are required during the dry season (April to November). A railway connects Port Herald to Blantyre. Additionally, the "Cape to Cairo" railway reached the Broken Hill mine in 1907 and the Belgian Congo frontier in 1909. Communication is further supported by the African trans-continental telegraph line founded by Cecil Rhodes, with a branch extending from Lake Nyasa to Fort Jameson.
