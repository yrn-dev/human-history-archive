# BONDAGER

## Definition and Usage
The term "bondager" is a word used generally to describe a servant. However, it has a specific application in Northumberland and the south of Scotland. In these regions, a bondager refers to a female outworker. This arrangement occurs when a married farm-labourer lives in a cottage attached to a farm; as a condition of his tenancy, the labourer undertakes to supply the bondager for field-labour. In some instances, the labourer is also responsible for providing board and lodging for the bondager. The system originated due to a dearth of field-labour.

## Etymological Origins
The term "bondager" is a trace of the early meaning of "bondage." While "bondage" is currently used as a synonym for slavery, it properly meant "tenure in villeinage." This shift in meaning resulted from an early confusion between two different roots:

*   **The root of "bind":** The word "bond," meaning "that which binds," is a phonetic variant of "band" and is derived from the Teutonic root *bindan*.
*   **The root of "dwell":** The obsolete word "bond" originally referred to a householder. This term is derived from the Old English *bonda* (seen in the word "husband") and the root of the verb *bua*, meaning to dwell or to have a house. This is cognate with the English "boor" and the German *Bauer*, and is related to the Latin *colere*.

In the laws of Canute, the "bond" (householder) was considered equal to the Old English *ceorl* (churl). Following the Norman Conquest, as the position of the churl became less free, the "bond" approximated the status of the "villein," and subsequently the "serf." The transition of the term toward the concept of serfdom and slavery was caused by the aforementioned confusion between the "bond" of the householder and the "bond" of binding.
