# Pan-German Radicals (Wolf Group)

The Pan-German radicals, specifically the group identified as the Wolf group, were a political faction within the broader landscape of German liberal and nationalist politics in Austria.

## Parliamentary Representation
According to archive records detailing party distributions, the Pan-German radicals of the Wolf group held 13 seats. They were categorized under the broader heading of German Liberals, alongside other groups such as the Populists (29 seats) and Progressives (15 seats). Additionally, the records note the presence of three unattached Pan-Germans.

## Context of German Nationalism in Austria
The Wolf group existed within a political environment where German opposition became increasingly racial in character, prioritizing the defense of German nationality. This movement, which can be traced back to 1870, originated among young Germans—particularly at the university of Vienna through a *Leseverein* (reading club)—who viewed the preservation and extension of their nationality as their sole objective and expressed little concern for constitutionalism.

Key characteristics of this broader nationalist movement included:
* **External Influence:** The movement was strongly influenced by figures outside of Austria, viewing Bismarck as a national hero and celebrating the anniversary of Sedan as a political festival.
* **Political Goals:** Representatives of this movement, such as Herr von Schoenerer, openly declared that Germans looked forward to union with the German empire.
* **Language Demands:** By 1881, various German opposition parties formed a club called the United Left, which demanded that German be expressly recognized as the official language and asserted that the defense of the Germans' position was a condition for the existence of the state.

## Political Conflict and Obstruction
The German Nationalists and Radicals were characterized by a willingness to use obstructionist tactics in parliament. A notable instance occurred in response to the language ordinances published by Badeni in May 1897. These ordinances mandated that government correspondence in Bohemia be conducted in the language in which it was first introduced and required a Czech language examination for government appointments after 1903. 

In response to these measures, which the Germans viewed with fear, the Nationalists and Radicals declared that no business would be conducted until the ordinances were repealed and Badeni was dismissed. This led to repeated motions to impeach ministers and the eventual proroguing of parliament in June, as no business was transacted.
