# Opsonic Action and Phagocytosis

Opsonic action refers to the effect a serum has on bacteria to make them more susceptible to phagocytosis, which is the process by which white corpuscles of the blood (leucocytes) ingest and destroy bacteria. This process involves two primary factors: the ingestion of the bacteria by the cells and the subsequent intracellular digestion. If either factor is absent or interfered with, phagocytosis fails as a means of defense.

## Demonstration and Measurement
Opsonic action can be demonstrated outside the body by creating a mixture consisting of a suspension of a specific bacterium, the serum being tested, and leucocytes from a normal person or animal. This mixture is placed in a thin capillary tube and incubated for half an hour at 37°C. Following incubation, a film preparation is made on a glass slide, stained, and examined microscopically.

To determine the opsonic power, the number of bacteria contained within a sample of leucocytes (for example, fifty) is counted to find an average. In cases of disease, a control using normal serum is conducted under identical conditions. The "phagocytic index" is then calculated by dividing the average number of bacteria found in the leucocytes of the tested case by the number found using the normal serum.

## Mechanisms of Action
Research by Wright and Douglas indicated that phagocytosis occurs when a small amount of normal serum is present, but is absent when normal salt solution is used instead. This suggests that serum contains substances that make organisms susceptible to phagocytosis. These substances act by combining with the organisms and producing an alteration in them, though they have no direct action on the leucocytes themselves.

The opsonin found in normal serum is highly labile and is rapidly destroyed when heated to 55°C. Serum heated to this temperature has practically no more effect on aiding phagocytosis than a normal salt solution. However, serum from an animal immunized against a specific bacterium possesses a special action in bringing about the phagocytosis of that organism, and this specific property is retained even after the serum is heated to 55°C. Consequently, it is generally accepted that there are at least two distinct classes of substances involved: thermostable immune opsonins, which develop as a result of active immunity, and the more labile opsonins of normal serum.

## Phagocytosis and Immunity
Metchnikoff observed that phagocytosis is present in animals immune to a given organism but is deficient or absent in susceptible animals. He further noted that the development of artificial immunity is accompanied by the appearance of phagocytosis. Additionally, when an anti-serum is injected into an animal, phagocytes that were previously indifferent may move toward and destroy bacteria.

The increased ingestion of bacteria seen in active immunity is attributed to the presence of immune opsonins in the serum, which act as true anti-substances. The apparent increase in leucocyte activity is therefore due to the preliminary effect of these opsonins on the bacteria, rather than an "education" of the phagocytes or an increase in the inherent digestive or ingestive activity of the cells.

## Related Biological Processes
Phagocytosis is often aided by chemiotaxis, which is the sensitiveness of leucocytes (and other unicellular organisms) to chemical substances in their surroundings. This allows cells to move toward a higher concentration of a substance (positive chemiotaxis) or away from it (negative chemiotaxis). Variations in chemiotaxis toward different organisms may depend on the opsonic content of the serum.

While phagocytosis is a significant part of the natural power to destroy bacteria, it is not regarded as the essence of immunity, but rather as evidence of its existence. Other factors influence the destruction of bacteria, such as the digestive powers of the leucocytes, which vary by animal species, and the direct bactericidal action of blood serum, which also varies between animals. For example, the serum of a white rat (immune to anthrax) kills the anthrax bacillus, whereas the serum of a guinea-pig (susceptible to anthrax) does not.
