# R. D. Hampden and the 1832 Bampton Lectures

## The Bampton Lectures
The Bampton lectures are a series of annual lectures delivered at the University Church in Oxford. They were established by the will of John Bampton (c. 1690–1751), an English divine and former canon of Salisbury who graduated with an M.A. from Trinity College, Oxford, in 1712. Bampton's will mandated that eight lectures be delivered each year on Sunday mornings during full term, specifically between the start of the last month of Lent term and the end of the third week of Act term.

The purpose of these lectures is to address specific theological themes, including:
* The confirmation and establishment of the Christian faith and the confutation of schismatics and heretics.
* The divine authority of the Holy Scriptures.
* The authority of the writings of the primitive fathers regarding the faith and practice of the primitive Church.
* The divinity of the Holy Ghost and the divinity of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ.
* The articles of the Christian faith as they are contained in the Nicene and Apostles' Creeds.

## Requirements and Administration
To be eligible to serve as a lecturer, an individual must hold at least a Master of Arts degree from either Cambridge or Oxford. Historically, the lecturer was chosen annually on the fourth Tuesday of Easter term by the heads of colleges. The regulations stipulate that no individual may be chosen to deliver the lectures a second time. 

The endowment for the position provides £120 for each lecturer, and there is a requirement that the lectures be published within two months of their delivery. While the series began in 1780, the frequency of elections changed in 1895 to alternate years due to a depreciation of the fund's revenue.

## R. D. Hampden's 1832 Contribution
R. D. Hampden was one of the scholars appointed to this series, delivering his lectures in 1832. His contribution to the archive of these theological discourses is titled *The Scholastic Philosophy in relation to Christian Theology*. 

Hampden is listed among a succession of notable lecturers who have addressed the University Church, including Heber (1815), R. Whately (1822), E. M. Goulburn (1850), H. L. Mansel (1858), H. P. Liddon (1866), E. Hatch (1880), C. Bigg (1886), C. Gore (1891), W. Sanday (1893), J. R. Illingworth (1894), and W. R. Inge (1899).

## Context of Scholastic Philosophy
The subject of Hampden's 1832 lectures, Scholastic philosophy, refers to a tradition of ecclesiastical philosophy. In the period following the dissolution of the school of philosophy at Athens in 529, philosophical activity often involved using "the old philosophy," such as that of Aristotle, to systematize Christian doctrine. 

The development of this tradition saw various stages and schools:
* **Foundations:** John of Damascus is credited as the real founder of medieval ecclesiastical philosophy, though the independent progress of this field was arrested due to his early attainment of canonical authority.
* **Medieval Schools:** Later scholasticism included the Scotist type of theology and the nominalist system of William of Occam. Gabriel Biel (d. 1495) was a famous professor of the nominalist system.
* **Key Figures:** Thomas Aquinas is noted as a primary scholastic of the West, a distinction the Byzantine period does not share. Other significant contributors include Bonaventura, who provided reasoned decisions on scholastic questions such as the *intellectus agens*, matter, and universals.
* **Integration:** Earlier figures like Gregory, the Fourth Doctor of the Latin Church, served as links between epochs by bringing the dogmatic theology of Augustine, Ambrose, and Tertullian into relation with later Scholastic speculation.
