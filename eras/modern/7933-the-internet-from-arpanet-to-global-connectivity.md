# The Internet: From ARPANET to Global Connectivity

## Overview
The evolution of the internet is not the story of a single invention, but rather a decades-long convergence of military strategy, academic collaboration, and commercial ambition. It began during the Cold War as a project of the U.S. Department of Defense’s Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA). The primary goal was to create a decentralized communication network that could withstand partial destruction—such as a nuclear strike—by utilizing "packet switching," a method of breaking data into small chunks that could travel via multiple paths to a destination.

By the 1970s and 80s, the focus shifted from mere connectivity to standardization. The development of TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol) by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn allowed disparate networks to "talk" to one another, effectively creating a "network of networks." However, the internet remained a tool for specialists until 1989, when Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web at CERN. By introducing HTML, HTTP, and the first web browser, Berners-Lee layered a user-friendly graphical interface over the existing infrastructure, triggering the explosion of global connectivity in the 1990s.

Historians often debate the "origin story" of the internet, specifically whether it was a deliberate attempt to survive a nuclear war or simply a pragmatic effort to allow researchers to share expensive computing resources. While the "survivability" narrative is popular, many early engineers argue that resource sharing was the primary driver.

## Key Facts
*   **Packet Switching:** Unlike traditional circuit-switching (used in telephony), packet switching allows data to be routed dynamically, ensuring efficiency and resilience.
*   **The DNS Revolution:** In 1983, the Domain Name System (DNS) was introduced, replacing the need for users to memorize numerical IP addresses with human-readable names (e.g., .com, .org).
*   **Decentralization:** The internet has no central "off switch" or governing body; it is a voluntary federation of networks governed by protocols.

## Did You Know?
1. **The First Message:** The first message sent over ARPANET in 1969 was intended to be "LOGIN," but the system crashed after the first two letters, making "LO" the first transmitted word.
2. **CERN's Gift:** In 1993, CERN placed the World Wide Web software in the public domain, ensuring it remained free and open for everyone to build upon.
3. **The "Internet" vs. "Web":** The internet is the hardware and protocols (the tracks), while the World Wide Web is the collection of pages and documents accessed via those protocols (the train).
