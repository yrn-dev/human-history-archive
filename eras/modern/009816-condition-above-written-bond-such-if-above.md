# Bond (English Law)

In English law, a bond is defined as an obligation by deed. Its primary design is to ensure that the party giving the bond, known as the obligor, fulfills a specific requirement, such as paying a sum of money or either performing or refraining from a particular act. To achieve this, the obligor binds himself to the obligee (the recipient) in a penalty.

## The Condition of Defeasance
A bond typically includes an added condition that allows the bond to be rendered void if the obligor meets the specified requirements. This condition is referred to as the "defeasance" because it undoes or defeats the bond. In a common money bond, the obligor may bind himself to a penalty (for example, £2000), but the condition of the bond stipulates that if the obligor, or his heirs, executors, or administrators, pays a lesser sum (usually half the penalty, such as £1000) plus interest at a specified annual rate without deduction by a certain date, the bond becomes void. If these conditions are not met, the bond remains in full force.

## Applications and Security
While often used for monetary payments, bonds are utilized to secure the performance of various other matters. Examples include:
* Guaranteeing the fidelity of a rent collector, a clerk, or an individual in an office of public trust.
* Ensuring a building contract is carried out.
* Securing that an intended husband settles a sum on his wife should she survive him.
* Ensuring a rival business is not operated by the obligor outside of specific limits of space and time.

Although covenants have largely superseded bonds in the practice of conveyancers due to convenience, bonds remain preferable in certain instances. For example, in a bond securing an annuity, a default allows for a judgment to be entered for the penalty. This stands as security for future payments, removing the need to bring a new action for every individual payment.

## Legal Remedies and Forfeiture
If the condition of the bond is not performed—meaning the obligor fails to pay the money by the stipulated day or fails to do or refrain from the specified act—the bond becomes absolute at law or forfeit. This charges both the obligor and his estate, as noted in section 59 of the Conveyancing Act 1881.

Historically, a forfeit bond allowed the entire penalty to be recovered at law, and payment made after the due date (*post diem*) could not be used as a defense. However, the court of chancery intervened to prevent oppression, viewing the penalty as a form of pledge to secure the bona fide repayment of the advanced sum rather than the substance of the debt. The court would only permit the recovery of the principal, interest, and expenses. This equitable relief was later given statutory recognition by an act of 1705, which stipulated that for common money bonds, payment of the lesser sum, interest, and costs constitutes full satisfaction.

## Breach of Special Conditions
For bonds involving special conditions, the remedy for the obligee is prescribed by an act of 1696, with procedures preserved by the Judicature Act. The obligee specifies the breaches, damages are assessed, and the court enters a stay of execution once the obligor pays those damages into the court.

A recurring difficulty for the courts is determining whether the sum in a special condition bond is a "true penalty" or "liquidated damages." If the parties have agreed upon the damages for a breach, the court will not interfere. This principle was established in the leading case of *Kemble v. Farren* (1829).

## Procedural Elements
A bond is typically signed, sealed, and delivered by the obligor in the presence of a witness. Recitals are often added to the document to explain the specific circumstances under which the bond was given. Since the date of the Judicature Act, an obligee of a common money bond can obtain a summary judgment by specially endorsing his writ under O. iii R. 6.
