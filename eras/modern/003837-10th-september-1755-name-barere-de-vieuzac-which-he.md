# Bertrand Barere de Vieuzac

## Early Life and Education
Bertrand Barere de Vieuzac was born on the 10th of September 1755 in Tarbes, Gascony. He assumed the name "Barere de Vieuzac" from a small fief that belonged to his father, who was a lawyer at Vieuzac. Barere continued to use this name long after the renunciation of feudal rights occurred on the 4th of August. 

He began his professional career in 1770, practicing as an advocate at the parlement of Toulouse. He quickly developed a reputation as an orator, and his flowing and brilliant style as an essay writer led to his election as a member of the Academy of Floral Games of Toulouse in 1788. Barere married at the age of thirty.

## Political Ascent and the States-General
In 1788, Barere made his first visit to Paris. The following year, in 1789, he was elected as a deputy to the states-general by the estates of Bigorre, with the assembly meeting in May. During this period, his liberal opinions, social qualities, manners, and personal appearance helped him establish a good standing among the various provincial deputies in Paris.

Initially, Barere attached himself to the constitutional party. While he was less known as a speaker within the Assembly, he gained recognition as a journalist through his paper, the *Point du Jour*. According to Aulard, the reputation of this paper was due less to its own qualities—as the reports of the National Assembly debates were dry and incomplete, though not inaccurate—and more to the fact that the painter David depicted Barere in the painting "Oath in the Tennis Court," showing him kneeling in a corner and writing a report of the proceedings for posterity.

## Shift to Republicanism and Legal Career
Following the flight of the king to Varennes, Barere shifted his allegiance to the republican party. Despite this move, he maintained a connection with the duke of Orleans, serving as a tutor to the duke's natural daughter, Pamela. 

Barere's political conduct was characterized by a lack of guiding principles or conscience; his actions were regulated solely by a determination to remain on the side of the strongest. After the National Assembly closed, he was appointed as one of the judges of the newly instituted court of cassation, serving from October 1791. He later became one of the most notorious members of the French National Convention. He died in 1841.

## Historical Records and Commentary
Several sources exist regarding the life of Barere, though their accuracy varies. The *Memoires de B. Barere*, published between 1824 and 1844 by David (d'Angers) and H. Carnot, are considered false, although they contain valuable information. A separate historical notice by Carnot, published in 1842, is described as very good. 

Other academic evaluations include the works of F. A. Aulard, specifically *Les Orateurs de la Constituante* (1882) and *Les Orateurs de la Convention* (1905). Additionally, an essay on Barere written by Macaulay for the *Edinburgh Review* (vol. 79) is described as eloquent but incorrect.
