# The Austrian Monarchy: Political and Military History

The history of the Austrian monarchy is characterized by its efforts to maintain imperial tradition, manage a diverse array of territories, and navigate complex diplomatic and military conflicts across Europe.

## Political Integration and the German Question
During the mid-19th century, the Habsburg monarchy relied on its army and the prestige of imperial tradition to survive political instability. Following the victory of Radetzky at Novara, Austria was once again able to utilize force to support its policies. In May 1848, the Austrian government sent delegates from its German provinces to the parliament of united Germany meeting at Frankfort.

This created a significant dilemma regarding the monarchy's place in a united Germany. There were two primary proposals: incorporating only the German provinces or incorporating the monarchy in its entirety. The Austrian government rejected the first option, as it would have split the monarchy in half and created a double allegiance. Conversely, German patriots opposed the second option because they could not accept the inclusion of a vast non-German population. While the only viable solution was the total exclusion of Austria—which eventually led to the crown of Germany being offered to Frederick William of Prussia—German statesmen were initially unable to break from the tradition of Austrian hegemony or the shadow of the Holy Empire. Consequently, Archduke John was appointed regent while an emperor was to be elected.

## Territorial Administration and Crown Lands
The Austrian monarchy managed various regions through shifting administrative structures. The Banat, liberated from the Turks by Prince Eugene of Savoy in 1716, was under military administration until 1751, when Maria Theresa introduced civil administration. After efforts by Count Claudius Mercy to regenerate the depopulated district through the construction of roads and canals and the attraction of German settlers, the Banat was incorporated with Hungary in 1779. Following the revolution of 1848-1849, it became a distinctive Austrian crown land, before being definitely incorporated with Hungary again in 1860.

Similarly, Carniola became part of Austrian possessions by the close of the 14th century under Rudolph IV. It remained so, except for a period between 1809 and 1813 when it was incorporated into the French Illyrian Provinces, eventually becoming a separate crown land in 1849.

In Belgium, Joseph II attempted to implement a new concept of human society between 1781 and 1787. An absolutist who despised the past, he issued sovereign decrees to secularize political society and incorporate the Catholic church in Belgium into the national Austrian church. These reforms included the Edicts of Tolerance (1781-82), which permitted non-Catholics to worship under restrictions and allowed them access to public offices and craft-guilds.

## The Establishment of Dualism
In 1867, a system of dualism was established between Austria and Hungary. Negotiations were led by Beust, the chancellor of the empire and minister-president of Austria. A primary point of contention was the national debt, which exceeded 3,000 million gulden. Hungary refused responsibility for debts contracted while they were deprived of constitutional liberties, agreeing only to pay 29 1/2 million gulden annually toward the interest. Austria assumed the management of the debt and the remaining interest, which exceeded one hundred million gulden annually. To resolve financial needs, a Customs Union was formed for ten years, with expenses split 70% by Austria and 30% by Hungary.
