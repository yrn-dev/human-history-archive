# The Great Depression: The Global Economic Collapse of the 1930s

## Overview
The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world. While often associated with the U.S. stock market crash of October 1929, the Depression was a complex global phenomenon resulting from a confluence of structural weaknesses in the post-WWI economy. It was characterized by a catastrophic drop in GDP, deflation, and unprecedented unemployment rates—reaching nearly 25% in the United States.

The crisis was exacerbated by a "perfect storm" of policy failures. The Federal Reserve’s decision to tighten credit and the implementation of the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 stifled international trade, turning a domestic recession into a global depression. In response, U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt launched the "New Deal," a series of experimental programs aimed at relief, recovery, and reform.

Historians and economists continue to debate the primary cause of the collapse. The **Monetarist school**, led by Milton Friedman, argues that the Federal Reserve caused the Depression by allowing the money supply to collapse. Conversely, **Keynesian economists** argue that a collapse in aggregate demand and a lack of government spending were the primary drivers.

## Key Facts
*   **Banking Failures:** Between 1929 and 1933, thousands of banks failed, wiping out the life savings of millions of citizens because deposits were not yet insured.
*   **The Dust Bowl:** An environmental disaster in the Great Plains, caused by severe drought and poor farming practices, forced "Okies" and other migrants to flee toward California.
*   **Global Reach:** Germany was hit particularly hard due to its reliance on American loans to pay WWI reparations, a factor that contributed to the rise of the Nazi Party.
*   **The New Deal:** This era saw the creation of the FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) and the SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) to prevent future crashes.

## Did You Know?
1. **The "Bank Holiday":** Upon taking office in 1933, FDR declared a four-day national bank holiday to stop the panic-driven "runs" on banks.
2. **Deflationary Spiral:** Prices dropped so sharply that consumers stopped spending, expecting prices to fall further, which ironically deepened the economic slump.
3. **The Gold Standard:** Many historians argue that countries that abandoned the gold standard earlier (such as Great Britain in 1931) recovered faster than those that clung to it.
