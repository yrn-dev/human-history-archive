# Boric Acid and Borax

## Chemical Properties of Boric Acid
Boric acid is characterized as a weak acid. In dilute aqueous solutions, it is ionized only to a very slight extent, which results in the production of a very small quantity of hydrogen ions. 

Because boric acid is a weak acid, its salts are subject to hydrolytic dissociation when placed in an aqueous solution. This chemical property can be demonstrated using a concentrated aqueous solution of borax. If litmus is added to the solution, followed by just enough acetic acid to turn the litmus red, the subsequent addition of a large volume of water will change the color of the litmus back to blue. This occurs because the dilution of the solution causes hydrolysis, producing sodium hydroxide. As sodium hydroxide is a "strong base," it is highly ionized and produces a comparatively large amount of hydroxyl ions. This creates an excess of hydroxyl ions in the solution, resulting in an alkaline reaction that turns the litmus blue.

## Borax: Forms and Production
Borax, also known as sodium biborate or sodium pyroborate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7$), is available in commerce in two primary forms:
* **Prismatic borax** ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$), referred to as "common" borax.
* **Octahedral borax** ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$), referred to as "jewellers'" borax.

Historically, alchemists used the term "borax" in a vague manner, which differs from the specific chemical definition used today.

## Natural Occurrence and Synthesis
Prismatic borax is found naturally in several regions, including Canada, Peru, Tibet, and Transylvania. In California, a large mass of crystallized borax is located in the bed of Borax Lake near Clear Lake; this natural deposit is of sufficient purity for use by assayers without preliminary purification.

The primary supply of borax is produced from the boric acid found in Tuscany. This is manufactured via two main methods:
1. Fusing boric acid in a reverberatory furnace with half its weight of sodium carbonate, then extracting the resulting mass with warm water.
2. Gradually adding boric acid to a solution of sodium carbonate in lead-lined, steam-heated pans, then concentrating the solution until borax crystallizes.

Additionally, borax can be prepared from naturally occurring calcium borate. In this process, the calcium borate is finely divided and mixed with the necessary amount of soda ash, fused, extracted with water, and concentrated until crystallization begins.

## Other Applications of Boric Acid
Boric acid is utilized in the production of specific chemical compounds. For example, F. Raschig obtained chloroazoimide ($\text{Cl}\cdot\text{N}_3$), a colorless and highly explosive gas, by acidifying a mixture of hypochlorite and sodium azide using either boric acid or acetic acid.
