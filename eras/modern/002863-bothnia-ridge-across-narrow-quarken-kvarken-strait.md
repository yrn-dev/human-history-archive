# The Quarken and the Gulf of Bothnia

The Quarken, also referred to as the Kvarken Strait, is a narrow strait that serves as a geographical boundary within the Baltic region. It is characterized by a ridge that separates the Gulf of Bothnia from the Bothnian Sea.

## Regional Geography and Hydrography
The Baltic basin is defined by several depressions and ridges. An unbroken ridge extending from Hango in Finland to Stockholm separates the Baltic basin proper from the Aland Haf (the depression between the Aland Isles and Sweden). North of the Aland Haf, another ridge marks the southern edge of the Bothnian Sea. The Gulf of Bothnia, the most northerly division of these waters, is separated from the Bothnian Sea by the ridge crossing the narrow Quarken or Kvarken Strait.

The broader Baltic area, which includes the Kattegat and Skagerrak, covers a total area of 166,397 square miles with a volume of 6,907 cubic miles. This results in a mean depth of 36 fathoms, a depth notably less than other arms of the sea of a similar area. In the deeper southern hollows, the seabed consists of hard clay or soft grey and brown mud, while shallow banks and low coasts typically feature small pebbles and fine sand in shades of brown, yellow, or white.

The circulation of water in the Baltic is influenced by these geological features. While upper layers of water (30 to 70 metres thick) maintain similar salinity across the great basins from the Gulfs of Finland and Bothnia to Ruegen, the deeper, salter, and oxygen-deficient waters are stagnant. The horizontal extension of these deeper strata is checked by the ridges that separate the deep hollows of the Baltic from one another.

## Navigation and Ice Conditions
Navigation in the northern reaches of the Baltic is heavily dictated by seasonal ice. The northern part of the Gulf of Bothnia freezes every winter. In the southern part of the Gulf, ice covers the coasts every winter, though it rarely reaches the central part. Drifting ice typically interrupts navigation from the beginning of May until the middle of November, although the port of Hernosand has remained open throughout entire winters.

The northern Quarken experiences traversable ice every third or fourth year. In contrast, the Aland Sea may be covered with thick ice suitable for traffic during very severe winters.

## Geological History
The current form of the Baltic and the Gulf of Bothnia is the result of a series of subsidences and elevations. During glacial times, a great subsidence allowed an arm of the sea to extend across Sweden, submerging the littoral up to the Gulf of Bothnia and including what are now Lakes Malar, Hjelmar, and Vener. During this era, the waters were salty enough to support oysters.

A subsequent upheaval restricted communication with the open sea to the Danish channels, causing the waters to become fresher and leading to the disappearance of oysters, though seals and various crustaceans became acclimatized. This was followed by further elevation, transforming the Baltic into a vast freshwater lake known as the Ancylus lake (named for the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*), which covered approximately 220,000 square miles, including Lake Ladoga, much of Finland, and the present Baltic area.

A later subsidence re-established communication through the Danish channels and reintroduced salinity. During this period, the Gulf of Bothnia experienced a greater depression than the Baltic proper; deposits from this epoch are 100 metres (328 ft.) thick near Hernosand, compared to only 25 metres (82 ft.) near Gotland.

The process of elevation that gave the Baltic its present physical condition is believed to be ongoing. Isobasic lines (lines of equal rate of elevation) traced by Dr. R. Sieger indicate that while movement is almost nil along the Gulf of Finland and the axial lines of the Baltic, the amplitude of elevation increases northwards toward the Gulf of Bothnia.
