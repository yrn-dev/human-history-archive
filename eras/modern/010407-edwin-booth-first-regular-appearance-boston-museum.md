# Edwin Booth

Edwin Thomas Booth (1833–1893) was a prominent American actor born on November 13, 1833, in Belair, Maryland. He was the second son of the actor Junius Brutus Booth (1796–1852), who had been born in London and established a popular career in America after removing there in 1821.

## Early Career and Development
Edwin Booth made his first regular appearance on September 10, 1849, at the Boston Museum. He performed as Tressel in Colley Cibber's version of *Richard III*, acting alongside his father, who played the title role. At the time, Booth was described as having a lithe and graceful figure, buoyant spirits, luminous and expressive eyes, and dark hair that fell in waving curls. While his father watched him with great interest, he also showed evident disappointment; similarly, other members of the theatrical profession believed the genius of the elder Booth had not been passed to the son.

Booth's first appearance in New York took place on September 27, 1850, at the National theatre in Chatham Street, where he played the character of Wilford in *The Iron Chest*. A year later, he took his father's place in the role of Richard III during his father's illness. It was only after the death of his parent that Edwin Booth secured an unassailable position on the stage. Between 1852 and 1856, he performed in the Sandwich Islands, Australia, and California, where he captivated audiences with brilliant acting that surprised those who had known him previously in the east.

## Professional Triumphs and Management
Following his international travels, Booth's dramatic successes were widely acknowledged. His performances as Richard, Richelieu, and Hamlet were pronounced superior to those of Edwin Forrest, and his portrayal of Sir Giles Overreach in *A New Way to Pay Old Debts* surpassed that of his father.

In 1862, Booth became the manager of the Winter Garden theatre in New York. Between 1864 and 1867, he produced a series of Shakespearian productions of unexampled magnificence, including *The Merchant of Venice*, *Othello*, and *Hamlet*. In the autumn of 1864, Edwin played in *Julius Caesar* alongside his brothers, John Wilkes and Junius Brutus (1821–1853). This period of success was interrupted in 1865 when his brother, John Wilkes Booth, assassinated President Lincoln.

From 1868 to 1869, Booth organized an excellent stock company and built his own venue, Booth's theatre, located at the corner of 6th Avenue and 23rd Street in New York. He produced various plays there, including *The Winter's Tale*, *Macbeth*, *Romeo and Juliet*, *Much Ado about Nothing*, *Julius Caesar*, and *The Merchant of Venice*. Booth was noted for using the true text of Shakespeare, a reform that preceded similar movements in England by many years.

## Later Life and Legacy
Despite his professional success, Booth's management was not economical due to his confiding and generous nature. In 1874, he lost his entire fortune and the theatre he had built. He eventually accumulated wealth again through arduous toil. He demonstrated his generosity by converting his spacious residence in Gramercy Park, New York, into The Players' club for the elect of his profession and other chosen members. He donated the house, his works of art, books, and stage mementos to the club, retaining only a single apartment for himself.

Booth's repertoire was extensive, including roles such as Petruccio, Benedick, Wolsey, Shylock, Lear, Macbeth, Iago, Othello, Richard II, and Richard III. He also played Ruy Blas, Don Cesar de Bazan, and Bertuccio in Tom Taylor's *The Fool's Revenge*. He was most famous for his portrayal of Hamlet, a role for which his eloquent sensibility and beauty made him peculiarly fitted; it is probable that he played this part more often than any other actor.

Internationally, Booth visited London in 1851, 1880, and 1882, achieving brilliant success at the Haymarket theatre. In 1882, he also visited Germany, where his acting was received with high enthusiasm. His final stage appearance was as Hamlet in Brooklyn in 1891.

Booth was married twice: first in 1860 to Mary Devlin (who died in 1863) and again in 1869 to Mary F. McVicker (who died in 1881). His daughter from his first marriage, Edwina Booth Grossman, published *Edwin Booth: Recollections* in 1894. Edwin Booth died on June 7, 1893.
