# Bombay Presidency

## Physical Aspects
The Bombay Presidency consists of a long strip of land situated along the Indian Ocean, extending from the north of Mysore to the south of the Punjab. The coastline is characterized as being regular and little broken, with the exception of the peninsula of Kathiawar, which lies between the Gulfs of Cutch and Cambay. The coast is rock-bound and difficult to access. While several bays serve as fairweather ports for coasting trade, only Karwar, Marmagoa, Karachi-in-Sind, and Bombay possess harbours sufficiently land-locked to protect shipping during the south-west monsoon.

The Bombay Peninsula (or Bombay Island) stands out from a coast of lofty hills. Its harbour is characterized by precipices and rocky islands with peaks of great height. The island itself is a plain approximately 3 miles broad and 11 miles long, flanked by two parallel lines of low hills. A neck of land stretching toward the south-west shelters the harbour on its eastern side, enclosing water between 5 and 7 miles wide. To the south-west lies Back Bay, a shallow basin about 2 miles wide that runs inland for approximately 3 miles. The city of Bombay is nucleated around a fort situated on a slightly raised strip of land between the harbour and the head of Back Bay. Before the construction of the Hornby Vellard embankment, the central low-lying plain was submerged by the sea at high tide.

## Urban Centers and Infrastructure
The city of Bombay features spacious streets for European commerce and handsome native bazaars where houses rise three or four storeys with elaborately carved front work and pillars. European commercial buildings and hotels are described as being on the American scale; the Taj Mahal hotel, built by the Tata family in 1904, is noted as the most modern and palatial hotel in India. European residents typically live in large compounds or gardens, with Malabar Hill—a high ridge running into the sea—serving as a favorite suburb.

Other notable locations include Baroda, situated on the river Viswamitri. Baroda is a station on the Bombay & Baroda railway, located 245 miles north of Bombay by rail. The city features parks, widened streets, and a water supply from Ajwa lake. Kirkee (or Kirki), located 4 miles northwest of Poona city in the Poona district, serves as the principal artillery station in the presidency and houses a large ammunition factory.

The province is well-supplied with railways, most of which concentrate at Bombay City. These include the Southern Mahratta, Rajputana-Malwa, Bombay, Baroda & Central India, Indian Midland, and Great Indian Peninsula systems. The North-Western line is an exception, entering Sind from the Punjab and terminating at Karachi. As of 1905, the total length of railway under the Bombay government open for traffic was 7,980 miles, excluding the system in Sind. Regarding irrigation, the water supply of the presidency does not lend itself to large irrigation works, with the exception of Sind.

## Administration and Justice
The government of Bombay is administered by a governor in council. The governor, appointed from England, serves as president, accompanied by two ordinary members appointed by the crown from the Indian civil service. These individuals act as the executive members of the government, with each executive council member overseeing one or two government departments. Each department is staffed by a secretary, an under-secretary, an assistant secretary, and clerks.

Legislative functions are handled by a legislative council. This body consists of the governor, his executive council, and between eight and twenty other persons, at least half of whom must be non-officials. In the districts, administration is carried out by assistant collectors, collectors, and supernumerary assistants. The political administration of native states is supervised by British agents stationed at principal native courts.

Justice is administered by a high court at Bombay, which consists of seven puisne judges and a chief justice. This is supported by assistant and district judges throughout the presidency's districts.

## Military Organization
Following a 1904 re-arrangement by Lord Kitchener, the old Bombay command was replaced by the Western army corps, led by a lieutenant-general and divided into three divisions:

*   **4th Division:** Headquartered at Quetta, comprising troops in the Sind and Quetta districts.
*   **5th Division:** Headquartered at Mhow, including the Bombay district north of the Tapti and the districts of Bundelkhand, Nerbudda, Nagpur, Deesa, and Mhow. Its brigades are located at Jhansi, Jubbulpore, and Nasirabad.
*   **6th Division:** Headquartered at Poona, comprising the Bombay district south of the Tapti, the Poona district, the Dharwar and Aurungabad districts, and the Belgaum district north of the Tungabhadra. Its brigades are located at Aden, Ahmednagar, and Bombay.

## Historical Development
In 1803, the presidency was limited to Bankot, Surat, Salsette, and the islands of the harbour. By 1827, it had expanded significantly. The Gujarat districts were taken over in 1805 and enlarged in 1818, while measures for the settlement of Mahi Kantha and Kathiawar occurred between 1807 and 1820. Following the defeat and capture of Baji Rao, the last of the peshwas, in 1817-1818, the presidency incorporated large portions of his dominions, including Nasik, Poona, Belgaum, Sholapur, Dharwar, Ahmednagar, and Kaladgi. These were settled by Governor Mountstuart Elphinstone (1819-1827), who codified laws, established schools and courts, and ruled on native lines.

Further expansions included the addition of Sind (1843), Aden (1839), and the 1853 lease of the Panch Mahals from Sindhia. During the tenure of Lord Elphinstone (1853-1860), the presidency avoided a general rising during the Mutiny, though local rebellions occurred in the southern Mahratta country, Gujarat, among the Bhils, and through troop outbreaks in Ahmedabad, Kolhapur, and Karachi. Under Sir Bartle Frere (1862-1867), the demand for Indian cotton in Europe caused by the American Civil War led to a peak in agricultural prosperity and a subsequent epidemic of speculation.
