# The Ashanti Conflict of 1873-1874

The war of 1873-1874 was a significant military conflict between the Ashanti kingdom and British forces in the Gold Coast protectorate. The conflict was the culmination of long-standing tensions regarding territorial sovereignty, the treatment of captives, and the control of strategic coastal fortifications.

## Causes of the War
Two primary events served as the immediate catalysts for the outbreak of hostilities. The most significant was the transference of the Elmina fort from Dutch to British control on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti regarded the Elmina as their subjects and maintained a "custom-note," through which the king of Ashanti received an annual payment from the Dutch. While the Dutch viewed this payment as a present for trade purposes, the Ashanti considered it a ground rent for the fort. Consequently, the Ashanti viewed the British occupation of the fort as an occupation of Ashanti territory.

A secondary, minor cause involved the captivity of four Europeans. In June 1869, an Ashanti force invaded Krepi, a territory located beyond the Volta. During this incursion, they captured Mr. J. Kuhne and Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, along with Ramseyer's wife and infant son; the child died shortly thereafter due to privation. Additionally, a French trader named Monsieur M.J. Bonnat was captured at a different location. These prisoners were taken to Kumasi. Although negotiations for their release had begun, they remained prisoners at the time the sale of Elmina occurred.

## Political Transition and Preparation
The political climate in Ashanti shifted significantly in the mid-19th century. King Kwaka Dua I, who ascended the throne in 1838, was a peace-loving monarch who encouraged trade. However, tensions persisted, including an attempt by the Ashanti to reassert authority over the Fanti in 1852 and a 1863 war sparked by the British refusal to surrender a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief. Following two Ashanti victories in that conflict, the prestige of the Ashanti increased, with Kwaka Dua I famously remarking that "the bush is stronger than the cannon" despite the British use of artillery.

Following the death of Kwaka Dua I in April 1867 and a subsequent period of civil war, Kofi Karikari was enstooled. Upon taking power, Karikari declared, "My business shall be war." This marked a shift toward aggression, and preparations were made throughout the kingdom to attack the Fanti tribes. The Ashanti delayed the start of the war until their preparations were fully complete, while officials in the Gold Coast underestimated the risk of hostilities.

## The Invasion and British Response
On January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force crossed the Prah and invaded the British protectorate. The invading force defeated the Fanti, instigated disputes at Elmina, and established a camp at Mampon near Cape Coast, causing significant alarm among the local population.

In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 to fund an expedition to punish the assailants and defend the territory. Sir Garnet Wolseley was dispatched as the British administrator. Upon his landing at Cape Coast on October 2, Wolseley discovered that the Ashanti forces, having been decimated by fever and smallpox, were preparing to return to their homes. Despite this, Wolseley determined to march on Kumasi. To support this advance, Captain John Glover, the administrator of Lagos, led a force of native levies to cooperate from the east and attack the Ashanti from the rear. During this period, the enemy broke up their camp and were harassed by native levies.

## Aftermath and Subsequent Rule
The war of 1873-1874 resulted in several states that had been dependent on the Ashanti declaring their independence and seeking British protection, though the British initially refused these requests. This inaction by the colonial office eventually contributed to the reconsolidation of Ashanti power.

Shortly after the war, Kofi Karikari was deposed and replaced by his brother, Mensa, who was placed on the "golden stool," the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's reign was characterized by tyranny and repeated human sacrifices. He also largely ignored the Fomana treaty; of the 50,000 oz. of gold stipulated as an indemnity, only 4,000 oz. were paid.

Following a revolution in 1883 and the brief reign of Kwaka Dua II, a desolating civil war occurred. This ended on March 26, 1888, when Prince Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king with the sanction of a Gold Coast government officer. While Prempeh initially brought peace and prosperity, tensions resurfaced in 1893 when he closed roads to traders. This led to further British interference, as the region was included in the British sphere of influence by international agreement. Prempeh declined to treat with the governor of the Gold Coast and failed to respond to British demands to accept a protectorate and a resident at Kumasi by October 31, 1895.
