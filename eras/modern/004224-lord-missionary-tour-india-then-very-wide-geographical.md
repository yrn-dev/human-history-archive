# Missionary Activity and Christian Expansion in India

The history of missionary efforts in India encompasses a wide range of eras, from the early apostolic period to the organized educational missions of the 19th century. These activities were characterized by diverse methods, including the distribution of sacred texts, the establishment of schools, and the work of religious orders.

## Early Apostolic and Ancient Missions
According to traditional accounts, Saint Bartholomew, one of the twelve apostles, traveled to India on a missionary tour following the ascension of the Lord. At that time, "India" served as a very wide geographical designation. A story recorded in Eusebius indicates that during his time there, Bartholomew left behind a copy of the gospel of St Matthew.

In a broader historical context, the reach of Christendom extended far beyond the limits of the Roman Empire, reaching into regions such as India, Persia, Armenia, Abyssinia, Germany, and Turkestan. By the fifth century, these widely scattered congregations were viewed as one people of God and one body of Christ. This unity was maintained through active inter-communication, the free exchange of letters and messengers, and the continual movement of evangelists and missionaries. Documents, including apostolic epistles and gospels, circulated widely among these communities.

## The Role of the Jesuits
The Order of Jesuits, founded by Ignatius of Loyola, provided the church with a powerful missionary and educational organization. The Jesuits served as the chief Christian missionaries in North America and India. Their contributions were noted not only for their missionary reach but for their impact on Catholic education, with their schools being regarded for a long time as the best in Christendom.

## 19th Century Educational Missions
In the 19th century, missionary strategies in India shifted toward educational methods to reach communities that had previously remained untouched. Alexander Duff, a Scottish missionary who arrived in Calcutta in May 1830 after being twice shipwrecked, identified that Protestant missions had previously been successful primarily among outcaste and low-caste peoples, specifically in south Travancore and Tinevelly.

Duff implemented a policy of educational mission to reach Mahommedan and Hindu communities, particularly high-caste Hindus. He established an English school where secular knowledge was taught from rudiments to university standards, with the Bible serving as the center of the school's work. Duff utilized the English language as a key to Western knowledge and as an instrument for higher education. 

This approach had several significant results:
* It secured the recognition of education as a missionary agency for Christian churches.
* It allowed Christian ideas to enter the minds of high-caste Hindus.
* It influenced the government of India's educational policy. 

On March 7, 1835, a government minute was adopted stating that the British government's objective in higher education should be the promotion of European literature and science among the natives of India, and that funds for education would be best used for English education alone.

## Interactions and Debates
Missionary efforts often intersected with other religious and social movements. For example, Dayanand established *Samajes* in various parts of India, the first of which was in Bombay in 1875. Dayanand, who preferred the term "Arya" over the term "Hindu," engaged in a series of tours where he debated publicly with Christian missionaries and orthodox pundits before his death in Ajmere in 1883.
