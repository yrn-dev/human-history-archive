# BRITISH EMPIRE

The British Empire is a term used for the aggregate of territories whose inhabitants, under various forms of government, ultimately recognize the British crown as their supreme head. The use of the word "empire" in this context is primarily for convenience and is not intended to be equivalent to the despotic empires of ancient history. By 1914, the empire represented a unique political combination brought together by the railway and the steamship.

## Governance and the Central State

At the center of the imperial system is the "crowned republic" of the United British Kingdoms. This entity includes Ireland, though its inclusion occurred against the will of a considerable portion of the Irish people. The government of the empire is directed by the British Parliament, which is composed of the three united parliaments of Scotland, Ireland, and England and Wales. 

The majority of this Parliament determines the policy, quality, and headship of the ministry, largely based on considerations of British domestic politics. This ministry serves as the effective supreme government for the rest of the empire, possessing the powers to declare war and negotiate peace.

## Self-Governing States and Protectorates

Beyond the central British States, the empire includes several "crowned republics" that are practically independent and self-governing. These states exist in alliance with Great Britain, and each has a representative of the Crown appointed by the ministry currently in office. These include:
* Australia
* Canada
* New Zealand
* South Africa
* Newfoundland, which is noted as the oldest British possession, dating to 1583.

Other territories exist under different administrative arrangements. Egypt is described as an ambiguous possession; while it nominally remains part of the Turkish Empire and retains its own monarch, the Khedive, it is under almost despotic British official rule.

## The Indian Empire

The Indian Empire is characterized as an extension of the empire of the Great Mogul. The British Crown and the India Office, which operates under Parliamentary control, assumed the role previously held by the original Turkoman dynasty. This empire includes "protected" and dependent states stretching from Burmah to Baluchistan, and includes Aden.

Historically, the British administration of the region was influenced by the East India Company. The "Bengal Establishment" originally encompassed north-eastern factories from Patna in Behar to Balasore on the Orissa coast. As British conquests expanded up the rivers, the term "Bengal" was applied to all of northern India. The presidency of Bengal eventually included all British territories north of the Central Provinces, extending from the Himalayas and the Punjab to the mouths of the Brahmaputra and Ganges. In 1831, the North-Western Provinces were created.

## British East Africa

In East Africa, British rule was established following a period where Arabs and Persians had maintained political supremacy from the 8th to the 11th centuries, leading to the Zenj empire. The history of the coastal towns prior to British rule is closely linked with Zanzibar. 

The interior of British East Africa became known to Europeans in the mid-19th century through the work of Baron Karl von der Decken and German missionaries Johannes Rebmann and Ludwig Krapf. Exploration continued with the Scottish traveller Joseph Thomson, who traversed the countries east of Victoria Nyanza (including Masailand) between 1883 and 1884, and Count S. Teleki, who discovered Lakes Stefanie and Rudolf in 1888. In this region, education is primarily managed by missionary societies providing elementary education and handicrafts, though government schools for Indians and Europeans exist in Nairobi and Arab schools are found in Mombasa.
