# The Baptist World Alliance and the Baptist Movement

## The Baptist World Alliance
The Baptist World Alliance was established in 1905, coinciding with the first Baptist World Congress held in London. The nature of the Alliance is defined in its constitutional preamble, which states that the organization was formed to manifest the "essential oneness in the Lord Jesus Christ, as their God and Saviour" among churches of the Baptist order and faith globally. Its primary goals are to promote service, co-operation, and a spirit of fellowship while maintaining the independence of each individual church and avoiding the assumption of functions belonging to existing organizations. The Alliance currently includes Baptists in every quarter of the globe.

## Baptist Organizations in America
In the United States, the Baptist movement experienced sectional divisions during the 19th century. Prior to 1844, sessions of the Triennial Convention were occasionally marked by acrimonious exchanges and harsh anti-slavery utterances from Northern members directed at Southern members. A controversy regarding slavery between Richard Fuller (1804-1876) and Francis Wayland led Southern Baptists to conclude that a separate organization for missionary work was necessary. Consequently, the Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845, establishing its own Sunday-school Board and Home and Foreign Missionary Boards. While Northern and Southern Baptists remained in perfect fellowship, they continued to utilize separate annual meetings and missionary boards. In 1905, a General Baptist Convention for America was created to promote denominational *esprit de corps*, comity, and fellowship, though it was designed not to undertake administrative work or interfere with sectional organizations.

Early Baptist activity in America included the 1682 organization of a small congregation at Kittery, Maine, by Humphrey Churchwood and William Screven (1629-1713). Persecution led Screven to migrate to South Carolina, where the First Baptist church in Charleston was organized around 1684. This center became highly influential, though it nearly vanished around 1746 due to the intrusion of Socinianism and the formation of neighboring churches. The church saw a revival in 1749 under Oliver Hart (1723-1795). The Charleston Association, formed in 1751, provided strength by raising funds for the education of ministers and domestic missionary work. Between 1787 and 1812, Baptist membership in South Carolina grew from 1,620 to 11,325, a rise attributed largely to the "Separate" Baptist movement led by Marshall and Stearns. Additionally, many negro slaves became Baptists in Georgia, the Carolinas, and Virginia; while most joined white churches, some were encouraged to establish their own churches in towns such as Savannah and Richmond.

## Development in Europe and the British Colonies
The modern Baptist movement appeared in nearly every European country during the 19th century. In Roman Catholic nations, churches were established by American or English missionaries: France in 1832, Italy in 1866 and 1884, and Spain in 1888. In Russia, Finland, and Protestant countries, the movement grew without such intervention. J. G. Oncken (1800-1884) established the first church in Hamburg in 1834, leading to the formation of churches in Denmark (1839), Sweden and Holland (1848), Switzerland (1849), Norway (1860), Rumania and Austria (1869), Hungary (1871), and Bulgaria (1884).

In the British Isles, the General and Particular denominations united in 1891 under the influence of Dr. John Clifford to form the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland. This union is voluntary, and some powerful churches remain independent. "Strict" Baptists form a separate denomination, often organized into geographical associations like the Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches. These Strict Baptists are further divided into "Vessel" and "Standard" parties, named after their respective organs, the *Earthen Vessel* and *Gospel Standard*. Strict Baptists are characterized by their insistence on baptism as a condition for Christian communion and their adherence to a type of Calvinistic theology. A Strict Baptist Missionary Society, refounded in 1897, conducts work in South India.

Baptists also established a presence in British colonies, including New Zealand, South Africa, Canada, and various Australian states (New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia, South Australia, and Tasmania). Work in South Africa is supported by the London-based Baptist South African Missionary and Colonial Aid Society.

## Education and Early History
The Baptist movement has long emphasized an educated ministry. Early leaders included Dr. John Gill, noted for his knowledge of Hebrew. Educational efforts were supported by bequests, such as those from Edward Terrill (d. 1685), which led to the Baptist Academy in 1720 and later the Bristol Baptist College (erected c. 1811). Other institutions include the Pastors' College (1856), the Baptist College at Manchester (1866), and the Midland Baptist College in Nottingham. There are also theological colleges in Ireland, Wales, and Glasgow, with a combined total of approximately 210 students.

Historically, the movement faced severe persecution; the last execution for heresy by burning in England was that of the Baptist Edward Wightman in 1612. The first Particular Baptist Church was formed in 1633 by a group dismissed from the Jacob church in Southwark. The Jacob church itself, which later became Baptist, included members such as William Kiffin (1616-1701), Hanserd Knollys, Henry Jessey, Praise-God Barbon, and John Lothropp.
