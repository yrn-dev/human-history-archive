# Flora

The term flora refers to the plant life characteristic of specific geographic regions. The distribution of these plants is heavily dependent on climatic conditions, with distinct patterns emerging across different continents and elevations.

## European and Arctic Distributions
In certain archipelagos, the flora is decidedly European; for example, in one such region, 400 of the 478 indigenous species belong to the European continent, while only four are found in America and forty are peculiar to the archipelago itself. Vegetation in these islands is notably rich in ferns, mosses, grasses, juniper, heath, and various shrubs. While tall-growing trees were almost entirely absent until the 19th century, several species were successfully introduced, including the European poplar, Bordeaux pine, chestnut, oak, elm, tulip-tree, Australian eucalyptus, and African palm-tree. Common fruits in this region include the lemon, orange, banana, citron, pomegranate, apricot, and Japanese medlar. Historically, the cultivation of indigo (introduced in the 16th century) and sugar-cane were practiced, though both have since been largely abandoned. Additionally, a native fern known as *Dicksonia culcita* (or *cabellinho*) is used to produce a silky material for mattress stuffing, which is exported to Portugal and Brazil.

In Greenland, the flora consists of approximately 310 species of vascular plants. The distribution is nearly equivalent between American and European-Asiatic elements, with about forty species being American, forty-four European-Asiatic, and fifteen being endemic.

## Asian Flora and Climatic Influence
The flora of northern Asia is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, though they differ in species rather than genera. Notable differences include the absence of all heaths and the oak east of the Ural. Principal mountain trees include birch, larch, and pines—the latter reaching a northern limit in Siberia at approximately 70 deg. N. On lower ground, poplars, alders, and willow are prevalent.

A flora of essentially European character extends along the warm temperate zone from the Himalaya to the Mediterranean. While many European species reach the central Himalaya, they are fewer in the eastern parts. In this zone, there is also a diffusion of Chinese and Japanese forms, which are most numerous in the eastern Himalaya.

In the hotter, wetter regions of eastern India, the tropical flora is continuous with the islands and peninsula of Malaya. As one moves westward, where winter cold increases and rainfall diminishes, this tropical character becomes less marked and rises to lower elevations. Conversely, the higher, cooler ranges of the Himalaya maintain a closer general approach to European forms, including alpine plants at the highest elevations that are believed to be identical to species in the north Arctic regions.

## Regional Variations in Asia and the Middle East
On the Tibetan plateau, increased dryness causes the flora to assume Siberian features, including many Siberian genera and species. Some of these forms extend into the plains of upper India and the rainy mountain regions. Tibet is also characterized by assemblages of marine plants growing near salt lakes at elevations of 14,000 to 15,000 ft.

In south-eastern Asia (including the Malayan peninsula, islands, and India between the 65th and 105th meridians), the indigenous plant species are estimated at 12,000 to 15,000. The dominant orders are Leguminosae, Rubiaceae, Orchidaceae, Compositae, Gramineae, Euphorbiaceae, Acanthaceae, Cyperaceae, and Labiatae. While tropical plants dominate the region as a whole, there is significant variation between arid and humid regions. Orchidaceae are present in specially large proportions, while Compositae are comparatively rare.

In northern Afghanistan, the flora approximates that of the western Himalaya. The evergreen oak of southern Europe (*Quercus Ilex*) is found in forests as far east as the Sutlej. Higher parts of Persia and Afghanistan are characterized by thistles, Boraginaceae, and gigantic Umbelliferae (such as *Prangos, Peucedanum, Bubon, Dorema, Galbanum,* and *Ferula*), some of which extend into Tibet. In Asia Minor and northern Persia, the flora differs little from southern Europe. Forests of *Corylus, Carpinus, Acer, Ulmus, Fagus,* and *Quercus* clothe the mountains where rain is abundant; of these, *Fagus* is the only genus not found on the Himalaya.

## Balkan and Central American Flora
The flora of Bulgaria is divided into several regions:
* **Northern Plain:** Resembles Russian and Rumanian steppes, featuring bulbous plants like the tulip, iris, crocus, and orchis in spring, followed by tall grasses and umbelliferous growths in summer.
* **Southern Plain:** Recalls the vegetation of Asia Minor and the neighborhood of Constantinople.
* **Black Sea Coast:** Features types of Mediterranean, Transcaucasian, and Crimean flora.
* **Elevated Basins:** Sofia and Samakov contain sub-alpine plants, while the hollow of Kiustendil reflects the vegetation of Macedonian valleys.
* **Mountain Groups:** The Balkans correspond with the Carpathians, while the Rila and Rhodope groups combine indigenous types with those of the central European Alps and Asia Minor. Some isolated types in the southern mountains correspond with those of the Caucasus.

In Central America, Honduras possesses a flora and fauna similar to its neighboring countries. The region is defined by two seasons: a dry season from November to May and a wet season from May to November.
