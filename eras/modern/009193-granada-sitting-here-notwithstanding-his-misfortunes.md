# New Granada and the Conquest of Granada

The term "Granada" appears in the archive's records in two distinct historical contexts: as a region in the Americas involved in revolutionary struggles for independence, and as a Moorish stronghold in western Europe during the Christian conquest of Spain.

## Revolutionary New Granada
In the early 19th century, New Granada was a site of significant political and military upheaval. Following a period of flight to Curacao, the revolutionist Bolivar returned to Cartagena in September 1812. He was commissioned to operate against Spanish troops on the Magdalena river, an expedition in which he was eminently successful, driving Spanish forces from post to post.

Bolivar eventually entered Venezuela, forcing his way to the towns of Truxillo and Merida with a force of no more than 500 men. After issuing a decree of "war to the death," he achieved a decisive victory over General Monteverde at Lastoguanes, leading to the triumphant entry into Caracas on August 4, 1813. While the patriots briefly held entire possession of the country by January 1814, royalist forces soon regained control. By the end of 1814, the royalists were once again the undisputed masters of Venezuela.

Following these defeats, Bolivar traveled to Tunja, where the revolutionary congress of New Granada was sitting. Despite his misfortunes and the efforts of personal enemies, he was treated with great consideration and appointed to lead an expedition against Santa Fe de Bogota. 

Later, in 1819, Bolivar reorganized his troops and crossed the Cordilleras to join General Santander, who commanded the republican force in New Granada. This strategy resulted in a sharp action on the heights of Tunja in July 1819 and a victory at Boyaca on August 7, which granted Bolivar immediate possession of all New Granada and Bogota.

## The Conquest of Granada in Spain
In western Europe, Granada served as the final foothold of the Moslems. Between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, Christians had recovered most of Spain, but a pause followed until the marriage of Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile united Spain to complete the Christian conquest.

The ten years' war for the conquest of Granada was characterized by ambuscades in mountain defiles, skirmishes, and the defense of towns and castles. During this conflict, Gonzalo (the future Great Captain) served under the count of Tendilla, the count of Aguilar, and the grand master of Santiago. Gonzalo's notable contributions included the defense of the advanced post of Illora and beating back a Moorish sortie while commanding the escort for Queen Isabella.

Granada finally fell in 1492. Gonzalo was among the officers chosen to arrange the capitulation and was granted land upon the peace. The fall of the city coincided with the period when Columbus, having failed to secure help from Henry VII of England, finally obtained three ships—the *Santa Maria* and two open boats—with the help of merchants from the town of Palos to begin his expedition across the unknown seas.
