# Czechs and the Other Slavs in the Austrian Empire

The political history of the Czechs within the Austrian Empire was characterized by a complex interdependence with other Slav races and various political factions. Their parliamentary strength and strategic goals often relied on alliances with other non-German groups and conservative elements to challenge German predominance and seek national recognition.

## Parliamentary Alliances and Strategy
The Czechs frequently sought alliances with other Slavs and conservative parties to increase their leverage within the empire's governing bodies. In one period of parliamentary activity, the Czechs refrained from obstruction specifically because they did not wish to forfeit an alliance with the Conservatives and the Poles, upon which their parliamentary strength depended. 

This interdependence was evident in the composition of the Reichsrath and the diets. At various times, the "Slav races" were viewed as a collective political force that the government sought to conciliate. For instance, Counts Taaffe and Alfred Potocki pursued policies to appease the Slavs, though such efforts often met with resistance from other ministry members who preferred to cripple opposition by altering election processes. When the Polish club, along with Slovenes and Tirolese representatives, left the House in March of one year, the resulting parliament consisted only of Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia, a situation deemed impossible to govern.

## National and Linguistic Struggles
A central point of contention for the Czechs and other Slavs was the preservation of their languages and nationalities. The constitution of 1867 established that all races in the empire had equal rights and an inviolable right to use their own language. This recognized the equality of "customary" (*landesueblich*) languages in public life, office, and school.

The application of this law led to significant disputes:
* **Language Rights:** In Bohemia, Czechs demanded that their language be recognized as customary even in almost completely German districts, such as Reichenberg.
* **Education:** The law of 1867 required elementary education in Slav districts to be provided in Slovenian or Czech. Under Minister Prazak, this was extended so that even small minorities of Slavs in towns were not compelled to attend German schools but were provided with their own.
* **Demographics:** Due to economic causes, Slavs increased more rapidly than Germans and moved westward into manufacturing districts and towns. This population shift spread their languages, leading to demands for nationality-specific government officials and the founding of Czech schools even in Vienna.

These developments were viewed with hostility by Germans, who insisted that German was the *Kultur-sprache*. Some German officials attempted to argue that Slovenian literature was insufficient to substitute German as a medium for higher education.

## Internal Czech Divisions
The Czech political movement was not monolithic and suffered from internal fractures that weakened its position. A primary divide existed between the "Old Czechs" and the "Young Czechs" (also known as Radicals).

The Old Czechs, led by Rieger, maintained a policy of passive opposition and an alliance with the clergy and the aristocracy (Feudalists). In contrast, the Young Czechs:
* Disliked the alliance with the clergy and aristocracy.
* Advocated for universal suffrage.
* Recalled Hussite traditions.
* Desired to take their seats in the diet and collaborate with Germans on political reform.

These internal quarrels reached a peak in 1891, when the Young Czechs emerged completely victorious over the Old Czechs. The Young Czechs also obstructed government measures—such as a proposed separation of German and Czech districts—on the grounds that such measures threatened the unity of the Bohemian country.

## The Quest for Recognition
Despite linguistic concessions, the primary objective of the Czechs remained the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom. While older party leaders might have been satisfied with their attained influence, the impatient Young Czechs pushed for more. Hopes were raised when Count Thun was appointed governor of Bohemia, as he was believed to favor the coronation of the emperor at Prague. However, Count Taaffe's policy was generally to play parties against one another to diminish the influence of parliament and secure a clear field for the government, rather than to alter the constitution.

## Relations with Other Slavs Outside the Empire
The bond between Czechs and other Slavs extended beyond parliamentary politics into military and emotional solidarity. During conflicts in Siberia, Czech soldiers chose to leave the Austrian army rather than fire upon their "brother Slavs." This solidarity was tested when Czech forces, attempting to reach France via the Amur Railway, were detained by Siberian Soviets. Despite the tension and the eventual resistance of the Czechs against their would-be captors, they had carefully avoided aggression against Russians until they were forced to resist disarmament.
