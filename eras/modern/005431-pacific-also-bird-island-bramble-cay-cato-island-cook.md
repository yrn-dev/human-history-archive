# British Possessions and Geography of the Pacific

## British Islands in the Pacific
Great Britain maintains a variety of island possessions across the Pacific Ocean. These include Bird Island, Bramble Cay, Cato Island, the Cook Islands, the Danger Islands, Ducie Island, Dudosa, Howland Island, Jarvis Island, Macquarie Island, Manihiki Islands, Nassau Island, Palmerston Island, Palmyra Island, Raine Island, Rakaanga Island, Rotumah Island, Surprise Island, and Washington or New York Island. Additionally, the British hold the Phoenix Group, the Purdy Group, the Willis Group, and Wreck Reef.

## The Kermadec Islands
The Kermadec Islands are a small group of hilly islands located in the Pacific at approximately 30° S., 178° W. They were named in 1791 after Huon Kermadec, a captain under D'Entrecasteaux. The islands are British possessions and are politically dependent on the colony of New Zealand, having been annexed in 1887. The largest island in the group is Raoul (also known as Sunday Island), which is 1600 feet high, thickly wooded, and has a circumference of 20 miles. The fauna and flora of the Kermadec Islands are largely similar to those found in New Zealand.

## Regional Access and Navigation
In the Far East, the Japanese empire controls the avenues from the Pacific Ocean to the Sea of Japan. Access to the Pacific from the southern and eastern coasts of Korea, as well as from Russia's Maritime Province, depends on Japanese good-will. This control is maintained through the Tsugaru Strait in the north, which Japan can close with mines, and the channel between the Japanese island of Kyushu and the Korean peninsula. While this southern channel is 102 miles wide, it is occupied by the island of Iki and the twin islands of Tsushima, both of which have belonged to Japan from time immemorial.

## Early Exploration and Human Migration
The islands of Oceania were originally uninhabited. Human arrival in the Pacific islands occurred much later than in other regions, with sea-going canoes carrying heliolithic peoples to the area at the earliest by 1000 B.C. 

Later maritime exploration of the region is documented in various authorities, including the works of H.H. Bancroft regarding the history of British Columbia and the voyages of Vancouver (1798) and Cook (1784). Early English navigators and buccaneers also traversed these waters; for example, William Dampier sailed round Cape Horn into the Pacific in August 1683 under a captain named Cook. During this expedition, Dampier visited Juan Fernandez and the Galapagos Islands before proceeding to Mexico.
