# Orchestral Music and Instrumentation

Orchestral music encompasses a wide array of instruments, compositions, and composers, ranging from the technical specifications of wind instruments to the development of symphonic forms and the careers of noted composers.

## Orchestral Wind Instruments

### The Bass Clarinet
The bass clarinet is essentially an A, Bb, or C clarinet that speaks an octave lower. Due to its length, its design differs from higher clarinets; the mouthpiece is attached via screws and a strong ligature to a silver or brass serpent-shaped crook, and the bell joint is bent upward, ending in a large bell shaped like a gloxinea. 

In terms of tone, the bass clarinet is less reedy than higher instruments and resembles the bourdon stop of an organ. Its lowest register is mellower than that of the bassoon, though it is somewhat hollow and lacks power. Because the instrument speaks slowly in its lowest octave, it is primarily used for melody notes or sustained bass, as rapid passages are impossible. Modern orchestral models may utilize various key-mechanisms, including the Boehm system, which solved early difficulties regarding the distance between finger-holes.

The compass of the modern orchestral bass clarinet generally matches that of higher clarinets but an octave lower. While notation is the same for the three types, the real sounds for the Bb instrument are one tone lower, and for the A instrument, 1-1/2 tone lower. In orchestral works, the tenor clef is frequently used, though the treble clef is sometimes employed.

### The Basset Horn
The basset horn is a transposing instrument with music written a fifth higher than the actual sounds. Its tone is rich and extremely reedy, particularly in the low and medium registers. While similar to the clarinet, it lacks brilliancy and is described as sensuous, mellow, and slightly sombre, making it well-suited for music of a funereal or elegiac character. Its technical capabilities mirror the clarinet, with the exception that the upper register is seldom used in orchestral music and the extra low notes (actual sounds F to A) can only be intoned staccato and slowly.

### The Horn
The horn's mouthpiece is a truncated cone or funnel, differing from the trumpet's cup. The proportions of the mouthpiece must relate specifically to the diameter and length of the air column. In orchestral settings, the first horns typically use a shallower and smaller mouthpiece to more easily obtain the higher harmonics. The second horn uses a different mouthpiece to better dwell on lower harmonics.

## Notable Composers and Works

### Ludwig van Beethoven
Beethoven's orchestral career began with a "masterly little comedy." His 2nd symphony is noted for its power and easy breadth. Later in his career, he produced the overture *Die Weihe des Hauses*, op. 124 (C), which is described as a magnificent essay in orchestral free fugue.

### Edward Alexander MacDowell (1861-1908)
Born in New York City, MacDowell was influenced by German romanticism, modern French models, and the "programme" idea of composition. His work is characterized by a search for delicate artistic expression and the use of American folk-song suggested by Indian themes. This is specifically evident in his orchestral *Indian Suite* (1896). His early orchestral work includes his first concerto for piano and orchestra in A minor, performed in Weimar in 1882. His broader output includes orchestral "poems" and suites, numbered from op. 9 to op. 62.

### Dudley Buck (1839-1909)
An American composer born in Hartford, Connecticut, Buck studied in Paris, Dresden, and Leipzig between 1858 and 1862. In 1875, he moved to New York to assist Theodore Thomas as a conductor of orchestral concerts. His orchestral contributions include a symphony in E flat and a symphonic overture titled *Marmion*, alongside various vocal works and operas.
