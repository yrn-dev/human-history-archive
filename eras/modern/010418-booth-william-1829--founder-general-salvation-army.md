# BOOTH, WILLIAM (1829- )

William Booth was the founder and "general" of the Salvation Army. Born in Nottingham on April 10, 1829, he experienced a strong religious turn at age fifteen under the influence of the Wesleyan Methodists, eventually becoming a local preacher within that body.

## Early Ministry and Development
In 1849, Booth moved to London. He later recounted that his passion for preaching in the open air led to his severance from the Wesleyans. He subsequently joined the Methodist New Connexion and was ordained as a minister; however, he left this organization in 1861 because he was not employed in the "travelling evangelization" he desired.

In 1855, Booth married Catherine Mumford, with whom he had four children. Both husband and wife engaged in preaching, working first in Cornwall and later in Walsall and Cardiff. While in Walsall, Booth organized a "Hallelujah band," consisting of others and converted criminals who provided public testimony of their conversion.

## Foundation of the Salvation Army
Booth returned to London in 1864, where he continued his services in the open air and in tents. He founded a body that underwent several name changes, known successively as the East London Revival Society, the East London Christian Mission, and the Christian Mission, before becoming the Salvation Army in 1878.

The Salvation Army is a quasi-military organization, and Booth based its "Orders and Regulations" on those of the British army. The organization operates through several specific methods:
* Holding processions and outdoor meetings.
* Visiting private houses, prisons, and public-houses.
* Conducting meetings in factories, theatres, and other unusual buildings.
* Utilizing the language of everyday life and popular song-tunes.
* Encouraging every convert to act as a daily witness for Christ in both private and public settings.

In its early years, the Army's "campaigns" faced violent opposition. A "Skeleton Army" was formed to disrupt meetings, and for many years, followers of Booth were imprisoned and fined as breakers of the peace. These disorders largely ceased after 1889.

## Global Expansion and Social Reform
Under the leadership of "General" Booth, who was an organizer, speaker, and indefatigable traveller, the Army expanded internationally. It reached the United States in 1880, Australia in 1881, and eventually spread to India, Ceylon, the European continent, and other regions.

In 1890, Booth published a work titled *In Darkest England, and the Way Out*. In this text, he proposed ten expedients to remedy vice and pauperism:
1. The city colony
2. The farm colony
3. The over-sea colony
4. The household salvage brigade
5. Rescue homes for fallen women
6. Deliverance for the drunkard
7. The prison-gate brigade
8. The poor man's bank
9. The poor man's lawyer
10. Whitechapel-by-the-Sea

A large portion of this scheme was implemented following liberal subscriptions of money.

## Public Recognition
While Booth's work was initially met with ridicule and opposition, this shifted toward the end of the 19th century to widespread sympathy as his results and genius were realized. This change in public perception was highlighted by the active encouragement of King Edward VII. At the King's instance, Booth was officially invited to attend the coronation ceremony in 1902. Furthermore, during a progress through England in 1905, the mayors and corporations of many towns received the "general" in state. His work also received cordial encouragement from authorities in the United States and elsewhere.
