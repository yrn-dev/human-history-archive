# The Ashanti Monarchy and the Golden Stool

The Ashanti are a powerful nation originating from bands of fugitives who, in the 16th or 17th century, were driven south by Moslem tribes from the Niger and Senegal regions. After settling in a region of impenetrable forest, they developed a national character of valor and rose to become a conquering nation. The Ashanti are divided into numerous tribes—including the Kumasi, Adansi, Kokofu, Bekwai, Juabin, Mampon, Nsuta, Nkwanta, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Ofinsu, and Adjisu—each with its own king. However, from the beginning of the 18th century, the king of Kumasi was recognized as the king paramount of the Ashanti.

## The Golden Stool and Governance
The paramount king of the Ashanti succeeds to the "golden stool," which serves as the symbol of sovereignty and authority. The government of the state was historically a combination of military aristocracy and monarchy. The social fabric was based on six great divisions or clans organized for war. The chiefs of these clans, along with certain sub-chiefs with hereditary rights, formed the King's Council. Unless the monarch possessed an exceptionally strong character, the council of chiefs often exercised more power than the king. Land in Ashanti is held in common by the tribes; any unallotted land is attached to the king or head chief and is referred to as "stool lands."

## The Reign of Kofi Karikari
Following the death of the peace-loving monarch Kwaka Dua I in April 1867, a period of civil war occurred before Kofi Karikari succeeded him. Upon being enstooled, Kofi Karikari declared, "My business shall be war." This stance led to preparations for attacks on the Fanti tribes, culminating in the war of 1873-74. 

The conflict was triggered primarily by the British transference of Elmina fort from the Dutch on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti viewed the Elmina as their subjects and resented the British occupation of what they considered their own territory. A secondary cause was the Ashanti captivity of four Europeans, including Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife, and infant son, as well as Mr. J. Kuhne and a French trader, Monsieur M.J. Bonnat. These captives were held in Kumasi, and Mr. and Mrs. Ramseyer were imprisoned by King Kofi Karikari for four and a half years.

## Deposition and Succession
One consequence of the war of 1873-74 was that several states dependent on Ashanti declared independence and sought British protection. Shortly after this war, the Ashanti deposed Kofi Karikari. His brother, Mensa, was placed on the golden stool. Mensa's reign was characterized as tyrannous and marked by repeated human sacrifices. He also violated nearly every article of the Fomana treaty; of the 50,000 oz. of gold stipulated for indemnity, only 4,000 oz. were paid.

In 1883, a revolution displaced Mensa, and he was succeeded by Kwaka Dua II, a young man who died in June 1884, only a few months after his election. The ex-king Kofi Karikari also died in June 1884. Following another desolating civil war, Prince Prempeh (who took the name Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888, with the sanction of a Gold Coast government officer.

## British Intervention and the 1900 Rebellion
While Prempeh initially brought peace and prosperity, tensions rose in 1893 when he closed roads to traders. The British government, viewing Ashanti as within its sphere of influence, demanded that Prempeh accept a British protectorate and a resident at Kumasi. Prempeh declined to treat with the governor of the Gold Coast and failed to reply to British demands by October 31, 1895.

After the deportation of Prempeh in 1896, no successor was appointed to the throne, and Prempeh himself was never "enstooled." The affairs of Kumasi were administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart. The Kumasi chiefs kept the golden stool hidden starting in 1896.

In 1900, a serious rebellion broke out involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes, who sought to wipe out the dishonor of Prempeh's deportation. On March 28, the governor of the Gold Coast, Sir F. Hodgson, demanded that the chiefs pay 4,000 oz. of gold annually and reproached them for hiding the golden stool. This led to attacks by Kumasi warriors against a party of Hausa sent to discover the stool. The rebels, numbering approximately 40,000, eventually attacked the fort at Kumasi on April 29, but were repulsed by a garrison that eventually grew to 700 men.
