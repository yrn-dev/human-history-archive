# Active Immunity

Immunity is defined as the non-susceptibility of an organism to a specific disease, or to experimental inoculation with a particular toxin or bacterium. This state is relative and depends on existing conditions; for instance, an animal may be susceptible during experimental inoculation but rarely suffer from the disease naturally if the necessary conditions for infection are absent in nature. Immunity exists in various degrees, and susceptibility can be increased by general or local diminished vitality.

## Types of Immunity
Animals that are naturally susceptible may acquire immunity through two primary paths: by successfully passing through an attack of the disease or through various methods of inoculation. Artificial immunity is divided into two chief varieties based on the mode of production:

*   **Active Immunity:** This involves a reaction or series of reactions produced within the body of the animal, typically through the injection of bacteria or their products. This method is practically applied in preventive inoculation.
*   **Passive Immunity:** This is produced by transferring serum from an actively immunized animal to a fresh animal. It is termed "passive" because it does not involve an active change in the tissues of the recipient animal. This method is applied in serum therapeutics.

## Mechanisms of Active Immunity
The artificial establishment of active immunity is based on the fact that recovery from certain infective diseases provides protection against subsequent attacks for varying periods. This led to the principle of producing a modified attack of a disease as a means of prevention, a concept previously used in smallpox inoculation.

Immunity likely results from substances introduced into the system during the disease rather than the disease itself. If doses of the poison are properly adjusted, immunity can be achieved without the occurrence of disease symptoms. There are two primary methods for producing active immunity:

1.  **Inoculation with Attenuated Viruses:** This involves using bacteria whose virulence has been diminished. Attenuation methods include growing the organism outside the body under unsuitable conditions, such as in the presence of weak antiseptics or at temperatures higher than the optimum. Virulence may also decrease when organisms are grown on ordinary artificial media, a process sometimes accelerated by passing air over the growth. Additionally, virulence for one animal species may lessen after the bacterium is passed through another species. Immunity can be gradually increased by using vaccines of increasing virulence, potentially reaching a high degree through "supervirulent" cultures obtained via the method of passage.
2.  **Injection of Dead Bacteria:** Immunity against a living organism can be produced by injecting the bacterium in a dead condition.

## Cellular and Serum Components
Active immunity is associated with the presence of "anti-substances" in the serum. These anti-substances, or antigens, have a specific combining affinity for their corresponding anti-substance, similar to a lock and key. Even after these substances disappear over time, the animal may remain immune for a period, which is believed to depend on an alteration in the body's cells, though the exact nature of this alteration is unknown.

A significant component of the immune response is phagocytosis, a process involving the ingestion of bacteria by cells (specifically leucocytes) followed by intracellular digestion. Leucocytes are guided to the focus of production by chemiotaxis—a sensitivity to chemical substances in their surroundings. Positive chemiotaxis occurs when a cell moves toward a greater concentration of these substances.

The increased ingestion of bacteria during active immunity depends on the presence of immune opsonins in the serum. Opsonins are anti-substances that make bacteria more susceptible to phagocytosis. While normal serum contains labile opsonins that are destroyed at 55°C, active immunity develops thermostable immune opsonins that retain their property after being heated to that temperature.

## Historical Development
Systematic research into immunity against bacterial diseases began around 1880. This progress was stimulated by Pasteur's demonstration that bacteria produce definite diseases and Koch's 1881 introduction of the "plate-method" and solid culture media, which simplified the separation and cultivation of bacteria. 

Pioneers in producing active immunity via attenuated viruses included Pasteur, Toussaint, and Chauveau in France, as well as Duguid, J. S. Burdon-Sanderson, and W. S. Greenfield in Great Britain. From approximately 1884, the work of Metchnikoff on the theory of phagocytosis provided a significant stimulus to the field.
