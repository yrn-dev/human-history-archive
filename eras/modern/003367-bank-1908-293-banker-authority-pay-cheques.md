# Banking and the Law of Cheques

## Definition and Nature of Cheques
A cheque is defined as a bill of exchange drawn on a banker and payable on demand. While most laws applicable to bills payable on demand apply to cheques, specific rules exist due to the legal relationship between the drawer and the drawee. Under the law, a person with a bank account is entitled to draw on it via cheques; however, the right to overdraw the account can only arise through a specific agreement.

The relationship between a banker and a customer is primarily that of debtor and creditor. Superadded to this is the banker's obligation to honour a customer's cheques, provided there is a sufficient and available balance. If a banker dishonours a cheque despite having sufficient funds, they are liable to the customer for substantial damages, even if no actual injury is proven. If several cheques are presented simultaneously and funds are insufficient to cover all of them, the banker is not required to discriminate between them and should pay as many as the funds allow.

## Authority to Pay and Its Termination
A banker's authority to pay a customer's cheques is terminated by several specific events: the death, insanity, or bankruptcy of the customer, or upon notice of an available act of bankruptcy committed by the customer. Additionally, authority may be determined by a countermand of payment.

For a revocation of authority to be effective, it must come to the banker's conscious knowledge and be unequivocal in both its terms and the method of communication. For example, a banker is not bound to act upon a telegram that remains unconfirmed.

## Presentation and Validity of Cheques
The practice of post-dating cheques—where a drawer seeks the results of a bill at a future date without the expense of a regular bill-stamp—is recognized under the Bills of Exchange Act 1882. According to section 13, subs. 1, a bill is not invalid because it is post-dated, ante-dated, or dated on a Sunday. Consequently, a banker cannot refuse to pay a cheque presented after its apparent date of issue simply because the banker knows it was post-dated. However, the banker is bound to refuse payment if the cheque is presented before the apparent date of issue.

Regarding the form of the instrument, it is considered a legitimate condition that a cheque be drawn in an ordinary recognized form. Some banks refuse to pay cheques drawn to "petty cash or order" or "wages or order." While such payees are not considered "fictitious or non-existent persons" under the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, they cannot endorse the cheque, and some banks are justified in refusing payment.

Furthermore, funds paid into an account so shortly before a cheque's presentation that they could not have passed through the bank's books are not treated as available for drawing. Additionally, a person with an account at one branch is not entitled to draw cheques on another branch where they have no account or are overdrawn.

## Crossing and Protection of the Banker
To prevent fraud and ensure safe transmission via post, a system of "crossing" is used. A cheque is crossed "generally" by drawing two parallel lines across it with the words "& Co." written between them; such cheques cannot be paid over the counter and must be presented by a banker. A "special" crossing includes the name of a specific banker, meaning it can only be presented through that banker. Cheques may also be marked "not negotiable."

Under the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, a banker who pays a crossed cheque in accordance with its ostensible tenor is protected under section 80 and the proviso to section 79. However, if a banker pays a crossed cheque contrary to the crossing, they are liable to the true owner for any resulting loss and may be unable to charge the customer.

Regarding the collection of crossed cheques, section 82 provides qualified immunity to the banker if certain conditions are met:
* The cheque and money must be received from a customer with whom the banker has existing, definite business relations.
* The cheque must already be crossed generally or specially to the banker (the banker's own crossing is inefficacious).
* The banker must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care for the true owner. This includes failing to ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct or taking a cheque marked "account payee" for a different account. Protection is lost if the banker acts as the owner of the cheque rather than a "conduit pipe" or agent; for instance, crediting a cheque as cash before the money is actually received is considered taking it as an owner.

## Other Banker-Customer Obligations
Bankers are bound to observe secrecy regarding a customer's account unless there is good cause for disclosure, an obligation that continues even if the account is overdrawn. 

In England, a cheque is not an assignment of funds. Therefore, if payment is refused, the holder of the cheque has no direct claim against the banker, but must seek remedy from the drawer or endorser. This principle also supports the English custom of refusing to pay part of a cheque's amount when funds are insufficient to cover the total. In contrast, in Scotland, banks are generally bound to pay whatever funds are available toward the satisfaction of the cheque.
