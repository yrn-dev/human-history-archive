# Heritable Bond

In the context of Scots law, a heritable bond is a specific legal instrument used to secure a debt. It is defined as a bond for money that is joined with a conveyance of land. This arrangement allows a creditor to hold the land as security for the debt owed to them.

## Legal Context in Scotland
The heritable bond exists within a broader framework of Scots law that includes various other specialized deeds and terms. For example, Scots law utilizes the "back-bond" (or "back-letter"), which is a deed used to qualify the terms of another deed or to declare the purposes for which a deed was granted. An example of this is an *ex facie* absolute disposition that is qualified by a back-bond to express the limited nature of the right held by the recipient, a structure similar to a deed of trust in English law.

Additionally, Scots law employs the term "lucrative succession" (*lucrativa acquisitio*). This refers to a situation where an heir receives a free grant of any part of heritable property during the lifetime of their ancestor.

## Comparison with General Bonds
While a heritable bond specifically involves land as security, the general concept of a "bond" (derived from the Teutonic root *bindan*, meaning "to bind") encompasses various obligations. In English law, a bond is an obligation by deed designed to ensure an obligor pays a sum of money or performs (or refrains from) a specific act. These often include a "penalty" and a "defeasance"—a condition that, if met, renders the bond void.

Other specialized types of bonds mentioned in legal archives include:
*   **Lloyd's Bonds:** Instruments used by railway companies to admit indebtedness for goods supplied or work done, allowing them to increase debt without technically violating their charter.
*   **Debenture Bonds:** Bonds secured only by a company's covenant, lacking a fixed or floating charge on assets.
*   **Bottomry Bonds:** Contracts of hypothecation where a ship's owner or master borrows money for emergencies by pledging the ship as security.
*   **Post Obit Bonds:** Bonds given by a legatee or expectant heir, payable after the death of the person from whom they expect an inheritance.
*   **Recognizance:** An obligation of record entered into before a court of record, distinguishing it from a standard bond.

## Legal Limitations and Enforcement
The law provides certain protections and restrictions regarding bonds. Bonds intended to secure actions contrary to legal policy—such as marriage brocage bonds or bonds for future cohabitation—are considered void. 

In the case of common money bonds, historical English law once allowed the recovery of the full penalty if a bond was forfeit. However, the court of chancery intervened to prevent oppression, viewing the penalty as a form of pledge rather than the substance of the debt. This was later codified in an act of 1705, which stipulated that payment of the lesser sum, along with interest and costs, would satisfy a common money bond.

## Etymological Distinctions
It is important to distinguish the legal "bond" (from "bind") from the obsolete "bond" meaning a householder. The latter originates from the Old English *bonda* (related to the verb *bua*, meaning to dwell) and is cognate with the German *Bauer*. Over time, this second meaning shifted toward the idea of serfdom and slavery due to confusion with the legal term for binding. A remnant of this earlier meaning is found in the term "bondager," used in Northumberland and south Scotland to describe a female outworker supplied by a married farm-labourer as a condition of tenancy.
