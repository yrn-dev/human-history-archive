# Benevolence and Related Concepts in History

The term benevolence appears across various historical, political, and philosophical contexts, ranging from a supreme moral virtue in Eastern philosophy to a specific mechanism for royal taxation in English history and a descriptor of diplomatic neutrality in naval warfare.

## Philosophical and Moral Conceptions
In the context of the soul and governance, benevolence is identified as a princely virtue, encompassing sympathy, pity, affection for others, magnanimity, and love. These are regarded as the highest attributes of the human soul. According to the teachings of Confucius and Mencius, benevolence is an indispensable requirement for any ruler of men. Confucius taught that when a prince cultivates virtue, people, lands, and wealth will follow, as virtue serves as the root from which wealth outcomes. Mencius further asserted that while individuals might attain power in a single state without benevolence, no one has ever held an entire empire without this virtue, noting that it is impossible to rule people who have not yielded the subjection of their hearts. Both philosophers defined the concept simply: "Benevolence—Benevolence is Man." Under feudal regimes, benevolence was seen as the force that provided deliverance from the worst kinds of despotism and absolutism.

## Benevolences in English Royal Finance
In English history, "benevolence" took on a specialized meaning referring to sums of money extorted by kings from their subjects without the consent of parliament, disguised as gifts. While sovereigns globally have demanded gifts, the specific terminology of "benevolence" is peculiar to England. 

The practice evolved over several reigns:
* **Edward II and Richard II:** Utilized forced loans to obtain funds.
* **Edward IV:** In 1473, the term "benevolence" was first used for royal demands for gifts. These were successful and targeted a limited number of people, avoiding widespread unpopularity.
* **Richard III:** Attempted to emulate Edward IV, leading to a 1484 act of parliament that abolished benevolences as "new and unlawful inventions." Despite this, Richard continued to demand them.
* **Henry VII:** Made the most extensive use of the system. In 1491, he used commissioners to obtain gifts, and by 1496, parliament enforced the payment of promised sums under penalty of imprisonment. His chancellor, Cardinal Morton, developed "Morton's Fork," a reasoning method where a man was deemed able to afford a gift if he lived economically (because he was saving) or if he lived sumptuously (because he was wealthy).
* **Henry VIII:** In 1545, he demanded a "loving contribution" from those possessing lands of a certain value.

## Political and Diplomatic Applications
The term has also been used to describe the intentions of rulers or the nature of international relations:

**The Reign of Joseph Bonaparte:** In May 1808, Napoleon called Joseph to take the crown of Spain. Although Joseph announced benevolent intentions to the Spaniards in a proclamation dated June 23, 1808, reconciliation was impossible due to Napoleon's treatment of Ferdinand VII. Joseph's sovereignty remained largely titular; he was frequently subordinate to Napoleon and eventually became a "mere instrument of a military power."

**Danish Monarchy:** Christian VI (1730-1746) is described as religious and benevolent. However, during his reign, the position of the Danish peasantry was at its worst. Despite his abolition of the national militia, agrarian difficulties persisted, leading to the militia's re-establishment in 1733, which bound peasants between the ages of nine and forty to the soil.

**Naval Neutrality:** During the conflict involving the Russian Baltic squadron under Admiral Rozhdestvensky in 1904-1905, the squadron faced the difficulty of maintaining coal supplies during an ocean voyage. This challenge was overcome due to the "very benevolent character" of the neutrality assumed by certain powers, allowing the squadron to reach Far Eastern waters in May 1905.

## Spiritual and Folk Beliefs
In various cultural beliefs, spirits are not universally viewed as malevolent. Among the Mpongwe of West Africa and the Eskimo, local spirits are generally regarded as inoffensive, though some may occasionally perform mischievous acts. Similarly, the *Petara* of the Dyaks are not seen as indiscriminately malignant. In European folk belief, spirits concerned with nature, such as the corn-spirit, are often conceived as neutral or benevolent, provided they are not irritated by the trespassing of humans on their domain.
