# Buffalo

Buffalo is a major commercial and industrial center in the United States, situated almost equidistant from New York, Boston, and Chicago. Its development has been driven by its favorable location for lake transportation and its position on the primary northern trade route connecting the East and West.

## Geography and Urban Layout
The city occupies an area of 42 square miles on a broad, undulating tract of land. The terrain rises gradually from the lake to elevations between 50 and 80 feet, with an average altitude of slightly less than 600 feet above sea level. Buffalo is characterized by a temperate climate, high land, and efficient water-supply and drainage systems, contributing to its status as one of the healthiest cities in the United States; its death rate was 14.8 per thousand in 1900 and 15.58 in 1907.

The city's layout, originally platted by Joseph Ellicott, was intended to resemble Washington, though the plan was later altered and not strictly followed. Modern Buffalo features spacious, broad streets—many of which are lined with trees—along with numerous squares and small parks. The municipal park system covers approximately 1,030 acres in a chain connected by driveways and boulevards. This system includes:
* **Delaware Park**: The largest park at about 365 acres, featuring a 46½-acre lake. The northern section of this park housed the Pan-American Exposition of 1901.
* **Cazenovia Park**, **Humboldt Park**, and **South Park** on the Lake Shore.
* **"The Front"**: A bluff overlooking the source of the Niagara river, which contains Fort Porter, a site where the United States government maintains a garrison.

Adjoining Delaware Park is the Forest Lawn cemetery. This site contains monuments to President Millard Fillmore and the Seneca chief Red Jacket (1751-1830), an orator and friend of the whites who warned Americans of danger after being approached by the Prophet and Tecumseh.

## History
The site was first visited by Jesuit missionaries and French trappers. In 1679, La Salle built his ship, the "Griffin," on the east bank of the Niagara river at the mouth of Cayuga Creek, and constructed Fort Conti at the river's mouth, though the fort was burned that same year. In 1687, the marquis de Denonville built a fort at the mouth of the river, which served as the predecessor to the fortifications known as Fort Niagara.

The area became known as the "Buffalo Creek region" as early as 1784, a name derived either from an Indian chief or from the bison and buffalo that Indian tradition says frequented the creek's salt licks. The first permanent white resident was Cornelius Winney, an Indian trader who built a cabin near the mouth of the creek around 1788-1789.

The land was part of the Phelps-Gorham Purchase and later the Holland Purchase (circa 1793), coming into the possession of the Holland Land Company. Joseph Ellicott, the company's agent and known as the "Father of Buffalo," laid out a town in 1801-1802 called New Amsterdam. While this name remained on company books until about 1810, the name Buffalo became more popular. The village served as the Niagara county seat in 1808, and the town of Buffalo was erected two years later.

During the second war with Great Britain, the region became a center for military operations, with British Fort Erie located directly across the Niagara river. In October 1812, Lieutenant Jesse D. Elliott captured the "Caledonia" and "Detroit" from Buffalo. Later, on December 30 and 31, 1813, the settlement was captured, sacked, and almost completely destroyed.

## Commerce and Communications
Buffalo serves as a vital hub for several modes of transport:
* **Rail**: Fourteen trunk lines pass through or have terminals in the city. A belt line transfer company's tracks encircle the city, with more than 500 miles of track within city limits.
* **Water**: Most Great Lakes steamship lines have an eastern terminus at Buffalo, providing freight and passenger connections to Milwaukee, Detroit, Cleveland, Chicago, and the "Head of the Lakes" (Duluth-Superior). The latter is served by the Great Northern Steamship Company. The port handles more than 5,000,000 tons annually and possesses a 20-mile waterfront with 8 to 10 miles of wharfs. Federal government improvements, costing over $2,000,000, added an outer stone and cement breakwater (4 miles long) and several inner piers and breakwaters.
* **Canal**: The Erie Canal, completed in 1825, has its western terminus here and provided the initial impetus for the city's commercial growth.
* **International Links**: Buffalo is connected to the Canadian shore via ferry and the International bridge. The bridge, which starts from Squaw Island, was completed in 1873 at a cost of $1,500,000.

## Government and Public Institutions
Buffalo is governed by a mayor elected for a four-year term and a bicameral city council under an amended 1896 charter. The mayor appoints the heads of the police, fire, civil service, parks, and health departments, while the city council elects the city clerk. The city is powered by manufactured gas and electricity generated by the water power of Niagara Falls. Gas is supplied via pipelines from the Canadian (Welland) and Ohio-Pennsylvania natural gas fields.

The city's public institutions include numerous well-administered charitable and correctional facilities. Hospitals include the Buffalo General, the Children's, the German, the Homeopathic, the Women's, the German Deaconess, the Riverside, the Erie county, the United States marine (Federal), and the Buffalo hospital of the Sisters of Charity, as well as a state hospital for the insane. Charitable organizations include the St. Joseph's, St. Vincent's, and Buffalo orphan asylums, the St. John's orphan home, the Ingleside home for erring women, and St. Mary's asylum for foundlings and widows. 

A notable institution is the Charity Organisation Society, founded in 1877 at the Fitch Institute. As the first such society in the United States, it has served as a model for similar institutions elsewhere.

## Media
The city's first newspaper, the *Gazette*, was a weekly established in 1811, becoming the daily *Commercial* in 1835. The first daily paper, the *Courier*, began in 1831. By 1908, the city had eleven daily papers (including three in German and two in Polish) and several weekly papers in German, Italian, and Polish.
