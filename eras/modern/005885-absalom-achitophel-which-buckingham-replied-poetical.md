# Absalom and Achitophel

*Absalom and Achitophel* is a celebrated work of English satire written by John Dryden. Published in two parts (Part I in 1681 and Part II in 1682, the latter co-authored with Nahum Tate), the poem is credited with marking a "new birth" of English satire, elevating the genre to a level comparable with ancient and modern international standards.

## Literary Style and Composition
Dryden, described as the master of expression for his time, utilized a combination of rhetorical and metrical skill, "Olympian unfairness," and a "deadly good temper" in the creation of the poem. The work is noted for its use of the couplet, a form Dryden refined over twenty years through his work in heroic tragedy, panegyrics, and dramatic prologues. The vividness of the portraits within *Absalom and Achitophel* is cited as a lasting quality of the work.

## Subject Matter and Characters
The poem presents a satirical depiction of political figures and rivalries. It focuses on the relations between Achitophel, who represents the Earl of Shaftesbury, and Absalom, who represents Walters, within the context of Charles, the "minstrel-king of Judah." 

The work further includes portraits of various political and literary figures:
* **Political Figures:** The poem depicts Shimei, Barzillai, and Jotham, who represent eminent Tories and Whigs.
* **Literary Figures:** The poets Og and Doeg are also featured.
* **Other Characters:** Dryden used the character of Zimri to describe Buckingham.

## Political Context and the Green Ribbon Club
The poem references the political climate of the 17th century, specifically mentioning the Green Ribbon Club. This association, which met at the King's Head tavern at Chancery Lane End, was founded around 1675 as a resort for those hostile to the court. Members were known for wearing a bow of green ribbon in their hats as a badge for mutual recognition during street brawls.

According to Dryden in *Absalom and Achitophel*, the chief attraction of the club was drinking, and its members used these gatherings to organize sedition. The club's membership included a diverse group of individuals, ranging from statesmen such as Shaftesbury, Buckingham, Halifax, and Bedford to remnants of the Cromwellian regime and various "profligates" and "scoundrels."

## Response by Buckingham
The depiction of Buckingham as "Zimri" in *Absalom and Achitophel* prompted a response from Buckingham himself. In 1682, he published a reply titled *Poetical Reflections on a late Poem ... by a Person of Honour*. While Buckingham possessed "undoubted but undeveloped poetical gifts," he is more widely recognized for other works, such as the satire *The Rehearsal* (1671), which targeted the heroic drama and Dryden.
