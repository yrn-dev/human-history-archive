# Bridge Construction and Strategic Utility

Bridges are structures designed to carry railways, waterways, or roadways across valleys, streams, or other roads and railways, while maintaining a passage way below. The terminology for these structures varies by function: "viaducts" typically refer to long bridges with several spans, "aqueducts" carry canals, and "culverts" are small-span bridges used for drainage. In railway contexts, an "overbridge" passes over the railway, while an "underbridge" carries the railway itself. While most bridges are fixed, some are "movable," such as "turning" or "swing" bridges that open for navigation. Additionally, "floating" bridges utilize pontoons moored in a stream to support a roadway.

## Engineering and Design Principles

The primary task of a bridge engineer is to determine the loads the structure must carry and proportion its parts so that stresses do not exceed safe limits. In many countries, these working stress limits for railway and public bridges are mandated by law. Modern metal bridge design relies on a recognized theory where every member has a calculated straining action and a definite function, reducing the need for empirical safety allowances.

Economy of material becomes increasingly critical as the span of a bridge increases. This is because the dead weight of the superstructure becomes a larger fraction of the total load; eventually, a point is reached where the dead weight imposes a limit on any further increase in span.

## Types of Bridge Construction

### Suspension and Arch Bridges
Suspension bridges utilize two or more chains—made of twisted wire strands, parallel wires, or links connected by pins—which pass over lofty piers on saddles and are anchored in rock chambers. A level platform is suspended from these chains via rods. This design allows for the use of hard-drawn wire, the strongest form of steel or iron. While cheap and suited for great spans, suspension bridges can be flexible. This flexibility can lead to failure; for example, in 1850, a bridge at Angers collapsed when 487 soldiers marched across it in step, resulting in 226 deaths. Notable examples include the Menai bridge (570 ft. span), the Pest bridge (666 ft. span), and the Fribourg bridge (870 ft. span).

The Niagara Falls and Clifton steel arch bridge serves as a replacement for the older Roebling suspension bridge. It features a two-hinged parabolic braced rib arch with side spans of 190 and 210 ft. The main span weighs 1629 tons. This construction is noted for using "bold expedients" to annul bending action and compression in the upper member at the crown.

### Timber and Stone Bridges
Early stone bridges in Great Britain include cyclopean bridges on Dartmoor, such as the bridge over the East Dart near Tavistock, which features three piers and stone slabs measuring 15 ft. by 6 ft. and is reputed to have lasted 2000 years. Timber bridges were also common; the brothers Grubenmann constructed a bridge at Schaffhausen in the mid-18th century with spans of 172 and 193 ft., and a Wittingen bridge with a 390 ft. span, which may be the longest timber span ever built.

## Military and Strategic Importance

In warfare, bridges are critical arteries of communication used to transport troops. The destruction of a bridge can sever these arteries, potentially leading to the loss of a battle. Consequently, important bridges—such as those across the Rhine in Germany—are often guarded by sentries, such as Landsturm men in dark-blue uniforms or grey overcoats.

### Bridge-heads and Fortifications
A bridge-head (French: *tête-du-pont*) is a fortification designed to cover a river crossing on one or both banks. Because moving an army across a bridge is slow and complicated, bridge-heads must be extended far enough to keep enemy artillery out of range and provide room for troops to form up on the far bank.

### Tactical Use
Tactically, the exit of a bridge is considered an exposed turn or defile. Such points can be targeted by machine-guns, which can concentrate fire with greater accuracy and distance than riflemen. In historical conflicts, bridges have served as key defensive positions, such as during the first battle of Bull Run on July 21, 1861, where Confederate forces guarded passages from the railway bridge down to the Stone Bridge.

## Historical Examples

Ancient records describe the bridging of straits for military movement. Mandrocles constructed a floating bridge over the Bosphorus for Dareios. Additionally, Dareios commanded the Ionians, Aiolians, and Hellespontians to build a bridge across the neck of the river Ister.

In the 12th century, the Bridgebuilding Brotherhood (*Fratres Pontifices*) emerged in southern France. Recognized by Pope Clement III in 1189, this confraternity built bridges, managed ferries, and maintained hospices at principal river fords.
