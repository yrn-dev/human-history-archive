## Ancient Religious Objects
The term "baetylus" (from the Greek *baitulos* or *baitulion*, and the Semitic *bethel*) refers to a sacred stone believed to be endowed with life. These objects were often meteoric stones used as symbols of gods or dedicated to them. 

In Greek mythology, the term specifically referred to a stone that Uranus and Gaea substituted for the infant Zeus; this stone was mistakenly swallowed by Cronus, who feared his children. This specific stone was preserved at Delphi, where it was anointed with oil daily and covered in raw wool during festal occasions. In Phoenician mythology, Baetylus is the name of one of the sons of Uranus.

## Central Asian and Iranian History
The Graeco-Bactrian kingdoms were eventually overthrown by the Arsacid empire in the west—specifically under Phraates II and Mithradates I, who conquered districts such as Areia (Herat)—and by Mongolian tribes in the north. These tribes, referred to by the Greeks as Scythians, included the Tochari (identified by the Chinese as the Yue-chi). 

According to Chinese sources, the Tochari entered Sogdiana in 159 B.C. and conquered Bactria in 139 B.C., ending Greek rule in eastern Iran within the following generation. Greek conquerors such as Apollodotus and Menander persisted longer in India, but by the middle of the 1st century B.C., the "Indo-Scythian" empire controlled western India and eastern Iran. This empire was led by the Kushan (Kushana) dynasty, with King Kanishka (ca. 123-153) serving as a great protector of Buddhism.

The Kushan dynasty began to decay in the 3rd century, and the Gupta empire was founded in India around A.D. 320. The Kushanas were subsequently reduced to eastern Iran, where they fought the Sassanids. In approximately 450, the Ephthalites (or "white Huns") subjected Bactria. They were followed by the Turks around A.D. 560, who subjugated the region north of the Oxus. Following the overthrow of the Sassanian empire by the Arabs, the region of Bactria and Iran up to the banks of the Jaxartes submitted to the rule of Islam and the caliph.
