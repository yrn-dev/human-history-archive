# The Baptist Movement

The Baptists are a body of Christians distinguished by their specific view of the ordinance of baptism. The central, defining belief common to all Baptists is that baptism should be administered only to believers. While some groups, such as the Mennonites, practice baptism via sprinkling or pouring, "modern" Baptists are characterized by the use of immersion.

## Origins and Early Development in England
Toward the end of the 16th century, various Anabaptist communities existed in England, particularly in London, Kent, and East Anglia. Notable figures included Robert Cooke, and the group was known for anti-paedobaptist positions as well as heretical views regarding the Virgin Mary; for the latter, Joan Boucher of Kent was burnt in 1550. While these communities raised the question of baptism, they were considered a foreign importation, and the rise of the modern Baptist churches is viewed as wholly independent of them.

The first Baptist church on English soil was formed in 1612 by Helwys, who returned from Holland to establish a church in Newgate Street, London. This congregation became the origin of the General Baptist denomination. Helwys and his followers were Arminians who rejected the Calvinistic doctrine of predestination. This church is credited with introducing the absolute doctrine of Religious Liberty to England, with member Leonard Busher authoring "Religious Peace: or a Plea for Liberty of Conscience."

## General and Particular Baptists
For a period, two distinct sets of Baptists existed in England with separate origins and no real intercourse as churches. The General Baptists were Arminian, influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists. The Particular Baptists were Calvinist, having sprung from the Independents. 

Both groups rejected infant baptism, though they were initially unpledged to immersion and rarely practiced it. Their doctrinal development focused on three disputes: the proper mode of baptism, the proper subjects, and the proper administrator. Agreement was eventually reached, and in 1644, seven Particular Baptist churches in London published a Confession of Faith. These churches were commonly, though falsely, called Anabaptist.

## Church Government and Organization
Baptists share the view with Congregationalists that each separate church is complete in itself. Consequently, each church has the power to make its own regulations and choose its own ministers to advance the kingdom of Christ. A small section, known as Scotch Baptists in the north of England and Scotland, maintains that a "plurality of elders" or pastors is necessary for a complete organization. In Scotland, the church at Keiss in Caithness (formed about 1750) was the first, followed by a larger church in Edinburgh in 1765.

Most churches are voluntarily united in associations based on geography. The Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland was formed in 1813 by Particular Baptists. In 1891, under the influence of General Baptist leader Dr. John Clifford, the General and Particular denominations united into this single body. However, the Union is purely voluntary and exerts no authoritative action over separate churches. Some prosperous churches remain aloof, and "Strict" Baptist churches form a separate denomination. These Strict Baptists are further divided into "Vessel" and "Standard" parties, named after their respective organs, the "Earthen Vessel" and "Gospel Standard."

As of 1909, the United Kingdom recorded 3,046 Baptist churches, 4,124 chapels, 424,008 members, and 2,078 pastors in charge.

## Global Expansion
Baptists expanded into the Americas and Europe. In Massachusetts, John Myles and his congregation sought refuge from the government of Charles II in 1663, settling in Rehoboth. In Boston, Thomas Gould and others formed a church covenant in May 1665. Despite a 1679 mandate from Charles II rebuking Massachusetts for denying liberty, the Boston Baptists faced severe persecution, including fines and imprisonment.

In the Quaker colonies, Baptists found greater religious liberty. Thomas Dungan helped found a church at Cold Spring, Pennsylvania, around 1684, and Elias Keach helped form the Pennepek church in 1688. The Philadelphia Association was established in 1707 to maintain sound doctrine and coordinate missionary and educational work. Seventh-day Baptist churches also formed within this association from 1705 onward.

In Europe, the movement spread during the 19th century. In Roman Catholic countries, missions were established by English or American missionaries: France (1832), Italy (1866 and 1884), and Spain (1888). In Protestant countries and Russia, the movement grew without such intervention. J. G. Oncken formed the first church in Hamburg in 1834, leading to the establishment of churches in Denmark (1839), Holland and Sweden (1848), Switzerland (1849), Norway (1860), Austria and Rumania (1869), Hungary (1871), and Bulgaria (1884). Baptist unions also exist in Canada, South Africa, New Zealand, and various Australian states.

## Education and Ministry
The denomination prioritized an educated ministry. Some pastors attended English universities, while others were self-taught, such as Dr. John Gill, noted for his Hebrew knowledge in his 9-volume "Exposition of the Holy Scriptures" (1746-1766). 

Educational institutions were established through bequests and societies, including the Baptist Academy (1720), the Bristol Education Society (1770), and the Bristol Baptist College (c. 1811). Other colleges were formed in Stepney (1810), Rawdon (1804), and Manchester (1866), the latter serving "Strict" Baptist views. The General Baptists maintained the Midland Baptist College in Nottingham since 1797. Additional theological colleges exist in Glasgow, Ireland, and Wales, with a total of approximately 210 students.
