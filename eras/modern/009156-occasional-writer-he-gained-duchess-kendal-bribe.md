# Occasional Writer

The "Occasional Writer" was the pseudonym used by a political figure and author who, beginning in 1727, penned a celebrated series of letters to the *Craftsman*. These writings were dedicated to attacking the Walpoles.

## Political Career and Return from Exile
The author of these letters had previously sought a pardon from the English government. In March 1716, he declared his final abandonment of the Pretender and offered to use his influence to ensure his friends withdrew, though he refused to betray any individuals or secrets. During his time away from England, he wrote *Reflexions upon Exile* and a letter to Sir W. Windham in 1717 explaining his position; the latter is regarded as one of his finest compositions, although it remained unpublished until 1753, following his death.

In 1723, he finally received his pardon through the mediation of the king's mistress, the duchess of Kendal. He returned to London in June or July of that year and offered his services to Walpole. However, Walpole responded coldly to his offers to help secure the accession of several Tories to the administration. During the subsequent winter, he served the government by gathering information in France.

## Legal Status and Literary Pursuits
In 1725, a peremptory order from the king—issued against the wishes of Walpole—led to the passage of an act that allowed him to hold real estate, although he was not granted the power of alienating it. Walpole succeeded in ensuring he remained excluded from the House of Lords.

The author purchased an estate at Dawley, near Uxbridge. There, he renewed his associations with Voltaire, Swift, and Pope. He became involved in the literary disputes of Pope and wrote the philosophy for the *Essay on Man*.

## Opposition and the Duchess of Kendal
In 1726, following the rupture between Pulteney and Walpole, the author attempted to organize an opposition alongside Windham and Pulteney. This period of political activity coincided with his work as the "Occasional Writer." To further his goals, he gained over the duchess of Kendal by providing her with a bribe of £11,000, which was sourced from his wife's estates.

## Personal Life
In 1717, the author formed a liaison with Marie Claire Deschamps de Marcilly, the widow of the marquis de Villette. He married her in 1720, following the 1718 death of Lady Bolingbroke, whom he had treated with cruel neglect. He also owned and resided at the estate of La Source near Orleans. While there, he studied philosophy and criticized the chronology of the Bible. During this time, he was visited by Voltaire, who expressed unbounded admiration for his politeness and learning.

## The Duchess of Kendal
The duchess of Kendal, born Ehrengarde Melusina in 1867 as the daughter of Gustavus Adolphus, count of Schulenburg, was the mistress of King George I. She had entered the household of Sophia, electress of Hanover, and became the king's mistress around 1690. After George I moved to England in 1714, she followed him and eventually supplanted Charlotte Sophia, Baroness von Kilmannsegge, as the king's primary favorite.

The duchess held significant influence with the king and was created duchess of Munster in 1716, followed by the title of duchess of Kendal. In 1723, Emperor Charles VI made her a princess of the Empire. She was known for her avarice, selling public offices, titles, and patent rights for large sums of money. One such sale was the privilege of supplying Ireland with a new copper coinage, sold to William Wood, which led to the publication of Swift's *Drapier's Letters*. In addition to her role in the "Occasional Writer's" pardon, she received £10,000 for procuring the recall of Bolingbroke from exile. She died on May 10, 1743, at Kendal House in Isleworth, Middlesex.
