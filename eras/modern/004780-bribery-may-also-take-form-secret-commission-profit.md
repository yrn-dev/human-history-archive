# Bribery

Bribery is defined as the offering or giving of payment in various shapes or forms to serve as a motive in the performance of functions where the proper motive should be a conscientious sense of duty. When this duty is superseded by the "sordid impulses" created by the bribe, the individual is considered corrupted; consequently, corruption is sometimes viewed as a term equivalent to bribery.

The act of bribery is generally divided into two primary categories: instances where a person possessing power is induced by payment to use that power unjustly, and instances where power is obtained by purchasing the suffrages of those capable of imparting it.

## Etymology and Nature
The word "bribery" originates from the Old French *briberie*, meaning vagrancy or begging. This stems from the Middle Latin *briba*, referring to a piece of bread given to beggars. In English, the term evolved through meanings of extortion, blackmail, and alms before arriving at its current definition of gifts given or received to corruptly influence others.

Bribery is described as a natural propensity that can only be removed by civilization or a powerful counteracting influence. This propensity manifests as the feeling that power should be used for the owner's own benefit and that benefits should be granted to those who pay the most rather than those who most deserve them. Because all immediate parties in a bribery transaction typically obtain what they desire and are satisfied, the crime is often more difficult to detect than fraud or violence, where an injured party can aid in detection.

## Legal Classifications and Penalties

### United States
In the United States, bribery is dealt with severely. In many states, the attempt to bribe or the act of bribery itself is classified as a felony. Punishments vary by jurisdiction but may include imprisonment for periods not exceeding ten years. In the context of elections, bribery is handled similarly to English law, resulting in the voiding of the election and the disqualification of the offender from holding office.

### England and Ireland
Under English law, the bribery of a juryman or a privy councillor is punishable as a misdemeanour. This classification also applies to the taking of bribes by ministerial or judicial officers. Common law also regards the selling and buying of public offices as a form of bribery.

Specific statutes provide further penalties:
*   **Customs Consolidation Act 1876:** Customs officers who take bribes face instant dismissal and a £500 penalty. Those offering or promising rewards to an officer to conceal acts of customs evasion or neglect duty forfeit £200.
*   **Inland Revenue Regulations Act 1890:** Bribery involving collectors, commissioners, or officers of the Inland Revenue carries a fine of £500.
*   **Extradition Act 1906:** Bribery is listed as an extraditable offence.
*   **Public Bodies' Corrupt Practices Act 1889:** This act addresses administrative corruption in bodies such as town, borough, or county councils, boards, select vestries, and other local government or public health bodies that administer rates. The offering, soliciting, or receiving of any loan, fee, gift, or advantage to induce an officer or member of such a body to act or forbear in regard to the body's affairs is a misdemeanour in England and Ireland, and a crime in Scotland. Conviction can lead to a fine not exceeding £500 and imprisonment (with or without hard labour) for up to two years. Offenders may be ordered to repay the bribe and may be disqualified from holding public office for seven years. Prosecution requires the consent of the lord advocate in Scotland or the solicitor-general or attorney in England and Ireland.

## Forms and Historical Instances

### Secret Commissions
Bribery may manifest as a secret commission, which is defined as a profit made by an agent during their employment without the knowledge of their principal.

### Judicial and Official Corruption
Judicial corruption has been described as an inveterate vice of imperfect civilizations. While the purification of the bench has been gradual across Europe—receiving an impulse in 16th-century France from Chancellor Michel de L'Hôpital—it has been remarkably rare in England. Aside from a 1384 statute (repealed in 1881), England has had little legislation specifically targeting judicial bribery. Notable historical cases include Sir William Thorpe (1351), Michael de la Pole (1387), Lord Chancellor Bacon (1621), Lionel Cranfield (1624), and Sir Thomas Parker (1725).

In the period between the Revolution and the end of Queen Anne's reign, corruption was prevalent in high official quarters; this era saw the expulsion of a speaker of the House of Commons for bribery and pecuniary dishonesty involving the great Marlborough. During the administration of Walpole, members of parliament were paid in cash for their votes.
