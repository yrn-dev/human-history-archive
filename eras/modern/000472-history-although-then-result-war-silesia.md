# Silesia

Silesia is a region in Germany that has been the subject of significant territorial disputes and military conflict, most notably between the powers of Prussia and Austria during the 18th century.

## Early History and Bohemian Rule
In the early medieval period, the region was part of the expansions of the Bohemian empire. Boleslav II, a ruler of Bohemia, established his authority over a large portion of Silesia, as well as over Moravia and parts of Poland. However, this empire did not endure; his son, Boleslav III, lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great, the king of Poland.

Later, in 1241, the region was the site of the battle of Liegnitz in Lower Silesia, where a mixed army of Germans and Poles was annihilated by Mongol hosts during their sweep across Asia and Russia.

## The War of the Austrian Succession
The most defining conflict regarding Silesia began in 1740, triggered by the accession of Maria Theresa to the Habsburg throne. Frederick II of Prussia sought to assert a claim to the region based on a 1537 treaty between Frederick II, duke of Liegnitz, Brieg, and Wohlau, and Joachim II, elector of Brandenburg. This treaty stipulated that the duchy should pass to the house of Brandenburg if the ducal line became extinct. When the last duke, George William, died in 1675, Austria refused to acknowledge the treaty and annexed the duchies.

In December 1740, Frederick II invaded Silesia without a declaration of war. Prussia was a small but highly organized power with an army of 100,000 men. The Prussian infantry was drilled to a level of perfection that allowed them to fire five shots for every three fired by the Austrians. Because the Austrian forces were disorganized following the Turkish wars, Frederick was able to overrun Silesia almost unopposed, eventually investing the strong places of Neisse, Brieg, and Glogau.

The Prussian victory at Mollwitz on April 10, 1741, drew other powers into the conflict. France, Spain, Bavaria, Saxony, and Sardinia joined the struggle, ambitious to expand at Austria's expense. England and Holland supported Austria.

## Military Campaigns and Territorial Shifts
The struggle for Silesia continued through several campaigns. In 1742, Frederick attempted to secure the region, but Maria Theresa’s opposition remained firm, bolstered by the support of the Hungarians. Frederick launched a foray into Moravia, capturing Olmuetz and investing Glatz, with his outposts reaching as far as Vienna. However, he was eventually forced to fall back to Kuttenberg in Bohemia.

Later, Prussian forces reached Prague on September 2, capturing the city six days later and advancing to Budweis. This success was temporary; Frederick was eventually outmaneuvered by the combined forces of Prince Charles and Marshal Traun. While Frederick regained Silesia, he did so with heavy losses, and the Austrians were unable to establish a foothold within Silesia itself.

The conflict concluded with the treaty of Dresden, which transferred Silesia from Austria to Prussia. The broader War of the Austrian Succession ended with the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748.

## Regional Towns and Geography
Several towns within the Prussian province of Silesia are noted for their historical and economic significance:

*   **Brieg:** Located on the left bank of the Oder, Brieg (known in early documents as *Civitas Altae Ripae*) obtained municipal rights in 1250. It served as the capital of an independent line of dukes from 1311 to 1675. The town was heavily bombarded by Prussian forces during the war of the Austrian succession and captured by the French and Bavarians in 1807. Its economy is based on cattle markets and the manufacture of leather, machinery, cigars, and various textiles.
*   **Löwenberg:** One of the oldest towns in Silesia, situated on the Bober. It became a town in 1217 and saw significant fighting during the Napoleonic wars. The town is known for its 13th-century Roman Catholic church and its gypsum and sandstone quarries. Nearby is the village of Hohlstein, owned by the Hohenzollern family.

## Aftermath and Austrian Compensation
The loss of Silesia prompted Austria to seek compensation elsewhere, specifically in Bavaria. This ambition led to the War of Bavarian Succession in 1777 when Joseph II claimed the inheritance of the elector Maximilian III. To avoid another struggle with Prussia, Maria Theresa overruled her son, and the dispute was settled by the treaty of Teschen, which granted Austria the Quarter of the Inn (Innviertel) and other districts.
