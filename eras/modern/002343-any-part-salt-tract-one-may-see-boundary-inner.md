# The Salt Tract and Regional Boundaries

The salt tract is a specific geographical feature found within a district that forms a strip of alluvial land between the sea and the hills. This district, which varies in breadth from 9 to 34 meters and covers an area of 2,085 square meters, is naturally divided into three distinct tracts: the salt tract along the coast, the arable tract (also known as the rice country), and the submontane tract (the jungle lands).

## Characteristics of the Salt Tract
The salt tract runs the entire length of the coast as a desolate strip a few miles wide. Its composition is purely alluvial and appears to be of recent date, with soil that possesses a distinctly saline taste toward the coast. The landscape is characterized by sandy ridges rising 50 to 80 feet high near the beach, which slope inland and are covered in low scrub jungle. The ocean-verge sandhills are carpeted with wild convolvulus and creepers.

Within this region, sluggish brackish streams flow between banks of fetid black mud. Further inland, the tract opens into scrub jungle and prairies of coarse long grass. While these areas harbor plenty of wild animals, there are very few hamlets and only occasional patches of rice cultivation.

## The Boundary of the Inner Arable Tract
From any location within the salt tract, one can see the boundary of the inner arable part of the district. This boundary is marked by long lines of trees. Every morning, villagers drive their cattle from the arable tract into the saliferous plains of the salt tract to graze.

The arable tract itself lies beyond the salt lands and constitutes the chief part of the district. It is a dead-level region of rich fields with soil that is more friable and lighter in color than that of Behar or Bengal, often splitting into small cubes with rectangular cleavage. A notable feature of this tract is the *pats* (meaning "cups"), which are depressed lands near river-banks—likely partially silted marshes—that produce the finest crops. While generally treeless, the arable tract features bamboo lanes and villages encircled by banyan, *pipal*, tamarind, and mango trees. Date-palms, palmyras, and screw-pines (an aloe-like plant with triple rows of hook-shaped thorns) are also found in the area.

## Other Regional Tracts and Salt Resources
Beyond the arable lands lies the submontane tract, an undulating country with red soil and numerous ravines at the foot of the hills. This area contains masses of laterite buried in hard ferruginous clay. At Kila Ambohata in Kopari, approximately 2 square meters are paved with flat, polished, dark-red slabs of this material. The region is characterized by mountain torrents and forests of sal jungle.

Outside of this specific coastal district, other salt-related geographical features exist:
*   **Bahadur Khel:** Located in the Kohat district of the North-West Frontier Province, this is an Indian salt-mine situated in a range of hills south of Bahadur Khel village. It features an exposed mass of rock-salt 4 miles long and a quarter-mile wide. The salt is extracted in large blocks using wedges and picks.
*   **Kohat District:** This region, covering 2,973 square meters, contains salt-mines in a low line of hills crossing the Teri Toi valley. The salt deposit is 1,000 feet thick and a quarter-mile wide, sometimes forming hills 200 feet high. The most extensive exposure is at Bahadur Khel on the south bank of the Teri Toi.
*   **Assam Frontiers:** Certain hill tribes, specifically the Nagas, prepare salt from brine springs found in their hills.

## Administrative and Linguistic Boundaries
In other contexts, "inner lines" and boundaries serve administrative or linguistic purposes. In certain districts of Assam, such as Sibsagar, Lakhimpur, and Darrang, a regulation from 1873 established an "inner line." This line marks the limit of guaranteed British authority; British subjects are prohibited from crossing it without special permission. These lines are monitored by military police at frontier posts.

Linguistically, a boundary line exists between the western and eastern dialects of Bengali, roughly positioned at the 89th degree of east longitude.
