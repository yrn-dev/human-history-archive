# Kwaja Amran (Kojak) Range

The Kwaja Amran Range, also referred to as the Kojak Range, is a geographical feature located in Baluchistan. It serves as a significant landmark in the region's topography and defines portions of the boundaries between different administrative and national territories.

## Geography and Boundaries
The Kwaja Amran (Kojak) Range forms the western boundary of an irregularly-shaped block of upland territory known as British Baluchistan. This region includes the Toba plateau and all the upper tributaries of the Lora. The range is closely associated with the Toba plateau, as the northern spurs of the plateau form the base of the Kwaja Amran Range.

In the northern section of Baluchistan, the boundary between Afghanistan and Baluchistan winds through open plains to the west of the Kojak. At this point, the boundary departs from the river to follow the western edge of the Toba plateau, which reaches a height of 8,000 feet. This boundary continues until it nears the railway station of New Chaman.

## Infrastructure and the Chaman Loop
The region is integrated into the broader transport network of British India via the railway that traverses Baluchistan through Quetta. This railway extends up to New Chaman on the Afghan-Baluch frontier. The archive notes an advanced loop that includes the Chaman railway terminus, positioning it as part of the western boundary of the upland territory of British Baluchistan. The entire northern boundary and the railway leading to New Chaman are under direct British control in one form or another.

## Geological Activity
The area west of the Kwaja Amran range is subject to geological instability. On December 20, 1892, a severe earthquake occurred due to the sudden yielding of the earth's crust along what appears to be an old line of fault. This seismic event resulted in a physical adjustment of the land, which was evidenced by a shortening of approximately 2-1/2 feet on the railway line that crossed the fault.

## Regional Context
The range is situated within a broader landscape characterized by dry and bracing climates, though extreme temperatures in the region can lead to severe forms of fever. The surrounding territory of British Baluchistan is leased from Kalat and administered as a distinctive province of British India. To the north and south of this province lies independent Baluchistan, which remains under British political control. 

The broader region of Baluchistan extends from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea, and from the borders of Afghanistan and Persia to those of Sind and the Punjab. The area is divided between British Baluchistan—which consists largely of tracts ceded by Afghanistan under the 1879 treaty of Gandamak—and foreign territories, including native states such as Las Bela and Kalat (which includes Kharan and Makran), as well as tribal areas held by the Bugti and Marri tribes.
