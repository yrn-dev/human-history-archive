# Bank Water

## Definition and Salinity
Bank water is a specific classification of marine water characterized by a salinity level of 32 to 34 pro mille. It is distinguished from other water types in the region, such as North Sea water, which possesses a salinity of 34 to 35 pro mille, and oceanic water, which has a salinity of 35 pro mille or higher.

## Geographical Distribution
Bank water forms a broad edging that covers the coast banks of several European nations, specifically Norway, Denmark, Germany, and Holland. More broadly, this water type is found throughout the continental coast of the North Atlantic and the North Sea.

## Presence in the Skagerrak
Bank water enters the Skagerrak from two primary directions: from the North Sea and from the north along the coast of Norway. It is probable that influxes occur from both of these directions. These movements are seasonal, with water entering from the south during August and September, and from the north during early spring and late winter.

The distribution of bank waters across different parts of the coast undergoes seasonal changes. These changes are complex and have been investigated by the Swedish Commission due to their remarkable relationship with the occurrence of various fisheries in the region.

## Regional Water Circulation
In the channels that connect the North Sea with the Baltic proper, circulation is complex and divided between inflowing and outflowing waters. Inflowing waters, which include bank water, are identified by having a salinity of more than 32 pro mille. In contrast, outflowing water—known as the "Baltic Stream"—always contains less than 30 parts pro mille of salt.

The Baltic Stream is a periodic surface current originating from a redundancy of fresh water caused by river discharge and rainfall exceeding evaporation. Due to the earth's rotation, the main part of this stream remains close to the coast of the Scandinavian peninsula. During the summer and spring, Baltic water is abundant enough to inundate the surface of the Skagerrak and Kattegat, whereas in winter, the sources of this current are largely dried up by the freezing of land water.
