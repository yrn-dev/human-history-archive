# Napoleon's Proclamation to the Magyars and its Consequences

## The Translation by Janos Bacsanyi
In 1809, Janos Bacsanyi, a Hungarian poet and patriot, translated a proclamation issued by Napoleon to the Magyars. Bacsanyi had a history of patriotic activity, having published the poem *The Valour of the Magyars* in 1785 and co-editing the *Magyar Museum* until its suppression by the government in 1792. He had also been imprisoned for two years in the state prison of the Spielberg near Bruenn following his involvement in the conspiracy of Bishop Martinovich in 1794.

## Consequences of the Proclamation
The act of translating Napoleon's proclamation was viewed as an anti-Austrian act. As a direct consequence of this action, Bacsanyi was forced to take refuge in Paris. He remained in exile until the fall of Napoleon, at which point he was handed over to the Austrians. The Austrian authorities permitted him to reside at Linz, though this residency was granted on the strict condition that he never leave the town. Bacsanyi remained at Linz until his death on May 12, 1845.

## Historical Context of the Napoleonic Era
The period of the Napoleonic Wars saw the banks of the Danube serve as the scenes of many bloody battles. During this era, the Habsburg monarchy underwent significant structural changes. In 1804, Francis II, the last of the Holy Roman emperors, proclaimed himself Francis I, emperor of Austria. This move was intended to prevent the House of Habsburg from being relegated to a position inferior to the Bonapartes should the Holy Roman Empire collapse or should Napoleon be elected as the successor to the throne of Charlemagne. 

The title of emperor of Austria replaced the title "Imperator Romanorum semper Augustus" when the Holy Empire ended in 1806. However, this title was often resented by Magyars and Slavs, as they felt it ignored their national rights and implied the superiority of the German elements within the monarchy.
