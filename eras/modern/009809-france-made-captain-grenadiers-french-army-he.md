# Charles Melchior Artus, Marquis de Bonchamps

## Biography and Early Military Career
Charles Melchior Artus, Marquis de Bonchamps, was a Vendean leader born around 1760 in Jouverteil, Anjou. He acquired his initial military experience during the American War of Independence. Upon returning to France, he was appointed as a captain of grenadiers within the French army.

## Role in the French Revolution
A staunch supporter of the monarchy, Bonchamps resigned his military command and retired to his chateau at St Florent following the outbreak of the French Revolution. In the spring of 1793, he was selected as a leader by the insurgents of the Vendee. His counsels are credited with contributing significantly to the success of the peasants' arms. During this period, he was present at the capture of Thouars, Bressuire, and Fontenay, where he sustained a wound.

## Death and Legacy
The insurgent forces were eventually weakened by dissensions among their leaders. In October 1793, the Vendeans suffered a severe defeat at the bloody battle of Cholet, during which Bonchamps was mortally wounded. He died the following day. It is reported that his final act was to pardon five thousand republican prisoners who had been sworn to be killed by his troops in retaliation for his death. A statue created by David d'Angers stands in his honor in the church of St Florent.

## The Grenadiers in the French Army
During the era in which Bonchamps served, the grenadiers were specialized soldiers. Originally, the role of a grenadier was to throw hand-grenades, a practice that began in the French *Regiment du Roi* in 1667, where four men per company were trained in the task. By 1668-1670, grenadier companies were established in that regiment and approximately thirty others of the French line.

Grenadier companies were composed of the most powerful men in a regiment. When grenades were no longer in use, these units remained as "crack" companies of their battalions, wearing distinctive headdress and taking the right of the line on parade. In the 18th and early 19th centuries, a typical infantry regiment included a light company and a grenadier company alongside its line companies.

Napoleon expanded this organization in the French army by forming divisions and brigades of grenadiers (and *voltigeurs*). These elite companies were frequently detached from their original regiments for special service and rarely returned to the line. This practice resulted in battalions losing roughly a quarter of their best men at the start of a war, leaving only average men in the line.

## Grenadier Equipment and Uniforms
Historically, grenadiers used firelocks and axes, the latter of which were used alongside grenades during the assault of fortresses. Their distinctive headdress consisted of a loose fur cap or a pointed embroidered cloth cap with flaps and a peak, both designed for light field service. Over time, the fur cap evolved into the tall "bearskin" utilized by various army corps and British guards.
