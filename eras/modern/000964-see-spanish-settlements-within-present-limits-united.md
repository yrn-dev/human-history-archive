# Spanish Settlements within the Present Limits of the United States

The Spanish presence within the current boundaries of the United States was characterized by strategic military outposts, the administration of vast colonial territories such as Louisiana and Florida, and complex diplomatic interactions with the burgeoning United States.

## Florida and Early Establishments
Spanish colonial efforts were established in the south of the North American continent. Their headquarters in this region were located at Fort St. Augustine in Florida. The historical record of these settlements is further detailed in the works of Woodbury Lowery, specifically in his two-volume study *Spanish Settlements within the Present Limits of the United States* (1903-1905) and his focused work on Florida covering the period from 1562 to 1574.

## The Administration of Louisiana
Louisiana was formally transferred to Spain on August 18, 1769. While the official language and law were changed to Spanish, the colony remained essentially French in character. The Spanish administration was noted for a liberal and conciliatory approach to governance, prioritizing the profit of the colony over strict adherence to Spanish colonial commercial regulations.

Key improvements during this period included:
* **Judicial and Administrative Reform:** The judicial system was improved, and a higher grade of officials was appointed. Many French Creoles were given offices.
* **Social Integration:** High-ranking officials encouraged intermarriages between the Spanish, French, and English.

Bernardo de Galvez (1756-1794), a popular and liberal governor, provided valuable aid to the United States during the American War of Independence. Between 1779 and 1781, Galvez conducted a series of campaigns that resulted in the capture of all significant posts in the British colony of West Florida.

## Territorial Interests and the Mississippi River
A primary focus of the Spanish period was the advancement of settlement in the western territories of the United States. This led to international intrigues involving France and Great Britain regarding the future of the valley and the supreme importance of New Orleans and the Mississippi River.

The United States sought free navigation of the Mississippi, a demand that led to several diplomatic milestones:
* **Treaty of 1794:** Spain recognized the Mississippi River as the western boundary of the United States, separating it from Louisiana.
* **Treaty of 1795:** Spain guaranteed the privilege of deposit at New Orleans and freedom of navigation.

Internal tensions existed during this era; for example, in 1787, the Spanish governor Estevan Miro intrigued with James Wilkinson of Kentucky, who had been arrested at New Orleans. Wilkinson unsuccessfully attempted to arrange for Kentucky to secede from the United States and join the Spanish.

## Transition and Retrocession
The geopolitical landscape shifted when Louisiana was retroceded to France. This transfer was unwelcome to the United States, as the American government preferred a weak neighbor over a powerful state like France. Robert R. Livingston, the American minister in Paris, was instructed by Secretary-of-State Madison to prevent the retrocession or, failing that, to purchase New Orleans and the Floridas (including West Florida) to secure American commerce on the Mississippi.

Tensions escalated when the Spanish governor suspended the right of deposit, leading to warlike votes in the U.S. Congress. Simultaneously, reports of a British expedition against New Orleans further pressured the diplomatic situation. Ultimately, Napoleon, facing the collapse of his American colonial schemes and failure in Santo Domingo, offered the territory.

## Economic Influence
Beyond territorial administration, Spanish currency played a role in the broader North American economy. The Spanish piece-of-eight (reals), also referred to as a dollar, was current in Spanish-American colonies and widely used in British North American colonies. Due to the lack of consistent coinage, these Spanish coins were often rated via tariff lists to align with pounds, shillings, and pence.
