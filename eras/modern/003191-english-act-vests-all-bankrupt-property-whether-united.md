# English Bankruptcy Law and the Vesting of Property

## Overview of Property Vesting
Under the English bankruptcy system, once a court adjudicates a debtor as bankrupt, the debtor's property vests in a trustee. This vesting occurs by operation of law and does not require a formal act of conveyance. This mechanism ensures the continuity of the office, as property passes from one trustee to another automatically if a trustee is removed, resigns, dies, or is released.

The scope of this vesting is broad; the English act vests all of the bankrupt's property regardless of whether it is located within the United Kingdom or in the colonies. Because this process denudes the bankrupt of all assets, it has been held that the bankrupt's discharge must be recognized in colonial courts, allowing the bankrupt to plead his discharge in England as a defense in those courts.

## Definition and Scope of Property
In English law, the property of a debtor held by a representative for the satisfaction of creditors or legatees is strictly termed "assets." All property of the debtor is considered assets, even if it has not been reduced into the debtor's possession.

The legal definition of "property" in this context includes:
*   Property of which the bankrupt is the true owner.
*   Property in the bankrupt's possession, disposition, or order within his business (with the true owner's consent) where he is the reputed owner. 

The doctrine of reputed ownership has been restricted in recent years by court decisions and the growth of trade customs, such as "hire and purchase" contracts.

## Trustee's Administration and Powers
The administration of the estate is divided between interim management and ultimate distribution. Interim preservation is supervised by officers responsible to the Board of Trade. Upon an order of adjudication, the official receiver becomes the trustee by operation of law. This official remains in the role until a trustee is appointed by the creditors and certified by the Board of Trade.

The trustee is required to take immediate possession of all property, including accounts, books, and deeds, and possesses the powers of a receiver in the High Court to enforce delivery. Following the payment of administration costs, the trustee must distribute the estate in dividends. 

With sanction from the Board of Trade or a committee, the trustee may:
*   Carry on the business for a beneficial winding-up.
*   Institute or defend legal proceedings.
*   Employ a solicitor.
*   Compromise claims and debts.
*   Raise money via mortgage or sell property on credit.

Without special sanction, the trustee may sell any part of the property for cash—including book debts and business goodwill—via private treaty or public auction.

## Void Payments and Settlements
The trustee's title to property relates back to the date of the first act of bankruptcy committed within the three months preceding the bankruptcy petition. This allows the trustee to invalidate and recover payments or assignments made to creditors during that period if the creditor had knowledge of the act of bankruptcy. Additionally, any payment made to give a creditor preference over others within three months of the petition is rendered void.

Settlements of property are also subject to voidance:
*   Settlements within two years are void unless made in good faith for valuable consideration or in consideration of marriage.
*   Settlements within ten years are void unless the settlor was solvent at the date of settlement (independent of the settled property) and the interest passed to the trustees upon execution of the deed.

## Obligations and Penalties for the Bankrupt
A bankrupt is legally bound to assist the trustee. Failure to deliver any part of his property constitutes contempt of court. Under the Debtors Act, criminal prosecution may occur if the bankrupt, intending to defraud, removes or conceals property valued at £10 or more, or fails to deliver books and documents. 

Other criminal liabilities include:
*   Mutilating, destroying, or falsifying accounts.
*   Permitting false debts to be proved on the estate without disclosure.
*   Obtaining property on credit via false representation within four months before the petition.
*   Obtaining credit for more than £20 without disclosing his status as an undischarged bankrupt (a misdemeanour under the act of 1883).

## Comparative Systems
### The British Empire
Many parts of the British Empire, specifically New Zealand and Australia (Victoria, South Australia, and Western Australia), have modelled their laws on the English system. Queensland, New South Wales, and Tasmania follow the English act of 1869, which emphasizes creditors' management over officialism. 

A key difference exists regarding the discharge of bankruptcy. In the United Kingdom, a bankrupt is not obligated to apply for discharge, resulting in a population of 70,000 undischarged bankrupts. In contrast, New Zealand, Victoria, and New South Wales require bankrupts to apply for discharge within a fixed period or face contempt of court.

### The United States
The U.S. system is governed by the National Bankruptcy Act of 1898 (amended in 1903). Unlike the English system, non-payment of debts is not an "act of bankruptcy." A debtor can only be adjudicated bankrupt on the ground of indebtedness with their own written consent. Acts of bankruptcy in the U.S. include transferring property to hinder creditors, preferring one creditor over others while insolvent, or making a general assignment for creditors' benefit.
