# God and Man: Legal and Theological Perspectives

The relationship between God and man has been interpreted through various lenses across history, ranging from strict legalistic frameworks and dualistic cosmologies to spiritual teachings on universal brotherhood and the nature of Christ.

## Legal Interpretations of the Divine Relation
In the late 17th and early 18th centuries, the English writer John Asgill (1659–1738) proposed a unique interpretation of the relationship between God and man based on the technical rules of law. In his 1700 tract, *An Argument proving that, according to the Covenant of Eternal Life revealed in the Scripture, Man may be translated from hence into that Eternal Life without passing through Death*, Asgill argued that the penalty of death is illegal for those who claim exemption. His reasoning was based on the premise that Christ had wiped out the sin of Adam, thereby removing the legal basis for the penalty of death. While this pamphlet brought Asgill professional success in Ireland, it was later deemed "blasphemous," leading to his expulsion from both the English and Irish parliaments.

## The Teachings of Jesus and Human Dignity
In contrast to legalistic interpretations, the personal teachings of Jesus are noted for marking a new phase in the moral and spiritual life of humanity. These teachings emphasize the universal Fatherhood of God and the implicit brotherhood of all men. Central to this vision is the sacredness of every human personality, viewed as a living temple of God. This perspective fostered a new respect for man as man and stood against the subjugation of man by man, as well as outrages upon human dignity such as gladiatorial combats.

## Christological Perspectives and the Nature of Christ
Various historical figures and texts have sought to define the nature of Christ and his interaction with humanity:

*   **Ignatius:** He described Christ as "God in man" and the "one only physician, of flesh and of spirit," characterizing him as both generate and ingenerate, and first passible and then impassible. Ignatius also emphasized the Virgin Birth and described the Eucharistic bread as the "antidote against death" and "medicine of immortality."
*   **Catholic Theology:** Later Catholic faith established the "pure and proper divinity" of Christ. This involved the belief that God himself, as the second person of an equal and consubstantial trinity, was manifested in the flesh, confined in the womb of Mary, and experienced human pain, anguish, and death on Mount Calvary.
*   **Marcion:** Following the influence of Cerdo, Marcion proposed an ethical dualism. He taught that man was created from matter by a "just and wrathful god" (the Demiurge) who imposed a strict law that no one could keep, leading to a universal curse. A higher, previously unknown God then sent his Son to redeem mankind. According to Marcion, Christ's death "purchased" the souls of the redeemed from the Demiurge.

## The Concept of Regeneration and Birth
The archive contains various views on the "birth" or "regeneration" of man and Christ:

*   **Regeneration of Man:** Some texts discuss spiritual re-birth. In 1 Peter, it is stated that God the Father begat the saints anew unto a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus, using "incorruptible seed" through the word of God.
*   **The Two Births of Christ:** Some homilists distinguish between the first birth of Christ (where Joseph the Carpenter was assumed to be his father) and his baptism. The baptism is regarded as a "second nativity" or "regeneration of Christ," which is considered to have more renown because the "God of majesty" is inscribed as his father.

## Divine Law and Human Institutions
The interaction between divine will and human law is further seen in the development of canon law. The Christian law of marriage was built upon Roman law and Jewish law, modified by principles introduced by Christ and his apostles. A primary principle is the indissolubility of marriage, based on the act of God joining man and wife as "one flesh." While some, such as St. Paul, viewed marriage as a concession to the weakness of the flesh or a gift of God, others later developed the idea of marriage as a sacrament, comparing the relationship between husband and wife to that of Christ and his Church.
