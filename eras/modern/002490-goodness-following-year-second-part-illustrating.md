# The Foundation of Moral Goodness

## Overview and Authorship
*The Foundation of Moral Goodness* is a tractate written by John Balguy (1686–1748), an English philosopher and divine. Balguy was born in Sheffield on August 12, 1686, and received his education at the Sheffield grammar school and St John's College, Cambridge, where he graduated with a B.A. in 1706. Following his ordination in 1710 and his appointment to the living of Lamesley and Tanfield in Durham in 1711, Balguy engaged in theological and philosophical discourse. He first entered the sphere of authorship under the pseudonym Silvius during the "Bangorian controversy," supporting Bishop Hoadley against High Church opponents.

## Publication and Structure
Balguy published the first part of *The Foundation of Moral Goodness* in 1727. In the following year, he released a second part titled *Illustrating and enforcing the Principles contained in the former*. This work followed a previous publication from 1726, *A letter to a Deist concerning the Beauty and Excellency of Moral Virtue, and the Support and Improvement which it receives from the Christian Religion*. In that earlier letter, Balguy argued that while the highest principle of morality is a love of virtue for its own sake, religious punishments and rewards serve as indispensable and valuable sanctions for conduct.

## Philosophical Objectives
The primary aim of *The Foundation of Moral Goodness* was two-fold:
1. To refute the theory proposed by Hutcheson regarding the basis of rectitude.
2. To establish the theory held by Clarke and Cudworth, which posits that virtue consists of conformity to reason.

According to this framework, virtue involves acting according to "fitnesses" that emerge from the eternal and immutable relations existing between agents and objects.

## Related Works and Concepts of Rectitude
Balguy further expanded upon these themes in a subsequent essay titled *Divine Rectitude: or, a Brief Inquiry concerning the Moral Perfections of the Deity, particularly in respect of Creation and Providence*. In this work, he attempted to demonstrate that the same moral principles that should guide human life also underlie the ways and works of God. He argued that goodness in the Deity is not merely a disposition toward benevolence, but rather a regard for harmony, beauty, and order.
