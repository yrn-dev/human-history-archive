# Colonial Conference of 1887

The Colonial Conference of 1887 was a gathering held in London during a period of shifting perspectives regarding the relationship between Great Britain and its colonies. It served as a forum where colonial opinion was sought and accepted on matters concerning imperial organization and defence.

## Historical Context of Imperial Consolidation
The conference occurred following a period between 1845 and 1875 dominated by the "Manchester school." During those thirty years, the prevailing view was that the development of colonial self-government was a preliminary step toward inevitable separation. This era was characterized by a general inclination to withdraw from imperial responsibilities, which provided foreign nations opportunities to profit and eventually led to a reaction, culminating in the scramble for Africa.

By the late 19th century, increasing pressure from foreign enterprise led to discussions regarding the value of an imperial federation for common interests. While imperial federation had been discussed in New Zealand as early as 1852 and suggested by the general association of Australian colonies in London in 1857, a shift in official policy emerged later. In 1872, Lord Beaconsfield repudiated the idea that self-government led to separation, instead describing colonial constitutions as part of a "great policy of imperial consolidation." This sentiment was furthered in 1875 by W. E. Forster, who advocated for imperial federation to replace dependence with association. The subsequent foundation of the Imperial Federation League in 1884, with Forster and later Lord Rosebery as presidents, marked a distinct step toward this goal.

## The 1887 London Conference and Preferential Systems
At the 1887 Colonial Conference in London, a formal proposal was submitted by the South African delegate to establish a preferential system within the empire. This proposal suggested imposing a 2% duty on all foreign goods, with the resulting proceeds dedicated to the maintenance of the imperial navy. To facilitate this, the delegate requested the denunciation of certain treaties with foreign nations that restricted trade between various parts of the empire.

While these requests were not immediately fulfilled, a strong feeling eventually grew in England against foreign engagements that hindered domestic trade arrangements between the mother-country and its colonies. Consequently, the relevant Belgian and German treaties were denounced in 1897.

## Evolution of Imperial Trade and Sentiment
Following the 1887 conference, there was a growing perception that Great Britain's foreign trade in manufactured goods helped enlarge home markets for colonial raw materials. This meant that any injury to Great Britain's foreign trade would necessarily react upon the colonies. This perspective was explicitly expressed at the Colonial Conference at Ottawa in 1894.

This shift in sentiment led to the relinquishment of demands that Great Britain impose differential duties on foreign goods in exchange for colonial concessions. Canada became the first major colony to implement this new sentiment in 1897 by introducing an imperial tariff, which granted a 25% deduction on customs duties for British goods without requiring reciprocal advantages. Canada offered the same advantage to all British colonies. In later years, New Zealand, Australia, and the South African states also granted preferential treatment to British goods.

## Legacy and Transition
The Colonial Conferences of 1887 and the years that followed demonstrated the enthusiastic loyalty of the colonies toward the crown, particularly during jubilee manifestations. These gatherings continued to evolve in their role in imperial organization until 1907, when the title of the meetings was changed to "Imperial Conference."
