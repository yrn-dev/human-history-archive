# Professor Sven Hedin

Professor Sven Hedin was a Swedish explorer and geographer recognized as one of the greatest modern Asiatic explorers. Between 1894 and 1896, Hedin conducted extensive travels through Tibet and the deserts of Takla Makan, as well as investigations into the glacial regions of the Sarikol mountains. His work is described as a monumental record of Asiatic research.

## Explorations in Takla Makan
During his second journey through the Takla-Makan desert in 1896, traveling from Khotan to Shah Yar, Hedin explored the ruins located between the Kiria Daria and the Khotan Daria. In this area, he discovered the remains of the city of Takla-Makan, which had been buried by sands. Hedin characterized the site as an "Asiatic Pompei," estimating it to be at least ten centuries old. His findings included vestiges of habitations, figures of Buddha, and a piece of papyrus containing unknown characters. He concluded that the city's inhabitants were of Aryan race, likely originating from Hindustan, and were Buddhist. He noted that the city predated the 8th-century Mahomedan invasion led by Kuteïbe Ibn-Muslim.

Hedin's discoveries of buried cities that flourished during the period of Buddhist ascendancy in High Asia are considered of the highest interest for the restoration of ancient and medieval Asian geography. These findings helped fill blanks in the identification of sites mentioned by early geographers and provided a fuller illustration of old pilgrim routes.

In 1895, Hedin followed a route through Yarkand, Yangi-Hissar, and Kashgar to Khotan. During this trip, he stayed for nine days at Ilchi, where he estimated the population to be 5,500 inhabitants, consisting of 500 Chinese and 5,000 Musulmans.

## The Lob-nor Investigations
Hedin sought to resolve the "knotty point" regarding the location of the Lob-nor. Traveling from Korla via a road at the foot of the Kurugh-tagh, between the mountains and the Koncheh Daria, he discovered a series of milestones (potaïs) and the ruins of two fortresses. These clay and wood pyramids indicated the existence of an ancient road with heavy traffic between Korla and a place to the south-east.

On April 4, 1896, after following the Ilek for three days, Hedin found a large sheet of water in the valley at the location marked by Richthofen and Chinese topographers for the Lob-nor. This body of water is divided by natives into Arka Kul, Tayek Kul, Kara Kul, and Avullu Kul, most of which are filled with reeds. Hedin later visited the Lob-nor discovered by Prjevalsky, reaching its western extremity, the Kara-buran, on April 17. He observed that while Prjevalsky had found an immense lake in 1885, it had been greatly reduced by the time Prince Henri d'Orléans saw it, and by Hedin's visit, it consisted only of pools of water. Based on these observations, Hedin asserted that Prjevalsky’s lake was of recent formation.

## Geographical and Scientific Contributions
Hedin's scientific work extended to the study of the Kuen-lun mountain ranges. He expressed agreement with F. Grenard and K. Bogdanovich that the direct orographical continuation of the Kuen-lun eastwards is identified with the parallel ranges of Arka-tagh and Kalta-alaghan, rather than the Astin-tagh.

His written contributions to the field include *Through Asia* (English translation 1898) and *Scientific Results of a Journey in Central Asia, 1899-1902*. His research also touched upon the hydrology of the region, specifically the Khotan-Darya, which rises in the Kuen-lun Mountains from the Yurun-kash and Kara-kash headstreams.

## Later Events
In a later period, Hedin was associated with the German Official Press Bureau. While delivering a public lecture in Heidelberg, he was arrested and detained for several days by the police on suspicion of espionage. The arrest was attributed to the police finding his intense enthusiasm for the greatness of Germany suspicious given that he was a foreigner. He was released after the Press Bureau established that he was not a spy.
