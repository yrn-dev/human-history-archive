# Bucharest

Bucharest (also written as Bucarest, Bukarest, Bukharest, Bukorest, and Bukhorest) is the capital of Rumania and the chief town of the department of Ilfov. The city is situated in a hollow and is built mainly on the left bank of the river Dimbovitza, which traverses the area from north-west to south-east. While low hills provide shelter to the south-west and west, the city is otherwise surrounded by drained marshes that extend toward the central Walachian plains.

## History and Foundation
The origins of Bucharest are subject to various theories. Local tradition attributes the city's founding to a shepherd named Bucur, though this legend is considered unhistorical. Other accounts suggest the name derives from the Albanian word *Bukur*, meaning joy, in honor of a victory won by Prince Mircea of Walachia (c. 1383-1419) over the Turks, leading to the city's nickname, "The City of Joy." Some attribute its foundation to the half-mythical first Walachian prince, Radu Negru (c. 1290-1314). Modern historians propose that the city began as a fortress built on the site of the Daco-Roman Thyanus to control approaches to Tîrgovishtea, the former Walachian capital.

The city served as a summer residence for the court before becoming the capital in 1698 under Prince Constantine Brancovan. Throughout its history, Bucharest has faced significant destruction and foreign occupation. It was burned by the Turks in 1595 and suffered from frequent earthquakes and plagues, including a devastating plague in 1813 that killed 70,000 inhabitants in six weeks. In 1847, fire destroyed a quarter of the city.

During the 18th and 19th centuries, the city was frequently disputed by Russia, Austria, and Turkey. It was occupied by Russians in 1828 and again from 1853 to 1854, followed by an Austrian garrison that remained until March 1857. Following the 1858 international congress for the organization of the Danubian principalities and the 1861 proclamation of the union of Moldavia and Walachia, Bucharest became the Rumanian capital. In 1866, an insurrection in the city drove Prince Cuza from his throne.

## Urban Development and Demographics
By 1900, the population of Bucharest was 282,071, which included 43,274 Jews and 53,056 aliens, the majority of whom were subjects of Austria-Hungary. The city covers more than 20 square miles.

Historically, the city was characterized by dimly lit lanes paved with rough stone blocks in clay soil, which often subsided. Drains were rare and epidemics were common. However, a period of gradual reform began with the accession of Prince Charles in 1866. These improvements included the installation of gas and electric light, the provision of pure water and sewerage, the construction of stone embankments for the river, and the introduction of electric or horse tramways.

The city's appearance is noted for its picturesqueness, featuring numerous gardens and churches with gilded or metal-plated cupolas. Older districts contain one-storeyed houses surrounded by acacias and poplars. The city's atmosphere is further shaped by its diverse inhabitants, including pedlars, Russian coachmen of the banished Lipovan sect, and Rumans and gipsies in brightly coloured native costumes.

## Religious Architecture
Bucharest contains a variety of religious structures. The Orthodox Greek churches are typically built of brick in a modified Byzantine style, featuring narrow windows and two or three towers. Their bells are usually housed in wooden porches resembling lych-gates, located about twenty paces from the church. The metropolitan church, built between 1656 and 1665, is shaped like a Greek cross with a broad cloister and four main entrances. Situated on high ground, this cathedral overlooks the city and commands a view of the Carpathians.

Other notable churches include:
* **St Spiridion the New (1768):** Described as the most beautiful and loftiest.
* **Doamna Balasa (1751):** Noted for its interior frescoes and exterior carved work.
* **Biserica Bucur:** An ancient church linked to the legend of the city's founder.

Many early churches have unknown founders and dates because the Greek clergy frequently obliterated Slavonic inscriptions. Additionally, the city hosts synagogues for the Jews, as well as churches for Armenians, Protestants, and Lipovans. Roman Catholics, who numbered nearly 37,000 in 1899, have three churches, including the cathedral of St Joseph, and the city serves as the seat of a Roman Catholic archbishop.

## Military and Diplomatic Significance
In the late 19th century, the Belgian military engineer Henri Alexis Brialmont visited Rumania to advise on national defenses. He presided over a scheme to transform Bucharest into a first-class fortress. This project was viewed as a menace by Austria, and Brialmont was placed *en disponibilité* by his own sovereign for undertaking the work without authorization.

The city has also been a site of international diplomacy. In 1812, it gave its name to a treaty resulting in the cession of a third of Moldavia and Bessarabia to Russia. Later historical records mention the renunciation of the treaties of Bucharest and Brest-Litovsk.
