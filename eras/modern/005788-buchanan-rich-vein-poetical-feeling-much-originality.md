# George Buchanan and Robert Williams Buchanan

The archive contains records of two distinct figures named Buchanan: George Buchanan, a Scottish scholar and humanist, and Robert Williams Buchanan, a 19th-century British poet, novelist, and dramatist.

## George Buchanan: Scholar and Poet

George Buchanan is recognized as one of the greatest scholars of Scotland, particularly noted for a mastery of the Latin language that was seldom surpassed by any modern writer. He wrote in Latin with a level of proficiency suggesting it was his mother tongue, employing a style characterized by elasticity and freshness rather than rigid adherence to any single classical author.

Beyond his linguistic command, Buchanan possessed a rich vein of poetical feeling and significant originality of thought. His literary contributions include:

*   **Translations:** He produced versions of the Greek plays and the Psalms that are regarded as more than mere translations.
*   **Poetry:** His smaller satirical poems are noted for their wit and happy phrasing. He authored two tragedies, *Baptistes* and *Jephthes*, which maintained a European reputation for academic excellence. Other poetic works include *Fratres Fraterrimi*, *Elegiae*, *Silvae*, two sets of verses titled *Iambon Liber* and *Hendecasyllabon Liber*, three books of *Epigrammata*, and a book of miscellaneous verse.
*   **Scientific Verse:** He wrote *De Sphaera* in five books, based on the poem of Joannes de Sacrobosco, to defend the Ptolemaic theory against the new Copernican view.
*   **Prose and Other Works:** His prose works include *Chamaeleon*, a satire in the vernacular against Maitland of Lethington (first printed in 1711), a Latin translation of Linacre's Grammar (1533), *Libettus de Prosodia* (1640), and *Vita ab ipso scripta biennio ante mortem* (1608).

George Buchanan's works have been preserved in two primary editions: one edited by Ruddiman in 1715 and another by Burman in 1725. His vernacular writings, including *Chamaeleon*, two letters, a tract on the Reformation of St Andrews University, and *Ane Admonitioun to the Trew Lordis*, were edited by P. Hume Brown for the Scottish Text Society. Records of his trial during the Lisbon Inquisition were published in 1906 by the Portuguese historian G.J.C. Henriques.

## Robert Williams Buchanan: 19th Century Writer

Robert Williams Buchanan (1841–1901) was a British poet, novelist, and dramatist born in Caverswall, Staffordshire. The son of Robert Buchanan, an Owenite journalist and lecturer, he was educated at the university and high school in Glasgow. During his studies, he formed a close friendship with the poet David Gray; the two traveled to London in 1860 seeking fame.

### Poetic and Literary Works
Buchanan's early poetic career began with the "tentative" volume *Undertones* (1863), followed by *London Poems* (1866), *Idyls and Legends of Inverburn* (1865), and *North Coast and other Poems* (1868). In these works, he demonstrated a sympathetic insight into humble life and a faculty for poetic narrative. He later attempted a more ambitious study in mysticism titled *The Book of Orm: A Prelude to the Epic* (1870). Other notable poems include "The Drama of Kings" (1871), "St Abe and his Seven Wives" (1872), "Balder the Beautiful" (1877), "The City of Dream" (1888), "The Outcast: a Rhyme for the Time" (1891), and "The Wandering Jew" (1893).

His poems were collected in three volumes in 1874, one volume in 1884, and finally as *Complete Poetical Works* in two volumes in 1901.

### Novels and Drama
Buchanan wrote a long series of novels, beginning with *The Shadow of the Sword* (1876). His early novels, including *God and the Man* (1881)—a tale of a family feud—are noted for their simplicity and breadth of treatment. Later novels include *The Martyrdom of Madeline* (1882), *Foxglove Manor* (1885), *Effie Hetherington* (1896), and *Father Anthony* (1898).

As a dramatist, he authored several successful plays, such as *Lady Clare* (1883), *A Man's Shadow* (1890), *The Charlatan* (1894), and *Sophia* (1886), which was an adaptation of *Tom Jones*.

### Periodical Contributions and Controversy
Buchanan was a frequent contributor to periodicals. He gained notoriety in October 1871 by writing an article for the *Contemporary Review* titled "The Fleshly School of Poetry" under the pseudonym Thomas Maitland. This critique was expanded into a pamphlet in 1872 and provoked responses from Mr. Swinburne and D.G. Rossetti. Rossetti responded in the *Athenaeum* with a letter titled "The Stealthy School of Criticism." Buchanan later regretted the violence of the attack and dedicated *God and the Man* to Rossetti, referring to him as the "old enemy."

His chief contributions to periodical literature are found in *David Gray and other Essays, chiefly on Poetry* (1868), *Master Spirits* (1873), *A Poet's Sketch Book* (1883), and *A Look round Literature* (1887). He also wrote *The Land of Lorne* (1871), a two-volume record of yachting on Scotland's west coast.
