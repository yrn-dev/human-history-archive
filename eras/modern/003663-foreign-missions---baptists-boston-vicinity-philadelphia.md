# Foreign Missions and Baptist Development in Boston, Philadelphia, and Vicinity

The development of Baptist foreign missions in the United States was characterized by an initial interest in international efforts, followed by the establishment of national organizational structures and educational institutions to support these endeavors.

## Early Missionary Interests and the India Mission
From the beginning of the 19th century, Baptist communities in Philadelphia, Boston and its vicinity, and Charleston, among others, demonstrated a deep interest in the missionary work conducted in India by the English missionary William Carey and his coadjutors, providing liberal financial support for these efforts.

A pivotal moment in American Baptist history occurred in 1812 with the conversion of Luther Rice and Adoniram Judson to Baptist views. Both men had been sent to open missionary work in India by the newly-formed American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions. Following his conversion, Judson appealed to his American brethren for support in missionary work among the heathen. Simultaneously, Rice returned to the United States to organize missionary societies intended to awaken interest in Judson's mission.

## Organizational Growth and the Triennial Convention
The movement toward organized missions accelerated in January 1813 with the formation of "The Baptist Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in India and other Foreign Parts" in Boston. This was quickly followed by the establishment of similar societies in the Southern, Middle, and Eastern states.

Recognizing the need for a national organization, thirty-three delegates from eleven states met in Philadelphia in May 1814. They organized the "General Missionary Convention of the Baptist Denomination in the United States of America for Foreign Missions." Because the convention's meetings were scheduled every three years, it became known as the "Triennial Convention." The organization appointed a Board of Commissioners, which was headquartered in Philadelphia until 1826, when the headquarters were transferred to Boston.

## Educational and Home Mission Expansion
The pursuit of foreign missions highlighted a profound need for a larger supply of educated ministers for both mission and home work. This led to the 1821 establishment of the theological department of Columbian College in Washington (now George Washington University), which was intended to serve as a national Baptist institution.

In addition to foreign efforts, the Triennial Convention began engaging extensively in home mission work from 1817 onward due to destitution on the frontiers. This work was further formalized in 1832 with the constitution of the American Baptist Home Mission Society. To disseminate information and increase interest in these educational and missionary enterprises, Luther Rice worked to establish an organ for communication.

## Regional Baptist Foundations
The missionary and organizational spirit of the 19th century was built upon earlier regional foundations in Philadelphia and Boston.

### Philadelphia and the Mid-Atlantic
Baptists were attracted to the Quaker colonies due to the presence of religious liberty. In 1684, Thomas Dungan, an Irish Baptist minister, helped found a church at Cold Spring in Bucks county, Pennsylvania. In 1688, Elias Keach helped form the Pennepek church. While services were held in Philadelphia under the Pennepek church from 1687, an independent organization was not established there until 1698.

In 1707, the Philadelphia Association was formed as a delegated body to maintain sound doctrine and set churches in order. This body became highly influential in promoting Baptist cooperation in educational and missionary work. By 1762, the Philadelphia Association began planning a denominational institution of learning, which eventually led to the 1764 chartering of the institution that became Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island.

### Boston and Massachusetts
Baptist presence in Massachusetts dates back to 1663, when John Myles and his congregation fled the government of Charles II to settle in Rehoboth. By May 1665, several English Baptists in the neighborhood of Boston, along with Thomas Gould of Charlestown, joined in a church covenant. These early Boston Baptists faced severe persecution, including fines and imprisonment, and were denied the use of their meeting-house. Despite a 1679 mandate from Charles II rebuking Massachusetts authorities for denying liberty to others, the Boston Baptists struggled for long after the 1689 Act of Toleration to gain recognition of their right to exist.
