# Communications in Bolivia

The development of communications in Bolivia has been historically shaped by its geographic isolation and a series of diplomatic treaties and conflicts with its neighbors, primarily Brazil, Chile, and Peru. Efforts to improve the country's infrastructure have focused on establishing railway connections to provide the isolated region with effective communication with the outside world and to revive mining and other industries.

## International Treaties and Railway Construction

In the early 20th century, Bolivia entered into significant agreements to expand its railway network. Under a 1903 treaty with Brazil, provisions were made to give north-eastern Bolivia access to the Amazon. Brazil agreed to construct a railway approximately 180 miles long around the falls of the Madeira. Additionally, Brazil paid L2,000,000 in cash, which Bolivia was designated to spend on railway construction within its own territory.

A separate treaty with Chile in 1904, ratified in 1905, focused on providing Bolivia with access to the coast. Chile agreed to build a railway from Arica to La Paz, spanning 295 miles; the section within Bolivia was to become Bolivian property fifteen years after its completion. Chile also committed to paying the interest (capped at 5%) that Bolivia might guarantee on capital invested in certain interior railways if constructed within thirty years. These interest payments were limited to L100,000 per year and L1,600,000 in total.

## Regional Railway Networks and Infrastructure

Prior to 1903, Bolivia's railway infrastructure was limited to a total of 430 miles. This included the Antofagasta and Oruro line (574 miles total, with 350 miles inside Bolivia), a 26-mile private branch to the Pulacayo mines, and a 54-mile line from Guaqui to Alto de La Paz. The latter is connected to the city of La Paz—which sits 1,493 feet below—by a 5-mile electric line. This system provides La Paz with access to the Peruvian port of Mollendo, located 496 miles away, and is expected to eventually provide communication with Cuzco.

Other regional contributions include a northern railway extension from Jujuy to the Bolivian frontier town of Tupiza, undertaken by Argentina. By 1907, several further lines were projected, including routes from La Paz to Cochabamba and the navigable waters of the Beni, from Viacha to Oruro, from Uyuni to Potosi, Sucre, and Tupiza, and from Arica to La Paz via Coro.

## Maritime Access and River Navigation

The loss of its maritime department during the war with Chile (1878–81) left Bolivia without direct seaports. Consequently, the country relies on the ports of Lake Titicaca (specifically Guaqui, which trades with Puno in Peru) and the affluents of the Paraguay and Madeira rivers. Because these routes require transhipment in foreign territory, transport costs are increased, and neighboring nations can exclude Bolivia from direct commercial intercourse with other countries.

Access to the Amazon via the Madeira river is currently hindered by a series of falls. The Bolivian port of entry, Villa Bella, is located above these falls at the confluence of the Mamore and Beni rivers, requiring a long and costly portage from the lower river. The railway planned by Brazil is intended to remedy this situation.

Other key river connections include:
* **Puerto Pacheco** on Bahia Negra.
* **Puerto Suarez** on Lake Caceres, which handles the bulk of trade in that direction and is about 1,600 miles from Buenos Aires by river.
* **Puerto Quijarro** on Lake Gaiba, a projected port.

Future direct river communication with Montevideo and Buenos Aires may be possible if the trade of southern Bolivia warrants the expense of opening a navigable channel in the Pilcomayo.

## Political and Territorial Context

The drive for improved communications was a priority for President Colonel Pando, who took office on October 26, 1899. Pando sought to improve the means of communication to revive the nation's industries while settling boundary disputes. 

One such dispute concerned the rubber-producing territory of Acre. Following a declaration of independence by the state of Acre and the dispatch of Bolivian troops in 1900, the matter was settled amicably via a November 1903 treaty. Bolivia ceded a portion of the Acre province to Brazil in exchange for a cash indemnity of $10,000,000. This treaty also modified the boundary line to provide Bolivia with better commercial facilities on the Paraguay river through a recession of approximately 1,158 square miles to Bolivia.

Regarding Chile, a 1895 treaty initially sought to ensure Bolivia's "free access to the sea," with Chile pledging to transfer Tacna and Arica to Bolivia if it acquired permanent sovereignty over them, in exchange for $5,000,000 silver. However, following secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina, Chile's attitude shifted. The final 1905 ratification forced Bolivia to cede the maritime provinces occupied since 1881, though it secured the Arica-to-La Paz railway and free transit through Chilean territory to certain coastal towns.
