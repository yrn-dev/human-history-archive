# Autopsy

An autopsy is a personal examination of a dead body, specifically conducted *post-mortem* (after death), for the purpose of ascertaining the cause of death. The term is derived from the Greek words *autos* (self) and *opsis* (sight, investigation). In some contexts, the term "necropsy," from the Greek *nekros* (corpse), is used to describe this process.

## Medical and Scientific Application
The practice of examining bodies after death has been utilized to advance the understanding of disease and physiology. For example, the physician Bichat used accurate dissection of bodies after death to study the history of diseases. By engaging in a series of examinations to determine the changes induced in various organs by disease, Bichat opened over six hundred bodies in less than six months.

In modern forensic practice, the Office of the Chief Medical Examiner may perform autopsies to determine both the cause and the manner of death. This process involves several specific medical investigations:

*   **Physical Trauma and Fractures:** Medical examiners analyze fracture patterns to distinguish between different types of death. For instance, the pattern of neck bone fractures can differ between manual compression of the neck and the sustained pressure associated with hanging.
*   **Skin and Vascular Indicators:** Examiners look for plethora (purple discoloration of the skin) and petechial hemorrhages (pinpoint bleeds in the skin caused by obstructed blood flow and bursting capillaries). The distribution of these indicators—such as whether they appear from the neck up or are limited to the eyes and mouth—can help distinguish between suicide by hanging and homicidal strangulation.
*   **Ligature Analysis:** The examination of a ligature furrow, including its width and the direction in which it peaks, provides evidence regarding the method of death.
*   **Toxicology:** Blood toxicology tests are conducted during the autopsy process to identify the presence of illegal substances or medications in the deceased's system.
*   **Signs of Struggle:** Examiners check for physical evidence of a struggle, such as bruises on the body, contusions to the knuckles, marks on the hands, or debris under the fingernails.

## Legal and Administrative Oversight
The investigation into sudden or violent deaths often involves legal officials known as coroners. In England, coroners have historically been categorized into three types: those holding office by virtue of their position (such as the lord chief justice of the king's bench), those appointed by charter or commission, and those chosen by election.

The duties of a coroner, as established by 4 Edward I. st. 2, include inquiring into instances where individuals are wounded, suddenly dead, or slain. Upon being commanded by honest men of the county or the king's bailiffs, the coroner proceeds to the location of the death and summons a group of four to six individuals from the nearest towns to testify under oath.

The administrative structure of the coroner's office has evolved over time. For example, the Local Government Act 1888 shifted the appointment of county coroners from freeholders to the county council. Additionally, an act of 1860 replaced a fee-based payment system with a salary based on a five-year average of fees, mileage, and allowances. In boroughs with a population exceeding 10,000 and a separate court of quarter sessions, the town council appoints the coroner, who continues to be paid by fees.
