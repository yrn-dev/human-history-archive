# The Columbia River and Regional Geography

The Columbia is a major river originating in the Rocky Mountains. Its course is characterized by a significant directional shift in its upper reaches before it eventually flows into the Pacific Ocean.

## Course and Path of the Columbia River
The Columbia rises in the Rocky Mountains, south of the point where the Fraser River begins. From its source, the river flows north for approximately 150 miles. During this northward stretch, it crosses the main line of the Canadian Pacific railway at Donald. After this point, the river bends abruptly and reverses its course, flowing south. 

As it moves southward, the Columbia recrosses the Canadian Pacific railway at Revelstoke. It continues through the Arrow Lakes located in the Kootenay country and enters the United States. The river eventually empties into the Pacific Ocean at Astoria, located in the state of Oregon. In its passage toward the Pacific, the Columbia cuts through the great mountain mass of the Cascades almost to sea-level; at a point known as the Dalles, the river is only about 100 feet above the sea. This location is noted for its tremendous rapids.

## Geological and Regional Context
The Columbia is one of several great rivers of the Pacific slope, including the Finlay, Fraser, and Kootenay. These rivers flow for portions of their upper courses through a remarkable valley of considerable geological antiquity located along the south-western side of the Rocky Mountains. This valley ranges from 1 to 6 miles in width and extends for a length of at least 800 miles.

The river's path is influenced by the dominant mountain systems of the region. In British Columbia, the landscape is defined by the Rocky Mountains to the north-east and the Coast Range to the south-west. Between these two systems lies the "Interior Plateau," an elevated tract of hilly country. The Columbia, along with the Fraser and Klamath rivers, cuts athwart the Cascade range to reach the Pacific.

## Transportation and Navigation
The rivers of this region, including the Columbia, are described as swift and frequently interrupted by rapids. Because of these physical characteristics, they are considered to be of indifferent value as means of communication for commercial purposes. The importance of these waterways as transport routes further diminishes wherever railway lines are constructed.

The Canadian Pacific railway is a primary infrastructure feature in the area. Its Pacific division enters British Columbia through the Rocky Mountains and runs for about 500 miles to its terminus at Vancouver. The Columbia's course is specifically marked by its two intersections with this railway line at Donald and Revelstoke.

## Related Coastal Geography
While the Columbia empties into the Pacific in Oregon, other rivers in the vicinity follow different paths. The Fraser River, for example, crosses the Coast Range via a deep, canton-like valley to empty into the Strait of Georgia. Further north, the Stikine River rises in British Columbia, but it crosses the coast strip of Alaska before it enters the Pacific Ocean.
