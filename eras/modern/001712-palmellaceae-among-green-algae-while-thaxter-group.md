# Palmellaceae and Related Biological Classifications

The Palmellaceae are a group of green algae that serve as a point of biological comparison for certain bacterial forms. Specifically, some bacteria, such as "Sarcina," possess algal analogues within the Palmellaceae.

## Bacterial Relationships and Algal Analogues
The schizomycetes, or bacteria, are minute vegetable organisms that lack chlorophyll and an ordinary nucleus, multiplying through repeated bipartitions. Because they lack ordinary sexual reproduction and a nucleus, they resemble the Schizophyceae (blue-green algae or Cyanophyceae). Consequently, these two groups are often united in the class Schizophyta, reflecting the view that most typical bacteria are derived from the Cyanophyceae.

However, the phylogeny of bacteria is considered doubtful as they may be a heterogeneous group. While some resemble Cyanophyceae, others show different affinities:
* **Palmellaceae:** As noted, "Sarcina" finds its algal analogues among the Palmellaceae of the green algae.
* **Myxomycetes:** The group Myxobacteriaceae, identified by Thaxter, suggests a relationship with the Myxomycetes.
* **Protozoa:** The formation of endospores and the existence of ciliated micrococci are characteristics not found in Cyanophyceae but are reminiscent of flagellate Protozoa, such as *Chromulina* and *Monas*.
* **Saccharomycetes:** Resemblances exist between bacterial endospores and the spore-formations in Saccharomycetes, particularly in species like *B. ventriculus* and *Bacillus inflatus* if they form more than one spore per cell.

## The Myxobacteriaceae
The Myxobacteriaceae represent a third division of bacteria, alongside the Trichobacterinae and Haplobacterinae. This group was founded by Thaxter, who in 1892 rediscovered *Chondromyces crocatus* (previously described by Berkeley in 1857 and misidentified as Hyphomycetes) and demonstrated its bacterial nature. Thaxter also described *Myxobacter*, which was later identified as *Polyangium vitellinum*, a form described by Link in 1795.

The distinguishing feature of Myxobacteriaceae is the formation of plasmodium-like aggregations. These bacteria build sporogenous structures of a definite form that superficially resemble the cysts of the Mycetozoa. These organisms are commonly found in the air and the alimentary canals of herbivorous animals, as well as on the dung of such animals. They are most easily obtained by maintaining exposed dung in the dark at temperatures between 30-35°C, a condition that favors bacterial growth while hindering the growth of common fungi.

## Other Biological Observations by Thaxter
Beyond the Myxobacteriaceae, the researcher Thaxter contributed to the understanding of other fungal and aquatic groups:

**Laboulbeniineae**
Thaxter conducted extensive research in America on the Laboulbeniineae, a group of approximately 150 species of fungi that live on insects, particularly beetles. These fungi consist of a small, dark brown erect receptacle (1-10 mm high) attached to the insect by a foot. The structure includes male organs (antheridia) that emit non-motile spermatia and female organs consisting of a procarp (composed of a trichogyne, a trichophoric cell, and a carpogenic cell). Thaxter demonstrated that spermatia fuse with the trichogyne, leading to the formation of asci containing ascospores. These plants are classified among the Ascomycetes.

**Monoblepharidaceae**
In the group of Phycomycetes, the genus *Monoblepharis* consists of aquatic forms living on fallen twigs in ponds and ditches. While first described by Cornu in 1871, the genus was lost sight of until 1895, when Roland Thaxter described several species from America.
