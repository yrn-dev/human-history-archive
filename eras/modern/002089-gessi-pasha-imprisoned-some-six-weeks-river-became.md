# Bahr-el-Ghazal

The Bahr-el-Ghazal is a river system characterized by its complex hydrography and susceptibility to natural obstructions. Like the Bahr-el-Jebel, the Bahr-el-Ghazal is prone to being choked by sudd. During the rule of the Mahdists, the accumulation of this obstruction became so severe that the river was almost blocked. Gessi Pasha was imprisoned in the sudd for approximately six weeks.

## Exploration and Cartography
Early Greek geographers, influenced by rumors of the Bahr-el-Ghazal's existence, imagined that the source of the Nile lay to the west in the direction of Lake Chad. The first map to indicate the course of the river with relative accuracy was published in 1772 by the French cartographer d'Anville. 

Following the ascent of the White Nile by Egyptian expeditions between 1839 and 1842, further exploration of the region took place. Between 1833 and 1865, John Petherick, a Welsh mining engineer, explored the Jur and the main stream of the Ghazal. In 1859, Giovanni Miani, a Venetian, penetrated the southern regions of the basin and provided the first reports of the Welle, a great river flowing west beyond the Nile watershed. This discovery was later confirmed between 1869 and 1871 by Georg Schweinfurth, who traversed the entire southern district.

Other notable surveys include:
* **Lejean (1862):** A Frenchman who surveyed the main river and published a map.
* **Alexandrine Tinne (1863):** Led a party including German scientists Hermann Steudner and Theodor von Heuglin to determine the western extent of the Nile basin. This expedition resulted in the deaths of five of its nine white members due to blackwater fever.
* **Gessi Pasha (1879-1881):** The Italian governor under General C. G. Gordon, who explored the river and its tributaries during efforts to destroy the slave trade in the Ghazal province.
* **Wilhelm Junker:** Explored the southern tributaries of the Ghazal around the same period as Gessi Pasha.

## Hydrography and Tributaries
The region's nomenclature has historically been a source of confusion, partly because different tribes use different names for the same streams. For example, the Lol is also referred to as the Kir. Additionally, the lower course of the Bahr-el-Homr is also called the Kir. There was a long-held belief in a third large river flowing between the Lol and the Bahr-el-Homr, also called the Kir, but this has been proven to be merely the lower course of the Lol of Bahr-el-Arab.

The Jur (also known as the Sue) was followed throughout its course by Lieutenant A. H. Dye and other members of a French mission led by Colonel J. B. Marchand. This mission reached Fashoda on the White Nile in July 1898 after crossing from the Congo in October 1897. Between 1905 and 1907, British naval officer R. C. Bayldon, along with Lieut. D. Comyn and Capt. C. Percival, explored the western and northern affluents of the Ghazal to clarify the area's puzzling nomenclature and hydrography.

## British Interventions and Navigation
Beginning in 1901 and continuing in subsequent years, British officers removed the sudd from the Jur, the Bahr-el-Ghazal, and other rivers. These efforts established uninterrupted steamboat communication during the flood season between Wau and Khartum, which are separated by a distance of approximately 930 meters.
