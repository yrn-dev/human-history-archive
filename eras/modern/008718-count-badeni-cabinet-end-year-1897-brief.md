# Count Badeni's Cabinet and the Language Ordinances of 1897

The cabinet of Count Badeni, serving in the Austrian government during the late 19th century, was defined by its attempts to manage national tensions within the empire, specifically regarding the status of the Bohemian language. These efforts culminated in a political crisis that led to the cabinet's collapse at the end of 1897.

## Political Context and Strategy
Following an election, Count Badeni submitted his resignation, which the emperor refused to accept. Consequently, Badeni sought political support from various nationalities to sustain his government. He focused on reaching agreements with the Poles—who were consistently supportive of the government—and the Young Czechs. Among the Young Czechs, moderate members were willing to negotiate to gain influence over the administration, provided the government granted further concessions regarding the use of language in Bohemia.

## The Language Ordinances of 1897
In May 1897, Badeni published a series of celebrated ordinances intended to remedy serious grievances in Bohemia and conciliate the region, although he did not consider it feasible to reopen the question of Bohemian autonomy. These ordinances established two primary mandates:

1. All documents and correspondence regarding any matter brought before government officials were to be conducted in the language in which they were first introduced. This effectively introduced the Czech language into government offices throughout the entire kingdom of Bohemia.
2. A requirement was set that after 1903, no individual could be appointed to a government post in Bohemia without passing an examination in Czech.

Earlier in 1897, Badeni had also issued a decree stating that, after a specific date, all government officials wishing to be employed in Bohemia would need to prove knowledge of both the German and Bohemian languages.

## Opposition and Parliamentary Paralysis
The ordinances were met with violent opposition from German Nationalists and Radicals, who viewed the measures as a threat. These groups declared that no official business should be conducted until the ordinances were withdrawn and Badeni was dismissed. The opposition utilized obstruction tactics in parliament, including repeated motions to impeach the ministers. 

Because no business could be transacted, parliament was prorogued in June. Badeni, a Pole, had not anticipated the severity of the reaction, as he possessed little experience in the western part of the empire. During the subsequent recess, the Germans refused to enter negotiations unless the ordinances were repealed. The agitation expanded across the country, resulting in serious disturbances and large meetings in Aussig and Eger, which were attended by Germans from across the frontier.

## Fall of the Cabinet and Aftermath
The feud between the Germans and Czechs, stemming directly from the 1897 language ordinances and indirectly from the growth of Slav influence (specifically Poles and Czechs) during the Taaffe era (1879-1893), paralyzed parliamentary life in Austria. This conflict caused the fall of Count Badeni's cabinet at the end of 1897.

Following a brief interval, Badeni was succeeded by Count Thun, and subsequently by Count Clary. The government of Count Clary repealed the decrees that had granted equal rights to the Bohemian language, which triggered troubles in Prague that were severely repressed by Austrian authorities. In the following ministries of Gautsch and Korber, Bohemians continued to assert their national rights and oppose the central government in Vienna.

## Impact on Austrian Governance
The crisis contributed to a period where political life in Austria was at a low ebb. During the closing years of the 19th century and the opening years of the 20th, the Austrian government was carried on by cabinets of officials utilizing paragraph 14 (the emergency clause) of the constitution. While ministers were nominally responsible to parliament, they were in practice responsible only to the emperor, leading to a situation where the constitution was observed in letter rather than in spirit.
