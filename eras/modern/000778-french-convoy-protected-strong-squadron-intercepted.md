# Interception of the French Convoy (1747)

During the naval conflict between Great Britain and France in the mid-18th century, the British government adopted a more energetic and coherent naval policy from 1747 until the war's conclusion in October 1748. This strategy involved maintaining a closer watch on the French coast and implementing effective measures to intercept communications between France and its American possessions.

## The Bay of Biscay Engagement
On October 14, 1747, a French convoy protected by a strong squadron was intercepted in the Bay of Biscay. The intercepting force was a well-directed and well-appointed British squadron of superior numbers. The engagement involved eight French ships and fourteen British ships.

The French forces were commanded by Admiral Desherbiers de l'Etenduere, who offered a very gallant resistance. The high quality of the French ships allowed Desherbiers to partially counteract the superior numbers of the British admiral, Sir Edward Hawke. While the warships were engaged in battle, the merchant vessels of the convoy continued toward the West Indies, supported by the small amount of protection Desherbiers could spare for them.

## Outcome and Aftermath
Despite the resistance of the escort squadron, most of the merchant vessels were subsequently intercepted and captured in West Indian waters. This disaster led the French government to recognize its helplessness at sea, and as a result, it made no further naval efforts.

This event followed an earlier British success in the spring of 1747. After the British government had allowed a French expedition under M. d'Anville to fail the previous year due to its own weakness, they took a more creditable approach in 1747. An overwhelming force under the command of Anson was employed in the Channel to intercept an important convoy destined for the East and West Indies sailing from L'Orient. That convoy was met, crushed, and either captured or driven back on May 3.

## Related Naval Operations of 1748
The final naval operations of this period occurred in the West Indies involving British and Spanish forces. A British squadron under Sir Charles Knowles attacked the coast of Cuba, while Admiral Regio commanded a Spanish naval force at Havana. Both sides sought to protect their own trade and intercept the other's. The British were particularly motivated by the prospect of capturing the Spanish homeward-bound convoy, which was expected to be laden with bullion from American mines.

On October 1, 1748, the two squadrons met in the Bahama Channel. Although the action was indecisive compared to later British fleet successes, the advantage lay with Sir Charles Knowles. However, he was unable to follow up this advantage due to the arrival of news that peace had been established in Europe.

The peace was arranged on terms of mutual restoration of conquests, indicating that no combatant had established final superiority. The French were able to treat on equal terms due to their military successes in Flanders and their conquests in the Bay of Bengal, while nothing had been taken from Spain.
