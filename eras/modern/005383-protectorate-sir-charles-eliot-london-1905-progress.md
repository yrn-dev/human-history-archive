# The East Africa Protectorate

The East Africa Protectorate was a British administrative territory established following the formal transfer of land from the Imperial British East Africa Company (known as "Ibea") to the British crown on July 1, 1895. This transfer took place at Mombasa, with the foreign office assuming responsibility for the administration. The protectorate included the small sultanate of Witu on the mainland opposite Lamu, which had previously been a German protectorate from 1885 to 1890.

## Early Administration and Political Transition
The early years of the protectorate were marked by a significant shift in regional influence. Coincident with the transfer to the imperial government, a revolt broke out due to a dispute over the succession to a chieftainship in the Mazrui, the most prominent Arab family on the coast. The conflict involved intense fighting and lasted ten months, concluding in April 1896 when the rebel leaders fled to German territory. Sir Charles Eliot noted that this suppression of the rebellion was a pivotal epoch, as it effectively substituted European influence for Arab influence; he described the coast as having been a "protected Arab state" before the rebellion and growing into a "British colony" afterward.

Administrative oversight evolved over the following decade. In April 1905, the administration of the protectorate was transferred from the foreign office to the colonial office. By 1907, the title of the chief official was changed from "commissioner" to "governor."

## Land Disputes and the Resignation of Sir Charles Eliot
Sir Charles Eliot served as commissioner from 1900. During 1903, the arrival of hundreds of prospective settlers, primarily from South Africa, prompted a decision to stop accepting applications for large areas of land. This policy was driven partly by concerns regarding the preservation of the Masai people's rights of pasturage.

A conflict subsequently arose between Sir Charles Eliot and the foreign secretary, Lord Lansdowne. Lord Lansdowne believed he was bound by pledges to a syndicate and decided to grant them a lease of 500 square miles. Simultaneously, he refused Sir Charles Eliot's request to conclude leases of 50 square miles each for two South African applicants. Sir Charles Eliot resigned his post on June 21, 1904, stating in a public telegram to the prime minister that he refused to execute instructions that he considered "unjust and impolitic," accusing Lord Lansdowne of granting a monopoly of land on "unduly advantageous terms" to the East Africa Syndicate while refusing grants to private individuals.

## Subsequent Governance and Colonization
Following the resignation of Sir Charles Eliot, Sir Donald W. Stewart, the chief commissioner of Ashanti, was appointed as his successor. Sir Donald arranged a settlement of land claims and induced the Masai, whose grazing rights had been threatened, to move to a different district. Sir Donald Stewart died on October 1, 1905, and was succeeded by Colonel Hayes Sadler, the commissioner of Uganda.

Efforts to expand colonization continued during this period. In August 1905, the Zionist Association declined an offer of 6,000 square miles of land for colonization after a commissioner's report on the land was received. By the end of 1905, the protectorate authorities had sold or leased over one million acres of land, approximately half of which was used for grazing.

## Legislative and Demographic Developments
To address the needs of an increasing number of white inhabitants, who had formed a Colonists' Association to promote their interests, a legislative council was established in 1907. This council included seats for representatives of the settlers. 

In the same year, a scheme was developed to assist the immigration of British Indians. These immigrants were intended for regions adjacent to the coast and to Victoria Nyanza, as these districts were considered unsuitable for European settlement.

## Documentation and Resources
The progress and resources of the protectorate have been documented through various official and academic sources. Sir Charles Eliot's 1905 work, *The East Africa Protectorate*, is cited as the most comprehensive account of the protectorate's economic resources up to the close of 1904. Other key records include:
* Annual *Reports* by the governor issued by the British government since 1896.
* The *Précis of Information* concerning the British East Africa Protectorate (1901), issued by the War Office.
* *Drumkey's Year Book for East Africa*, first issued in 1908.
* The *Final Report of the Uganda Railway Committee* (1904), which summarizes the construction of the Uganda railway.

Historical perspectives on the region's foundation and company rule are provided by J.W. Gregory in *The Foundation of British East Africa* (1901) and P.L. McDermont in *British East Africa or Ibea* (1895). Additional specialized records include Joseph Thomson's *Through Masai Land* (1886) regarding explorers, Bishop A.R. Tucker's *Eighteen Years in Uganda and East Africa* (1908) regarding missionary work, and various geological reports by J.W. Gregory, Hans Meyer, and H.B. Muff.
