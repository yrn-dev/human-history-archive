# Barracks and Military Accommodations

Barracks are buildings used for the accommodation of naval or military forces. These structures include quarters for men, non-commissioned officers, warrant officers, and officers, as well as accessory buildings such as regimental offices, stores, shops, vehicle sheds, stables, and recreation establishments and messes. While the term typically refers to permanent brick or stone structures used for peace occupation, hut barracks made of wood-lined corrugated iron are also used, often in connection with training grounds. These hut barracks provide less accommodation than permanent structures, with conditions closer to a military encampment.

## European Systems of Accommodation

### German and French Barracks
A significant number of French and German barracks are constructed as large blocks of three or four storeys. These single buildings contain all necessary accessories and accommodation for a complete regiment or battalion, including single and married non-commissioned officers, officers, and men. This single-block system is favored for its architectural appeal, compactness, and the ease of supervision it provides. However, some modern barracks in these countries utilize a pavilion system consisting of separate blocks.

The arrangement and allotment of accommodation for the French and German armies are similar to the British system, though they differ in detail. The cubic and floor space allotted per man is slightly less. Unless located on a country site, accommodation for officers is generally not provided except to a limited extent, although the German army now provides every regiment with a public-funded officers' mess-house. Married quarters are available for some non-commissioned officers, but not for privates.

### British Barracks
In modern British infantry battalions, unmarried soldiers are housed in barrack-rooms typically designed for twelve men. This arrangement is preferred over larger rooms as it provides more privacy while maintaining comradeship. Each man is allotted 600 cubic feet of air. Rooms feature windows on both sides, with beds spaced so that no more than two are between any two windows. Ventilation is managed via high inlet ventilators and an extract shaft warmed by a chimney flue. Each soldier has a shelf and locker over their bed for their kit, with their rifle placed near the head of the bed.

Ablution rooms with foot-baths and basins are located near the barrack-room door, while water-closets and urinals are separated by a lobby or located in external blocks. Baths are typically found in a central bath-house near the cook-house. To keep the air in sleeping quarters pure, food is served in lofty, well-ventilated dining rooms warmed by hot water. Additionally, a small single room is provided for the sergeant in charge for every two to four barrack-rooms.

### American Barracks
American barracks are noted for a superior internal finish and design, catering to a potentially higher class of recruit. These facilities usually follow the separate block system. They are centered around a soldiers' club or post-exchange, which serves as a combined gymnasium, sergeants' mess, and recreation establishment with an attached bath-house. Canteens that sold liquor were abolished in 1901.

## Military Use and Adaptation During War

During wartime, barracks may be repurposed for other needs. In some instances, barracks have been converted into clearing hospitals. In Treves, for example, barracks, schools, and churches were requisitioned for hospital use. In such settings, wounded soldiers—including French prisoners—may be treated. Some wounded soldiers have expressed a preference for remaining in hospitals to avoid being treated as ordinary prisoners in concentration camps or barracks alongside other prisoners, such as Indians, Moroccans, and Senegalese negroes.

Additionally, existing fortifications can be converted into military housing. The French citadel at Lille, for instance, served as a German barracks, housing German soldiers, military cars, and captured guns.

## International and Colonial Barracks

### German East Africa
In German East Africa, barracks were established in various seaports. In Bagamoyo, a town of approximately 18,000 people that served as a trade entrepot and starting point for caravans to the great lakes, the buildings include barracks, a mosque, a Hindu temple, a government school for natives, and the residence of the administrator. In Kilwa Kivinje, the government house and barracks are fortified and surrounded by public gardens.

### China
Following the Boxer Protocol of 1901, Germany held various properties in China. However, Germany later renounced privileges and indemnities resulting from that protocol, including the return of barracks for munitions of warships, wireless plants, wharves, and other public property in the German concessions of Hankow and Tientsin, as well as other Chinese territories (excluding Kiao-Chau).
