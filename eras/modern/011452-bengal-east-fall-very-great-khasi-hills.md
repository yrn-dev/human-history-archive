# Khasi and Jaintia Hills

The Khasi and Jaintia Hills constitute a district of British India located within the Hills division of Eastern Bengal and Assam. The region occupies a central plateau situated between the valleys of the Surma and the Brahmaputra. Covering an area of 6,027 square miles, the district had a population of 202,250 according to the 1901 census, reflecting a 2% increase over the preceding decade.

## Geography and Natural Divisions
The district is characterized by a succession of elevated table-lands separated by steep ridges that run east and west. On the southern side, the mountains rise precipitously from the Surma or Barak valley. The region features multiple plateaus: the first is approximately 4,000 feet above sea level, while a second, higher plateau houses the station of Shillong at 4,900 feet. Behind Shillong lies the Shillong range, which reaches a highest peak of 6,450 feet. To the north, towards Kamrup, there are two additional plateaus of lower elevation.

The general landscape of these table-lands resembles undulating downs covered in grass, though they lack large timber. Vegetation varies by elevation; at 3,000 feet, indigenous pine forests predominate. The highest ridges contain magnificent clumps of timber trees, including chestnuts, magnolias, and oaks, located in sacred groves that have been preserved from wood-cutters due to superstition. These groves also support wild cinnamon, rhododendrons, and rare orchids. The area's water systems consist of mountain torrents that often flow through narrow gorges.

## Climate and Rainfall
The Khasi range is noted for experiencing the heaviest rainfall known to occur anywhere in the world. This is caused by the range presenting an abrupt front to moist, vapour-bearing winds flowing up from the Bay of Bengal. Located about 100 miles north-east of Calcutta, the hills flank Bengal on the east, where the rainfall is very great.

At an elevation of approximately 4,500 feet, the ten-year average rainfall exceeds 550 inches. Records indicate that as much as 150 inches has been measured in a single month, and 610 inches in one year.

## Economy and Resources
The Khasi Hills have served as a source of lime for Lower Bengal since time immemorial, with inexhaustible quarries located along the southern slopes. Additionally, coal of fair quality is found in several locations, and the district contains a few small coal-mines.

## People and Administration
The region is inhabited by the Khasis, a peculiar race speaking a language of the Mon-Anam family. They follow a rule of matriarchal succession and are known for erecting monolithic monuments over their dead. In 1901, the Khasis numbered 107,500. They live in primitive communities under elective chiefs known as *Siems*. There are 25 such chiefs who exercise independent jurisdiction and pay no tribute, though they remain in political subordination to the British government.

The Jaintia Hills, inhabited by the Syntengs (a cognate tribe to the Khasis), formerly existed as a petty Hindu principality. Following the British conquest of the Khasi Hills in 1833 and the annexation of the Jaintia Hills in 1835, the Syntengs were subjected to a moderate income tax. This led to rebellions in 1860 and 1862, which were suppressed by the Khasi and Jaintia Expedition of 1862-63.

In 1901, the district included 17,321 Christians, primarily converts of the Welsh Calvinistic Mission.

## Infrastructure and History
The headquarters of the district were moved from Cherrapunji to Shillong in 1864, and Shillong subsequently became the capital of the province of Assam. A cart-road measuring 97 miles in total length runs north from Cherrapunji through Shillong to Gauhati on the Brahmaputra.

The district was the center of a major earthquake on June 12, 1897. The disaster destroyed every permanent building, caused numerous landslips, and broke up the roads. While the immediate loss of life was recorded at 916, hundreds more died later due to a malignant fever.
