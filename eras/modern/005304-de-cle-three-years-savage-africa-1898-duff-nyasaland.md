# British Central Africa and Related Works

## Overview of British Central Africa
British Central Africa is a general designation for British protectorates located in South Central Africa, north of the Zambezi river. More specifically, it refers to a large territory bounded by Lake Tanganyika at 8° 25' S. and the river Shiré near its confluence with the Zambezi at 17° 6' S., and extending between 26° 30' E. (the river Luengwe-Kafukwe) and 36° 10' E. (the district of Mlanje). 

The western boundary of this region is defined by a line running from near the source of the Lualaba on the Belgian Congo's southern boundary to the western source of the Luanga river. From there, the boundary follows the Luanga to its junction with the Luengwe-Kafukwe, with the main course of the Kafukwe serving as the delimiter down to the Zambezi. This region encompasses the Nyasaland Protectorate, North Eastern Rhodesia, and a portion of North Western Rhodesia.

## Administrative History and Divisions
The term "British Central Africa" was originally applied by Sir H.H. Johnston to all territories under British influence north of the Zambezi intended for a single administration. However, because Barotseland and other Rhodesian territories could not be connected with the direct British administration north of the Zambezi, the name was officially confined in 1893 to the British protectorate surrounding Lake Nyasa and the Shiré. In 1907, the official title of this protectorate was changed to the Nyasaland Protectorate.

The political divisions of the region are as follows:
*   **Nyasaland Protectorate:** This area, comprising the Shiré province and districts around Lake Nyasa, is administered by a governor under the orders of the colonial office. The governor is supported by an executive council and a nominated legislative council of at least three members.
*   **North Eastern and North Western Rhodesia:** These western districts are governed by two administrators from the British South Africa Chartered Company, who act in consultation with the colonial office and the governor of Nyasaland.

## Regional History and Exploration
European penetration of the region began with vague Portuguese wanderings in the 16th and 17th centuries, followed by the journey of Portuguese official Dr F.J.M. de Lacerda e Almeida from Tete on the Zambezi to the vicinity of Lake Mweru. The modern history of the area began in 1859 when David Livingstone discovered Lake Nyasa after penetrating the Shiré river. Livingstone's further explorations of Lake Mweru, Lake Bangweulu (where he died in 1873), and the south end of Tanganyika established British interests in the region.

Following Livingstone's death, missionary societies and Scottish settlers entered the area to begin evangelization and oppose Portuguese expansion from the Zambezi and Moçambique provinces. This period saw the rise of the African Lakes Trading Corporation, which entered a conflict with Arabs settled at the north end of Lake Nyasa. This struggle for possession of the country lasted from approximately 1885 until 1896. During this unofficial war, the Corporation enlisted volunteers, including Mr. (later Sir) Alfred Sharpe and Captain (later Sir F.D.) Lugard, both of whom were wounded. In 1889, Sir H.H. Johnston was dispatched to resolve the dispute between the Arabs and the Corporation and to protect friendly native chiefs from Portuguese aggression, leading to the creation of the British protectorate and sphere of influence north of the Zambezi.

## Inhabitants and Language
The indigenous population is of the Negro type, specifically belonging to the Bantu family. While the population is generally black or chocolate-coloured, the physique of the native Awankonde at the north end of Lake Nyasa may recall the Nilotic negro. There are legends of unmixed Bushman tribes in mountain districts such as Mount Mlanje, and Bushman stone implements have been discovered at the south end of Tanganyika. Additionally, Arabs from Zanzibar settled in the country starting no earlier than the beginning of the 19th century.

Linguistically, the region features various Bantu speech forms. In the plateau country between the river Luapula, the south end of Lake Tanganyika, and the north end of Lake Nyasa, the dialects are of an archaic type. Kibemba (or Kiwemba) and the Subiya of southern Barotseland are noted as being close to the original Bantu mother-language. These languages are connected to the Bantu languages of Uganda via dialects in the Kafukwe basin and northern Nyasaland.

## Geography and Fauna
The region's river nomenclature is noted as being perplexing. The Luanga (or Lunga) is a tributary of the Luengwe-Kafukwe (often called the Kafue), whereas the Luangwa (or Loangwa) is an independent affluent of the Zambezi. 

The local fauna includes the common or true duiker (*Cephalophus grimmi*), a small to medium-sized antelope found in bush-country ranging from the Cape to Nyasaland and the Zambezi, extending north to Angola.
