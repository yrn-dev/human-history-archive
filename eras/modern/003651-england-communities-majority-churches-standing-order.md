# England Communities a Majority in the Churches of the Standing Order

The term "churches of the standing order" refers to the dominant religious establishments in colonial New England. In many of these communities, the majority within these churches maintained a position of authority and often opposed new religious movements, particularly during the period of the "Great Awakening" between 1740 and 1743.

## Opposition to the Great Awakening
In several New England communities, the majority in the churches of the standing order bitterly opposed the new evangelism associated with the Great Awakening. This opposition led individuals who were influenced by the movement to feel constrained to organize their own separate entities, known as "New Light" or "Separate" churches. 

These "Separate" churches faced severe persecution from the dominant party. The persecution was so significant that the "Separates" were denied even the limited privileges that Baptist groups had managed to obtain. The primary conflict between the "Separates" and the churches of the standing order centered on the requirement for membership; the "Separates" objected to the standing order's refusal to insist upon personal regeneration as a necessary term for membership.

## Transition to Baptist Views
The insistence on regenerate membership led many in the "Separate" churches to question the consistency of administering baptism to unconscious infants. In several instances, entire "Separate" churches concluded that infant baptism lacked Scriptural warrant and viewed it as a primary cornerstone of "state-churchism." Consequently, these congregations transformed themselves into Baptist churches.

## Persecution and the Standing Order in Massachusetts
The intolerance of the authorities and the standing order was evident in various Massachusetts communities throughout the 17th century. In 1651, Clarke, Joseph Crandall, and Obadiah Holmes of the Newport church held a meeting in a private house in Lynn, Massachusetts. They were arrested and forced to attend the church services of the standing order. Because they held an unlawful meeting and refused to participate quietly in the public service, they were imprisoned, fined, and maltreated.

Similarly, in 1663, John Myles and his Baptist congregation settled in Rehoboth, Massachusetts. They were permitted to remain only on the condition that their meeting-place be established at a considerable distance from that of the standing order. In Boston, a church formed in May 1665 by English Baptists and others who had adopted Baptist views faced severe persecution. Members were frequently fined and imprisoned, and they were denied the use of a building they had constructed as a meeting-house. Despite the Act of Toleration of 1689 in England and a 1679 mandate from Charles II rebuking Massachusetts authorities for denying liberty of conscience, the Boston Baptists struggled to gain recognition of their right to exist.

## Religious Declension and Institutional Shifts
The decades preceding the Great Awakening were characterized by religious declension, where evangelistic efforts were paralyzed by Socinianized Arminianism. This shift affected several prominent churches:
* **The First Church, Providence:** Became Arminian and remained aloof from the evangelism of Whitefield and Edwards.
* **The First Church, Boston:** Became Socinianized and discountenanced the revival.
* **The First Church, Newport:** Was split by Arminianism; the remaining nominally Calvinistic group was divided over the laying on of hands and showed no sympathy for the Great Awakening.
* **The First Church, Charleston:** Was wrecked by Socinianism.

During this era, some individuals within the established academic and religious order also shifted their views. Henry Dunster, the first president of the college at Cambridge (Harvard), became convinced by 1653 that baptism should be reserved for "visible believers only." Due to his refusal to hide these views, he left his position in 1654 under circumstances of considerable hardship.
