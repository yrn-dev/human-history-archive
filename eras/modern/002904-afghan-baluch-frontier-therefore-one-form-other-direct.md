# Baluchistan and the Afghan-Baluch Frontier

Baluchistan is a region within the borders of British India, named after its dominant race of inhabitants. Geographically, it extends from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea, and from the borders of Afghanistan and Persia to those of Sind and the Punjab.

## Administrative Divisions
The territory is divided into two primary divisions. British Baluchistan is a portion of British India under a chief commissioner, covering an area of 9,403 sq. m. This area consists mainly of tracts ceded to the British government by Afghanistan via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879 and was formally declared part of British India in 1887.

The second division consists of foreign territories under the superintendence of the same officer acting as agent to the governor-general. These territories are further split into three subdivisions:
*   **Directly Administered Districts:** These include the eastern portion of the Quetta tahsil, the Bolan Pass, and the entirety of the Chagai and Zhob political agencies, totaling 36,401 sq. m. Some of these areas are tribal lands where revenue is collected, while others are held on lease from the khan of Kalat.
*   **Native States:** Covering 79,382 sq. m., this includes Las Bela and the state of Kalat, which encompasses Kharan and Makran.
*   **Tribal Areas:** These cover 7,129 sq. m. and are in the possession of the Bugti and Marri tribes.

Due to the administration of the northern boundary, the north-eastern corner, and the railway running through Quetta to New Chaman on the Afghan-Baluch frontier, these areas are under direct British control in one form or another.

## The Bolan Pass and Infrastructure
The Bolan Pass is a critical route on the Baluch frontier that connects Sibi and Jacobabad with Quetta. It has played a significant role in British campaigns in Afghanistan. Following the 1879 treaty of Gandamak, the route came under direct British control and was chosen for the first alignment of the Sind-Pishin railway. 

The railway originally followed the Bolan stream from Sibi south-west to the plateau, but floods forced the abandonment of this path. The line now follows the Mashkaf valley, joining the Bolan at Mach. While an alternative route exists through the Harnai valley (starting at Nari), the Harnai route is used for ordinary traffic, while the Bolan loop is reserved for emergencies. The pass is characterized by extreme geography, including the Khundilani gorge with 800 ft. conglomerate cliffs and the Sir-i-Bolan, where limestone rocks narrow the passage to barely three riders abreast. The climate is extreme, with very high summer temperatures and destructive ice-cold winds in winter. Since the founding of the Quetta agency in 1877, the pass has been secured from Baluch marauders, specifically the Marris.

## Frontier Districts and Borderlands
To the east and north, several districts mark the transition between Baluch and Pathan territories.

**Dera Ghazi Khan** is a long, narrow strip of 5,306 sq. m. sloping from western hills to the Indus river. The population in 1901 was 471,149, mostly Baluch Mahommedans. The geographical boundary between the Baluch and Pathan races in the hills roughly corresponds to the northern limit of this district. Frontier tribes here include the Bozdars, Khosas, Lagharis, Gurchanis, Mazaris, Mariris, Bugtis, Kasranis, and Khetvans.

**Dera Ismail Khan** is a town and district of 3,403 sq. m. located near the right bank of the Indus. The town is named after Ismail Khan, a Baluch chief who settled there in the late 15th century. In 1901, the population was 252,379, consisting chiefly of Baluch and Pathan Mahommedans. The district serves as a borderland for Pathan and Baluch tribes, though the Pathan element predominates. The Sheranis and Ustaranas are the primary frontier tribes. The town features a large bazaar for Afghan traders and maintains through trade with Afghanistan via the Gomal Pass.

**Bannu** is a district of 1,680 sq. m. lying north of the Indus. Its population in 1901 was 231,485, mostly Mahommedans. The region is inhabited by the Marwats (a Pathan race), the Waziri Pathans, and the Bannuchis (a mongrel Afghan tribe). The frontier is marked by the Dawaris, Battannis, and Waziris.

## The Baluch People
The Baluch are not a homogeneous race, but a mixture of people of Persian, Dravidian, and Arab extraction. The Rind Baluchis, such as the Bugti and Marri tribes of the Suliman Mountains, are considered to be of Arab extraction. 

Compared to the Afridi Pathan, the Baluch is described as less turbulent, less bloodthirsty, and less fanatical, owing to a tribal organization and a lack of blind allegiance to priests. Physically, the Baluch is shorter and more wiry than the Pathan, though chiefs of the Bugti or Marri clans are noted for their stately dignity and Semitic features resembling the Bedouin. Culturally, they are described as frank, faithful to their word, and viewing courage as the highest virtue. They are expert riders, and the Baluch breed of horses is celebrated in northern India. While they are first-rate fighting men and traditional bandits, they rarely enlist in the Indian army. As husbandmen, they are considered poor due to a lack of industry and a pride that views manual labor as degrading.
