# Bristol

Bristol is an English seaport, city, municipal, county, and parliamentary borough located approximately 118½ miles west of London. It is situated primarily in Gloucestershire, though it extends partly into Somersetshire. The city is positioned in a basin-like hollow roughly 4 miles in diameter, with the Avon river forming the boundary between Somerset and Gloucestershire. The city's nucleus rests on a narrow valley-floor where the Avon is confined by considerable hills; to the west, the valley becomes a gorge crossed by the Clifton Suspension Bridge, and to the north, the city extends up the heights.

## History and Governance
Bristol's growth is attributed almost entirely to trade rather than its role as a military post, a shire town, or the site of a great religious house. While near Roman and British camps, there is no evidence of settlements from those eras. A Saxon settlement began to grow around the year 1000 at the junction of the Avon and Frome rivers. Under Danish rule during the reign of Canute, Bristol became a market for English slaves and began a wool trade with Ireland. By the time of the Domesday survey, it was a royal borough governed by a king-appointed reeve.

Following the Conquest, the town and castle were granted to Robert FitzHamon. His daughter Mabel brought Bristol as a dowry to Earl Robert of Gloucester in 1119. Earl Robert strengthened the castle and used it as a base to harry the neighborhood and sell prisoners as slaves to Ireland. 

The city's governance shifted from manorial to municipal over time. A charter granted by John in 1190 allowed burgesses to hold by burgage tenure and prohibited non-burgesses from selling corn, wool, cloth, or leather in Bristol. Around this time, a commune was established on a French model, and in 1200, Robert FitzNichol became the first mayor. The mayor was elected by the merchants of the merchant gild, the first documentary evidence of which appears in 1242. The city also hosted many craft gilds, including those of fullers, tuckers, and weavers, as well as the Gild of the Kalendars, which focused on social, educational, and religious work.

## Trade and Harbour
The city's economy has historically been driven by trade. Early industries included tanning and soap-making, with wool exports to the Baltic and a wine trade with southern France. 

The Bristol harbour was created in 1809 by converting a branch of the Frome and the Avon into "the Float," involving the creation of two basins and a new channel for the Avon. The water area at a fixed level is about 85 acres, with four dry docks. The port extends from the mouth of the river to Hanham Mills on the Avon and down the Severn estuary. In 1884, the city acquired the Portishead and Avonmouth docks. 

The Royal Edward dock at Avonmouth, constructed by the corporation in 1902 and opened by King Edward VII in 1908, is accessible to the largest vessels. It features a lock 875 feet long and 100 feet wide. Portishead dock on the Somerset shore covers 12 acres. Bristol maintains significant trade with the West Indies and America, importing cattle, hides, timber, ore, oils, fruit, and grain, while exporting salt, tin, cotton goods, manufactured oils, and machinery.

## Open Spaces and Landmarks
The most extensive open spaces are the Clifton Downs and Durdham, which border the river near the gorge on the Gloucestershire side. Other notable parks include:
* **Victoria Park**: Located south of the river near Bedminster station.
* **Eastville Park**: Located by the Frome, north-east of the city.
* **St Andrew's Park**: Located to the north near Montpelier station.
* **Brandon Hill**: An eminence west of the cathedral providing a view over the city. It features a modern tower commemorating John Cabot and his sons (Sanctus, Sebastian, and Lewis) and the fourth centenary of the discovery of America.

Other city memorials include statues of Samuel Morley (1888), Queen Victoria (1888), Edmund Burke (1894), and Edward Colston (1895), the latter of whom is honored with annual banquets. The High Cross on College Green was established in 1850.

## Education and Institutions
Bristol's educational landscape includes University College (1876), which served as the nucleus for the university of Bristol, chartered in 1909. Other institutions include:
* **Clifton College**: Opened in 1862 and incorporated in 1877, featuring an observatory, museum, and physical science school.
* **Company of Merchant Venturers Technical College**: Established in 1885 to provide commercial, technical, and scientific education; its buildings were destroyed by fire in 1906.
* **Colston's girls' day school**: Opened in 1891, offering calisthenics and domestic economy.

Charitable institutions include the Royal Victoria home, the Queen Victoria jubilee convalescent home, the royal hospital for women and sick children, and a general hospital opened in 1858.

## Infrastructure and Architecture
The city is served by the Midland railway (connecting to the north of England via Derby, Birmingham, Worcester, and Gloucester) and the Great Western railway (connecting to London, the southern and south-western counties, and South Wales via the Severn tunnel). Both companies utilize Temple Meads as the central station.

Architecturally, the old parts of Bristol contain early examples of half timber work, consisting of timber frameworks with fillings of brickwork, plaster, or tiles.
