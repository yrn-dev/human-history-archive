# The Compacts

In historical and legal contexts, the term "compact" refers to formal agreements, treaties, or constitutional frameworks established between parties to define powers, secure alliances, or maintain specific religious and social standards.

## Ancient Greek Compacts
In ancient Greece, the term *Rhetra* denoted a solemn compact. These agreements were either originally emanating from the gods or subsequently sanctioned by them, with the deities serving as parties to the agreement. An example of such a compact is the ancient treaty between the Heræans and the Eleians. 

The Rhetra of Lykurgus, which emanated from the Delphian god, required the people, the senate, and the kings to bind themselves to the gods and to one another to obey its terms. Similarly, the words of Tyrtæus indicate the existence of compacts involving the kings, the senate, and the people.

Later, Athens proposed liberal principles of confederacy which were formally ratified by the Athenians and the deputies of the confederates. This compact of alliance was inscribed on a stone column and placed beside the statue of Zeus Eleutherius (the Liberator) to symbolize freedom from Sparta and the maintenance of freedom against other enemies, such as Persia. This original compact established a synod of confederate deputies as the competent judge for those charged with treason against the confederacy. To ensure security, the compact stipulated that any Athenian citizen who spoke or voted in the Athenian assembly contrary to the document's tenor would be tried before the synod and could be condemned to the severest punishment.

## Japanese Political Compacts
In Japanese history, compacts have been used to resolve military conflicts. During a conflict involving Hideyoshi and Mori, the desirability of peace was acknowledged because Hideyoshi's army controlled the communications between the western and eastern parts of Chugoku. However, Mori's uncles, Kohayakawa and Kikkawa, initially rejected any compact that required sacrificing the life of Shimizu. 

A resolution was eventually reached when Shimizu, the castle commandant, expressed his willingness to die for his comrades and liege-lord. Following a final meal of wine and fish provided to the garrison on the 5th of the sixth month, Shimizu committed suicide and a compact between Hideyoshi and Mori was signed. Although the subsequent death of Nobunaga led some to suggest disregarding the compact to attack the retreating enemy, Kikkawa and Kohayakawa opposed this, arguing that keeping faith would secure the friendship of Hideyoshi and his fellow barons and prevent the house of Mori from being disgraced.

## The United States Constitutional Compact
In the context of the United States, a specific resolution defined the union of the states not as a system of unlimited submission to a general government, but as a compact under the style of a Constitution and its amendments. Under this compact, the states constituted a general government for special purposes and delegated definite powers to it, while reserving the residuary mass of right to their own self-government.

According to this view, the acts of the general government are void and unauthoritative whenever it assumes powers not delegated to it. Each state acceded to this compact as an integral party. Furthermore, the compact established that the government was not the exclusive or final judge of the extent of its own delegated powers; instead, as with any compact between parties lacking a common judge, each party maintains an equal right to judge for itself regarding the measure of redress and infractions.

## The Bohemian Compacts
In Bohemia, the "Compacts" were central to the political and religious struggles surrounding the Bohemian throne. During the election of Prince Vladislav of Poland at Kutna Hora on May 27, 1471, Polish envoys promised that Vladislav would maintain the Compacts. This promise was a victory for the national party and reflected strong anti-clerical feelings in Bohemia, contrasting with the position of Matthias of Hungary, who was an unconditional adherent of Rome.

King Vladislav remained faithful to his promise to maintain the Compacts, which included not preventing Bohemians from receiving communion in both kinds. Despite this, his general policy was reactionary regarding religious controversies and state matters. He appointed government officials in Prague from the section of the Utraquist party closest to Rome, leading to the persecution of the Bohemian Brethren (extreme Hussites) and subsequent riots in Prague.
