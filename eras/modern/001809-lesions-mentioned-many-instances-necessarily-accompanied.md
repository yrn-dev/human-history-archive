# Lesions and Associated Clinical Phenomena

In medical pathology, lesions—structural changes in the tissues—are frequently linked to a variety of functional disturbances, clinical symptoms, and systemic effects. The nature of these accompaniments varies depending on the site of the lesion, the degree of the affection, and the specific nature of the disease.

## Functional Disturbances and Bacterial Disease
In many instances, lesions are necessarily accompanied by clinical symptoms or functional disturbances. In the context of bacterial diseases, certain symptoms occur even when correlated structural changes have not yet been demonstrated. The most significant of these is fever, which is accompanied by increased protein metabolism and disturbances in the respiratory and circulatory systems. Other common occurrences include nervous symptoms such as paralysis, convulsions, spasms, coma, and somnolence. These phenomena are attributed to the disturbance of the molecular constitution of living cells.

While some of these symptoms are associated with alterations in metabolism, others show no demonstrable physical change. Additionally, toxins may act on various glands to either increase or diminish functional activity, a process analogous to the action of certain drugs. Except for the growth of tumours, nearly all important phenomena encountered in disease can be experimentally produced through the injection of toxins or bacteria.

## Systemic and Local Manifestations of Bacterial Invasion
The distribution and accompaniment of lesions vary by the type of bacterial invasion:

*   **Septicaemias:** In extreme cases, such as pneumococcus septicaemia in rabbits or anthrax septicaemia in guinea-pigs, bacteria multiply freely in the blood stream. This causes symptoms of general poisoning. Widespread toxic action is indicated by lesions such as capillary haemorrhages and cloudy swelling, the latter of which may be followed by fatty degeneration in internal organs. In humans, often due to streptococci, organisms are primarily found in the capillaries of internal organs and may not be detectable in peripheral circulation during life.
*   **Secondary Extension:** In some diseases, a well-marked local lesion is produced first, followed by secondary extension via the blood or lymph stream. This results in the formation of corresponding lesions elsewhere, such as secondary nodules, tubercle, or abscesses. In typhoid fever, clumps of bacilli are found in internal organs (especially the spleen) and there is secondary invasion of the mesenteric glands, though tissue change around them may be minimal.
*   **Localized Infection:** In other cases, bacteria are restricted to one area, and symptoms result from absorbed toxins. Examples include tetanus (restricted to a wound), diphtheria (restricted to the region of the false membrane), and cholera (practically confined to the intestine).

## Organ-Specific Lesions and Their Effects

### The Brain
Lesions in the motor area of the brain can result in partial epilepsy or Jacksonian epilepsy. These attacks are often caused by the depression of the skull's vault due to trauma. A distinguishing feature of this manifestation is that the patient remains conscious throughout or loses consciousness late, allowing them to watch the progress of the spasm.

### The Kidneys
The results of morbid processes in the kidney are categorized into three areas: the actual lesions produced, the effects of those lesions on the body at large, and the effects on the composition of the urine. Kidney affections may be primary (limited to the kidney) or secondary (an accompaniment of disease in other parts of the body).

In Bright's disease—an inflammation of the kidney—lesions are likely produced by the excretion of toxic substances formed in the body or ingested (such as lead or alcohol). In cases following scarlet fever, the glomerular structures are mostly affected, with the glomerular tuft becoming atrophied and the capsules thickened by fibrous tissue. The epithelium of the convoluted tubules undergoes degeneration and is shed, forming casts in the urine and blocking the tubules, which can lead to an increase in kidney size.

Regarding growths of the kidney, tuberculous disease may be ascending (starting in the urinary bladder, epididymis, or testicle and traveling via lymphatics or the ureter) or descending (reaching the kidney via blood vessels). In descending tuberculosis, the lesion is likely bilateral and appears as miliary tubercles (scattered granules) especially in the cortex.

### The Lungs
In bronchiectasis (the dilatation of the bronchi), the position of the dilatation is determined by the primary lesion. This condition may follow phthisis, chronic pneumonia, acute pneumonia, broncho-pneumonia, or both acute and chronic bronchitis. Mechanical causes include new growth or pressure from an aneurism causing bronchial or tracheal obstruction. Emphysema is a very common accompaniment to this condition.
