# Bagdad Vilayet

The Bagdad vilayet is a province of Asiatic Turkey situated between the Syrian desert and Persia. It encompasses a significant portion of ancient Babylonia. Historically, from the mid-17th century until the mid-19th century, the region was annexed by the Turks and served as the largest province of the Turkish empire, occasionally functioning as an almost independent principality.

## Territorial Evolution and Geography
The original boundaries of the vilayet extended from the river Khabor in the west to the Persian frontier in the east, and from Mardin in the north to the Persian Gulf in the south. Its size was reduced over time: in 1857, western portions were added to the sanjak of Zor; in 1878, the Mosul vilayet was created from its northern districts; and in 1884, the Basra vilayet was formed from its southern sanjaks. Currently, the vilayet extends on the Tigris from a point slightly above Tekrit to a point just below Kut el-Amara, and on the Euphrates from a point slightly above Anah to a point somewhat below Samawa.

The geography of the region varies by latitude. North of Bagdad, the land between the two rivers is a waterless, arid limestone steppe. South of Bagdad, the terrain consists of alluvial soil deposited by the rivers. While this soil possesses great potential for fertility, the land is absolutely flat and prone to inundations during flood seasons, which can leave the immediate surroundings of Bagdad underwater. For the remainder of the year, much of the area is either a barren desert or composed of swamps and lagoons. Much of the vilayet is currently desert due to the neglect of irrigation canals.

## Population and Settlement
The settled population is located entirely along the banks of the Tigris and Euphrates, as well as the lagoons and canals westward of the Euphrates between Nejef and Kerbela. The capital is Bagdad. Other towns along the Euphrates include Anah, Hit, Ramadieh, Musseyib, Hilla, Diwanieh, and Samawa, while Tekrit, Samarra, and Kut el-Amara are located along the Tigris. These towns generally have populations ranging from 3,000 to 10,000. The sacred cities of Nejef (Meshed Ali) and Kerbela (Meshed-Hosain) each have populations between 20,000 and 60,000.

Beyond the river banks, the region is inhabited by wandering Arab tribes. The most powerful is the Shammar tribe, which ranges across all of Mesopotamia; in January and February, they descend toward Diwanieh in numbers that cause alarm even in Bagdad. Some tribes in the marshes are in a transition from nomadic to settled life and possess large herds of buffaloes, while others keep horses, sheep, goats, asses, and camels. Additionally, semi-sedentary populations known as *Madan* live in reed huts clustered around mud castles called *meftul*.

## Governance and Society
The vast bulk of the population consists of Arab and Persian Shi'as, who view the Turks as tyrants and foreigners. Turkish rule is primarily confined to the cities, towns, and villages along the river banks where garrisons are stationed. The *Madan* and Bedouin Arabs remain practically independent, paying uncertain tributes to the Turkish government and engaging in constant internal warfare. However, since the period of Midhat Pasha (1868-1872), the Turkish government has gradually increased its control, expanding taxation and conscription.

## Religion and Pilgrimage
The vilayet contains four major centers of pilgrimage for Shi'ite Moslems: Samarra, Kerbela, Nejef, and Kazemain (a suburb of Bagdad). These sites attract tens of thousands of annual pilgrims from India, Persia, and surrounding regions, many of whom bring their dead for burial near the sacred tombs. Historically, the district of Tekrit was also a chief locality for the Jacobites.

## Transport and Infrastructure
Bagdad is located approximately 500 miles from the Persian Gulf. Steam communication is maintained with the port of Basra via English and Turkish steamer lines. British steamers began operating on the Tigris following Colonel F. R. Chesney's 1836 expedition. Navigation on the Tigris between Bagdad and Korna is slow and uncertain due to the flat alluvial soil, which causes the river to shift channels, especially during floods. In the dry season, shifting shoals and flats create grounding hazards. Furthermore, the digging of irrigation canals by inhabitants on the eastern bank reduces river water and contributes to channel shifts.

Above Bagdad, sailing vessels of 30 tons or more navigate to Samarra. Local transport in the Bagdad area utilizes the *kufa*, a circular basket-work boat covered in bitumen, and *kellek* rafts.

## Health and Other Locations
Cholera is endemic in parts of the vilayet. The bubonic plague was stamped out by the government in 1875 but has reappeared in recent years, likely introduced by pilgrims from Persia or India.

Kut el-Amara, a small town on the east bank of the Tigris where the Shatt-el-Haï departs, serves as an important Turkish post for controlling the lower Tigris and as a coaling station for steamers traveling between Bagdad and Basra.
