# Rawlinson

The name Rawlinson appears in the historical archive across several distinct contexts, including the history of private book collecting, military command during the Great War, and scholarly introductions to classical texts.

## Book Collecting and Libraries
In the 17th and 18th centuries, book collecting often served as a means of forming libraries intended for public use. Thomas Rawlinson (d. 1725) was known for his immense accumulations of books; however, upon his death, these materials provided the basis for a series of auctions. His brother, Richard Rawlinson (d. 1755), allowed these books to be sold, though Richard's own best volumes were bequeathed to Oxford. This period marked a broader trend toward public ownership of private collections, exemplified by the foundation of the British Museum in 1753.

## Military Command of Sir Henry Rawlinson
Sir Henry Rawlinson served as the commander of the British Fourth Army. During the Great War, he was entrusted with the capture of Peronne, a strong position defended by the 2nd Prussian Guards. The town was protected by the marshy Somme to the south and west, and the low ridge of Mont St. Quentin to the north. Sir Henry Rawlinson assigned the task of taking the town to the Australian Corps. In a single day of fighting, the corps captured Mont St. Quentin and Peronne, an exploit that Sir Henry Rawlinson described as the finest single action of the war.

In other military operations, General Rawlinson was tasked with organizing a relieving force at Ghent and Bruges. His commands included the use of available armed motor-cars, aeroplanes, and armoured trains (including the naval ratings who operated them). He was also responsible for coordinating with naval protection, specifically three monitors held in readiness to cover potential re-embarkations at Zeebrugge or Ostend.
