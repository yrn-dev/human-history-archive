# Avenida Alvear

Avenida Alvear is a thoroughfare in Buenos Aires specifically noted for the elegance of its private residences. It represents a shift in the city's architectural landscape, moving away from earlier domestic styles toward more modern, costly, and attractive residences.

## Architectural Evolution of Buenos Aires
The traditional style of domestic architecture in Buenos Aires was introduced from Spain and utilized across all Spanish colonies. This style featured one-storey buildings grouped around one or two *patios*, which opened onto the street via wide doorways. These traditional residences were characterized by flat roofs with parapets designed for defense and windows on the street that were heavily barred.

However, the introduction of foreign culture and customs, alongside the domiciliation of wealthy foreigners, gradually modified both public and domestic architecture. In modern Buenos Aires, the prevailing style for residences and public edifices has become a lavishly decorated French renaissance.

## Urban Landmarks and Districts
While Avenida Alvear is distinguished by its elegant private homes, other areas of the city are noted for different architectural contributions:
*   **Avenida de Mayo:** This area is recognized for its display of business and public edifices that are elaborately ornamented. Notable buildings on or near this avenue include the new municipal hall and the new congress hall on Calle Callao, which was completed in 1906 at a cost of approximately £1,300,000.
*   **Belgrano and Flores:** These suburban districts are distinguished by their attractive gardens and country-houses.

## Public Edifices and City Infrastructure
Buenos Aires features several noteworthy public buildings, including the Casa Rosada (the government-house), which faces the Plaza 25 de Mayo and partially occupies the site of a fort built by Garay in 1580. Other significant structures include the mint, the *bolsa* (exchange), a distributing reservoir, and various modern educational buildings.

The city's commitment to higher education is evidenced by the university of Buenos Aires, which had 3,562 students in 1901 and includes several faculties such as medicine and law. The city also maintains four national colleges, three normal schools, various technical schools, a national museum, a national library, an aquarium, and a zoological garden.

## Social and Economic Character
The residents of Buenos Aires, known as *Porteños*, frequently refer to their city as the "Paris of America." This characterization is reflected in their fondness for amusements and the arts, spending significant time and expense on diversions ranging from horse-racing and *pelota* to Italian opera. The city provides various social, athletic, and sporting clubs, as well as two or three large public baths.

Economically, Buenos Aires serves as the primary manufacturing center of the republic. The city's industrial establishments number in the thousands, with capital reaching hundreds of millions of dollars. Despite this wealth, the city faces issues with overcrowding, with one-fifth of the population living in tenement-houses, or *conventillos*.
