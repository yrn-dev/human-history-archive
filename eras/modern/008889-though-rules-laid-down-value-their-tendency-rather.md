# The Application and Impact of Established Rules

Across various domains of human history—including literature, law, medicine, and military science—the establishment of formal rules and codes has served to create uniformity, provide guidance, and define professional standards. While these rules often provide essential structure, their application varies from the strict legal mandates of sovereign assemblies to the flexible axioms of tactical combat.

## Literary Codes and the Influence of Boileau
In the realm of French literature, the publication of *L'Art poetique* in 1674 by Boileau established a code for future French verse, mirroring the role of Horace's *Ars Poetica* in Latin. Boileau's work did not only provide rules for poetic language but also analyzed different types of verse composition and the principles specific to each. The work is divided into four books: the first and last focus on general precepts and the rule of *bon sens*, the second addresses the ode, satire, epigram, elegy, and pastoral, and the third focuses on epic and tragic poetry.

The influence of Boileau's maxims extended to English literature through Pope's *Essay on Criticism* and a translation revised by Dryden. However, the impact of these rules is viewed with nuance; while they are considered of value, their tendency is to render poetic efforts too mechanical and to hamper them.

## Legal and Administrative Regulations
The definition and status of "rules" vary significantly depending on the governing body that enacts them. In ancient Athens, the sovereign assembly laid down rules for its own decisions and debates; these were considered laws, and any breach was termed "illegal." In contrast, the English House of Commons is not a sovereign assembly, and therefore the rules it establishes for debates are categorized as orders of the House rather than laws. Consequently, a breach of these orders is regarded as irregular or informal rather than illegal. A similar distinction existed in the French Chamber of Deputies prior to February 1848, where proceedings were governed by *le réglement de la Chambre*. By March 1849, the National Assembly retained this expression despite being a sovereign assembly whose sanctioned rules are, in fact, laws.

In the commercial sector, the York-Antwerp Rules were introduced to create uniformity and certainty in adjusting General Average (G.A.) losses. These rules provided definiteness where there had been a lack of definition. For example, Rule XIII established a definite scale for deductions from repair costs regarding "new for old," replacing uncertain customary rules. Additionally, Rule XVII established a rule for contributory values to replace the varying rules used across different countries regarding freight and ship contributions.

## Monastic and Royal Prescriptions
Religious and royal life has also been shaped by specific rules. St. Basil the Great developed a monastic system that serves as the basis for monasticism in the Slavonic and Greek Churches. His "Longer" and "Shorter" Rules function more as catechisms of spiritual life than as corporate regulations. Basil advocated for a cenobitical life over an eremitical one, emphasizing that work and fasting should not interfere with prayer. His system required common meals, common work, and common prayer seven times a day.

In ancient Egypt, the laws prescribed both the quality and quantity of the king's food and drink. These rules focused on satisfying the cravings of nature rather than pleasing the palate, leading some historians to suggest they were designed by a physician concerned with the prince's health rather than a legislator.

## Technical and Medical Axioms
In specialized fields, rules often emerge as axioms based on practical necessity. In military science, it has been laid down as an axiom in all armies that machine-guns must deliver fire in "bursts" and only on favorable targets. This rule was established to prevent the strain on the weapon and the waste of ammunition caused by prolonged firing at rates between 350 and 700 rounds per minute.

In medicine, the use of atropine in cardiac affections remains dubious and obscure. It can only be laid down that the drug serves as a temporary stimulant in emergencies and can relieve cardiac pain when used internally or as a plaster. Additionally, it is recognized as a valuable antidote for poisoning by muscarine, prussic acid, and pilocarpine.
