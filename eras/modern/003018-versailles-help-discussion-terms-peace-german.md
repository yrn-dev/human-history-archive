# Versailles and the German Empire

The location of Versailles has served as a recurring site for the consolidation of German power and the subsequent negotiation of peace terms across different eras of European history. Its role spans from the proclamation of the German Empire in the 19th century to the Peace Conference following the First World War.

## The Proclamation of the German Empire (1871)
Following the invasion of France by united German forces in August 1870, the French suffered significant defeats at Wörth and Gravelotte, and later at Sedan on September 1st. After the surrender of Paris in January 1871, the triumph of the Germans was consolidated. On January 18, 1871, the King of Prussia was proclaimed German Emperor in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles. 

This event was marked by pomp, ceremony, and a great array of military uniforms. During this historic assembly of German princes, the grand-duke of Baden was the first to hail the King of Prussia as the German emperor. This act of unity was credited to Bismarck and the sword of the Hohenzollerns, though a common literature and language had long since provided a foundation for such unity. The creation of this new state opened a new volume of European history, resulting in a mighty state equipped with learning and science. Conversely, France was left impoverished, divided, and stripped of Lorraine and Alsace.

## Characteristics of the New Germany
The empire created at Versailles was a mixture of the narrowest European political traditions and fresh material and intellectual forces. Germany became the most educational state in the world, setting the pace for its rivals and neighbors. This commitment to education and the application of scientific methods to social and industrial development allowed Germany to become a leading manufacturing and trading power. The nation led the world in the production of chemical products, dyes, and optical glass, and its steel output eventually surpassed that of the British.

## Diplomacy and Finance at Versailles
In 1870, the role of specialized expertise became evident in the peace discussions at Versailles. Ludwig Bamberger, a German economist and politician who had previously been exiled to Switzerland, London, Holland, and Paris, was summoned by Bismarck to Versailles. Due to his intimate acquaintance with finance and France, Bamberger assisted in the discussion of the terms of peace. Bamberger later became a leading authority on economics in the German Reichstag, where he advocated for a gold currency and the specific form of the German Imperial Bank, while opposing bimetallism and Bismarck's later policies of colonial development and protection.

## The Peace Conference at Versailles
In a later historical reversal, Versailles again became the site of a peace gathering, though the circumstances differed fundamentally from those of 1871. The Peace Conference at Versailles was characterized as a gathering ill-adapted for anything beyond carrying out the logical conclusions of war conflicts and defeats. 

Unlike the 1871 assembly, the deliberations of this conference excluded the defeated parties; the Bulgarians, Turks, Austrians, and Germans were permitted no share in the discussions and were required only to accept the dictated decisions. The choice of the Hall of Mirrors as the meeting place was viewed as particularly unfortunate from the perspective of human welfare, as it served as a melodramatic reversal of the scene where the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871.

## Other Historical Contexts of Versailles
Beyond the major political shifts of the 19th and 20th centuries, the influence of Versailles extended into German social structures and regional culture. In the 17th century, the court of Versailles may have influenced the use of the word "Baron" in Germany as an equivalent to the *Freiherr* (free lord of the Empire). Additionally, during the period of monarchies and experimental governments, various German princely and ducal courts attempted to ape the splendors of Versailles. For example, Frederick the Great of Prussia established his own version of Versailles at Potsdam, where the court read French literature and spoke the French language.
