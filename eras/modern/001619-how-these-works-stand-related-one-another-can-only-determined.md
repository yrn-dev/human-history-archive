# Internal Evidence and the Relation of Works

In the study of historical and philosophical texts, the determination of how various works relate to one another often depends on specific methodologies of analysis, ranging from the examination of internal evidence to the study of stylistic evolution and chronological markers.

## The Works of Roger Bacon
For the works of Roger Bacon, the relationship between different texts can only be determined through internal evidence. His body of work includes smaller pieces, primarily focused on alchemy, which are considered unimportant and lack ascertainable dates of composition. 

Bacon's primary activity is seen to begin with the *Opus Majus*, a work described by Whewell as both the Organum and the Encyclopaedia of the 13th century. Prior to the *Opus Majus*, Bacon wrote several tracts. These include *Computus Naturalium*, a work on chronology likely dating to 1263, and the short treatise *De Secretis Operibus*, which may have been composed before 1249 if its dedication is authentic. In the *Opus Tertium*, Bacon provides a summary of the first part of the *Opus Majus* (sometimes called *De Utililate Scientiarum*), where he discusses the four *offendicula*—causes of error consisting of custom, authority, the opinion of the unskilled many, and the most dangerous error: the concealment of real ignorance with a pretence of knowledge. Through these works, Bacon demonstrates his perception of the necessity of encyclopaedic treatment and the unity of science.

## The Johannine Writings
The group of writings bearing the name of John—consisting of the Gospel, the Epistles, and the Apocalypse—stand largely apart from other New Testament texts. While they are subject to active discussion, there is a general consensus that they are closely connected to one another and were produced toward the end of the 1st century, likely making them the latest New Testament writings (with the exception of 2 Peter).

The Apocalypse is plausibly dated near the year 93 by Harnack and Reinach, while the other writings may date to the reign of Domitian (81-96), though some critics suggest a later limit. These works are connected to John of Ephesus, a prominent figure of the late 1st century. However, significant differences remain regarding the personal identity of John—specifically whether he is the apostle, the son of Zebedee, or the "beloved disciple"—and whether the author of the Apocalypse is the same individual who wrote the Gospel and Epistles.

## The Revelations of the Qur'an
The revelations put forth in Mecca are categorized into distinct groups based on style and composition. The oldest are a considerable number of short suras that differ significantly in strain and composition from the Medina pieces. Another cluster of Meccan suras shows obvious affinities with the style of the Medina suras, placing them in the later part of the Prophet's work in Mecca. Between these two extremes are suras that mark the transition from the first period to the third.

While Professor Weil first distinguished these three periods, the lines of division are not sharp, and some suras may fit into multiple groups. It is considered impossible to establish a probable chronological arrangement of individual revelations within these groups. In the absence of clear allusions to determinable events, attempts to arrange the parts based on the psychological development of the Prophet are viewed as relying on subjective assumptions or fancies.

## Philosophical Relations and Systems
In the realm of philosophy, the relationship between works and systems is often defined by the correction of predecessors. Immanuel Kant's vindication of the object and refutation of subjective idealism are understood through his effort to correct defects in the teachings of his predecessors. This includes a reaction against pantheistic and mechanical explanations of the universe.

Similarly, the metaphysical system of Leibnitz, featuring self-determined monads, is understood in light of the assertion of the rights of the subject against the atoms of the materialist and the substance of Spinoza. Leibnitz argued that while sense-given material is necessary for thought, the soul contains notions—such as identity, cause, substance, and being—that the senses cannot provide.

In the case of Lotze, the key to his theoretical philosophy is found in his metaphysics, which is the focus of both his first and last larger publications. Lotze's objective was to remodel notions of the existence of things and their connections to make them consistent and thinkable, opposing the attempt to establish general laws that a Creator must have followed (as seen in Hegel) or the attempt to trace the genesis of notions (as seen in modern theories of knowledge).
