# Civil Unrest and Political Conflict in Vienna (1848)

The city of Vienna experienced a period of intense political instability and renewed trouble during 1848, characterized by conflicts between the radical populace, the imperial government, and various ethnic nationalities within the Austrian monarchy.

## Early Insurrections and Government Concessions
Following the fall of Metternich, Austria lacked a central government for several months. On March 14, the emperor authorized the establishment of a National Guard in Vienna, and the city came under the rule of a committee composed of citizens and students. This group exerted influence over the ministry and imperial affairs. 

The government attempted to address public opinion by proposing a central committee of local diets on March 15, and later proclaiming a constitution on April 25. This constitution covered the monarchy, excluding Lombardo-Venetia and Hungary. However, the document sparked anger in radical Vienna due to its provisions for an indirectly elected deputy system and a partly nominated senate. It also faced protests from Poles and Czechs.

Tensions escalated on May 13 with the establishment of a Central Committee, followed by a fresh insurrection on May 15. The government yielded to these pressures, recognizing the Central Committee, granting the National Guard a role in politics, and promising a National Convention based on a single chamber elected by universal suffrage.

## Imperial Flight and the Committee of Public Safety
On May 17, the emperor left Vienna for Innsbruck, citing his health. From there, he issued a proclamation on May 20 that confirmed the concessions made in March but ignored those from May 15. This flight caused a revulsion of feeling in Vienna. When the government attempted to close the university to disperse students, another outbreak occurred on May 26. The ministry again conceded to the insurgents' demands, handing over the public treasury and the responsibility for maintaining order to a newly formed Committee of Public Safety.

## National Rivalries and the "Race War"
The Jacobinism of the Vienna democracy was not representative of widespread opinion, even among Germans in Austria, and its "loud-voiced Germanism" created opposition among other races. Various nationalities used the March troubles to press their own claims:
* **Bohemia:** A separate constitution was proclaimed on April 8 after elections for the Frankfort parliament failed due to opposition from conservative German aristocrats and Czechs.
* **Croatia:** On March 23, the diet of Agram elected Baron Joseph Jellachich as ban, a concession to southern Slav agitation.
* **Galicia:** Count Stadion proclaimed a new constitution on March 18.

In Galicia, Count Stadion won over Ruthenian peasants by abolishing feudal dues and seeking official recognition of their language alongside Polish before bombarding Cracow on April 26. A significant turning point occurred in Prague on June 12, when a rising of the populace and Czech students broke out. Prince Windischgraetz suppressed this rising on June 16. This victory signaled a "universal race war" where national rivalry superseded the idea of constitutional liberty. Consequently, Slav nationalities aligned with the imperial government, viewing the radicals in Budapest and Vienna as their primary enemies.

## The Fall of the Vienna Radicals
Renewed trouble eventually broke out in Vienna as the radical populace clashed with both the government and the Slav majority of the Reichsrath. While German democrats sought aid from the Hungarian government, the Magyar passion for constitutional legality caused delays.

By October 7, Emperor Ferdinand fled from Schoenbrunn to Olmuetz, a Slav district, and issued a proclamation calling for those who loved "freedom" and "Austria" to rally to the throne. Meanwhile, the Slav majority and members of the Right left the Reichsrath in Vienna, scheduling a meeting of the diet at Bruenn for October 20. This left only a "rump of German radicals" in the capital, who were impotent and controlled by students and the proletariat.

On October 11, Windischgraetz announced his intention to march against the rebellious city. He was appointed commander-in-chief of all Austrian armies (excluding Italy) and field-marshal via an imperial rescript on October 16. Although an ex-officer of Napoleon named Bern hastily organized the city's defense, the lack of Hungarian assistance rendered the effort futile. Windischgraetz began his attack on October 28 and became master of the city on November 1.
