# The American Continent

The American continent is characterized by a diverse range of biological regions and historical patterns of human migration, maintaining significant botanical and faunal connections with other landmasses, particularly in Asia and the Southern Ocean.

## Biological Regions and Flora
The continent is divided into distinct biological zones. The Neotropical, or Tropical American region, is defined by the equatorial zone. In the north, there exists a broad and irregular band of country where North and South American forms are mixed.

The flora of the American continent shows a manifest relation to that of North China and Japan. Many temperate forms extend from the American continent to the Himalaya, reaching almost as far as Kashmir. These connections indicate a clear botanical link between North America and eastern Asia. Additionally, certain plant groups exhibit wide distribution across the hemisphere; for example, the *Bambuseae* occur throughout the tropical zone and extend into sub-tropical and temperate zones. In the Andes of South America, these plants reach the snow-line.

Beyond the mainland, the vegetation of Kerguelen Island is noted by Sir J. D. Hooker as being of great antiquity. He suggests that this vegetation may have originally reached the island from the American continent, noting that it possesses no affinities with Africa.

## Human Migration and Early Inhabitants
There is no convincing evidence of human presence in America before the end of the Pleistocene. It is suggested that a relaxation of the climate, which allowed Neolithic tribes to advance and reindeer hunters to retreat into Siberia and Russia, may have enabled humans to wander across the land now divided by the Bering Strait to reach the American continent.

From this point of entry, humans spread southward over successive ages. Upon reaching South America, these populations encountered various extinct creatures that were still flourishing, including the glyptodon—a monstrous armadillo with a huge tortoise-like shell—and the giant sloth (*Megatherium*). Evidence of this coexistence is supported by the discovery by Roth of a human skeleton buried beneath the shell of a glyptodon.

Records indicate that all human remains found in America, including the earliest, are of an Amer-Indian character. The archive notes that there were no preceding races of "sub-men" in America; rather, man was fully man upon entering the continent, as the "old world" served as the nursery for the sub-races of mankind.

## Colonial Expansion and Trade
The development of the American continent was heavily influenced by trade and the establishment of colonies. Trading companies were responsible for founding most of the American and West Indian colonies. In the north-western districts of Canada, colonization was similarly driven by a trading company. These activities in the temperate and tropical regions of the world formed the basis upon which the British empire was established.
