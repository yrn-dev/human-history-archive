# Orchester gebrauchliche wichtigere Instrumente

The study of instruments commonly used in the orchestra involves an examination of their mechanical evolution, their application by composers, and the technical principles that govern their sound.

## The Bassoon
The bassoon has been a preferred instrument for many great masters, with the notable exception of Handel. Beethoven utilized the instrument extensively in his symphonies, composing independent parts characterized by originality and beauty. Other significant works for the bassoon include Bach's mass in B min., which features parts for two bassoons, a concerto in Bb for bassoon and orchestra (Kochel, No. 191) by Mozart, and a concerto in F (op. 75) for full orchestra by Weber.

The instrument's history is linked to its prototypes, the bombard and the pommer; J. J. Quantz credits France with the transformation of the bombard (pommer) into the bassoon. Technical advancements have continued over time; for instance, the wind-instrument-maker W. Heckel of Biebrich-am-Rhein, acting on the instigation of Wagner, produced bassoons with an additional key that extended the compass downwards to A.

## The Horn
The French horn is a conical bore instrument that employs several devices to alter its pitch: crooks, valves, and slides. The slide is the oldest of these devices and likely served as the inspiration for the crook. While crooks allow a performer to play in various keys on a single instrument, valves and slides operate instantaneously to provide a chromatic compass.

Modern French horns are typically constructed with two or three valves:
* **First Valve:** Adds tubing to lower the pitch by a tone (e.g., a horn in F plays as though it were in E$\flat$).
* **Second Valve:** Opens a passage to shorter tubing to lower the pitch by a semitone (playing as though the instrument were in E).
* **Third Valve:** Lowers the pitch by a tone and a half.

From a technical perspective, the horn tube behaves as an open pipe. While Victor Mahillon viewed the tubes of wind instruments played by lips or reeds as closed, E. F. F. Chladni maintained that the mouthpiece end is considered open in all wind instruments except reed instruments. Regardless of the view, a closed pipe with a trunco-conical bore—where the bell diameter is at least four times the mouthpiece diameter—behaves like an open pipe when set in vibration by a reed, producing a consecutive scale of harmonics.

## Kettledrums
Kettledrums, which derive their name from "drum" and "kettle" (a covered metal vessel), are used both in orchestras and in military bands, such as those of the cavalry regiments and Life Guards. 

Historically, there has been a distinction between the military and musical use of the instrument. Until the end of the 18th century, the two notes obtainable from a pair of kettledrums were primarily used to mark or vary rhythm, similar to the technique of side drums, rather than as musical notes contributing to harmonies. This contrasts with more modern musical applications, such as the solo for four kettledrums found in Meyerbeer's *Robert le Diable*.

## Historical Documentation and References
The development and use of orchestral instruments have been documented in various historical treatises and methods. Notable works include:
* **Bassoon:** Etienne Ozi's *Nouvelle Methode du Bassoon* (1788, 1800), J. B. J. Willent-Bordogny's *Gran Methodo completo per il Fagotto* (1844), and Joseph Froehlich's *Vollstaendige Musikschule fuer alle beym Orchester gebrauchliche wichtigere Instrumente* (1811).
* **Kettledrums:** J. Georges Kastner's *Méthode complète et raisonnée de timbales*, which describes and illustrates early mechanical kettledrums.
* **General Orchestration:** Works such as *Das neu-eroffnete Orchester* (1713) and *Der vollkommene Kapellmeister* (1739) provide further insight into the orchestral landscape.
