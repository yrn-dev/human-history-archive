# The Delegations of the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy

The Delegations were common institutions established to exercise political control and the constitutional right of voting money applicable to the common affairs of the monarchy. These bodies were designed based on the principle of dualism, which established a complete parity between Hungary and the other territories of the king.

## Composition and Election
Each Delegation consisted of sixty members who were chosen for a term of one year. The membership was divided by the method of election: one-third of the members were chosen by the Hungarian Table of Magnates and the Austrian Herrenhaus (the Upper Houses), while the remaining two-thirds were chosen by the Hungarian and Austrian Houses of Representatives.

In Hungary, the Delegation was elected by each House as a whole. In Austria, however, the Delegation was chosen by the representatives of the separate territories rather than the House as a whole. For example, Galicians chose seven delegates, Bohemian representatives chose ten, and those from Trieste chose one. This distinction resulted in a comparative weakness for Austria, as the Austrian Delegation included representatives of various groups that might oppose the government, allowing them to maintain that opposition within the Delegation.

## Functions and Responsibilities
The Delegations were the bodies to which the common ministers were responsible and to which the estimates for joint services were submitted. There were three ministers appointed for common purposes:
1. Finance
2. War
3. Foreign Affairs

The minister of war maintained control over the common army, though the laws regarding army recruitment had to be voted on separately in each parliament. The minister of finance presented the common budget to the Delegations; however, the Delegations possessed no actual legislative power and could not vote taxes or raise money. Once the budget was passed by the Delegations, the necessary funds had to be provided by the separate parliaments. The proportion of the contribution from each half of the monarchy was fixed every ten years.

Additionally, each Delegation held the right to arraign and call to account the common ministers and to formulate resolutions independently. Members of both Delegations enjoyed constitutional immunity and were irresponsible in the exercise of their office.

## Procedural Operations
The monarch summoned the Delegations annually, alternating the meetings between Budapest (Pest) and Vienna. To ensure that the Delegations did not overshadow the parliaments that appointed them, it was mandated that they not sit together. Each delegation held separate sittings, both of which were public.

Communication between the two Delegations was conducted in writing, with every document accompanied by a translation in either German or Magyar. If the Delegations failed to reach an agreement after three such written interchanges, a common plenary sitting was convened. This joint session consisted of an equal number of members from both delegations. During this plenary sitting, no discussion was permitted; the members decided the question by a common vote in silence, with a simple majority being sufficient for a decision. For any common decision of both houses to be valid, it required the sanction of the monarch.

An example of this process occurred in 1869 during a joint sitting to settle a budget point that Hungary had accepted but Austria had rejected; in this instance, the Tirolese and Poles voted in favor of the Hungarian proposal.
