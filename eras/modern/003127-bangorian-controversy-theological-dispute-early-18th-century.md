# BANGORIAN CONTROVERSY

The Bangorian controversy was a theological dispute that took place in the early 18th century. The conflict originated in 1716 following the posthumous publication of a work by George Hickes, the bishop of Thetford, titled *Constitution of the Christian Church, and the Nature and Consequences of Schism*. In this text, Hickes excommunicated all churchmen with the exception of the non-juring churchmen.

## The Position of Benjamin Hoadly
Benjamin Hoadly, who had been newly appointed as the bishop of Bangor, responded to Hickes's publication with a work titled *Preservative against the Principles and Practices of Non-Jurors*. In this reply, Hoadly recommended his own Erastian position and proposed that sincerity should be the only test of truth.

Hoadly further expanded his views in a famous sermon preached on March 31, 1717, before George I, titled *The Nature of the Kingdom or Church of Christ*. In this discourse, Hoadly presented several provocative arguments:
* He impugned the idea that any visible church existed.
* He ridiculed the value of tests of orthodoxy.
* He expressed contempt for the claims of the church to govern itself through the state.

Hoadly identified the church with the kingdom of Heaven, arguing that it was "not of this world" and asserting that Christ had not delegated His authority to any representatives.

## Institutional and Political Response
In May, a committee appointed by the Lower House of Convocation reported on Hoadly's book and sermon. While the bishops and the archbishop would have taken steps in response, the government intervened and prorogued Convocation until November. Hoadly denied that this government intervention occurred at his request.

Following these events, Hoadly wrote *A Reply to the Representations of Convocation*. He also engaged with several principal critics, including Francis Hare (then dean of Worcester), Andrew Snape (provost of Eton), and Thomas Sherlock (then dean of Chichester). As a result of their opposition, these three men, along with Robert Moss, the dean of Ely, were deprived of their royal chaplaincies. Hoadly chose not to answer William Law, who is described as the most brilliant of his antagonists, though Law was comparatively unknown at the time.

## Supporters and Consequences
The controversy saw support from other figures, such as White Kennett. An English bishop and antiquary, Kennett had joined the Low Church party and opposed the Sacheverel movement. In the Bangorian controversy, Kennett supported Bishop Hoadly's side with considerable bitterness and great zeal.

The most significant result of the controversy was the silencing of Convocation. Although the body had appeared to be settling into its proper work of dealing with the real exigencies of the church, it did not meet again after the dispute involving Hoadly arose.
