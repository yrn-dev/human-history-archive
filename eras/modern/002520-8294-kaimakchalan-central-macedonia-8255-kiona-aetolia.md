# Regional Geography and History of Central Macedonia and Aetolia

## Geography of the Balkan Peninsula
The Balkan Peninsula is the most easterly of the three large peninsulas forming the southern extremities of the European continent, covering an area of 184,779 square miles. Its surface is almost entirely mountainous. The northern boundary is generally marked by the courses of the Save, Kulpa, and Danube rivers, stretching from the Adriatic Sea near Fiume to the Kilia mouth of the Danube. The peninsula is bounded by the Black Sea, the Sea of Marmora, and the Aegean to the east, the Mediterranean to the south, and the Ionian and Adriatic seas to the west.

The region's complex mountain systems include the Dinaric Alps, the ranges of Bosnia, and the Rhodope chain, which traverses the center of the peninsula. Other significant heights include the Shar Dagh, the mountains of Montenegro and Albania, and the Pindus range, which continues into the heights of Aetolia and Acarnania. Notable summits include Olympus (9,794 ft.), which overlooks the Gulf of Salonica, and Liubotrn in the Shar Dagh (8,898 ft.).

## Central Macedonia: Physical and Human Geography
Macedonia is divided into two nearly equal portions by the river Vardar (Axius), which serves as the primary route from Central Europe to the Aegean. The Vardar rises in the Shar mountains near Gostivar, drains the elevated plain of Tetovo, and passes through Usküb and Veles before reaching the Gulf of Salonica. Other major rivers in the region include the Mesta, which drains the Razlog valley, and the Struma, which falls into the Aegean near the site of ancient Amphipolis.

The rural population of Macedonia is largely described as Slavonic. Slavs first crossed the Danube in the 3rd century, with major immigrations occurring in the 6th and 7th centuries. These populations drove the latinized people of Macedonia into highland districts such as Olympus, Agrapha, and Pindus. In central Macedonia, a Mongolian physical type is found, extending as far west as Dibra and Ochrida. Most independent authorities agree that the bulk of the Slavonic population in Macedonia is Bulgarian, based primarily on language.

## Political History of Macedonia
Central Macedonia was included in the dominions of the Bulgarian chief Krum between 802 and 815. Later, it formed part of the empire of the Bulgarian tsar Simeon (893-927). Following Simeon's death, Bulgarian power was revived in western Macedonia under the Shishman dynasty, with Tsar Samuel (976-1014) ruling over Melnik, Vodena, Veles, and Usküb. After Samuel's defeat by Emperor Basil II in 1014, Greek domination lasted for a century and a half.

The region saw various subsequent occupations. In 1185, Normans under William of Sicily captured Salonica. Following the 1204 fall of Constantinople, the feudal kingdom of Thessalonica was established under Boniface, marquis of Montferrat, before being overthrown in 1222 by Theodore, despot of Epirus. Bulgarian rule was later re-established under Ivan Asen II.

In the modern era, the 1878 Treaty of San Stefano included all of Macedonia, except the Chalcidic peninsula and Salonica, in the principality of Bulgaria. This was reversed by the Treaty of Berlin, which returned Macedonia to Turkish administration. During World War I, Bulgaria occupied the Kavala, Serres, and Drama districts of Greek Macedonia starting in the spring of 1916.

## Aetolia and Doris
In ancient geography, Doris was a small district in central Greece located between Mount Parnassus and Mount Oeta. It was considered the alleged mother-country of the Dorian conquerors of the Peloponnesus and was characterized by petty wars with the Phocians and Oetaeans. The inhabitants of Doris may have been exterminated during conflicts between Macedonia and Aetolia.

Aetolia was home to a numerous and warlike nation that lived in scattered, unwalled villages and utilized light armor. Among the Aetolian tribes were the Apodotians, the Ophionians, and the Eurytanians. The Eurytanians were the largest tribe in Aetolia and were noted for speaking a language that was exceedingly difficult to understand and for eating their flesh raw.

During the Peloponnesian War, the Athenian Demosthenes operated near Naupactus and interacted with the Aetolians. In one campaign, Eurylochus advanced through Locrian territory and was joined by the Aetolians to lay waste to the land and take the unfortified suburb of Naupactus. Eurylochus later withdrew to Proschium in Aetolia and other areas such as Pleuron and Calydon (formerly called Aeolis) before dismissing the Aetolians to coordinate with the Ambraciots.
