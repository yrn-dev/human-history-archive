# Agriculture

## Origins and Early Development
The development of agriculture is linked to the progression of early human populations. It is probable that the ancestors of the white and whitish Neolithic peoples mastered agriculture, domesticated cattle, and tamed the dog in regions such as north Africa, India, Arabia, Persia, or Asia Minor, or in areas now submerged beneath the Indian Ocean or the Mediterranean Sea. This occurred while Neanderthal men lived in the bleak climate of glaciated Europe. Approximately twelve thousand years ago, Neolithic peoples—who were the most advanced peoples in the world at that time—were scattered across Asia, north Africa, and Europe.

The adoption of agriculture tended to tie human communities to the specific regions where it could be most conveniently practiced, which encouraged differentiation. This contrasts with hunting peoples, such as the Indians of the Labrador country who follow caribou herds across a territory as large as France, or nomad peoples, such as certain Kalmuck tribes who travel nearly a thousand miles between winter and summer pasture.

## Regional Agricultural Profiles

### Bohemia
Bohemia possesses a thriving rural population and a suitable climate, resulting in a highly developed agricultural sector. Over 50% of the total area is under cultivation. The most fertile regions include the "Garden of Bohemia" round Leitmeritz, the "Paradise" round Teplitz, and the "Golden Road" round Koniggratz. While wheat, barley, rye, and oats are the principal products, large tracts of land have been shifted to beetroot cultivation due to competition from Hungarian wheat. 

The staple food for the population is the potato. Other notable products include high-quality flax and hops, for which the Saaz district is celebrated. Plums and other fruits are abundant and serve as major export articles. Land use is distributed as follows: forests cover 29.01%, meadows 10.05%, pastures 5.05%, and gardens 1.35%. Beyond crop cultivation, bee-cultivation and the rearing of large flocks of geese (particularly in the south) are important. Bohemian peasants have also successfully pursued pisciculture for centuries, and there has been fair success in rearing silkworms.

### Cornwall and the Scilly Isles
In Cornwall, approximately seven-tenths of the total area is under cultivation. Oats are the only important grain crop, while mangolds, swedes, and turnips comprise the bulk of green crops. The region is particularly famous for market-gardening near Penzance and the Scilly Isles, where the climate supports the growth of asparagus, broccoli, and early potatoes for the London market. Flowers and fruit are also grown for the market. The area maintains a large number of sheep and cattle, the latter being chiefly of the Devonshire breed, with nearly 60,000 acres of hill pasture.

### Other European and Mediterranean Regions
In Belle-Ile-en-Mer, an island off the coast of France, the inhabitants engage in agriculture and fisheries. The soil is productive where cultivated, and the mild climate allows myrtle and the fig-tree to grow in sheltered spots. The island is also known for a highly prized breed of draught horses.

In the province of Avila in central Spain, agriculture varies by section. The northern portion is mainly agricultural, though the soil is often rocky in the valleys of the Sierra de Avila and the climate fluctuates between extreme heat and severe cold. The southern division, characterized by rugged granitic sierras and well-watered valleys, focuses on stock-breeding as an important industry.

## Environmental and Climatic Factors

### Soil and Climate Variations
Agricultural success is heavily dependent on local conditions. In certain English counties, the soil is varied; for example, the valley of the Medway round Maidstone is highly fertile due to deep loam and brick-earth subsoil. In contrast, London Clay is heavy and stiff, requiring proper manuring to be fruitful. In other regions, the climate and soil are better suited for green crops and grass than for grain. In such areas, oats typically predominate over barley and wheat.

### Climatic Change and Desiccation
Environmental shifts can lead to the decline of agricultural productivity. In the Baluch highlands, evidence of Arab irrigation works and cultivation indicates a once-flourishing agricultural state. However, widespread desiccation has occurred, potentially due to the destruction of forests, erosion, or denudation. These climatic changes, rather than tribal disturbances, are attributed as the cause for the disappearance of the Arabs from agriculture and trade in that region.

## Livestock and Specialized Farming
Livestock breeding varies by region and purpose:
* **Cattle and Sheep:** In some regions, the black polled Galloway is the general cattle breed, while Ayrshires are used for dairying and cheese-making. Sheep rearing includes Blackfaced and Cheviots on high ground.
* **Horses:** Specialized breeds include the small, hardy Galloways used for agricultural work and stock, as well as Clydesdales.
* **Pigs:** Pig-rearing is a significant pursuit in some areas, with pork supplied to English markets; one region saw a 50% increase in pig numbers during the last quarter of the 19th century.
* **Apiculture:** Bee-keeping is noted as an important industry in both Bohemia and other shires, where honey is held in high repute.
