# Herbert Spencer

Herbert Spencer was an influential figure in 19th-century thought, contributing to the fields of biological evolution, sociology, and the classification of sciences. His theories on evolution and the structure of society were widely discussed and integrated into the intellectual currents of his time.

## Contributions to Evolutionary Theory
Spencer was a key proponent of biological evolution. His work elaborated on causes of evolution originally suggested by Lamarck and Buffon. These hypotheses rested on the concept of "acquired characters" and their hereditary transmission, a foundation that was later challenged by August Weismann.

In the realm of zoology, Spencer's work on variation and the bearing of insect habits on theories of use-inheritance and selection was noted alongside the contributions of other scientists such as G.H.T. Eimer and A. Weismann. His influence extended into English thought during the third quarter of the 19th century, where his beliefs in biological evolution were combined with Hume's empiricism.

## Sociological Theories and the Militant Society
Spencer developed a "dictum" regarding the relationship between the type of society and the social position of women. He posited that in a "militant society," the position of woman is necessarily low, and only improves as the society transitions to become more industrial.

This theory was examined in the context of Old Japan. In that society, the characteristics of a militant type of society were exclusively confined to the samurai class, who experienced the least freedom for women. Conversely, the classes above the samurai (the *daimio* and *kugé* nobles) and below them (peasants, tradesmen, and mechanics) exhibited characteristics of an industrial type. Among the lower social classes, such as small artisans, the position of husband and wife was more equal.

## Influence on Philosophy and Religion
Spencer's ideas had a significant impact on various thinkers and religious figures. In America, the preacher Beecher was familiar with the works of Spencer, as well as those of Charles Darwin and Matthew Arnold.

In philosophy, the reaction against certain doctrines—which viewed man simply as a result of natural forces—was led by figures like Green. These critics argued that reducing the human mind to unrelated atomic sensations rendered philosophy futile and made any theory of conduct unmeaning, as they believed intelligible life requires a personal self with the power and knowledge to act.

## Classification of Sciences
Spencer also engaged in the systematic organization of knowledge. His attempts to create a scheme for the division of the sciences are compared to earlier classifications established by Bacon, as well as later attempts by Cournot, Comte, and Ampere. The classification by Bacon had previously served as the foundation for the division of sciences adopted in the great French *Encyclopedie*.
