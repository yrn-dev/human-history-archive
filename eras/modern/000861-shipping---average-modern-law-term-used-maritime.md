# Average in Shipping

In modern maritime law, the term "average" is used in commerce to describe expenses or damages that result from accidents of navigation. This term is divided into two distinct categories: general average and particular average.

## General Average
General average occurs when expenditures are incurred or sacrifices are made for the purpose of preserving the freight, cargo, and ship from the effects of a peril of the sea. The fundamental principle of general average is that any loss incurred for the benefit of all coadventurers should be compensated by all of them. This is done through a subsequent contribution from all concerned parties, which is distributed rateably according to the values of their respective interests.

Historically, the term "average" referred exclusively to what is now known as general average. Because the application of this principle to the complex cases of maritime commerce led to diverse usages across different countries and time periods, technical and sometimes arbitrary rules were required to ensure consistency.

## Particular Average
Particular average refers to a partial loss or damage that happens to the freight, goods, or ship due to an unavoidable or fortuitous accident. Unlike general average, these losses do not involve a general contribution. Instead, the loss is borne by the insurers or the parties to whose property the misfortune occurred. The expression "particular average" was introduced later for the convenience of distinguishing these losses from those for which a general contribution could be claimed; strictly speaking, the term is not considered accurate.

## Etymology and Evolution
The derivation of the modern term is uncertain, though it corresponded with the French *avarie*. Early spellings included *averia*, *avaria*, and *averays*. It originally meant a specific tax on goods, and later, in maritime law, it referred to any charge payable by the owner of goods sent by ship that was additional to the freight. The concept of equitable distribution inherent in this maritime sense eventually transferred to the colloquial meaning of "average" as a medium among various quantities, a common rate, or an equalization of amount.

This is distinct from a separate sense of "average" found in old law, derived from the Law-Latin *averagium* and the Domesday Book *avera* (the day's work given by the king's tenants to the sheriff). This older version related to feudal service rendered by tenants to superiors and survived in the Scottish phrase "arriage and carriage."

## The York-Antwerp Rules
To overcome the difficulties of inconsistent rules across different maritime nations, a set of uniform rules was developed. This effort began in May 1860 at a congress in Glasgow presided over by Lord Brougham and assisted by Lord Neaves. Subsequent congresses took place in London (1862) and York (1864), resulting in the "York Rules."

The "Association for the Reform and Codification of the Law of Nations" later held conferences at the Hague (1875), Bremen (1876), and Antwerp (1877). These conferences modified the York Rules, and the resulting body of rules was named the "York and Antwerp (or York-Antwerp) Rules." These rules were adopted into contracts of insurance and affreightment. Due to certain points remaining unsatisfactory or insufficient, a further conference was held in Liverpool in the autumn of 1890 by the same Association.
