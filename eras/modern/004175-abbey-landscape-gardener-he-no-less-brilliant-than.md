# Westminster Abbey

Westminster Abbey is a prominent site in London associated with the interment of notable figures and the display of artistic works.

## Interments and Monuments
The Abbey serves as a place of burial for distinguished individuals from various fields. Sir Charles Barry (1795-1860), an English architect known for his masterpiece, the Houses of Parliament, had his remains interred there. Additionally, the actress Ann Street Barry, who was known for her impersonations of Desdemona and Lady Randolph at Covent Garden, was buried in the Abbey following her death on November 29, 1801.

The site also houses sculptural works. William Kent (1685-1748), a painter and architect, produced a statue of Shakespeare located within the Abbey; however, this work is described as mediocre and serves as a stamp of his limited powers as a sculptor.

## Comparison of Landscape Gardening Traditions
The archive contains extensive records on the evolution of landscape gardening, contrasting English and Japanese approaches.

### English Landscape Gardening
William Kent is described by Horace Walpole as the "father of modern gardening." Kent was the first in England to advocate for the natural over the artificial. He rejected the mathematical symmetry of ground plans and the "clipped monstrosities" of topiary art using holly, box, or yew. Instead, he sought to imitate the variety of nature, utilizing perspective and the principles of light and shade. He also released streams from marble basins and conventional canals. In some instances, Kent's pursuit of truth was extreme, such as when he planted dead trees in Kensington gardens.

Sir Charles Barry was also noted as being as brilliant a landscape gardener as he was an architect.

### Japanese Landscape Gardening
In Japan, landscape gardening developed into an exact science, particularly during the Muromachi epoch. Buddhist monks achieved a harmony between art and nature, aiming to create water-scapes and landscapes within small parks that did not feel restricted. The first treatise on the subject was written by Gokyogoku Yoshitsune in the early thirteenth century. Tradition credits a Chinese bonze named Nei-issan (or I Ning), a delegate from Kublai Khan, with planning the first proper landscape garden in Japan at the temple Kencho-ji in Kamakura.

Japanese gardening evolved from the artificial "no-niwa" (moor garden) to the pure landscape park. Unlike European parks, which are planned based on nature's indications or a geometrical scale of comeliness, Japanese gardens are designed to evoke special emotions or a particular mood.

### Miniature Gardening
The Japanese also developed the art of miniature landscape gardening, producing balanced water-scapes and landscapes within areas of two or three square feet. A more refined version of this art involves dwarfing trees and shrubs so they remain an accurate facsimile of forest trees, despite being only inches tall after hundreds of years. These trees are grown in pots that are themselves examples of the keramist's craft, with some well-trained trees costing as much as £200.
