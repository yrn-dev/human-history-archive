# Rights of Voting and Levying Taxes

The right to vote on, levy, or control taxes has historically served as a primary point of contention and a mechanism for asserting political autonomy across various regions and governance systems. These rights have been tied to constitutional decrees, regional privileges, and the struggle between representative bodies and sovereign authorities.

## The Estates of Bohemia
In Bohemia, the right to vote on taxes was a central element of the struggle against the absolutist government of the house of Habsburg. Following a decree published by Ferdinand in 1627, the ancient free constitution of Bohemia was formally suppressed. While the sovereign held exclusive legislative power and the diet was restricted to discussing matters presented by the king's representatives, the estates retained the right of voting taxes. However, they were specifically forbidden from attaching any conditions to the grants of money provided to the sovereign.

By 1845, the estates of Bohemia began to oppose the government of Vienna, affirming their right of voting the country's taxes based on the constitution of 1627. In 1847, the estates sought to expand this influence by proposing more stringent control over the country's finances and increasing the representation of towns at the diet. These efforts were interrupted by the revolutionary outbreak of 1848. On March 11 of that year, a public meeting in Prague petitioned the government of Vienna for liberal reforms and a general diet elected on an extensive suffrage. Emperor Ferdinand later declared that equality of rights would be secured for both nationalities in Bohemia and that a new meeting of the estates, including representatives from principal towns, would be convoked.

## The Basque Fueros
The Basques maintained a high degree of independence through safeguards known as *fueros*. A primary safeguard among these was the provision that no contribution or tax could be paid or levied to the crown until all wrongs had been redressed and all petitions heard. The vote regarding such payments was the final act of the cortes or the junta. 

Under this system, money was not paid as a tax or a demand of right, but rather as a voluntary "free gift." These payments were made in a lump sum, and the municipalities and the junta were left entirely in charge of the levying and repartition of the funds.

## Taxation and Suffrage in Belgium and the United States
In Belgium, the new constitution was based on medieval privileges and charters, such as the "Joyeuse Entrée" of Brabant. Among the modern liberties added to this framework was the granting of the right to vote to all Belgians who paid a specific amount in taxes.

In the United States, the federal government utilized income taxes to fund expenses during the Civil War. An Act of Congress on August 5, 1861, imposed a 3% tax on incomes (with an $800 exemption), though no tax was assessed under that specific law. A subsequent act signed on July 1, 1862, imposed a 3% tax on incomes up to $10,000 and 5% on those above that amount, with a $600 exemption. This tax was first levied in 1863 and abolished in 1872. While the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of this tax in 1880, ruling it an excise tax rather than a direct tax, a later income tax act passed as part of the Wilson Bill on August 27, 1894, was declared unconstitutional on May 20, 1895. The court ruled that the tax was a direct tax not apportioned among states by population, necessitating a constitutional amendment before a federal income tax could be imposed.

## Other Administrative and Territorial Tax Systems
Different regions have employed varied methods for managing revenue and territorial rights:

*   **Japan:** In ancient Japan, territorial rights were confounded. The ruler held the competence to impose taxes, while the territorial owner (usually the *uji no Kami*) exercised this right. The *uji no Kami* collected a percentage of produce from those leasing the land and presented a portion to the Court.
*   **Swiss Cantons:** In one specific canton, taxes are never counted in the revenue and expenditure accounts. This creates an apparent deficit that is met by a land tax, which varies annually to provide the necessary amount, ensuring there is never a real deficit.
*   **Danube Region:** Under Article VI of the treaty of London (1871), powers possessing the shores of a part of the Danube were authorized to levy a provisional tax on vessels of every flag to extinguish debt contracted for the execution of works.
