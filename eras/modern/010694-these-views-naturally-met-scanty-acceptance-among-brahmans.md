# The Brahmans and the Caste System of India

## Role and Duties of the Brahmans
The Brahmans constitute a hereditary sacerdotal class in India whose influence has historically tempered the power of absolute monarchies, particularly in the Ganges valley by the 6th century B.C. According to the caste-definitions of Manu, the duties assigned to Brahmans by Brahma include teaching, reading the Vedas, sacrificing, assisting others in sacrifice, and the giving or receiving of alms depending on whether the individual is rich or indigent. 

While the Brahmans have maintained a monopoly over Vedic learning—which is described as a superficial acquaintance with Manu and the Puranas—their actual professional activities vary. Although they have succeeded in excluding other castes from sacred employment, only a portion of the class is engaged in sacred study, religious ceremonies, or religious begging. Other members of the Brahman caste have served as domestic servants, water-carriers, and privates in the army.

## The Development of Caste and Supremacy
The institution of caste is a division of the population based partly on racial distinctions. It rests on the social idea that specific functions are committed to certain classes and the religious idea of an indelible stain resting on certain men. The power of the religious class grew from their role in defining rules of purity and the penalties for their violation. Because the Hindus were passionately attached to national hymns—which were believed to require perfect pronunciation to avoid the anger of the gods—only the priests could teach or chant them.

The assertion of Brahman supremacy was not a natural process but involved "hard fighting." This is illustrated by the account of Parasurama, a Brahman hero who is said to have filled the five large lakes of Samauta with the blood of the Kshatriya caste after clearing the earth of them "thrice seven times." Some theories suggest that the substitution of blood-sacrifices for offerings of soma wine, clarified butter, and parched grain was an adaptation of military banquets to achieve political supremacy.

## Origins and Theoretical Frameworks
The archive contains several contradictory and often unintelligible accounts regarding the origin of caste. Some texts describe it as a late episode in creation, born from non-entity, abstract principles, the mortal Manu, or different parts of different gods. Other accounts claim it was coeval with creation and existed in perfection during the Krita period before falling into sin. Some traditions suggest that only Brahmans existed at first.

Historically, access to the gods through sacrifice and prayer was open to all classes. In the period when the Vedas were composed, many non-Brahmans acted as priests, and some rose to Brahmanhood through "austere fervour." While earlier literature shows Brahmans receiving instruction from Kshatriyas, later texts like the Puranas and Manu suggest that Kshatriyas who were not submissive to Brahmans faced death. For example, Visvamitra, son of Gadhi, obtained Brahmanhood as a reward for his submission.

## Modifications and External Influences
The system described by Manu differs from existing Indian castes in several ways; for instance, Manu does not mention an artisan caste, nor does he state that eating food prepared by another caste results in a loss of caste. 

The teachings of Buddha profoundly modified the system. Buddha taught that all who believe in him cease to be Sudras, Vaisyas, Kshatriyas, or Brahmans, similar to how rivers lose their names upon mingling with the Ganges. Following Buddha, Sudra dynasties ruled in various parts of India, and the Cayets (a race of Sudras) held a near-monopoly on public offices under the Mogul dynasty. However, Buddha did not seek to abolish caste entirely; records indicate a Brahman Pundit who embraced Buddha's doctrines still felt the need to wash from head to foot after being touched by his king.

Other historical observations include:
* **Greek Accounts:** Megasthenes, at the court of Sandracottus, enumerated seven castes: councillors, inspectors, warriors, artisans, herdsmen, agriculturists, and sophists. He suggested any Hindu could become a Brahman, likely confounding Brahmans with fakirs or hermits.
* **Christian Missions:** To convert southern India, Jesuits such as Fra dei Nobili and Xavier adopted Brahman customs, including wearing yellow or cavy dress, using sandalwood paste on their foreheads, and performing frequent ablutions.

## Brahmans in Culture and Later Reform
In Indian drama, the *vidushaka* (the buffoon and humble associate of the prince) is always a Brahman or the pupil of one. This character is characterized by a desire for a quiet life and a particular interest in eating. Additionally, the social position of courtesans in drama is such that association with them is not regarded as a disgrace even to Brahmans.

In more recent history, Dayanand introduced views that met with "scanty acceptance" among the Brahmans. Consequently, he turned to the masses and established *Samajes* in various parts of India, starting in Bombay in 1875. He adopted the term "Arya" as a more dignified alternative to the term "Hindu," which he viewed as slightly contemptuous. Dayanand engaged in public debates with Christian missionaries and orthodox pundits until his death at Ajmere in 1883.
