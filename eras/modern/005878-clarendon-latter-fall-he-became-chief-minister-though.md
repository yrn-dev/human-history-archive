# Clarendon and the Rise of Buckingham

## Political Conflict and the Fall of Clarendon
The political landscape of the mid-17th century was marked by a severe rivalry between Clarendon and the Duke of Buckingham. Buckingham, who had been barred from attaining high office due to the influence of Clarendon, directed his efforts toward the chancellor's ruin. This opposition manifested in several parliamentary and personal conflicts. In 1666, Buckingham organized parties in both houses of parliament to support a bill prohibiting the import of Irish cattle, a move intended partly to oppose Clarendon and partly to thwart the duke of Ormonde. During the debates on this bill, Buckingham asserted that anyone opposing it possessed either an "Irish understanding" or an "Irish interest," leading to a challenge by Lord Ossory. While Buckingham avoided the encounter, Ossory was sent to the Tower.

Further instability occurred on December 19 during a conference between the two houses, where Buckingham engaged in a physical scuffle with the marquess of Dorchester. During the fight, Buckingham pulled off Dorchester's periwig, and Dorchester ended the encounter with some of the duke's hair in his hand. Clarendon noted that such a flagrant misdemeanour had never previously offended the dignity of the House of Lords. Although both peers were sent to the Tower, they were released after apologizing.

## The Fall and Return of Buckingham
Buckingham's opposition to the government eventually cost him the favor of the king. He faced accusations of treasonable intrigues and was accused of casting the king's horoscope. On February 25, 1667, his arrest was ordered and he was dismissed from all his offices. He remained at large until June 27, when he surrendered and was imprisoned in the Tower. However, his imprisonment was brief; he was released by July 17 and restored to his appointments and royal favor by September 15. Following his restoration, Buckingham took an active role in the prosecution of Clarendon.

## Buckingham as Chief Minister
Upon the fall of Clarendon, Buckingham became the chief minister. Despite this primary role in government, he held no high office at the time except for that of master of the horse, a position he purchased from the duke of Albermarle in 1668. 

During his period of influence, Buckingham pursued several academic and religious interests. He was elected chancellor of Cambridge in 1671 and high steward of Oxford university in 1672. In matters of faith, he favored religious toleration, which earned him praise from Richard Baxter. His support for these views was evident in his backing of a scheme of comprehension in 1668 and his advice regarding the declaration of indulgence in 1672. Additionally, in the case of Skinner, he upheld the original jurisdiction of the Lords.

## Related Political Figures: The Earl of Bristol
Parallel to these events was the activity of Digby, the 2nd earl of Bristol, who succeeded to the peerage in January 1659 and was made K.G. that same month. Upon returning to England at the restoration, Bristol found himself relegated to secondary importance and excluded from office due to his religion. This led to a period of restless activity in parliament and violent hostility toward Clarendon.

Bristol's attempts to undermine Clarendon included a June 1663 effort to upset Clarendon's management of the House of Commons. When this intrigue was exposed by Charles, Bristol was forced to exonerate himself before the House, where he admitted to "enlarging" his accounts in a manner that was described as "comedian-like speech." Following a violent confrontation with the king in July, during which Bristol issued a threat that he would do something to "awaken him out of his slumbers," Bristol impeached Clarendon in the Lords for high treason on July 10. After the charge was dismissed, Bristol renewed the accusation and was expelled from court, remaining in concealment for two years to avoid apprehension.

Bristol eventually renounced his Roman Catholicism and declared himself a Protestant at his house in Wimbledon in January 1664, likely to avoid charges of recusancy. He was welcomed back to court when the fall of Clarendon became a desired outcome, taking his seat in the Lords on July 29, 1667.
