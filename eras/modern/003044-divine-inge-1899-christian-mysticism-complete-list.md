# Divine and Mystical Concepts in Religious History

The intersection of divinity, mysticism, and faith has played a central role in the development of various religious and philosophical systems, from the mystic cults of ancient Greece to the scholastic theology of the Middle Ages and the institutionalized lectures of the English church.

## Greek Paganism and Early Mysticism
In ancient Hellas, religion generally served the state, with priests acting as state officials. In this environment, speculative thought and artistic creation were viewed as attributes of divinity. While public worship was common, distinct mystic brotherhoods and cults existed at Samothrace, Andania, and Eleusis. From the beginning of the 7th century, a wave of mysticism—strengthened by the Orphic brotherhoods and the power of Dionysus—spread from the north. Orphism, in particular, possessed a clear apostolic purpose, preaching to both barbarians and Hellenes, regardless of whether they were free or bonded. This period saw a "Salvationist" tendency, where the promise of salvation was based on mystic communion with the deity.

Over time, Greek paganism was penetrated by Oriental worships and ideas, leading to increased mysticism and a movement toward monotheism. Although Christianity eventually appeared to obliterate the old Hellenic religion, the former lent the latter much of its religious terminology and hieratic organization.

## Early Christianity and the Supernatural
In its first ages, Christianity was often viewed as a rebellious movement because it denied the divinity of Caesar. This conflict reached a peak under Emperor Diocletian (303 and following years), whose official persecution required Christians to offer sacrifices to the emperor.

Early Christians frequently operated on "mystic ground," believing in extraordinary events such as prophecy, visions, and deliverance from death or sickness through the supplications of the church. This deep impression of supernatural truths is identified as faith, which some regarded as the only merit of a Christian and a pledge of future felicity and divine favor. Some rigid doctors argued that moral virtues were devoid of efficacy in justification if they were practiced by infidels.

The early development of the faith also involved an exchange of ideas with the surrounding world. The "brilliant mind" of Paul added mystical significance to the career of Jesus, who had called for a new birth into a kingdom of love and the renunciation of self. However, some converts moved away from these plain doctrines toward complicated ceremonies and theories. The writings of Augustine, Plotinus, and Origen reflect this interaction with the Alexandrian world's teachings, including Philonism, Gnosticism, and Neoplatonism.

## Scholasticism and the Philosophy of Bonaventura
In the era of scholastic doctors, Bonaventura presented a mystical and Platonizing mode of speculation that contrasted with the Aristotelian scholasticism of Thomas Aquinas and the physical science of Roger Bacon. Bonaventura rejected the authority of Aristotle, attributing to his influence the heretical tendencies of the age, including the doctrine of the eternity of the world.

Bonaventura's philosophy was based on a version of Platonism understood through the Alexandrian school, the author of mystical works known as Dionysius the Areopagite, and St Augustine. He proposed that ideas exist as thoughts of the divine mind rather than *in rerum natura*. 

In his view, while reason can discover some moral truths, others can only be apprehended through divine illumination. To achieve this, the soul must use prayer, meditation, and the exercise of virtues. The supreme end of life is an ecstatic union with God—a union of intense absorbing love and contemplation—though this is a hope for the futurity and cannot be fully reached in this life.

## The Bampton Lectures
The study of divinity was further institutionalized in England through the Bampton lectures. Established by the will of John Bampton (c. 1690–1751), a member of Trinity College, Oxford, and canon of Salisbury, these lectures began in 1780. The endowment provides £120 for each lecturer, who must be a Master of Arts from Oxford or Cambridge.

The lectures are delivered annually (though since 1895, elections occur in alternate years due to revenue depreciation) at the University Church in Oxford. The mandated subjects include:
* The divinity of the Holy Ghost and the divinity of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ.
* The divine authority of the Holy Scriptures.
* The articles of the Christian faith found in the Nicene and Apostles' Creeds.
* The authority of the primitive fathers regarding the faith and practice of the primitive Church.
* The confirmation of the Christian faith and the confutation of schismatics and heretics.

Notable lecturers have included H. P. Liddon (1866), who spoke on *The Divinity of our Lord*, J. R. Illingworth (1894) on *Personality, Human and Divine*, and W. R. Inge (1899) on *Christian Mysticism*.
