# The Dual Monarchy at the Close of the 19th Century

The Austro-Hungarian Monarchy, also known as the Dual Monarchy, was a central European state consisting of two independent states: Hungary (unofficially Transleithania or the "lands of St Stephen's Crown") and Austria (unofficially Cisleithania or the kingdoms and lands represented in the council of the empire). By 1901, the monarchy had a population of 45,405,267 inhabitants and occupied approximately one-sixteenth of the total area of Europe.

## Constitutional Foundation and Structure
The governance of the Dual Monarchy was based on three primary pillars: the Pragmatic Sanction of Emperor Charles VI (April 19, 1713), which settled succession within the Habsburg-Lorraine dynasty via primogeniture and lineal succession to male heirs (and female heirs in case of male extinction) while ensuring the monarchy's indivisibility; the diploma of Emperor Francis Joseph I (October 20, 1860), which introduced the constitutional form of government; and the *Ausgleich* or "Compromise" of February 8, 1867.

Under this system, Austria and Hungary remained completely independent of one another, each maintaining its own government and parliament. Unity was preserved through a common head of state—bearing the titles Emperor of Austria and Apostolic King of Hungary—and the joint administration of specific affairs.

## Joint Administration and Common Affairs
Common affairs affecting both halves of the monarchy were managed by three joint ministries:
*   **Foreign Affairs:** The ministry of foreign affairs and of the imperial and royal house conducted international relations and concluded international treaties. However, treaties involving territorial changes, state burdens, or commercial matters required the assent of both parliaments.
*   **War:** The ministry of war administered all military affairs, except for the Hungarian *Honveds* and the Austrian *Landwehr*, which were managed by national defense ministries. The monarch held supreme command over the total armed power of the Dual Monarchy.
*   **Finance:** The ministry of finance managed the joint budget, the joint state debt, and common expenditures. Until 1909, this ministry also administered the provinces of Bosnia and Herzegovina (excluding matters dependent on the minister of war). A joint supreme court of accounts audited these finances.

## Political Crises at the Turn of the Century
At the close of the 19th century, both states of the Dual Monarchy experienced severe political crises. In Austria, parliamentary life became paralyzed due to a feud between Czechs and Germans. This conflict resulted indirectly from the development of Slav influence (specifically Poles and Czechs) during the Taaffe era (1879-1893) and directly from the Badeni language ordinances of 1897.

Because of this paralysis, the Austrian government was operated by cabinets of officials utilizing paragraph 14 of the constitution, an emergency clause. While ministers were nominally responsible to parliament, they were in practice responsible only to the emperor. Consequently, during the final years of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th, the constitution was observed in letter rather than spirit, and political life reached a low ebb.

## Economic and Commercial Relations
Despite political instability, the monarchy's foreign policy, commercial affairs, and finances were conducted with success since 1870. The state saw a rise in credit, the disappearance of chronic deficits, the payment of part of the unfunded debt, and the establishment of a sound currency.

Trade between the two states was governed by a customs and commercial agreement first concluded in 1867 and renewed every ten years. This agreement, renewed in 1899, established the monarchy as a single customs and commercial territory, including the principality of Liechtenstein and Bosnia and Herzegovina. Common principles were applied to:
*   Customs legislation and commercial affairs.
*   Legislation on duties for mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer.
*   Coinage, legal tender, and the principles of the Austro-Hungarian Bank.
*   Railways affecting the interests of both states.

## Territorial and Political Evolution
The monarchy's boundaries were defined by Russia and Rumania to the east, the Adriatic Sea, Italy, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, and the German Empire to the west, the German Empire and Russia to the north, and Rumania, Servia, Turkey, and Montenegro to the south. 

Following the Berlin Treaty of 1878, the principalities of Bosnia and Herzegovina (which had a population of 1,591,036 in 1895) were placed under Austro-Hungarian administration, though Turkey remained the suzerain. These territories were annexed in 1908, a move recognized by the Powers in 1909.

The period between 1903 and 1907 is noted as a highly important era of development. The Austro-Hungarian crisis of 1903-1906, combined with the introduction of universal suffrage in Austria, raised questions regarding whether the original dual system remained a permanent form of polity.
