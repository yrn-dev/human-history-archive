# Lettres sur l'origine des sciences and Lettres sur l'Atlantide de Platon

The *Lettres sur l'origine des sciences* (1777) and *Lettres sur l'Atlantide de Platon* (1779) are works authored by Jean Sylvain Bailly, a French astronomer, orator, and historian of science. These texts form part of a broader body of work in which Bailly applied his scientific background to the study of ancient history and the development of human knowledge.

## Author Biography
Jean Sylvain Bailly was born in Paris on September 15, 1736. Although he was initially intended for a career as a painter and later spent time writing tragedies, he was drawn to science through the influence of Nicolas de Lacaille. Bailly's scientific contributions include the calculation of the orbit for the comet of 1759 (Halley's) and the reduction of Lacaille's observations of 515 zodiacal stars. 

His academic achievements led to his election as a member of the Academy of Sciences in 1763. He published the *Essai sur la theorie des satellites de Jupiter* in 1766, followed by a dissertation in 1771 titled *Sur les inegalites de la lumiere des satellites de Jupiter*. Bailly also established a literary reputation through his *Eloges* of figures such as Moliere, Corneille, Leibnitz, Lacaille, and Charles V, which were collected in 1770 and 1790. He was admitted to the French Academy on February 26, 1784, and to the Academie des Inscriptions in 1785.

## Scope and Content of the Works
Following his admission to the academies, Bailly focused on the history of science. This period of his career produced several major titles, including *Histoire de l'astronomie ancienne* (1775), *Lettres sur l'origine des sciences* (1777), *Histoire de l'astronomie moderne* (1779-1782), *Lettres sur l'Atlantide de Platon* (1779), and *Traite de l'astronomie indienne et orientale* (1787).

In the *Lettres sur les Sciences* and *Lettres sur l'Atlantide*, Bailly presents a system or "romance" that utilizes allegorical interpretation to explain ancient myths. He posits that various mythological figures and events are symbols for astronomical phenomena. Specifically, Bailly argues that the annual death and revival of Osiris and Adonis, as well as the phoenix of the Edda, serve as allegorical symbols representing the return and absence of the sun in the Arctic regions.

## Critical Reception
Bailly's approach to history and science was characterized by some as being influenced by the philosophy of the great Buffon. While his works are noted for their erudition, critics have observed that this scholarship was sometimes marred by "speculative extravagances." His theories regarding the Arctic sun and mythological allegory have been described as "ingenious," though they represent a philosophy that can be difficult for "the coldest reason" to withstand.
