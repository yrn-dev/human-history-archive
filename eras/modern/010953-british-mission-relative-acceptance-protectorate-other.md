# British Mission Relative to the Acceptance of a Protectorate and Other Matters

The British government utilized various diplomatic and military missions to establish protectorates and spheres of influence across Africa and other global regions. These efforts often involved negotiating treaties, demanding the cessation of specific practices, or intervening in local political disputes to secure British jurisdiction.

## The Mission to Ashanti
In the late 19th century, the British government sought to formalize its influence over Ashanti. Following a period of instability and a desolating civil war, Prince Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888, with the sanction of a Gold Coast government officer. However, by 1893, tensions rose between Ashanti and tribes within the British protectorate, leading Prempeh to order the closure of roads to traders.

Because Ashanti was included in the British sphere of influence by international agreement, the British government dispatched a mission to Prempeh. This mission presented several specific demands:
*   The fulfillment of the terms of the 1874 treaty.
*   The acceptance of a British protectorate.
*   The acceptance of a British resident at Kumasi.

King Prempeh refused to treat with the governor of the Gold Coast and instead sent informal agents to England, though the secretary of state refused to receive them. Prempeh failed to reply to the mission's demands within the three-week grace period, which expired on October 31, 1895.

## Missions in Dahomey and West Africa
British efforts in the region of Dahomey focused on checking the "aggressive spirit" of the king. In 1861, England annexed the island of Lagos to achieve this goal. Subsequent missions were sent to the king of Dahomey to address specific humanitarian and commercial concerns. In 1863, Commander Wilmot, R.N., and in 1864, Sir Richard Burton were dispatched on missions to induce the Dahomeyans to abandon slave-trading and human sacrifices, though these efforts were unsuccessful.

Later, in the region of Northern Nigeria, the province of Kontagora was brought under British domination in 1901 following a military expedition. This action was taken to prevent threats against the British military station of Jebba and to stop "audacious slave-raiding" within British protected territory. In 1903, Emir Ibrahim was reinstated after agreeing to the abolition of the slave trade within the province and taking an oath of allegiance to the British crown.

## Protectorates in Central and Southern Africa
In South Central Africa, the British established various protectorates north of the Zambezi river. The region known as British Central Africa (later the Nyasaland Protectorate in 1907) was established on the Shiré and around Lake Nyasa. 

In the Barotse kingdom, the British South Africa Company established rule in regions north of the Middle Zambezi. While the company appointed an administrator and raised a native police force under a British officer, it did not interfere in internal Barotse affairs. Although the missionary Francois Coillard initially advised delaying the arrival of white men to protect his mission, Lewanika, the paramount chief of the Barotse, petitioned the "Great White Queen" to assume a protectorate over his dominions.

## Global Protectorates and International Recognition
The British Empire established numerous other protectorates and settlements through treaties, cessions, and declarations:
*   **Asia and Pacific:** Protectorates were assumed over North Borneo (1888), Brunei (1888), and various Western Pacific Islands by 1900.
*   **Africa:** Protectorates were declared in Somaliland (1887), Bechuanaland (1885-1891), and Papua (1884). In Nigeria, the territory was divided into North and South Nigeria in 1900.
*   **Egypt:** A British Protectorate over Egypt was declared on December 18, 1914. This status was later recognized by Germany, which renounced its treaties with Egypt and agreed not to intervene in negotiations between Great Britain and other powers regarding the territory.
