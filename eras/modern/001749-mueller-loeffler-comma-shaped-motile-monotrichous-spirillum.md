# Spirillaceae and Related Motile Bacteria

The Spirillaceae is a family of bacteria characterized by vegetative cells that are cylindrical in shape but curved to a varying degree spirally. Within this family, divisions occur perpendicular to the long axis of the cell.

## Genera of the Spirillaceae
The family includes several distinct genera based on the degree of curvature and the nature of their motile organs:

*   ***Vibrio* (Mueller-Loeffler):** These organisms are comma-shaped and motile. Their motility is described as monotrichous.
*   ***Spirillum* (Ehrenb.):** These cells exhibit a stronger curvature than *Vibrio*, forming open spirals. They are motile and characterized as lophotrichous.
*   ***Spirochaete* (Ehrenb.):** These organisms are spirally coiled in numerous close turns. While they are motile, this movement appears to be due to flexile movements, as no cilia are found.

## General Bacterial Characteristics
Most bacteria, including those in the Spirillaceae, are either parasitic or saprophytic forms due to an absence of chlorophyll. While a few secrete coloring matters, the majority are colorless. Typical bacterial cells are approximately 0.001 mm (1 micron) in diameter and range from two to five times that length, though variations in size exist.

## Motility and Cilia
Independent movement in many bacteria is effected by special motile organs known as cilia or flagella. These are fine protoplasmic outgrowths of the cell that appear to be attached to the cell-wall, although some sources suggest they derive from the central protoplasm and pass through pores in the wall. 

Because these structures are invisible under ordinary illumination in living cells or unstained preparations, they can only be made clearly visible through special methods of preparation and staining first introduced by Loeffler. The number of cilia can vary depending on the medium, and they may only be present during short periods of the organism's life.

## Spore Formation and Structure
In the vegetative state, some *Spirillum*-like Schizomycetes undergo spore-formation. In certain instances, a *Spirillum* may contain many spores which are liberated when the parent cells break up.

Regarding internal structure, the existence of a nucleus in bacteria has been a subject of significant discussion. In most forms, a nucleus similar to those found in higher plants has not been demonstrated. Instead, deeply-staining "chromatin-granules" have been observed and considered by some to be a rudimentary nucleus, though this comparison is viewed by others as resting on a slender basis. However, some of the largest bacteria appear to possess nuclei with a typical structure and mode of division.
