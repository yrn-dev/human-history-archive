# Boracite

Boracite is a mineral characterized by its optical anomalies and specific crystalline properties. It was first observed in 1787 within the salt deposits at Luneburg in Hanover, where small crystals with sharply defined faces are found embedded in anhydrite and gypsum.

## Physical and External Characteristics
The external form of boracite crystals is cubic with inclined hemihedrism, sharing the same symmetry as tetrahedrite and blende. Depending on the predominant form, the habit of the crystals may vary between the cube, the tetrahedron, or the rhombic dodecahedron. In some instances, penetration twins are observed with a tetrahedron face serving as the twin-plane.

Visually, boracite crystals range from translucent to transparent and possess a vitreous lustre. While they are primarily white or colourless, they are frequently tinged with green, yellow, or grey. The mineral has a specific gravity of 3.0 and a hardness of 7 on the Mohs scale.

## Pyroelectric Properties
As first observed by R.J. Hauy in 1791, boracite crystals are markedly pyroelectric. When a cubic crystal is heated, it becomes negatively electrified on four corners and positively electrified on the four opposite corners. In crystals where the rhombic dodecahedron predominates, the antilogous poles (the larger and bright tetrahedral faces) are associated with negative electrification, while the analogous poles (the smaller and dull tetrahedral faces) become positively electrified upon heating.

## Optical Anomalies and Internal Structure
Although boracite appears to follow cubic symmetry, Sir David Brewster observed in 1821 that the mineral is doubly refracting when examined in polarized light. Thin sections of the mineral reveal a division into definite optically biaxial areas and twin-lamellae. 

Analysis of sections indicates that a rhombic dodecahedral crystal is actually a compound (pseudo-cubic) crystal composed of twelve orthorhombic pyramids. The bases of these pyramids coincide with the dodecahedral faces, and their apices meet at the center of the crystal. Other crystal forms exhibit different types of internal structures.

## Thermal Effects and Modifications
The optical characteristics of boracite change when the mineral is heated. At a temperature of 265 degrees, the crystals suddenly become optically isotropic. Upon cooling, the complex internal structure reappears.

Various theories have been proposed to explain these anomalies. Some observers suggest they are the result of internal strains or alteration. Others propose that the crystals originally grew as truly cubic at temperatures exceeding 265 degrees. Current evidence suggests the existence of two crystalline modifications of the boracite substance:
*   A cubic modification that is stable above 265 degrees.
*   An orthorhombic (or monoclinic) modification that is stable at lower temperatures.

This phenomenon is strictly analogous to silver iodide, which possesses rhombohedral and cubic modifications at different temperatures. However, while both rhombohedral and pseudo-cubic crystals of silver iodide (iodyrite) are found in nature, only pseudo-cubic crystals of boracite have been encountered.
