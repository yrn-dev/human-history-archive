# British Baluchistan and the Baluchistan Agency

The Baluchistan Agency is a political administrative entity extending from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea and the Persian frontier. Within this agency, the territory is divided into two primary divisions: British Baluchistan, which is a portion of British India, and foreign territories that remain under the administration or superintendence of the same officer acting as agent to the governor-general.

## British Baluchistan
British Baluchistan is an official province of British India under the authority of a chief commissioner. It is described as an irregularly shaped block of upland territory. Its boundaries include the Toba plateau and all upper Lora tributaries to the north, following the main water-divide between the Lora and the Zhob. To the west, it rests on the Kwaja Amran (Kojak) Range, including an advanced loop for the Chaman railway terminus. It reaches south through Shorarud to Nushki and extends to the east to embrace the Thal Chotiali valley, while including the basins of the Nari and Bolan as far as Sibi to the south-east.

The province covers an area of 9,403 square miles. Officially, it includes the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts were originally Afghan but were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, though they had nominally been handed over to Kalat forty years prior. The territory was formally declared part of British India in 1887. British Baluchistan is brought under the ordinary forms of civil administration used in British India, and its administrative staff is typically composed of military officers.

## The Baluchistan Agency and Political Control
The Baluchistan Agency encompasses a variety of political statuses. While British Baluchistan is a direct province, the rest of the agency consists of foreign territories. These are further divided into three subdivisions:
*   **Native States:** This includes Las Bela and the state of Kalat (which includes Kharan and Makran), covering 79,382 square miles.
*   **Tribal Areas:** Areas in the possession of the Bugti and Marri tribes, covering 7,129 square miles.
*   **Directly Administered Districts:** These include the Chagai and Zhob political agencies, the Bolan Pass, and the eastern portion of the Quetta tahsil, totaling 36,401 square miles. Some of these are tribal areas where revenue is collected, while others are held on lease from the khan of Kalat.

The agent to the governor-general of India, supported by political assistants, exercises supreme control over the agency. While some districts within the agency are as independent as those in Afghanistan, the overall political status of the province resembles that of the native states of the Indian peninsula. The degree of independence in various districts generally varies in proportion to their distance from Quetta.

## Strategic and Administrative Interests
The British maintain a strategic presence in the region to ensure order and security. The occupation of the Zhob valley is used to hold the Pathan tribes of the Suliman hills in check, while the central position at Quetta serves to safeguard the security of the Baluch hills and Kalat, and prevents an advance toward India via Kandahar.

In 1890 and 1891, politico-military expeditions led to the occupation of the Zhob valley and the establishment of the central cantonment of Fort Sandeman. This extension of military occupation, reaching Wana north of the Gomal, reduced the independent Pathan tribes of the Suliman mountains to order and ended border raiding on the Indus plains. Additionally, in July 1899, the Nushki district and Niabat were transferred to the British agency via a perpetual lease in exchange for a perpetual rent of Rs. 9,000 per annum, securing control of the highway to Seistan.

## Geography and Climate
The hills of British Baluchistan are formed in part by Jurassic rocks, with Triassic beds and *Fusulina* limestone found along the south side of the upper Zhob. The broader mountain ranges consist mainly of Tertiary and Cretaceous beds arranged in folds. In north-western Baluchistan, there is an extensive development of conglomerates and volcanic tuffs.

The climate of British Baluchistan is described as dry, bracing, and exhilarating, and is generally favorable for Europeans. However, extreme temperatures can lead to severe forms of fever.
