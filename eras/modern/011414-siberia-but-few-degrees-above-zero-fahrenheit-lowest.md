# Siberia

## Geography and Topography
Siberia is the portion of Asia located between the Arctic Ocean and the mountainous belt that bounds Turkestan, Mongolia, and Manchuria to the north. The region is characterized by an immense, broken high plateau that extends from the south-west toward the north-east, decreasing in both altitude and width as it advances. This plateau is bordered by high ridges that eventually subside in the north-west into high plains reaching 1500 to 2000 ft., before finally dropping to lowlands a few hundred feet above sea-level.

The region is traversed by some of the largest rivers in the world, specifically the Lena, Yenisei, and Ob. To the west, the Ural Mountains feature an average altitude of 2000 to 3000 ft., with the highest summits not exceeding 6000 ft. and some passes as low as 1400 ft. The southern half of the Ural range contains the primary mining districts of Russia.

## Climate and Temperature
Siberia is characterized by extreme variations in temperature. The mean temperature along the north coast of eastern Siberia is only a few degrees above zero Fahrenheit. In the northern latitudes, winters are long and of extreme severity; minimum temperatures remain nearly as low as zero Fahrenheit even down to the 35 deg. N. latitude circle. While summers in these northern latitudes are short, they are hot, with maximum heat levels that are only slightly less than those found in tropical countries further south.

The range of temperature in Siberia is very great. Near the coast, the difference between the mean of the hottest and coldest months exceeds 100 deg. Fahr., and the difference between extreme temperatures is even greater. Generally, the difference between the monthly means is hardly ever less than 60 deg. Fahr. Extreme temperatures in the region are estimated to range from maxima of 80 deg. to 90 deg. Fahr. to minima between -40 deg. and -70 deg. Fahr.

## Soil and Vegetation
The surface of Siberia is often peaty or swampy, though rainfall is not heavy. This precipitation is sufficient to support vegetation compatible with the temperature conditions, including shrubs and grasses on the plains and pine forests on the mountain-sides.

A notable characteristic of the region is the frozen soil, specifically in the vicinity of Yakutsk. In this area, the earth is permanently frozen to a depth of more than 380 ft., where temperatures remain 5 deg. or 6 deg. Fahr. below the freezing point of water. At a depth of 50 ft., the temperature is approximately 15 Fahr. below the freezing point. Summer heat only thaws the surface to a depth of about 3 ft. Despite these frozen conditions, the land is capable of producing crops of other grain and wheat from fifteen to forty fold.

## Population and Wildlife
The population of Siberia is very scanty, and cultivated tracts are restricted to more than settled districts and are small in extent. The towns in the region are entirely Russian. The indigenous races are nomadic Mongols who are described as peaceful but in a backward state of civilization.

In terms of wildlife, Siberia is part of the vast Palaearctic subregion. One of the genera peculiar to this subregion found in Siberia is *Eurynorhynchus*, the spoon-billed sandpiper.
