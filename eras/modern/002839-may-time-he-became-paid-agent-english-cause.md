# Paid Agents in Historical and Legal Contexts

The role of the paid agent appears across various historical, commercial, and legal contexts, ranging from political espionage and diplomatic influence to professional brokerage and law enforcement investigations.

## Political and Diplomatic Agents
In the context of 16th-century Scotland, Balnaves served as a paid agent for the English cause. His involvement began in May following his release by the arrival of Hertford's fleet. Balnaves was an active defender of the castle of St Andrews, though he did not participate in the murder of Beton. His services were compensated by the English crown; he received £100 from Henry VIII in December 1546 and was later granted an annuity of £125 by Protector Somerset in 1547. In addition to these payments, he was appointed as the English paymaster of the forces in St Andrews. Even after being taken prisoner to Rouen by the French in July, Somerset continued his pension. Balnaves remained useful to the English government by providing information, for which Mary Tudor rewarded him with £50 in June 1554.

In ancient Athens, the use of paid agents was a tool of foreign influence. Philip of Macedon utilized Athenian citizens as secret agents to serve his interests. Demosthenes alluded to a dangerous party within Athens that was in communication with and likely paid by Philip. Such corruption extended beyond Athens to other locations, including Olynthus, Potidæa, and Amphipolis, where Philip purchased corrupt partisans among the leaders of his enemies to facilitate his successes.

## Commercial and Professional Agents
In the commercial sector, specific types of agents operate under established trade customs. An insurance broker is an agent whose primary business is effecting marine insurance policies. The broker is employed by the party seeking insurance, handles the payment of premiums to the underwriter, and manages the policy and subsequent payments in the event of a loss. While the broker is employed by the assured, they are paid a commission by the underwriter.

Similarly, ship-brokers act as commission agents and often serve as ships' managers. Their role is to act as agents for ship owners to find purchasers for vessels, or conversely, to find ships for intending purchasers. They also facilitate the finding of charterers for ship-owners or ships for charterers, effecting the charter-party in the process.

## Law Enforcement and Legal Agents
In modern legal proceedings, "case agents" are utilized by government bodies such as the FBI to manage investigations and communicate with victims. In the matter involving Jeffrey Epstein, case agents were tasked with notifying victims about a Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA). 

Internal conflicts arose regarding the duties of these agents. One case agent expressed discomfort with informing victims about the monetary damages provision of the NPA, fearing it could create impeachment material if the case proceeded to trial. However, supervisory directions from Villafafia required agents to meet with victims to provide information regarding the conclusion of the federal investigation and the terms of the NPA. This was based on the belief that victims needed to be informed of their right to seek damages. Furthermore, the Victims' Rights and Restitution Act (VRRA) requires agents to keep victims apprised of case developments.

## Other Agency Roles
Agents have also functioned as representatives of social and humanitarian causes. Jacques Pierre Brissot, a French Girondist and president of the Société des Amis des Noirs, acted as an agent of that society during a visit to the United States in 1788.
