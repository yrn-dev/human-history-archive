# Parliament of 1640

The year 1640 was a pivotal period in English political history, characterized by financial instability, religious conflict, and the summoning of multiple parliamentary bodies by King Charles.

## Origins and the Short Parliament
The necessity for summoning a Parliament in 1640 arose from a combination of military failure and financial insolvency. In 1638, King Charles attempted to extend the characteristics of the Church of England—which was described as half-Catholic and half-Protestant—to Scotland. This effort was met with resistance in Scotland, where Presbyterianism, a non-sacramental and non-sacerdotal form of Christianity, had been established as the national church.

The resulting Scottish revolt led to mutinies among the English levies raised by Charles to fight them. These events, coupled with the insolvency that naturally followed a "spirited" foreign policy, left the King without trustworthy troops or money. Consequently, Charles summoned a Parliament in 1640, known as the Short Parliament, which he dismissed within the same year.

## The Long Parliament and Political Factions
Following the dismissal of the Short Parliament and an attempt to utilize a Council of Peers at York in 1640, Charles summoned his final Parliament in November of that year. This body became known as the Long Parliament.

The Long Parliament was composed of various members with diverging loyalties. Sir Harbottle Grimston, representing the borough of Colchester, sat in both the Short Parliament and the Long Parliament. Grimston quickly became a prominent figure in the popular party, vigorously attacking Archbishop Laud and serving on important parliamentary committees.

Other members, such as John Ashburnham, represented the interests of the Crown. Ashburnham, the member of parliament for Hastings, utilized his position in the Long Parliament to render services to the King by reporting on the proceedings of the house.

## Regional Representation and Military Context
The parliamentary landscape of 1640 also saw the return of representation for certain boroughs. For example, the borough of Honiton, which had not returned members to parliament since 1311, began returning two members again starting in 1640.

Simultaneously, the political instability of the era was mirrored by military movements. In 1640, William Ker, the 3rd earl of Lothian, marched with the Scots into England. He was present during the routing of the English at Newburn, an event that led to his appointment as the governor of Newcastle-on-Tyne. Additionally, the period saw bloodless expeditions, such as the one that concluded with the Pacification of Berwick in 1640.
