# Patrick Branwell Brontë

## Family Background and Early Life
Patrick Branwell Brontë (1817–1848) was the only son of Patrick Brontë, a clergyman of the Church of England, and Maria Branwell. He was one of six children; his siblings included sisters Charlotte, Emily Jane, and Anne, as well as Maria and Elizabeth. His father, born in Ireland and educated at St John's College, Cambridge, served as the perpetual incumbent of the parish of Haworth in the West Riding of Yorkshire for the final forty-one years of his life.

In his early years, Branwell showed artistic leanings and wrote both prose and verse. Because of these inclinations, the family's slender resources were strained to allow him to enter the Royal Academy as a pupil in 1835. During this period, he was considered the idol of his sisters and his aunt.

## Professional History and Decline
Branwell's professional life was marked by instability and a lack of reputable achievement. After a month of extravagance in London following his entry into the Royal Academy, he returned home. He continued his art studies in Leeds for a period, though it is assumed he received no commissions.

His subsequent employment history included the following roles:
* Tutor to the son of a Mr. Postlethwaite in Barrow-in-Furness.
* Booking-clerk at Luddenden Foot and Sowerby Bridge station on the Leeds & Manchester railway (the latter position beginning ten months after his time in Barrow-in-Furness).
* Tutor for the family of a clergyman named Robinson at Thorp Green, where his sister Anne was employed as a governess.

Ultimately, Branwell returned to Haworth. He spent his remaining time loafing at the village inn and frittering his life away in "painful sottishness," shocking his sisters with his excesses.

## Relationship with Sisters and Death
Despite the literary distinction achieved by his sisters, Branwell remained detached from their intellectual pursuits. While a story invented long after his death attributed to him a share in his sisters' novels—specifically Emily Brontë's *Wuthering Heights*—Charlotte Brontë distinctly stated that her brother never knew his sisters had published any work. In the final two years of his life, he was too muddled and besotted by the effects of drink to participate in their interests.

Patrick Branwell Brontë died in September 1848. His death was viewed as a tragedy, having disappointed the hopes centered upon him. Charlotte Brontë noted that their father naturally placed more thought in his only son than in his daughters.
