# The Silent Engine: The Role of the Peasantry in the Russian Revolution

While most historical accounts of the 1917 Russian Revolution focus on the urban proletariat of Petrograd and the ideological maneuvers of Vladimir Lenin, the revolution was not merely a city-based phenomenon. To understand the rise of communism in Russia, one must look beyond the factories to the vast countryside. The Russian peasantry, comprising over 80% of the population, provided the essential—and often volatile—foundation upon which the Bolsheviks built their state.

The relationship between the Bolsheviks and the peasants was one of convenience rather than shared ideology. While Lenin spoke of a "dictatorship of the proletariat," the peasants were primarily interested in *Chernyi Peredel* (the Black Repartition)—the seizure and redistribution of land from the nobility to those who worked it.

## Land, Bread, and the Tactical Alliance

In 1917, the Provisional Government failed to address the land question, hoping instead to resolve it through legal channels after the war. The Bolsheviks capitalized on this hesitation. By issuing the "Decree on Land," Lenin effectively legalized the spontaneous seizures of estates already occurring across the countryside. 

This was a masterstroke of political pragmatism. The Bolsheviks did not actually support the small-scale peasant farming model; their goal was collective, state-run agriculture. However, by endorsing the peasants' immediate desire for land ownership, they neutralized potential rural opposition during the critical months of the October Revolution.

## The Friction of War Communism

The alliance between the city and the country fractured during the Russian Civil War (1917–1923). To feed the starving cities and the Red Army, the Bolsheviks implemented "War Communism," which included *prodrazvyorstka* (forced grain requisitioning). Armed detachments were sent into villages to seize surpluses by force.

This led to widespread peasant uprisings, most notably the Tambov Rebellion (1920–1921). The brutality of these requisitions forced Lenin to perform a strategic retreat. In 1921, he introduced the New Economic Policy (NEP), which allowed peasants to sell their surplus grain on the open market. This temporary return to limited capitalism saved the regime from total collapse, though it created a tension between the state and the "Kulaks" (wealthier peasants) that would eventually culminate in Stalin’s violent collectivization in the 1930s.

## Scholarly Perspectives

Historians continue to debate whether the peasantry were "passive" participants who were manipulated by the Bolsheviks, or "active" agents whose own agrarian revolution happened parallel to, but independent of, the political revolution in the cities.

### Key Facts
* **Demographics:** Roughly 80-85% of the Russian Empire's population were peasants in 1917.
* **The Decree on Land:** A pivotal Bolshevik policy that abolished private land ownership and handed control to peasant committees.
* **War Communism:** The period of extreme state control and forced requisitioning used to sustain the Red Army.
* **NEP (New Economic Policy):** A tactical shift in 1921 that reintroduced market elements to stabilize the rural economy.
* **The Tambov Rebellion:** One of the largest peasant revolts against Bolshevik rule, suppressed with extreme military force.

### Did You Know?
* **The "Green Armies":** During the Civil War, many peasants formed "Green Armies" that fought against both the "Red" Bolsheviks and the "White" monarchists to protect their local villages.
* **The Kulak Label:** The term "Kulak" originally meant "fist," implying a peasant who gripped the land tightly or exploited others.
* **Land Hunger:** Despite the emancipation of serfs in 1861, peasants remained in deep poverty due to "redemption payments" owed to the state for decades.
