# Audit and Verification

An audit is the process of verifying a record to ensure it serves as a true and complete statement of the transactions involved. This verification is essential in systems of book-keeping—the systematic recording of business transactions—to guard against dishonesty and to allow individuals or corporations to ascertain the particulars of their transactions and their cumulative financial effects.

## Types of Book-keeping Audits
In the context of commercial or financial operations, audits may be conducted by different entities depending on the size and nature of the organization. For large concerns and public entities, audits are ordinarily undertaken by professional accountants. In organizations with large book-keeping staffs, a "staff audit" or "internal check" may be performed by the staff itself, organized so that members check each other's work.

## Government and Public Administration
Various governments and public departments utilize audits to maintain financial accountability and oversight:

*   **Japan:** In the historical administration of the Tokugawa estates, accounts for cash receipts were compiled by the administrator of accounts (*kane-bugyo*) and subsequently audited by officials known as *katte-kata*, an office presided over by a member of the *waka-doshiyori* or *roju*. In later governance, the Board of Audit is tasked with verifying and confirming the final account of the State's revenue and expenditure, which is then submitted by the Government to the Imperial Diet along with the Board's verification report.
*   **United States Department of Justice:** The Office of the Inspector General (OIG) Audit Division conducts audits on various systems and contracts. For example, a 2008 audit of the Victim Notification System (VNS) identified concerns regarding templates that users could not alter to fit specific cases, leading to confusing or contradictory notifications. Other OIG audits have focused on the Federal Bureau of Prisons, including reviews of contracts with Spectrum Services, Inc. and CoreCivic, Inc.
*   **United Kingdom Board of Trade:** Under the act of 1883, the bankruptcy department of the Board of Trade exercises general supervision over administrative work related to bankruptcy. This department conducts audits of the accounts of trustees.

## Bankruptcy and Trustee Oversight
In bankruptcy proceedings, the audit of accounts is a critical mechanism for ensuring the equitable distribution of a debtor's assets. Trustees' accounts must be audited by a committee of inspection at least once every three months. Every six months, and at the close of the administration, the cash books, records, and vouchers must be submitted to the Board of Trade for a final audit, accompanied by the committee's certificate of audit.

Failure to render accounts for audit without good cause can be grounds for the Board of Trade to object to a person's appointment as a trustee. Additionally, if a trustee retains more than £50 in hand for more than ten days without a satisfactory explanation, they may be removed from office, lose all claims to remuneration, and be surcharged with interest at a rate of 20% per annum.

## Historical Practices in Athens
In ancient Athens, it was general practice for magistrates going out of office to undergo a trial of accountability and an audit. Following the fall of the Four Hundred oligarchs, members of that body were called to undergo this process. Those who did not appear for the trial faced condemnation to exile, fines, or having their names recorded as traitors. While some were condemned to partial political disability or fines, many who appeared were acquitted, reportedly through the use of bribes paid to the auditing officers, known as *logistæ*.
