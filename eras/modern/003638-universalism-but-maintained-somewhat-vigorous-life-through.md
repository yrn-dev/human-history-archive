# Universalism and Related Historical Developments

## The Providence Church and Universalism
In the history of the Providence church, a period of decline followed the withdrawal of Williams. During this time, the congregation was led by members including Gregory Dexter, Chad Brown (d. 1665), William Wickenden, and Thomas Olney (d. 1682). A schism occurred in 1652, dividing the church into two factions. One group, consisting of Olney and a minority of members, maintained a belief in particular redemption and rejected the ordinance of the laying on of hands. The opposing majority, which included Dexter, Brown, and Wickenden, contended for the laying on of hands as an indispensable requirement for fellowship and advocated for general redemption.

Following the death of Olney in 1682, his party became extinct. The surviving portion of the church became involved in Universalism and Socinianism. Despite these shifts, the church maintained a somewhat vigorous life. Through the efforts of Wickenden and others, it exerted considerable influence in various regions, including New York, Connecticut, and Newport. Additionally, Gregory Dexter emerged as a leading statesman in Providence Plantations and Rhode Island, alongside Clarke and Williams.

## Universal Systems of Ethics in the Roman Empire
During the decline of the Roman state, the principles that had previously maintained the purity and vigor of Sparta and Rome were extinguished. In this environment of a despotic and declining empire, the influence of Pagan superstition provided very feeble support for the cause of virtue. 

Under these circumstances, the progress of Christianity was observed by prudent magistrates. The religion was seen as diffusing a universal system of ethics among the people that was benevolent, pure, and adapted to every condition of life and every duty. This system was recommended as the reason and will of the supreme Deity and was enforced by the sanction of punishments or rewards in eternity. Constantine's favor toward Christians may be attributed to his esteem for their moral character and his belief that the propagation of the gospel would inculcate public and private virtue. The apologist Lactantius further suggested that the establishment of Christianity could restore the felicity and innocence of the primitive age.

## Spartan Hegemony and Universal Influence
In ancient Greece, the empire of Sparta achieved a state that was nearly universal over Greece, though this was not undisputed. Following the revolution in Thebes, the balance of power in Greece was altered, and Spartan empire was thereafter only maintained through more or less effort until its eventual complete overthrow. 

At the end of 405 B.C., the Spartan empire maintained its full grandeur for slightly more than ten years, until the naval battle of Knidus in 394 B.C. While this defeat destroyed its maritime ascendency and fleet, Sparta maintained its power on land until 371 B.C., when it was defeated by the Thebans at Leuktra. During this period, Sparta utilized a system of garrisons and harmosts in the islands and dependent cities on the continent. Additionally, Lysander had universally established native dekarchies throughout the maritime dependencies, although these did not last as long as the harmosts.
