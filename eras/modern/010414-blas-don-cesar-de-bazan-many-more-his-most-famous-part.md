# Edwin Booth

Edwin Booth was a prominent actor known for his extensive repertoire and his contributions to the theatrical landscape of New York. His professional achievements included the establishment of his own venue, Booth's theatre, located at the corner of 6th Avenue and 23rd Street in New York between 1868 and 1869. Through this theatre, Booth organized a high-quality stock company that produced various plays, including *The Merchant of Venice*, *The Winter's Tale*, *Julius Caesar*, *Macbeth*, *Much Ado about Nothing*, and *Romeo and Juliet*. Booth was noted for utilizing the true text of Shakespeare in these productions, a reform that occurred many years before similar changes took place in England.

## Career and Notable Roles
Booth's acting career was marked by a wide array of roles. His parts included:
*   **Shakespearean roles:** Macbeth, Lear, Othello, Iago, Shylock, Richard II, Richard III, Benedick, and Petruccio.
*   **Other roles:** Wolsey, Richelieu, Sir Giles Overreach, Brutus (from Payne), Bertuccio (in Tom Taylor's *The Fool's Revenge*), Ruy Blas, and Don Cesar de Bazan.

His most famous part was Hamlet. He was considered peculiarly fitted for this role due to his eloquent sensibility and his extraordinary beauty and grace. It is probable that Booth played Hamlet more often than any other actor before or since. His final appearance in this role took place in Brooklyn in 1891.

## International Success and Personal Life
Booth achieved significant success abroad, visiting London in 1851, 1880, and 1882, where he performed at the Haymarket theatre with brilliant success. He also visited Germany in 1882, where his acting was met with the highest enthusiasm.

In his personal life, Booth was married twice. His first marriage was to Mary Devlin in 1860, who died in 1863; they had one daughter, Edwina Booth Grossman, who later published *Edwin Booth: Recollections* in 1894. In 1869, he married Mary F. McVicker, who died in 1881.

## Financial Ventures and The Players' Club
Despite his professional success, Booth's management was not economical, as he possessed a confiding and generous nature. In 1874, he lost his entire fortune along with the grand dramatic structure he had built. However, through arduous toil, he was able to accumulate wealth once again.

Demonstrating his generosity, Booth converted his spacious residence in Gramercy Park, New York, into a club called The Players'. This club was intended for the elect of the acting profession as well as members of other professions they chose to include. Booth transferred the house, his works of art, his books, and many invaluable mementos of the stage to the property of the club, retaining only a single apartment for himself. He died in this apartment on June 7, 1893.
