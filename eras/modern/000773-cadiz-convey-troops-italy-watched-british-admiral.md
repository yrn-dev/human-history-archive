# Spanish Troop Conveyance from Cadiz to Italy

## Naval Operations at Cadiz
During a period when Spain was focused on the Italian policy of its king, a Spanish squadron was fitted out at Cadiz for the purpose of conveying troops to Italy. This movement was monitored by the British admiral Nicholas Haddock. The British blockading squadron eventually had to be forced off due to a lack of provisions, which allowed the Spanish admiral, Don Jose Navarro, to put to sea.

## French Intervention and the Retreat of Haddock
As Admiral Navarro sailed, he was followed by the British force. However, by December 1741, Navarro had been joined by a French squadron commanded by M. de Court. The French admiral declared that he would provide support to the Spaniards if they were attacked. Consequently, Admiral Haddock retired. 

At this time, France and Great Britain were not yet in an open state of war, although both nations were involved in the struggle in Germany. Great Britain acted as the ally of Maria Theresa, the queen of Hungary, while France supported the Bavarian claimant of the empire.

## Deployment to Toulon and Subsequent Hostilities
Following their union, Navarro and M. de Court proceeded to Toulon, where they remained until February 1744. They were watched by a British fleet under the command of admiral Richard Lestock until Sir Thomas Mathews was appointed as minister to the court of Turin and commander-in-chief.

While partial manifestations of hostility occurred between the British and French in various seas, an avowed war did not officially begin until the French government issued a declaration on March 30, which Great Britain answered on March 31. This official declaration followed French preparations to invade England and a collision in the Mediterranean between Mathews and the allies.

## The Battle of February 11
On February 11, a confused battle took place involving the British fleet and the allies. The centre and van of the British fleet engaged the centre and rear of the allied forces. The engagement was characterized by significant mismanagement:

*   **Sir Thomas Mathews:** Described as hot-tempered and "puzzle-headed," Mathews fought with spirit but lacked direction, breaking the formation of his fleet and fighting in a disorderly manner.
*   **Admiral Richard Lestock:** Lestock took no part in the action, citing contradictory orders from Mathews. The two men were on the worst possible terms.

The mismanagement of the British fleet during this battle caused deep anger among the public, which subsequently led to a drastic reform of the British navy.
