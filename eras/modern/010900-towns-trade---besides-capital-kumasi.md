# Towns and Trade in Ashanti

The urban and commercial landscape of Ashanti is characterized by a few significant towns, with the capital, Kumasi, serving as the primary distributing center for the region and its hinterland. While other towns serve specialized industrial or commercial roles, the development of these centers has been heavily influenced by geography, colonial conflict, and the introduction of modern infrastructure.

## Kumasi: The Capital
Kumasi is situated on a low rocky eminence, extending across a valley to an opposite hill within a clearing of dense forest. The town's name, more correctly *Kum-ase*, means "under the okum tree," referring to the numerous such trees found in its streets. By 1906, the population of the town was 6,280, increasing to over 12,000 when including the suburbs.

At the height of its prosperity, from the middle of the 18th to the middle of the 19th century, Kumasi was a city of high culture with broad, regular streets; its main avenue measured 70 yards in width. The city featured a large market-place to the south-east and a "Spirit House" located in a grove of trees, which served as the place of execution. The royal palace was constructed of red sandstone and is believed to have been modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina.

The city suffered significant destruction during British interventions. On February 6, 1874, the forces of Sir Garnet Wolseley blew up the sandstone palace. Following the withdrawal of British troops, the town was only partially rebuilt, and a new royal palace was constructed of clay rather than brick. Further damage occurred in 1896, when fire destroyed several ancient houses in the royal and priestly suburb of Bantama, and again during the revolt of 1900.

Modern Kumasi features a fort built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner and can house a garrison of several hundred men. The town also contains European and native hospitals, stations for the Wesleyan and Basel missions, and leading mercantile houses located near the railway station. Native houses are built of red clay, often richly ornamented and enclosed in compounds.

## Other Important Towns
Beyond the capital, Ashanti has few important towns. Obuassi, located in the south-west, is the center of the gold-mining industry. In the north, the towns of Nkoranza, Atabubu, and Kintampo are noted. Kintampo, situated approximately 130 miles north-east of Kumasi, is a town of considerable size. It serves as a meeting-place for traders from the coast and the Niger countries. While it was formerly one of West Africa's great marts for ivory and slaves, it has transitioned into a depot for government stores and a center for the kola-nut commerce. Historically, Kintampo's role as a commercial capital and a junction for caravan routes from the east and north contributed to the fact that Kumasi's population did not exceed 20,000 in the first quarter of the 19th century. Other notable locations include Wam on the western border.

## Commerce and Industry
The Ashanti demonstrate high skill in various manufactures, particularly in the weaving of cotton, as well as in pottery and gold work. Artistic elaboration is evident in goldsmiths' work and silver-plate; in 1874, masks of beaten gold were discovered in the king's palace at Kumasi, showing a perceptible influence of Moorish art.

Trade is driven by several key products:
*   **Minerals:** Gold is found in the western districts. The Obuassi mines provided most of the gold exported from the Gold Coast in 1902 and subsequent years. In 1905, the gold output from Ashanti was 68,259 oz., valued at £254,790.
*   **Agriculture:** The kola nut is in great demand. Since 1905, cocoa plantations have been established, primarily in the eastern districts. Other exports include gum copal, tobacco (cultivated in the north), and rubber from the *Funtumia elastica* tree.

Trade routes extend northwards to the Niger countries and southwards to the coast. While some trade was diverted to the French port of Assini due to wars with England, trade between Kumasi and Cape Coast improved following the suppression of the 1900 revolt. Revenue for the region is derived from liquor licenses, caravan taxes, contributions from gold-mining companies, and rents from government land.

## Communications and Infrastructure
The primary means of travel through the dense forest are tortuous paths too narrow for wheeled vehicles, though a palanquin borne by four carriers is a favorite mode of transport. A wide road of 141 miles connects Cape Coast to Kumasi, and ferries are maintained for crossing swamps and rivers.

Infrastructure improved significantly with the completion of the railway from Sekondi to Kumasi in September 1903. This line passes through the auriferous region and the gold-fields of Obuassi and Tarkwa. The arrival of the railway prompted merchants from Gold Coast ports to open branches in Kumasi, leading to a rapid expansion of the town and its emergence as the primary distributing center for the region by 1906. Additionally, Kumasi is connected to the coast and the Northern Territories via telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service.
