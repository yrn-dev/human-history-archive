# Badajoz

## Provincial Overview
Badajoz is a frontier province located in western Spain. Formed in 1833 from districts previously belonging to the province of Estremadura, it is the largest province in the kingdom, covering an area of 8,451 square miles. Its boundaries are defined by Caceres to the north, Cordova and Ciudad Real to the east, Seville and Huelva to the south, and Portugal to the west. As of 1900, the province had a population of 520,246.

The landscape of Badajoz consists primarily of a desolate and monotonous plain that is either flat or slightly undulating, though low ranges of hills exist in various districts. The primary river is the Guadiana, which flows from east to west across the north of the province. Fed by many tributaries, the river-beds only fill with significant volumes of water during certain seasons, often allowing the river to be forded with ease.

## Climate and Agriculture
The province experiences extreme temperatures, with fierce north and north-west winds during the winter and intense heat in the summer. In the Guadiana valley, intermittent fevers are prevalent during the hot months. Rainfall is generally scanty, and only a small portion of the land is irrigated. Much of the territory is covered by thin forest, bush, or pasture.

Due to these conditions, the cultivation of fruit, olives, and vines, as well as general agriculture, is in a backward state. However, Badajoz possesses more livestock than any other Spanish province. It is particularly noted for its acorn-fed swine, which are celebrated throughout Spain for their bacon and hams. Additionally, large herds of goats and sheep thrive in areas where the pasture is too meager for cattle.

## Economy and Industry
The mineral resources of the province are under-exploited due to poor communication and a lack of water; in 1903, only 26 of nearly 600 registered mines were operational, producing lead and small amounts of copper. Local industries are of limited importance and include the production of soaps, oils, cork, leather, and coarse cotton and woollen stuffs.

Commercial interests are more significant than industrial ones, largely due to the transit trade with Portugal, which is facilitated by seven custom-houses. Despite this, many parts of the province remain inaccessible except by roads, which are described as ill-maintained.

## The City of Badajoz
The capital city of Badajoz is situated on the left bank of the river Guadiana, close to the Portuguese frontier and located on the Madrid-Lisbon railway. In 1900, its population was 30,899. The city serves as the official residence of the captain-general of Estremadura and is the see of a bishop.

The city is built on a slight eminence topped by the ruins of a Moorish castle. Its defenses—consisting of a strong wall, bastions, a broad moat, outworks, and forts on surrounding heights—give it an appearance of great strength. A magnificent granite bridge crosses the river between the castle-hill and the fort of San Cristobal; this bridge was originally built in 1460, repaired in 1597, and rebuilt in 1833. The city's cathedral, dating from 1258, features massive embattled walls that resemble a fortress.

The city is the birthplace of the painter Luis de Morales (1509-1586) and the statesman Manuel de Godoy, duke of Alcudia (1767-1851). Two retouched paintings by Morales are preserved in the cathedral. The city's economy relies on transit trade with Portugal and the manufacture of pottery, leather goods, and woollen and linen goods.

## History of the Capital
Badajoz first rose to importance under Moorish rule and became the capital of a small Moorish kingdom in 1031. Although the Portuguese held the city temporarily in 1168, it remained independent until 1229, when it was captured by Alphonso IX of Leon.

As a frontier fortress, the city was subject to numerous sieges. It was beleaguered by the Portuguese in 1660 and by the Allies in 1705 during the War of the Spanish Succession. During the Peninsular War, the French unsuccessfully attacked the city in 1808 and 1809. However, on March 10, 1811, the Spanish commander Jose Imaz was bribed into surrendering the city to Marshal Soult's French forces. A British army under Marshal Beresford attempted to retake the city; while they defeated a relieving force at Albuera on May 16, the siege was abandoned in June.

## Principal Towns
Other notable towns within the province include:

*   **Don Benito**: A thriving town founded in the 15th century by refugees from Don Llorente who fled Guadiana floods. Located on the Madrid-Badajoz-Lisbon railway, it had a population of 16,565 in 1900. It is known for its trade in wine, wheat, and fruit (especially melons), and manufactures oil, soap, flour, brandy, cloth, and linen.
*   **Azuaga**: A town with a population of 14,192 in 1900, located on the Belmez-Fuente del Arco railway. It serves as the central market for livestock from upland pastures watered by the Bembezar (a tributary of the Guadalquivir) and the Matachel (a tributary of the Guadiana). Its local manufactures include pottery and coarse woollen goods.
