# Fugitives and the Laws of Return

The history of fugitives encompasses a wide array of political, military, and legal contexts, ranging from the surrender of political rivals in West Africa to the systemic legal frameworks for the return of enslaved persons in the United States.

## The Ashanti Empire and the Assin Fugitives
In the early 18th century, the Ashanti power was established by Osai Tutu, who expanded the empire through the conquest of the Mahommedan countries of Banna and Gaman, as well as the state of Denkera in 1719. By 1800, King Osai Tutu Quamina sought to open communications with white nations. Around 1807, two chiefs of the Assin, having been defeated in battle, sought refuge with the Fanti people on the coast. When the Fanti refused to deliver up these fugitives, Osai Tutu invaded their country, defeating them and driving them toward the sea.

The Ashanti reached the coast near Anamabo, where a British fort was located. Despite mediation efforts by the governor, the town was destroyed and 8,000 inhabitants were slaughtered. Following the conflict, a truce was reached. Colonel G. Torrane, the governor of Cape Coast from 1805 to 1807, met with the king and determined to surrender the fugitive Assin chiefs. While one chief escaped, the other was delivered up and subsequently put to death by the Ashanti. Furthermore, Torrane concluded an agreement acknowledging the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland and delivered half of the fugitives held in the Anamabo fort to the Ashanti; most of the remaining fugitives were sold as slaves by Torrane and his council.

## Fugitive Slave Laws in the United States
In the United States, "Fugitive Slave Laws" refers to statutes passed by Congress in 1793 and 1850 to ensure the return of negro slaves who escaped into other states or public territories. Early provisions for the return of fugitives appeared in the 1643 Articles of Confederation of the New England Confederation, which required only the certificate of one magistrate and provided no jury trial.

The Ordinance of 1787 for the Northwest Territory further established that any person escaping into the territory from whom labor or service was lawfully claimed in the original states could be lawfully reclaimed. This principle was integrated into Article IV, Section II of the Federal Constitution, stating that no person held to service or labor under the laws of one state would be discharged from such service by escaping into another, but shall be delivered up on claim.

Specific legislation enacted on February 12, 1793, authorized state magistrates or Federal judges to decide the status of an alleged fugitive without a jury trial. This led to the "Underground Railroad" in the early 19th century and the passage of Personal Liberty Laws in northern states. For example, Indiana (1824) and Connecticut (1828) provided jury trials for fugitives, while New York and Vermont did so by 1840. Following the 1842 Supreme Court decision in *Prigg v. Pennsylvania*, which ruled that national authorities must carry out national law rather than state authorities, several states—including Massachusetts, Vermont, Pennsylvania, and Rhode Island—forbade state officials from enforcing the law.

The second fugitive slave law, enacted on September 18, 1850, as part of the Compromise Measures, introduced special commissioners with concurrent jurisdiction. Under this law, fugitives were prohibited from testifying in their own behalf, no jury trial was provided, and penalties were imposed on individuals who aided negroes to escape or on marshals from whom a fugitive escaped.

## Military and Political Fugitives in Antiquity
In ancient history, the status of fugitives often shifted based on military fortune. During Hannibal's invasion of Italy, after the disaster at Cannæ, Marcellus took command of the fugitives collected at Canusium to demonstrate that the Romans would not deliver up the country to the enemy. Similarly, during the wars with the Gauls, those who fled after being routed by Camillus were pursued and slain, while others were killed by inhabitants of neighboring towns.

In the conflict involving the Persians and Hellenes, the cavalry provided service to fugitives by keeping close to the enemy, thereby separating the fugitives of their own side from the Hellenes. In Macedonia, Brutus and Cassius described their own initial flight from Italy as having been "like the most despicable fugitives," lacking money, arms, ships, or soldiers, before eventually collecting a force to struggle for Roman supremacy.

Other instances of flight were driven by political survival. When the Molossians revolted and drove out King Æakides, the infant Pyrrhus was spirited away by Androkleides and Angelus. To secure help from inhabitants across a swollen river, one of the fugitives wrote a message on oak bark using the tongue of a brooch, which was thrown across the water to explain the child's danger.

## Fidelity and Betrayal in Japan
In Japanese history, Yoritomo emphasized the principle of fidelity. He publicly declared Yasuhira a traitor for killing his guest, Yoshitsune, despite the fact that Yasuhira had been following Yoritomo's own orders. Yoritomo further executed a disloyal retainer who had taken the head of Yasuhira while the latter was a fugitive fleeing the Kamakura armies.
