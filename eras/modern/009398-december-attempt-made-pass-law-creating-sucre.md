# The Capital Dispute and Political Transition of Bolivia

## The Conflict Over the National Capital
In December, an attempt was made to pass a law that would establish Sucre as the perpetual capital of the republic. Prior to this attempt, Sucre had shared the role of capital with Oruro, Cochabamba, and La Paz. This legislative move triggered an open revolt in La Paz. The resulting conflict led to a battle on January 17 of the following year, located approximately 40 miles from La Paz, where government forces were defeated, resulting in the loss of forty-three men and a colonel.

Following this victory, the insurgent leader, Colonel Pando, gained a significant following. He marched upon Oruro and entered the town on April 11, 1899, after achieving a complete defeat of the government troops. In the aftermath, Dr. Severo Alonso sought refuge in Chilean territory, and Colonel Pando established a provisional government.

## The Presidency of Colonel Pando
Colonel Pando was elected to the presidency without opposition and officially entered office on October 26. He is described as a capable and strong chief magistrate. During his tenure, Pando focused on improving the country's means of communication to revive other industries and mining. He also managed two difficult boundary settlements with Brazil and Chile.

The dispute with Brazil centered on the rubber-producing territory of Acre. This region was rich in resources, but the majority of those working in the rubber industry were Brazilians who resisted the authority of Bolivian officials. This tension culminated in the state of Acre declaring independence, prompting Bolivia to send troops in 1900 to restore order. To avoid hostilities with Brazil, President Pando pursued an amicable diplomatic settlement. A treaty was signed in November 1903, which established a new boundary line and ceded a portion of the Acre province to Brazil in exchange for a cash indemnity of $10,000,000.

## Geography and Urban Centers
The total area of the republic, adjusted for the 1903 loss of territory to Brazil, is 515,156 square miles. While several provincial capitals exist, only four have served as the national capital: Oruro, Cochabamba, La Paz, and Sucre. Although Sucre was previously chosen, the capital was located at La Paz following the revolution of 1898, as La Paz is more accessible and serves as the commercial metropolis.

Other prominent towns include several in the department of Potosi: the silver mining center of Pulacayo (population 6,512), where 3,200 men work in surface works and mines; Uyuni (population 1,587), a highway convergence point and railway junction; and Tupiza (population 1,644), a mining and commercial center near the Argentine frontier. Additionally, Viacha (population 1,670) serves as a railway station 14 miles from Alto de La Paz. In the department of Cochabamba, Totora (3,501) and Tarata (4,681) are important trading centers, while Ascension (population 4,784) in the department of Santa Cruz is a large mission station.

## Education and Language
Bolivia maintains a compulsory and free school system, though progress in education has been limited. As of 1901, official estimates recorded 733 primary schools with 41,587 pupils and 938 teachers. The maintenance cost was 585,365 bolivianos, averaging 14.07 bolivianos per pupil. School enrollment was low, with only one in 43.7 of the population enrolled. While nearly half of the schools are maintained by the national government, others are supported by private means, the Church, and municipalities.

The educational system is practically supervised by the Church, though a cabinet minister directs public instruction. Secondary instruction is managed by universities. In 1901, there were 14 institutions of superior instruction, including universities in Oruro, Santa Cruz, Potosi, Tarija, Cochabamba, La Paz, and Sucre. All provide law instruction; the first four also provide theology, and the first three provide medicine. The universities at La Paz and Sucre (which dates from colonial times) are the only ones considered sufficiently equipped to merit the title. Other specialized institutions include a military academy at La Paz, a mining and civil engineering school at Oruro, an agricultural school at Umala, and commercial schools at Trinidad and Sucre.

Regarding language, Spanish is used by the dominant minority. However, the majority of the population are natives who speak Aymara, Guarani, and Quichua. Many Indians do not understand Spanish and resist efforts to learn it; even the *cholos* (mestizos) are often more familiar with native idioms than with Spanish.
