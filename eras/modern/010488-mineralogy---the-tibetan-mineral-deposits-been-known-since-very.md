# Mineralogy

Mineralogy is the scientific study of minerals, their properties, and their distribution within the earth's crust. The field encompasses the study of crystalline structures, the chemical composition of rocks, and the historical development of mineral analysis and classification.

## Boron Deposits and Borax
Boron compounds are found in various global deposits, often resulting from evaporation in lakes, marshes, or salines. These deposits typically consist of mixtures of borates, gypsum, and alkaline salts such as chloride, sulphate, and sodium carbonate. The boron in these salt lakes is likely supplied by solfataras and hot springs of volcanic origin, similar to those that charge the lagoons of Tuscany with boric acid.

In Tibet, mineral deposits have been known since early times. Crude material was formerly exported to Europe as *tincal* for the production of other boron salts and pure borax. Tibetan deposits include:
* **Pugha:** Located on the Rulangchu (a tributary of the Indus) at an elevation of 15,000 ft. Impure borax, known as *sohaga*, occurs here over approximately 2 sq. m., covered by saline efflorescence. Rain and snow, followed by evaporation, produce successive crops of the material.
* **Rudok:** Located east of Pugha, these lakes contain a purer material known as *chu tsale* or water borax.
* **Tengri Nor:** Great lakes located north of Lhasa where purer material is also found.

More recent commercial sources of boron compounds include the Atacama desert in South America and the Mohave desert on the borders of Nevada and California. In California, fine isolated crystals of borax—some weighing a pound and reaching 7 inches in length—have been found in the mud and marshy soil of Borax Lake in both San Bernardino and Lake counties.

## Composite and Fibrous Minerals
Certain minerals are characterized by their complex composition or fibrous structure.

### Greywacke
Greywacke (from a German term for grey earthy rock) refers to impure, gritty, highly composite rocks of the Palaeozoic systems, specifically frequent among Cambrian and Silurian rocks. They are typically dull-coloured (grey, black, yellow, or brown) and occur in thick or thin beds alongside limestones and slates. They are abundant in the Lake district of England, the south of Scotland, and Wales. 

Greywackes contain a wide variety of minerals, primarily plagioclase, orthoclase, quartz, graphitic carbonaceous matters, calcite, and iron oxides. Coarser varieties may contain fragments of quartzite, gneiss, slate, chert, felsite, and various schists. Other present minerals include pyrites, sphene, augite, hornblende, garnet, apatite, epidote, tourmaline, and chlorite. While generally not fossiliferous, organic remains may appear in associated finer beds. Through increased metamorphism, greywackes can transition into sedimentary gneisses, chloride schists, and mica-schists.

### Asbestos
Asbestos is a fibrous mineral that varies in character. It is categorized primarily into two types:
* **Amphibole-asbestos (Hornblende-asbestos):** This variety, which includes anthophyllite or tremolite (and occasionally pyroxene), is usually grey or white. It can be harsh and brittle, though Italian asbestos can reach lengths of 5 or 6 ft.
* **Serpentine-asbestos (Chrysotile):** This variety occurs in narrow veins and produces yellowish or greenish fibres. These fibres are typically 2 or 3 inches long but possess great tensile strength, elasticity, and a silky lustre.

Depending on the form, asbestos is known by trivial names: "mountain flax" when silky and flexible, "amianthus" for finer kinds, and "mountain paper," "mountain cork," or "mountain leather" when the fibres form a felted mass.

## Tin and Other Igneous Rocks
Cassiterite is the mineralogical name for tin-stone, the common ore of tin, consisting of stannic oxide (tin dioxide, $\text{SnO}_2$). It crystallizes in the tetragonal system, typically as 8-sided or 4-sided prisms with vertical striations and pyramidal terminations. It can appear as "needle-tin" (slender crystals), "sparable tin" (slender prismatic crystals with an acute 8-sided pyramid), or "wood-tin" (fibrous forms). Through the disintegration of vein-stones and tin-bearing rocks, it becomes "alluvial tin" or "stream tin" as grains and fragments in stream beds.

Other igneous rocks include dolerites, which are among the commonest igneous rocks and are often used for kerbstones and road-mending. Dolerites are found wherever basalts occur. A specific group called proterobases features brown hornblende replacing augite, while mica-dolerites contain brown mica. Teschenites are rocks characterized by the presence of analcite, which fills interspaces between other minerals.

## Notable Mineralogists
Several scholars have contributed to the advancement of mineralogy and petrology:
* **Baron Wolfgang Xaver Franz Kobell (1803-1882):** A German mineralogist who invented the stauroscope in 1835 to study the optical properties of crystals and introduced new methods of mineral analysis.
* **Paul Heinrich von Groth (1843- ):** A German mineralogist and curator of minerals at the state museum in Munich who published *Physikalische Krystallographie* and *Tabellarische Ubersicht der einfachen Mineralien*.
* **Alfred Louis Olivier Legrand Des Cloizeaux (1817-1897):** A French mineralogist who specialized in the optical properties of crystals, polarization, and the determination of different felspars.
* **Joseph Marie Elisabeth Durocher (1817-1858):** A French geologist and mining engineer who studied the genesis of igneous rocks. In his *Essai de petrologie comparee* (1857), he proposed that igneous rocks derive from two coexisting magmas beneath the crust: basic and acid.
