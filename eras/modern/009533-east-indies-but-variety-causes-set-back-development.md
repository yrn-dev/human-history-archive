# East Indies

The East Indies represent a vast region of trade, colonization, and diverse natural resources that became a focal point for European expansion starting in the late 15th century. The opening of a sea route to the region around the Cape of Good Hope in 1498 significantly altered global commerce, shifting the dyewood trade from Italy to Portugal and Spain. This new route allowed East Indian products to reach Europe directly, bypassing older trade paths through Asia Minor and Persia, and introduced European markets to Eastern art-fabrics and their production methods.

## The Dutch East India Company
Formed in 1602 and dissolved in 1798, the Dutch East India Company was a dominant force in the region. The company established its headquarters at Batavia in Java, founded in 1619 on the ruins of the native town of Jacatra. The company's operations were extensive, involving diplomatic relations with Japan and China, and the conquest of footholds in Ceylon and the Malay Archipelago. Between 1638 and 1658, the Dutch expelled the Portuguese from Ceylon, and they took Malacca in 1641. In 1667, a treaty with native princes established Dutch power in Sumatra.

The company reached its peak of prosperity around 1669, at which time it maintained 40 ships of war, 150 trading ships, and 10,000 soldiers, paying a dividend of 40%. However, the company pursued a rigid monopoly, following instructions given to its first governor-general, Pieter Both, in 1608 to ensure Holland held the entire trade of the East Indies. This policy led to violent conflict with the English. By 1632, the Dutch had driven the English almost entirely from the Malay Archipelago and the Spice Islands, leaving the English in a precarious position at Bantam in Java. A notable point of hostility occurred in 1623 with the judicial murder and torture of English factors at Amboyna.

## British Settlements and Bombay
The English also sought to establish a seat of power and trade in the East Indies, eventually designating Bombay for this purpose. The development of Bombay was initially hindered by cholera and plague, caused by the silting of creeks between its islands. Following the amalgamation of the old and new companies in 1708, the governor's seat moved from Surat to Bombay. The city's fortifications were completed in 1718, and the population grew from 16,000 in 1718 to 70,000 by 1744, aided by administrative reforms and settlers arriving from Gujarat.

Bombay's strategic importance as a naval base was enhanced by the construction of a dock in 1750 and another in 1762. The city's growth continued through the late 18th century; by 1780, the population reached 113,000. Further growth occurred after a famine in 1803, which drove people from the Deccan and Konkan to seek work in the city. During this era, the English also expanded their influence through alliances, such as a 1733 treaty with the Siddis for mainland supplies and a 1755 alliance with the peshwa to acquire Bankot. In 1770, a famine in China led the Chinese government to mandate more land for grain, which sparked the beginning of the cotton trade with China via Bombay.

## Regional Geography and Economy
The East Indies encompass various vassal states and diverse ecological zones. Boni, a vassal state of the government of Celebes in the Dutch East Indies, is located on the Gulf of Boni in the south-west peninsula of Celebes. It covers an area of 2,600 square miles and is inhabited by approximately 70,000 people of the Bugis race. Boni produces sugar-cane, cotton, coffee, tobacco, and rice, and is known for highly esteemed breeds of horses and buffaloes.

The region is also noted for specific commercial products, such as date sugar. This is produced from the sap or toddy of the *Phoenix sylvestris* (toddy palm). When the juice is not boiled into sugar, it is either consumed fresh or distilled into arrack.

## Health and Epidemics
The warm climates of the East Indies have been subject to infectious fevers, most notably dengue. This disease is characterized by sudden fever, severe headache, erythema, and rheumatic pains in the muscles and joints, followed by a measles-like eruption. While rarely fatal except in cases of extreme weakness, it occurs in epidemics. One of the earliest known epidemics took place in the East Indies and Egypt between 1779 and 1780. Later, a major epidemic between 1870 and 1775 extended across the tropical portions of the East, reaching as far as China.
