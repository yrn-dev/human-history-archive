# The Booth Family and Individuals of the Name Booth

The name Booth is associated with several prominent figures across different centuries and professions, most notably in the fields of theater, social reform, and sociology.

## Barton Booth (1681–1733)
Barton Booth was an English actor born into a good Lancashire family. He received his education at Westminster school, where his performance in the Latin play *Andria* sparked his interest in the stage. Although he was originally intended for the church, Booth left Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1698. He began his professional acting career in Dublin, making his debut as Oroonoko.

Upon returning to London after two seasons in Ireland, Booth received the full assistance of the actor Betterton, who had previously withheld active aid, likely due to considerations regarding Booth's family. Between 1700 and 1704, Booth appeared at Lincoln's Inn Fields, achieving immediate success as Maximus in *Valentinian*. He later performed at the Haymarket with Betterton from 1705 to 1708, followed by a twenty-year tenure at Drury Lane.

Booth established his reputation as a tragedian through the title role in Addison's *Cato*. His other significant roles included Brutus, Hotspur, and the ghost in *Hamlet*, a performance said to have no superior. He was also noted for his roles as Othello, Mark Antony, Timon of Athens, and the character Lothario in Rowe's *Fair Penitent*. His portrayal of Lear was considered comparable to that of Garrick. Booth was married twice; his second wife, an actress named Hester Santlow, survived him. He died on May 10, 1733, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

## The Theatrical Booths of America
The Booth family of the 19th century was a significant presence in American theater. Edwin Booth, described as lithe and graceful with luminous eyes and waving dark curls, made his first regular appearance on September 10, 1849, at the Boston Museum, playing Tressel alongside his father, who played Richard in Colley Cibber's version of *Richard III*. While the elder Booth watched with interest, he also showed disappointment, and peers felt the father's genius had not passed to the son.

Edwin Booth debuted in New York on September 27, 1850, as Wilford in *The Iron Chest*. He took over the role of Richard III a year later during his father's illness, though he only secured an unassailable position on the stage after his father's death. Between 1852 and 1856, he performed in the Sandwich Islands, Australia, and California, captivating audiences with brilliant acting. His performances as Richelieu, Richard, and Hamlet were pronounced superior to those of Edwin Forrest, and his success as Sir Giles Overreach surpassed that of his father.

In 1862, Edwin became the manager of the Winter Garden theatre in New York, producing magnificent Shakespearian productions from 1864 to 1867. However, this period was marred in 1865 when his brother, John Wilkes Booth (1839–1865), assassinated President Lincoln. The three Booth brothers—Junius Brutus (1821–1853), Edwin, and John Wilkes—had performed together in *Julius Caesar* the previous autumn.

Edwin Booth later built his own theatre at 6th Avenue and 23rd Street in New York (1868–1869), where he used the true text of Shakespeare. Despite his success, his generous and non-economical management led to the loss of the theatre and his entire fortune in 1874. He eventually regained his wealth and converted his Gramercy Park residence into The Players' club for members of his profession and others. He died in a single apartment he kept for himself on June 7, 1893. His most famous role was Hamlet, which he played more often than perhaps any other actor. He was married twice: first to Mary Devlin (d. 1863) and later to Mary F. McVicker (d. 1881). He had one daughter, Edwina Booth Grossman.

## William Booth (1829– )
William Booth was born in Nottingham on April 10, 1829. Influenced by Wesleyan Methodists at age fifteen, he became a local preacher. After moving to London in 1849, his passion for open-air preaching led to a split with the Wesleyans. He later joined the Methodist New Connexion as an ordained minister but left in 1861 to pursue active "travelling evangelization."

In 1855, he married Catherine Mumford, and together they preached in Walsall, Cardiff, and Cornwall. In Walsall, they organized a "Hallelujah band" composed of others and converted criminals. In 1864, Booth returned to London, preaching in tents and the open air. He founded an organization that went through several names—the East London Revival Society, the East London Christian Mission, and the Christian Mission—before becoming the Salvation Army in 1878.

The Salvation Army is a quasi-military organization modeled on the British army. It utilizes outdoor meetings, processions, popular song-tunes, and visits to prisons and public-houses. Early efforts faced violent opposition from a "Skeleton Army," leading to the imprisonment and fining of Booth's followers. By 1880, the Army expanded to the United States, followed by Australia, India, Ceylon, and Europe.

In 1890, "General" Booth published *In Darkest England, and the Way Out*, proposing ten expedients to remedy vice and pauperism, including farm and city colonies, rescue homes for fallen women, and a "poor man's lawyer." By the end of the 19th century, Booth received widespread sympathy. He was invited to the coronation ceremony in 1902 by King Edward VII and was received in state by various corporations during a 1905 progress through England.

## Charles Booth (1840– )
Charles Booth was an English sociologist born in Liverpool on March 30, 1840. He was the chairman of the Booth Steamship Company and a partner in Alfred Booth & Company, a firm involved in the Brazil trade.

Booth dedicated significant money and time to studying the statistical aspects of social questions. His findings were published in *Life and Labour of the People in London* (1891–1903), which aimed to describe the conditions of different classes and the numerical relationship between depravity, misery, poverty, and regular earnings. The work is noted for its maps using shades of color to represent poverty street by street. He also focused on the aged poor, publishing statistics in 1894 and works on Old-age pensions in 1891 and 1899, proposing a general pension of five shillings a week for all aged persons. He married the daughter of Charles Zachary Macaulay in 1871 and was made a privy councillor in 1904.
