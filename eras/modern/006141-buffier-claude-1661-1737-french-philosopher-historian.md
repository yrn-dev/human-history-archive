# BUFFIER, CLAUDE (1661-1737)

Claude Buffier was a French historian, educationalist, and philosopher. Born on May 25, 1661, in Poland to French parents, he moved to France shortly after his birth, where his family settled in Rouen.

## Education and Early Career
Buffier received his education at the Jesuit college in Rouen. At the age of nineteen, he was received into the Jesuit order. His tenure in Rouen ended following a dispute with the archbishop, which compelled him to leave the city. After spending a short period in Rome, he returned to Paris to join the college of the Jesuits, where he remained for the rest of his life. He was regarded as an admirable teacher who possessed a great power of lucid exposition.

## Philosophical Contributions
Buffier is best known for his work *Traité des vérités premières*, published in 1717. The primary objective of this work was the discovery of the ultimate principle of knowledge. Buffier concluded that this principle is found in the sense of one's own existence and the feelings experienced within oneself. While this position is substantially the same as that of Descartes, Buffier rejected the use of the *a priori* method.

To understand and identify what exists distinct from the self, Buffier argued that "common sense" is necessary. He defined common sense as a disposition placed in all or most men by nature. This disposition enables individuals, upon reaching the age and use of reason, to form a common and uniform judgment regarding objects that differ from the internal sentiment of their own perception. He specified that this judgment is not the result of any anterior judgment.

## Eternal Verities and Probability
Buffier posited that the truths which this natural disposition obliges humans to accept can be neither disproved nor proved. He noted that these truths are practically followed even by individuals who reject them from a speculative standpoint. 

However, Buffier did not attribute absolute certainty to these truths of "common sense." Absolute certainty, in his view, characterizes only the knowledge of one's own existence or logical deductions derived from one's thoughts. Instead, the truths of common sense possess only the highest probability. Consequently, Buffier suggested that a person who rejects these truths should be considered a fool, although such a person is not guilty of a contradiction.

## Critical Reception
Buffier's writings have been characterized by some as having an appearance of shallowness and a lack of metaphysical insight, a result of his aversion to scholastic refinements. Critics have noted that he failed to indicate the nature of the necessity and universality he ascribed to his "eternal verities." Despite these criticisms, he was among the earliest thinkers to recognize the psychological aspects of knowledge.
