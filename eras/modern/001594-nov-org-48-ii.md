# Novum Organum

The *Novum Organum* (abbreviated in archival sources as *Nov. Org.* or *N. O.*) is a foundational work by Francis Bacon detailing his scientific method and philosophical framework. The text addresses the nature of scientific progress, the classification of causes, and the systematic removal of intellectual errors.

## The Baconian Method and Scientific Progress
Bacon’s work proposes a specific method for the advancement of science. However, the text indicates that Bacon himself anticipated that the progress of science would eventually render his own method antiquated (*Nov. Org.* i. 130). 

The theoretical analysis provided in his method serves as a bridge to practical application; the final step of theoretical analysis provides the initial means for practical operation. This approach is contrasted with other philosophical traditions, such as those of Aristotle.

## The Concept of Idola
A significant portion of the *Novum Organum* is dedicated to the concept of *Idola*. This terminology is borrowed from Plato, where it refers to fleeting or transient images of real things. In Bacon's framework, *Idola* are used in connection with Platonic Ideas but are contrasted with them, serving as representations of false appearances.

## Classification of Causes and Physics
Bacon provides a classification of causes within his work, though some aspects of this classification—and his notion of "form"—are noted to be derived from Aristotle. 

A central tenet of his approach to physics is the rigid exclusion of the consideration of final causes, as the knowledge of final causes does not lead to practical works. Despite this exclusion in physics, Bacon does not posit a total opposition between physical and final causes. He suggests that, in the ultimate resort, the mind is led to view the universe as the work of reason and to refer facts to Providence and God. Additionally, the idea of the final cause is considered fruitful in sciences that deal with human action (*Nov. Org.* i. 48, ii. 2).

## Prima Philosophia and the Scale of Nature
Bacon introduces the conception of *Prima Philosophia*. This involves a "scale of nature" that corresponds to a scale of ascending axioms. Within this framework, Bacon discusses motions (*N. O.* ii. 48). 

Critics and later scholars have noted that Bacon does not explicitly detail how such a science is to be constructed, nor how it can maintain an independent method while serving as a receptacle for generalizations from various other sciences—a content that necessarily varies as those sciences progress.
