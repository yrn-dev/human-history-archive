# The German-Latin Block

## Political Composition and the 1907 Election
In the context of Austrian political allotments, a specific electoral arrangement existed involving various ethnic constituencies. During the general election of 1907, the distribution of seats was modified, which formally overturned the fiction that Austria was primarily a German country. Instead, the structure revealed a country that was preponderantly Slav, though it maintained a German facade and dynasty.

The allotment of seats resulted in the Germans holding 233 seats, placing them in a permanent minority when compared to the 259 seats held by the Slavs. To offset this, a "German-Latin block" was formed, which included the German constituencies as well as the "Latin" seats (consisting of Italian and Rumanian representatives). This combined block totaled 257 seats.

## Dissolution of the Block
Despite the numerical strength of the German-Latin block, it ceased to exist in practice. This dissolution occurred because the Italians began to tend toward cooperation with the Slavs rather than with the Germans. 

Other shifts occurred during this redistribution of power, most notably for the Ruthenes, whose representation was trebled. However, this increase was still considered far from being proportioned to their actual numbers. Because German parties anticipated that any future revisions to the allotment would be to their disadvantage, they stipulated that any alteration to the law would require a two-thirds majority.

## Imperial Administration and Racial Rivalries
The broader Austrian government attempted to manage simmering racial rivalries through a system of strategic military garrisons. To keep these disintegrating forces in check, the army was deployed such that Hungarian regiments were garrisoned in Italy, Italian regiments guarded Galicia, Poles were stationed in Austria, and Austrians were placed in Hungary.

To prevent the infiltration of "revolutionary" ideas from outside the dominions, the government established a "Chinese wall" of censors and tariffs. Internally, the growth of Liberalism was countered through the re-establishment of clerical control over education and rigid supervision of the press. While the government encouraged the revival of national languages and literatures in places like Croatia and Bohemia—hoping to divert interest from politics toward scientific pursuits—the administrative system remained stagnant. Provincial diets and estates (revived in various regions between 1816 and 1828, including Carniola, Galicia, Tirol, Vorarlberg, and the circle of Salzburg) were not representative, as eligibility was limited to nobles, clergy, and a few town delegates.
