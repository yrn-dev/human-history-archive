# Bulgarian Proposals and Balkan Diplomacy (1897)

## Overview of Balkan Political Combinations
The growth and development of the Balkan nations were significantly hindered by international jealousies stemming from the Eastern Question. To counter foreign interference and resolve national aims through compromise, various Balkan politicians explored the possibility of forming a combination of young states. Early advocacy for such a union came from the Servian statesman Ristich. During the reaction against Russia following the war of 1877, informal discussions were held regarding a confederation, with some suggestions that a constitutional or reformed Turkey could be included. While this movement received favorable regard from Prince Alexander of Bulgaria and King Charles of Rumania, the project was eventually abandoned due to the coercion of Greece by the powers, the Servo-Bulgarian War, and the revolt of Eastern Rumelia, all of which increased rivalry between the races.

## The 1891 Offensive Alliance Proposal
In 1891, the idea of a Balkan combination was revived in a modified form by Tricoupis. Rather than a broad confederation, Tricoupis suggested an offensive alliance of Balkan states specifically directed against Turkey. The goal of this proposed alliance was the partition of the Sultan's possessions within Europe. Although this scheme found favor in Servia, it was frustrated by Stamboloff, who denounced the plan to the Porte.

## The 1897 Proposal for Pacific Action
In 1897, Bulgaria put forward a proposal for joint pacific action. The objective of this initiative was to obtain reforms in Macedonia. However, this proposal was rejected by Greece.

## Regional Tensions and the Macedonian Question
During the late 19th century, the principle of autonomy in Macedonia was viewed with suspicion in Belgrade and Athens, where it was seen as a means to ensure Bulgarian predominance and delay the ultimate partition of the region. In Athens, the growth of the Bulgarian movement caused significant alarm due to fears that Macedonia would be lost to Hellenism. 

In 1896, the *Ethniké Hetaerea* sent numerous bands into the southern districts of Macedonia to provoke a war between Greece and Turkey, a goal achieved following the outbreak of trouble in Crete. During the subsequent Greco-Turkish War, Bulgaria and Servia did not interfere due to pressure from Russia, Austria, and other great powers. While the Greek reverses during this conflict benefited the Bulgarian movement, the Turkish authorities shifted their attitude toward the Bulgarian element in 1897 following the discovery of a hidden arms dépôt at Vinitza. This led to the distribution of arms among the Moslem population and the use of extreme, often barbarous, methods of repression.

## Subsequent Unrest and International Intervention
Instability continued into the early 20th century. In 1902, Servians obtained the nomination of Mgr. Firmilian, a Servian, to the archbishopric of Usküb with Russian assistance. That same year, efforts were made to provoke a rising in the Monastir district by Colonel Yankoff (lieutenant to General Tzoncheff), and bands entered the Razlog district under General Tzoncheff's direction. These movements failed and were met with merciless repression.

By 1902, the situation necessitated the intervention of the great powers. The Russian and Austrian governments, acting in concert since April 1897, developed a scheme for reforms. Although the Porte attempted to forestall this by promulgating its own project in November 1902—appointing Hilmi Pasha as Inspector General of the Rumelian viláyets—the two powers persisted. On February 21, 1903, they presented an identical memorandum to the Porte proposing reforms in finance, police, and administration, including the use of "foreign specialists" to reorganize the gendarmerie.
