# Influence of William Wickenden and the Providence Church

## The Providence Church and the Schism of 1652
Following the withdrawal of Williams, the Providence church experienced a period of feeble existence. During this time, its leading members included Gregory Dexter, Chad Brown (who died in 1665), William Wickenden, and Thomas Olney (who died in 1682). 

In 1652, the church was split by a schism. A majority of the members, including Dexter, Brown, and Wickenden, advocated for the laying on of hands as an indispensable requirement for fellowship and contended for general redemption. In contrast, Thomas Olney and a minority of the members rejected the laying on of hands as an ordinance and maintained the doctrine of particular redemption. The party led by Olney became extinct shortly after his death in 1682.

## Expansion of Influence
The surviving portion of the Providence church subsequently became involved in Universalism and Socinianism. Despite the earlier period of weakness, the church maintained a somewhat vigorous life. Through the efforts of William Wickenden and others, the church exerted considerable influence in several regions, including Newport, New York, and Connecticut.

## Baptist Activity in New York and New England
William Wickenden's influence extended into the New Netherlands (New York), where antipaedobaptists had been settling since 1643. In 1656, Wickenden traveled to Flushing, where he administered ordinances and evangelized. These actions resulted in Wickenden being heavily fined and banished.

Other Baptist activities occurred concurrently in the region. In 1643, Lady Deborah Moody settled at Gravesend after leaving Massachusetts due to her antipaedobaptist views. In 1656, Francis Doughty, an English Baptist who had spent time in Rhode Island, was banished after baptizing converts in the same region. Later, from 1711 onward, Valentine Wightman of Connecticut made missionary visits to New York, eventually assisting in the organization of a church in 1714 after baptizing Nicolas Eyres and others.

## The Newport Church and Massachusetts
The Newport church also extended its influence into Massachusetts. By 1649, a group of Baptists led by Obadiah Holmes had formed at Rehoboth; however, due to the intolerance of the authorities, they eventually became members of the Newport church. 

In 1651, Joseph Crandall, Obadiah Holmes, and John Clarke—a physician of theological acumen and religious zeal—conducted a religious visit to Lynn, Massachusetts. While meeting in a private home, the three men were arrested and forced to attend the services of the standing order. They were imprisoned, fined, and maltreated for refusing to participate quietly in public service and for holding an unlawful meeting. In 1652, while in England on public business, Clarke published *Ill News from New England*, which provided a reasoned plea for liberty of conscience and an account of the proceedings at Lynn.

## Related Figures in Rhode Island
Alongside Wickenden, other figures played significant roles in the development of the region. John Clarke had arrived in Boston in November 1637 and later assisted fugitives—including William Coddington, John Wheelwright, and Anne Hutchinson—in forming a colony on the island of Aquidnek (Rhode Island). By 1641, "professed Anabaptists" were present on the island, with Clarke likely serving as their leader. 

Other contributors to the Newport company included Robert Lenthall, who arrived from Massachusetts in 1640, and Mark Lucar, who arrived around 1644. Lucar, having been baptized by immersion in London in 1642, became one of the most active members of the Newport church and is considered a connecting link between American and early English Baptists. Additionally, Gregory Dexter became a leading statesman in Providence Plantations and Rhode Island, serving alongside Clarke and Williams.
