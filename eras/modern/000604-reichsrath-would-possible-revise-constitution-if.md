# Reichsrath

The Reichsrath served as the central parliamentary body for the Austrian empire, acting as the venue for the ratification of fundamental laws and the ongoing struggle between centralist and federalist political visions. Its history was marked by frequent conflicts over constitutional validity, racial representation, and the balance of power between the central government in Vienna and the various territories.

## Establishment and the Ausgleich
The formation of a functional Reichsrath was a practical necessity driven by the requirements of the Hungarians, who demanded that the *Ausgleich* (compact) be ratified by a parliament. In early 1867, Belcredi, acting as a Federalist, suspended the 1861 constitution and ordered new elections for the diets to elect deputies to an extraordinary Reichsrath. This move was opposed by German Liberals, who argued that the February constitution remained in force and that changes must be introduced by a regular Reichsrath. Following Belcredi's dismissal, Beust became minister-president on February 7, 1867, and issued a new edict ordering the diets to elect a Reichsrath according to the valid constitution.

When the Reichsrath eventually met, the government held a large majority. All races except the Czechs were represented, and the *Ausgleich* was ratified almost unanimously. This allowed for the creation of special legislation for territories officially designated as "the kingdoms and lands represented in the Reichsrath."

## Constitutional Framework and Powers
The fundamental laws established by the Reichsrath formally created a parliamentary government characterized by the responsibility of ministers and complete control over the budget. These laws included clauses guaranteeing personal liberties and rights. However, the influence of the Poles, supported by the Slovenes, Dalmatians, and Tirolese, prevented German Liberals from introducing direct elections to the Reichsrath.

A significant feature of the system was the Delegation. Rather than being chosen by the House as a whole, the Delegation was elected by representatives of separate territories (for example, Galicians chose 7 delegates, while Trieste chose 1). This structure meant the Delegation did not necessarily represent the majority of the chamber of deputies, allowing opposing groups to maintain their resistance even within the Delegation. This was evidenced in 1869 when the Poles and Tirolese voted in favor of a Hungarian budget proposal that Austria had rejected.

## Political Conflict and Obstruction
The Reichsrath was frequently the site of intense ethnic and political strife. In 1868, the nationalist movement in Bohemia and Galicia challenged the constitution. Czech leaders presented a "declaration" demanding the full restoration of the Bohemian kingdom and asserting that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia. Similarly, Polish parties in Galicia sought increased powers for their local diet and threatened to leave the Reichsrath.

In 1870, after the rejection of Galician demands, the Polish club, Tirolese, and Slovenes left the House, leaving only 110 members—primarily Germans. Later, in February 1871, a ministry led by Count Hohenwart and A. E. F. Schaeffle attempted to appease Federalists and Clericals. By securing a minority for Liberals in the Reichsrath, they sought to revise the constitution, provided the Czechs consented to attend. The government eventually recognized the separate existence of the Bohemian kingdom and promised the emperor would be crowned king at Prague. This led the Germans, now in a minority, to leave the diet and prepare for resistance.

## Paragraph 14 and Late-Century Instability
A recurring point of contention was Paragraph 14 of the Constitutional law of 1867. This clause allowed the emperor to proclaim orders requiring Reichsrath assent in cases of "pressing necessity" if the Reichsrath was not in session, provided they were signed by the ministry and presented to the Reichsrath within four months.

Germans contended that applying this clause to the *Ausgleich* was invalid. This dispute led to the retirement of Thun in September 1899. His successor, Count Clary, withdrew the offending ordinances and pledged not to use Paragraph 14. However, Czech obstruction prevented the passage of a necessary excise law, forcing Clary to resign after less than three months. A subsequent ministry of officials ignored the pledge and used Paragraph 14 for state purposes, eventually making way for a ministry under Herr v. Koerber. By June 1900, the Czechs again disrupted proceedings, demanding the restoration of their language in the internal service of Bohemia.

## Historical Precedents and Dissolution
Earlier attempts at constitutional unity had faced similar failures. A Reichsrath transferred to Kremsier once discussed "fundamental rights" and the reconciliation of unity with dualism, but the assembly was dissolved following the battle of Kapolna in 1849. 

In another period of crisis, the radical populace of Vienna conflicted with the Slav majority of the Reichsrath. On October 7, Emperor Ferdinand fled to Olmuetz. Members of the Right and the Slav majority of the Reichsrath subsequently left Vienna to meet at Bruenn, leaving only a "rump of German radicals" in the capital before the city was taken by Windischgraetz on November 1.
