# Audit of Accounts

An audit is the verification of a record to ensure it serves as a true and complete statement of the transactions involved. This process is essential to guard against dishonesty. In the context of book-keeping—the systematic recording of business transactions for individuals or corporations—the ideal is to combine a maximum of record with a minimum of labor. In large or public concerns, audits are typically conducted by professional accountants. In organizations with large book-keeping staffs, a "staff audit" or "internal check" may be performed by the staff itself, where members check one another's work.

## Bankruptcy Trusteeship and Auditing
In bankruptcy proceedings, the accounts of a trustee are subject to strict auditing requirements. The committee of inspection must audit the trustee's accounts at least once every three months. Every six months, as well as at the conclusion of the administration, the trustee must submit cash books, records, and vouchers, along with the committee's audit certificate, to the Board of Trade for a final audit.

Failure to maintain proper financial discipline can lead to severe penalties. If a trustee retains more than £50 in hand for more than ten days without a satisfactory explanation, they may be removed from office, lose all claims to remuneration, and be surcharged with interest at a rate of 20% per annum.

## Trustee Appointment and Oversight
A trustee may be appointed by a majority in value of the creditors voting at a meeting, or the appointment may be delegated to the committee of inspection. All such appointments require confirmation by the Board of Trade. The Board may object to an appointment if the person is unfit, lacks impartiality due to their relationship with the debtor or creditors, if the creditors did not act in good faith, or if the appointee was previously removed from office for misconduct or failing to render accounts for audit without good cause.

Before confirmation, the trustee-elect must provide security to the Board of Trade, which must be maintained throughout their tenure or the trustee faces removal. If creditors fail to appoint a trustee, the Board of Trade may do so, though the creditors may supersede this appointment at any time.

## Trustee Remuneration
The remuneration of a trustee is fixed by the creditors or an authorized committee and must be a percentage of the dividends and the amount of the realization. The Board of Trade may review and fix the remuneration if the bankrupt proves it is excessive or if one-fourth of the creditors (in number or value) dissent from the resolution. 

Trustees are prohibited from receiving remuneration for services in other capacities (such as auctioneer or solicitor) beyond what is voted to them as trustee. They may not share their remuneration with the bankrupt, the solicitor, or other persons employed in the bankruptcy, nor may they accept any personal benefit or gift in connection with the proceedings.

## Historical and International Examples of Auditing
The practice of auditing accounts has appeared in various historical and governmental contexts:

*   **Ancient Athens:** It was general practice for magistrates leaving office to undergo a trial of accountability and an audit. Following the fall of the Four Hundred oligarchs, each member was called to such an audit. Those who failed to appear faced exile, fines, or being recorded as traitors. While some were condemned to partial political disability or fines, many were acquitted, reportedly through bribes paid to the auditing officers, known as *logistæ*.
*   **Tokugawa Japan:** Cash payments were transported to the castles of Osaka or Yedo under the care of the finance administrator (*kane-bugyo*). The *kane-bugyo* compiled and rendered the accounts, which were then audited by officials called *katte-kata*, presided over by a member of the *waka-doshiyori* or *roju*.
*   **Imperial Japan:** The Board of Audit is responsible for verifying and confirming the final account of the State's revenue and expenditure. This verified account, along with the Board's report, is then submitted by the Government to the Imperial Diet.

## Administrative and Legal Audits
In modern administrative contexts, audits are used to identify systemic failures. For example, a 2008 audit by the U.S. Department of Justice's Office of the Inspector General (OIG) regarding the Victim Notification System (VNS) found that users could not alter templates to fit specific cases, leading to confusing or contradictory notifications. Additionally, the U.S. DOJ OIG has conducted audits and reviews regarding the Federal Bureau of Prisons, including its use of restrictive housing for inmates with mental illness, medical staffing challenges, and specific contracts with entities such as CoreCivic, Inc. and Spectrum Services, Inc.

In English bankruptcy history, the Act of 1869 introduced the comptroller in bankruptcy. This independent official was tasked with examining trustees' accounts and calling them to account for any omission, neglect, or misfeasance, referring such matters to the court for disciplinary action.
