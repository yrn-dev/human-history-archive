# Asiatic Geographical Surveys

The standard of accuracy in Asiatic geographical surveys has been raised to a level that was considered unattainable fifty years prior. This advancement is largely attributed to the far extensions of the Indian triangulation, which provided the necessary basis for a vast amount of exploratory survey of a strictly geographical character.

## Indian Triangulation and Extensions
The Indian triangulation has been pushed outwards on all sides through systems involving varying degrees of irregularity. These extensions reach through Baluchistan and Afghanistan to the eastern districts of Persia, and follow the coast of Makran to that of Arabia. Furthermore, these surveys have included the farther mountain peaks of Nepal and have branched into Siam and towards western China.

While a direct process of earth measurement has not yet brought Asia into connection with Europe via the expansion of principal triangulation, a topographical connection has been established between Indian and Russian surveys. This connection demonstrates that the deductive methods used by both nations to determine the co-ordinate values of fixed points agree sufficiently that no difficulty in adjustment between Russian and Indian mapping is expected for future Asiatic cartography.

## Regional Survey Progress and Limitations
By the close of the 19th century, western Asia—excluding Arabia—was considered to be freed from geographical perplexity. However, enormous areas in eastern Siberia, Mongolia, and China remained in a primitive stage of nebulous uncertainty.

In other specific regions, the state of surveying varies:
*   **Asiatic Turkey:** There is no general government-directed survey. Mapping depends on boundary commissions, railway interests, or compilations such as the Russian general map (1880-1885) and R. Kiepert's *Asia Minor* (1904-1908). Notable actual surveys include those of the Sinai Peninsula (1870), Palestine (1880), Arabia Petraea (1907), and the Aden territory (1905).
*   **Persia and Afghanistan:** Mapping remains dependent on compilations, including a Russian staff map (1886) and Colonel Sir T. H. Holdich's map of Persia (1897-1899). Surveys of interest were necessitated by the settlement of boundaries in Seistan (1870) and northern Afghanistan (1883).
*   **Cyprus:** An excellent map based on surveys by Major (Lord) H. H. Kitchener was published in 1884.

## Central Asian Geography and Trade Routes
Recent survey connections between India, Kashmir, and Chinese Turkestan explorations have redefined the geography of the region. It is now established that the Kuen-lun mountains—extending from the frontiers of Kashmir, north of Leh, east to the Chinese province of Kansu—do not mark the southern limit of the Turkestan depression or the Gobi. Instead, they are the northern limit of the Chang Tang (the elevated highland steppes of Tibet).

From a point at approximately 83 deg. E. longitude, a minor system branches north-east, culminating in the Altyn Tagh and extending to the Nan Shan mountains north of the Koko Nor basin. This creates a system of elevated valleys known as Tsaidam, situated 8,000 to 9,000 ft. above sea-level, which acts as an intermediate steppe between the lowest and highest regions east of Lop Nor. This geographical discovery helps explain why great trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed so far north.

## Historical and Scientific Evolution
The transition to modern Asiatic geography involved the disappearance of traditional nomenclature. Early data from medieval travellers and Marco Polo were handled with caution in Sanson's 1659 map, though it introduced confusion via features from Edrisi. The application of "real science" by D'Anville and De l’Isle, combined with Jesuit surveys and the maps of Martini (who published *Novus atlas sinensis* in 1655 based on Chinese surveys), improved the field. Consequently, the study of Polo's work shifted toward determining the true localities described under corrupted or obsolete names.

## Trans-border Surveys and Immigration
Trans-border surveys to the west and north of India have established that central Asiatic tides of immigration entered the peninsula through only two gateways:
1.  **The North-West:** The Kabul valley, including the group of passes across the Hindu Kush (from Khawak to Kaoshan). This route was used by hordes such as the Huns, Goths, Jats, Yue-chi, and Sacae to enter the Punjab.
2.  **The West:** The Makran route.

The surveys indicate that while some migrants came from eastern Asia, none entered India via eastern passes, as the Himalayan barrier prevented such overflows, leaving only "ethnographical flotsam" in the northern valleys. Most historic invasions from central Asia followed the direct route from Kabul to Delhi and Peshawar.
