# Contributions and Collective Levies

The concept of contribution appears across various historical contexts, ranging from voluntary civic support and corporate capital to forced war levies and cultural traditions involving fire.

## Civic and Military Contributions
In the history of Rome, individual zeal and patriotism have served as a primary source of funding when public treasuries were depleted. During the siege of Lilybæum, the Roman public treasury was at a low ebb, necessitating contributions from individuals to fit out a new fleet. Citizens contributed to the common expense according to their personal circumstances and advanced money upon public security. This collective effort allowed for the rapid preparation of two hundred ships; in some instances, a single man fitted out a ship at his own charge, while others were equipped through the combined contributions of two or three individuals.

In contrast, contributions in the context of conquest often take the form of forced levies. During conflicts involving German armies in the West, war contributions were imposed on towns as punishment for civilian interference. For example, after civilians fired from windows during street-fighting, a fine of two hundred thousand francs was levied on a town. Subsequent sniping from houses led to the war contribution being increased by half a million francs, a sum the Maire was tasked with raising within forty-eight hours. According to the usage of war, such contributions are viewed as a punishment for civilians who fire from ambushes or act as *franctireurs* after a decisive fight has ended.

Similarly, the Carthaginian general Hannibal utilized contributions to sustain his military campaigns in remote countries. Despite facing a powerful faction at home that refused him supplies, Hannibal maintained his army by levying contributions in the countries of his allies and quartering his troops upon them.

## Corporate and Legal Contributions
Contributions also define the structure of corporate governance and insurance liability. The Dutch West India Company, founded on June 3, 1621, organized its general governing board based on the amount of capital contributed by its five branches. Amsterdam, contributing four-ninths of the capital, held eight directors. Zealand, subscribing two-ninths, had four directors. Rotterdam, the North Department, and Groningen each contributed one-ninth of the capital; Rotterdam was represented by two directors, while the other two districts appointed one director each.

In maritime law, the concept of General Average (G.A.) involves contributions to cover losses incurred for the general safety of a voyage. Legal disputes have arisen regarding whether an insurer is liable for G.A. contributions when a shipowner is the sole party interested in the voyage. In the case of the *Brigella* (1893), a claim was disallowed because there were no other contributories (such as cargo or freight owners), meaning there was no basis for a G.A. contribution. However, in *Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co.* (1901), cargo underwriters were held liable to pay a contribution for damage done to a ship (cutting away masts for safety) even though the ship, freight, and cargo belonged to the same person.

## Cultural and Ritual Contributions
The term "bonfire" has historical associations with collective contribution. While originally meaning a "fire of bones" (appearing as *ban fyre ignis ossium* in the *Catholicon Anglicum* of A.D. 1483), some rejected theories suggest the word derived from "boon-fire." This theory posits that bonfires were "contribution" fires, where every person in the neighborhood contributed a portion of the material. This is compared to the "contributed Ploughing Days" in Northumberland, known as *Bone-daags*.

In ancient China, contributions took a literary and ritual form. Authors contributed poetry and wrote their own funeral orations. These orations, along with paper houses, sedan-chairs, and horses, were solemnly consumed by fire. This practice was based on the belief that burning these items would allow them to reach the world of spirits to provide honor and joy to the deceased.
