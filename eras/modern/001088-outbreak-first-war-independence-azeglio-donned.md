# Massimo d'Azeglio

Massimo Taparelli, Marquis d'Azeglio (1798–1866), was an Italian author and statesman born in Turin in October 1798. Descended from an ancient and noble Piedmontese family, he was the son of Cesare d'Azeglio, a high-ranking court official and officer in the Piedmontese army. At age sixteen, Massimo accompanied his father to the Vatican as an extra attaché following the return of Pope Pius VII to Rome.

## Early Life and Artistic Pursuits
Although he initially held a commission in a cavalry regiment, d'Azeglio relinquished it due to health reasons. During his time in Rome, he developed a passion for music and art, eventually deciding to become a painter. Despite the disapproval of the narrow Piedmontese aristocracy, his father consented, and Massimo spent several years in Rome supporting himself through his painting.

In 1830, d'Azeglio returned to Turin, and following his father's death in 1831, he moved to Milan. For twelve years, he was active in the city's artistic and literary circles and became an intimate of the novelist Alessandro Manzoni. Marrying Manzoni's daughter, d'Azeglio shifted his primary focus from art to literature. He wrote two historical novels, *Ettore Fieramosca* (1833) and *Niccolo dei Lapi* (1841), which utilized political allusions to highlight the problems of foreign domination in Italy and to stir national sentiment.

## Political Activism and the First War of Independence
D'Azeglio's political engagement intensified in the 1840s. In 1845, he acted as an unauthorized political envoy to Romagna to assess the region's conditions. In 1846, he published a pamphlet titled *Degli ultimi casi di Romagna* in Florence, an act that led to his expulsion from Tuscany. During the subsequent period in Rome, he shared the enthusiasm for the perceived liberalism of Pope Pius IX and, similar to Balbo and V. Gioberti, supported the idea of an Italian confederation under papal auspices.

At the outbreak of the first war of independence, d'Azeglio donned the papal uniform and served under General Durando in the defense of Vicenza, where he suffered severe wounds. While recovering in Florence, he was expelled from Tuscany for a second time due to his opposition to the ruling democrats.

## Ministerial Career in Piedmont
In early 1849, King Charles Albert of Sardinia invited d'Azeglio to form a cabinet. He refused the offer at that time, believing it impossible to renew the campaign and unwilling to sign a peace treaty with Austria under the prevailing internal and external conditions. Following the defeat of Novara on March 23, 1849, and the subsequent abdication of Charles Albert, d'Azeglio was again asked to form a cabinet by the new king, Victor Emmanuel II.

D'Azeglio accepted the position despite the difficult circumstances. He dissolved the Chamber, summoned a new one, and concluded a peace treaty which was subsequently ratified. He remained in office for three years, during which time he managed the country's affairs with ability and tact, improved diplomatic relations, and opposed the claims of the Roman Curia. While other parts of Italy faced despotism, the king in Piedmont maintained the constitution.

In 1850, d'Azeglio invited the rising politician Count Cavour to join the ministry. However, in May 1852, declarations made in the Chamber by Cavour and another cabinet member, Farini, pushed the ministry toward an alliance with the Left and Rattazzi. Disapproving of this direction, d'Azeglio resigned. He later formed a second ministry at the king's request, excluding Farini and Cavour. He resigned for a final time in October of that year due to ill health and dissatisfaction with colleagues, suggesting Cavour as his successor.

## Later Life and Political Views
Following the death of Cavour in 1861 and his two brothers in 1862, d'Azeglio lived a more retired life, though he continued to write and serve as a deputy. His primary interests remained the relations between the kingdom of Italy (formerly Piedmont) and revolutionists like Mazzini, as well as the "Roman question."

D'Azeglio believed that Italy should be unified solely through the Franco-Piedmontese army, avoiding any connection with conspirators. Regarding Rome, he proposed that the pope maintain full spiritual independence and nominal sovereignty over the city, while the capital of Italy be located elsewhere and Romans be granted Italian citizenship. He expressed strong disapproval of the 1864 convention between the pope and the Italian government.

D'Azeglio spent his final years at his villa in Cannero working on his memoirs. He died of fever on January 15, 1866. His autobiographical work, *I Miei Ricordi*, was published posthumously in 1866, though it remained incomplete.
