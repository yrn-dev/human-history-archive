# Temperatures and Climatic Phenomena of Asia

## Temperature Extremes and Distribution
Asia contains some of the highest and lowest temperatures observed on the globe. In southern Asia, the highest mean temperature attained is not much above 82 deg. Fahr. Conversely, northern Asia contains some of the lowest temperatures, with the mean temperature of the north coast of eastern Siberia being only a few degrees above 0 deg. Fahr.

The isothermals of mean annual temperature across northern Asia follow regular curves, with western branches situated at a higher latitude than eastern ones. Generally, a reduction of 1 deg. of latitude corresponds to a rise of approximately 1/2 deg. Fahr. up to 30 deg. N, where the mean temperature is about 75 deg. Fahr. South of this point, the increase in temperature occurs more slowly.

## Seasonal Variations and Regional Factors
The northern portion of Asia is characterized by winters of extreme severity and long duration. Even as far south as 35 deg. N latitude, minimum temperatures can drop nearly to 0 deg. Fahr. Summers in these northern latitudes are hot, though short; the maximum summer heat is only slightly less than that found in tropical regions further south.

Several factors modify these temperatures:
*   **Oceanic Influence:** Proximity to the ocean provides a moderating effect, particularly in the eastern and southern regions where the land consists of peninsulas or islands.
*   **Elevation and Surface Conditions:** In Persia, Afghanistan, and the central part of Asia, great elevations above sea level exaggerate winter cold. In these same regions, surface sterility caused by low rainfall increases summer heat. Because the dry soil lacks vegetation or moisture, solar heat accumulates on the surface and cannot be released via evaporation.
*   **Radiation:** During winter, long and clear nights lead to a prolonged loss of heat through radiation, an effect intensified by air with low vapour content or surfaces covered in snow.

## Solar Heat and Tropical Modification
In areas north of the tropic of Cancer, high summer temperatures are explained by the increased length of the day at higher latitudes. For example, at 45 deg. latitude, the day is 15-1/2 hours long, compared to 13-1/4 hours at 20 deg. latitude. While the heating power of the sun's rays at noon on June 21st is only about 2% different between these two latitudes, the accumulated heat received during the longer day at the higher latitude is approximately 11% greater.

In the tropical region, periodical summer rains act as a significant modifying cause, arresting the increase of summer heat and giving the climate peculiar characteristics.

## The South-West Monsoon
The south-west monsoon carries heated air from the Indian Ocean over southern Asia. This air is highly charged with watery vapour and releases rain when its temperature is reduced. This reduction occurs primarily along the Burmo-Siamese peninsula and the coasts of India, where continuous mountain ranges interrupt the wind current. As the air is forced to rise over these mountains, it is rarefied, its specific capacity for heat increases, and its temperature falls, leading to the condensation of vapour.

## Permafrost in Siberia
The climate of northern Asia is further illustrated by the frozen soil near Yakutsk in Siberia. In this region, the earth is permanently frozen to a depth exceeding 380 ft., where temperatures remain 5 or 6 deg. Fahr. below the freezing point of water. At a depth of 50 ft., the temperature is approximately 15 Fahr. below freezing. Summer heat only thaws the surface to a depth of about 3 ft. Despite these conditions, the land is capable of producing crops of other grain and wheat from fifteen to forty fold.

## Influence on Flora
The distribution of life forms in Asia is closely dependent on these climatic conditions. The flora of northern Asia is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, though species vary. In Siberia, the northern limit of the pine is approximately 70 deg. N. While pines, birch, and larch are principal trees on the mountains, poplars, alders, and willow are found on lower ground; heaths and oak are absent east of the Ural. A flora of essentially European character extends from the Himalaya to the Mediterranean, with Japanese and Chinese forms appearing most frequently in the eastern Himalaya.
