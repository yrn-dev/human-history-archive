# Edwin Booth

Edwin Booth (1833–1893) was a prominent American actor, born on November 13, 1833, in Belair, Maryland. He was the second son of the actor Junius Brutus Booth (1796–1852).

## Early Career and Development
Booth made his first regular stage appearance on September 10, 1849, at the Boston Museum, playing Tressel in Colley Cibber's version of *Richard III* alongside his father, who played Richard. While Booth was noted for his buoyant spirits, graceful figure, and expressive eyes, his father viewed his performance with evident disappointment. Other members of the theatrical profession similarly felt that the genius of the elder Booth had not been passed to his son.

On September 27, 1850, Booth made his New York debut at the National theatre in Chatham Street as Wilford in *The Iron Chest*. A year later, he stepped into the role of Richard III due to his father's illness. However, it was only after his father's death that Booth established an unassailable position on the stage. Between 1852 and 1856, he performed in the Sandwich Islands, Australia, and California, where he captivated audiences with brilliant acting, surprising those who had known him previously in the east.

## Professional Triumphs and Management
As his career progressed, Booth's dramatic achievements were widely acknowledged. His performances as Richelieu, Richard, and Hamlet were considered superior to those of Edwin Forrest, and his portrayal of Sir Giles Overreach in *A New Way to Pay Old Debts* surpassed that of his father.

In 1862, Booth became the manager of the Winter Garden theatre in New York. Between 1864 and 1867, he presented a series of Shakespearian productions of unexampled magnificence, including *The Merchant of Venice*, *Othello*, and *Hamlet*. 

From 1868 to 1869, Booth built his own venue, Booth's theatre, located at the corner of 6th Avenue and 23rd Street in New York. He organized a stock company that produced plays including *Macbeth*, *Julius Caesar*, *The Winter's Tale*, *Romeo and Juliet*, *Much Ado about Nothing*, and *The Merchant of Venice*. Booth was a pioneer in using the true text of Shakespeare, a reform that occurred in England many years later. While his ventures were generally successful, his generous and confiding nature led to uneconomical management. In 1874, he lost both his theatre and his entire fortune.

## Personal Life and Later Years
Booth's career was marked by a period of significant distress in 1865 when his brother, John Wilkes Booth, assassinated President Lincoln. In the autumn of 1864, the three Booth brothers—Edwin, John Wilkes (1839–1865), and Junius Brutus (1821–1853)—had performed together in *Julius Caesar*.

Booth married twice: first to Mary Devlin in 1860 (who died in 1863) and later to Mary F. McVicker in 1869 (who died in 1881). His first wife bore him a daughter, Edwina Booth Grossman.

After recovering his wealth through arduous toil, Booth converted his residence in Gramercy Park, New York, into The Players' club for members of the acting profession and other chosen professionals. He donated the house, his works of art, books, and stage mementos to the club, retaining only a single apartment for himself. He died in that apartment on June 7, 1893.

## Artistic Legacy and Repertoire
Booth's most famous role was Hamlet, a part for which his eloquent sensibility, beauty, and grace were peculiarly suited; he likely played this role more often than any other actor. His extensive repertoire included:
* **Shakespearean roles:** Lear, Othello, Iago, Shylock, Macbeth, Richard II, Richard III, Benedick, Petruccio, and Brutus (Payne's).
* **Other roles:** Wolsey, Richelieu, Sir Giles Overreach, Ruy Blas, Don Cesar de Bazan, and Bertuccio in Tom Taylor's *The Fool's Revenge*.

Internationally, Booth visited London in 1851, 1880, and 1882, achieving brilliant success at the Haymarket theatre. He also visited Germany in 1882, where his acting was received with high enthusiasm. His final stage appearance took place in Brooklyn in 1891, where he performed as Hamlet.

## Sources and Documentation
Several works document Booth's life and career, including *Life and Art of Edwin Booth* (1893) by William Winter, who also edited Booth's prompt-books in 1878. Other sources include *Edwin Booth* (1893) by Lawrence Hutton, *The Elder and the Younger Booth* (1882) by A.B. Clarke, and *Reminiscences of a Dramatic Critic* (1902) by Henry A. Clapp. In the series *Actors and Actresses of Great Britain and America*, edited by Brander Matthews and Lawrence Hutton, Booth contributed recollections of his father, and Lawrence Barrett contributed an article on Booth. Additionally, his daughter published *Edwin Booth: Recollections* in 1894.
