# Government Loans and National Banking Institutions

The relationship between national governments and banking institutions has historically been characterized by the provision of large-scale loans to stabilize state finances, manage national debts, or fund military operations. These arrangements often serve as the foundation for the establishment of central banking systems or the reorganization of national financial structures.

## The Bank of England
The Bank of England was founded in 1694, with its establishment originating from a loan to the government totaling £1,200,000. This loan was subscribed to in just over ten days, between June 21 and July 2, 1694. Following this, on July 10, the subscribers appointed Michael Godfrey as deputy-governor and Sir John Houblon as governor.

From its inception, the Bank of England was entrusted with the management of the British national debt. It acted as the government's banker, handling the registration of stock transfers and the payment of interest on the debt, which was originally paid half-yearly and later changed to quarterly. This position provided the bank with significant pre-eminence, bolstered by a monopoly as the only joint-stock bank allowed in England and Wales until 1826, and the privilege of issuing notes that became legal tender everywhere except at the bank itself starting in 1833.

## Banking and Government in the United States
In the United States, the Bank of North America was authorized by Congress on May 26, 1781, to address the needs of the continental government. Robert Morris, the financier, was empowered to create the bank with a capital of $400,000. The institution began business on January 7, 1782, and obtained a charter from the state of Pennsylvania due to doubts regarding the continental congress's power to charter a bank. During its first eight years, it worked to restore order to Federation finances, loaning $1,249,975 to Morris in his capacity as government superintendent of finance. Of this amount, $996,581 was repaid in cash, while the remainder was settled by the government surrendering its stock in the bank.

Later, as part of Alexander Hamilton's system for organizing Federal government finances under the 1789 constitution, the first Bank of the United States was incorporated in 1791. It possessed a capital of $10,000,000, divided into 25,000 shares of $400 each. While the government subscribed to one-fifth of the capital, it used a roundabout process that effectively resulted in the bank loaning that amount to the treasury. Additional loans were made by the bank to the government, which increased the obligation to $6,200,000 by the end of 1795. To meet these obligations, the government sold its bank stock, disposing of all holdings by 1802 at a profit of $671,860. A 1811 statement submitted by Gallatin to Congress indicated the bank's resources totaled $24,183,046, including $14,578,294 in loans and discounts, $5,009,567 in specie, and $2,750,000 in United States stock.

## Japanese War Loans and the London Market
In 1904, 1905, and 1906, Japan raised enormous war loans using modern methods to minimize disturbances to the money market. These loans were issued by three banks: one Japanese institution representing the Japanese government, a leading London bank, and the principal British bank operating in China. 

To manage the cash flow, "application money" deposited by investors was lent back into the short loan market as quickly as possible, often on the afternoon of the issue. As the scrip for each loan became "fully paid," the banks held very large sums of proceeds. The Japanese government had full disposal of these funds but acted considerately to avoid embarrassing the London money market. While the government promised not to withdraw the loans raised in London in gold, it remained free to employ the money as it saw fit. Consequently, the government transferred a portion of these deposits to the Bank of England.

## Other National Banking Functions
In France, the Bank of France managed various financial and political crises, including the 1848 overthrow of Louis Philippe's government, the 1870 war with Germany, and the 1871 Commune in Paris. It also handled the payment of a war indemnity that was not completed until 1873. By December 23, 1906, the bank held approximately £35,000,000 in deposits, with £14,000,000 of that amount being on account of the treasury.
