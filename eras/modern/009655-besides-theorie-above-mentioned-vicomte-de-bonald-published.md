# Louis Gabriel Ambroise, Vicomte de Bonald

Louis Gabriel Ambroise, Vicomte de Bonald (1754–1840), was a French politician and philosopher. He was born on October 2, 1754, at Le Monna, located near Millau in Aveyron.

## Political Career and Life
Due to his dislike of the principles of the French Revolution, Bonald emigrated in 1791. During this period, he joined the army of the prince of Conde and eventually settled in Heidelberg. Upon returning to France, he was viewed as an object of suspicion and was forced to live in retirement.

His public life resumed in 1806 when he became associated with Fievee and Chateaubriand in the management of the *Mercure de France*. In 1808, he was appointed as a councillor of the Imperial University, an institution he had frequently attacked. Following the restoration, Bonald served as a member of the council of public instruction and sat as a deputy in the chamber from 1815 to 1822. In this role, his speeches were characterized as being on the extreme conservative side, and he advocated for the implementation of literary censorship.

In 1822, Bonald was appointed minister of state and presided over the censorship commission. He was made a peer the following year, though he lost this dignity in 1830 after refusing to take the oath. He had been a member of the Academy since 1816. After 1830, he withdrew from public affairs and retired to his seat at Le Monna, where he died on November 23, 1840.

## Philosophical Works and Theories
Bonald is associated with the theocratic school of thought. This school opposed the rationalism of the previous era, emphasizing the principle of authority and placing order and revelation above progress, freedom, and individual reason. Bonald specifically reduced the problem of the origin of society to the problem of the origin of language, maintaining that language was a divine gift.

His first major work, the highly conservative *Theorie du pouvoir politique et religieux* (published in three volumes in 1796; new edition in two volumes, Paris, 1854), was condemned by the Directory. Other published works include:
* *Essai analytique sur les lois naturelles de l'ordre social* (1800)
* *Du divorce considere au XIXe siecle* (1801)
* *Legislation primitive* (1802)
* *Recherches philosophiques sur les premiers objets de connaissances morales* (two volumes, 1818)
* *Melanges litteraires et politiques, demonstration philosophique du principe constitutif de la societe* (1819, 1852)

A first collected edition of his works appeared in 12 volumes between 1817 and 1819, and a later edition was produced by the Abbe Migne in three volumes in 1859.

Critics have described Bonald's style as pure, vigorous, ornate, and remarkably fine. While his works contain many fruitful thoughts, it has been noted that his system barely qualifies as a philosophy; he is characterized as a dilettante in abstract thought whose strength resided in the sincerity and vigour of his statements rather than in the cogency of his reasoning.

## Family and Legacy
Bonald had four sons:
* **Victor de Bonald (1780–1871):** He followed his father into exile and later served as the rector of the academy of Montpellier. He lost this post during the Hundred Days but regained it at the second restoration before resigning in 1830. His writings include *Moise et les geologues modernes* (1835) and *Des vrais principes opposes aux erreurs du XIXe siecle* (1833), as well as a biography of his father.
* **Louis Jacques Maurice (1787–1870):** A cardinal (1841) who was condemned by the council of state for a pastoral letter attacking the *Manuel de droit ecclesiastique* by Dupin the elder. In 1851, he advocated in the senate for the use of force of arms to maintain the temporal power of Rome.
* **Henri (d. 1846):** A contributor to legitimist journals.
* **Rene:** Served as the interim prefect of Aveyron in 1817.
