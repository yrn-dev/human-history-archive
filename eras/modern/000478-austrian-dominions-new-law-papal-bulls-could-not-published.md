# Austrian Dominions and the Regulation of Papal Bulls

## Religious Reforms under Maria Theresa
The reign of Maria Theresa is characterized as the true epoch of transition from medieval to modern conditions in Austria, particularly through her social, educational, and religious reforms. Although Maria Theresa was a devout Catholic and devoted to the Holy See, she and her ministers were influenced by a prevailing reaction against the pretensions of the papacy. This anti-papal tendency, referred to as Febronianism, gained significant traction among both the clergy and the laity within the Austrian dominions.

As a result of this movement, a new law was established stating that papal bulls could not be published without the consent of the crown. Furthermore, the state forbade bishops from maintaining direct intercourse with Rome. Other measures taken to assert the rights of the state over the church included the curtailment of privileges for religious orders and the placement of clergy education under state control.

Despite these assertions of state power, Maria Theresa exhibited reluctance in some matters. She did so when carrying out the papal bull that suppressed the Society of Jesus. Additionally, while she declared herself against persecution, she remained unmoved by the views of Joseph and Kaunitz, who favored toleration.

## Educational Shifts
Parallel to the religious restrictions, a revolution occurred in the monarchy's educational system. Control of education was removed from the church, and universities were modernized and remodelled. This process included the introduction of new faculties and the transfer of the study of ecclesiastical law from the field of theology to that of jurisprudence. An elaborate system of secondary and elementary education was also established, which remained largely unchanged until 1869.

## Later Church-State Relations
In 1870, the proclamation of papal infallibility led the government to declare that the concordat had lapsed, citing a fundamental change in the character of the papacy. This doctrine was opposed by nearly all Austrian prelates; many voted against it during the council and only declared their submission with great reluctance. Following this period, laws regulating the position of the Church were passed in 1874. While these tensions existed, the Old Catholic movement did not achieve significant progress in Austria.

## Historical Context of Papal Bulls
A papal bull is a formal document issued by the Pope. Historically, some bulls were used for specific ecclesiastical purposes, such as the *In Coena Domini*, which was first published in 1363. This annual bull served as a statement of ecclesiastical censure against crimes such as piracy, forgery, sacrilege, schisms, heresies, and the infringement of ecclesiastical and papal privileges. However, because the sovereigns of Europe—both Catholic and Protestant—viewed this specific bull as an infringement of their own rights, Pope Clement XIV discontinued its publication in 1770.

In other historical contexts, papal bulls have been used to secure monopolies. For example, a papal bull and an agreement between Portugal and Spain once secured a monopoly on oversea commerce and religious propagandism for the Portuguese in Japan.
