# Karawanken and Wochein Railways

The Karawanken and Wochein railways were components of a larger infrastructure project within the Dual Monarchy, specifically designed as a "second railway route to Trieste." This project aimed to reduce the distance between the Adriatic, Salzburg, and South Germany by creating a line that passed under the southern and central Alpine ranges of Austria.

## Construction and Engineering
The railway route was developed by boring tunnels through several hill ranges, with the principal sections of the line named after the ranges they pierced. These included the Wochein, Karawanken, and Tauern hills. The project was planned such that sections of the line would be opened to traffic as soon as they were completed, with the goal of finishing the entire work during 1909. Due to its engineering, the line is considered one of the most interesting railway routes in Europe.

## Strategic and Political Objectives
The construction of these railways served both strategic military purposes and domestic political goals:

**Military Strategy**
The railways were constructed during a period of internal crisis and external threat. The Dual Monarchy faced strained relations with Italy between 1902 and 1903 due to the irredentist attitude of the Zanardelli cabinet, a situation that made war seem imminent. In response, the state strongly fortified strategic points on the Dalmatian and Istrian coasts, the chief passes into Italy, and the southern Tirol. In the interior, the Karawanken, Wochein, and Tauern railways were built, partly to facilitate the movement of troops toward the Italian border.

**Political Expediency**
The railways were also part of a broader effort by the premier, Dr. von Koerber, to restore the parliament to normal working conditions following the general election of 1901. To overcome obstruction and reach a compromise between Czechs and Germans, Koerber promised large public works that interested both groups. In addition to several canal projects (including connections between the Danube, Oder, Moldau, Elbe, Vistula, and Dniester), the cabinet proposed and the Chamber sanctioned the "second railway route to Trieste."

## Financial Impact
The cost of these public works, including the railways, significantly exceeded the estimates originally sanctioned by parliament. It has been contended that the parliamentary adoption of the Budget in 1901-1902 cost the state £100,000,000 for these works. While some of these projects were considered necessary or desirable, they were often begun hastily under the pressure of political expediency.
