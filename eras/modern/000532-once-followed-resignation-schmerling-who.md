# Political Transitions in the Austrian Empire (1861–1866)

The mid-19th century was a period of significant constitutional and governmental instability within the Austrian Empire, characterized by attempts to balance the national aspirations of various ethnic groups with the authority of the Habsburg monarchy.

## The Constitution of 1861 and Anton von Schmerling
On February 26, 1861, a new constitution for the entire monarchy was proclaimed. This document had been elaborated by Anton von Schmerling. While the constitution did not satisfy the national aspirations of the different races within the empire, it provided Austria with a period of temporary popularity in Germany. During this time, the liberalism of the Habsburg monarchy was viewed favorably when contrasted with the "reactionary" policy of Prussia, where Bismarck was defying the majority of the diet to increase Prussian military power.

## Internal Resistance and Governmental Reorganization
Despite the 1861 constitution, which established a common parliament for the whole empire, the Czechs and the Magyar nation refused to recognize its validity. The Magyars specifically demanded the restoration of the independence of the kingdom of Hungary.

The necessity of reaching an agreement with the Hungarians was recognized by the empire even before the outbreak of the war of 1866. In June 1865, Emperor Francis Joseph visited Pest. During this visit, he replaced the chancellors of Hungary and Transylvania—Counts Nadasdy and Francis Zichy, who supported the February constitution—with Count Majlath, a leader of the old conservative magnates. This change in leadership was immediately followed by the resignation of Schmerling, who was succeeded by Count Richard Belcredi.

Following these events, the Reichsrath was prorogued on September 20, an action equivalent to the suspension of the constitution. In December, the emperor personally opened the Hungarian diet, delivering a speech from the throne that recognized the validity of the laws of 1848.

## The War of 1866 and its Aftermath
The political landscape was further disrupted by the conflict with Prussia. The breach between the two German powers was forced by the Schleswig-Holstein Question, leading to the campaign of 1866 and the Austrian collapse at the field of Koniggratz on July 3.

The war of 1866 marked the beginning of a new era for the Austrian empire. Under the treaty of Prague, signed on August 23, 1866, the emperor surrendered the position in Germany held by his ancestors for centuries. Consequently, Salzburg, Bohemia, Tirol, and Austria ceased to be German, cutting off eight million Germans from political union with their fellow-countrymen. Additionally, the surrender of Venetia ended the last remnants of Habsburg domination in Italy.

## The Restoration of Hungarian Autonomy
The defeats on the field of battle allowed the Hungarian diet to dictate its own terms for reorganization. The diet revoked all attempts made over the previous hundred years to absorb Hungary into a greater Austria and rejected any union between Hungary and other parts of the monarchy that was not based on the Pragmatic Sanction. 

An agreement was reached stating that the emperor would take the ancient oath to the Golden Bull and be crowned at Pest. Under this arrangement, Hungary—which included Croatia and Transylvania—was to have its own parliament.
