# Afghanistan and Persia: Flora, Geography, and History

## Botanical Characteristics
The flora of Afghanistan and Persia varies significantly by region and altitude. In the higher elevations of both Persia and Afghanistan, thistles and Boraginaceae are abundant. These districts are further characterized by gigantic Umbelliferae, including genera such as *Bubon*, *Dorema*, *Ferula*, *Galbanum*, *Peucedanum*, and *Prangos*, some of which extend into Tibet.

In northern Afghanistan, the flora approximates that of the contiguous western Himalaya. This includes the evergreen oak of southern Europe, *Quercus Ilex*, which is found in forests as far east as the Sutlej. In northern Persia and Asia Minor, the flora differs little from that of southern Europe. Where rainfall is abundant, mountains are clothed in various Coniferae and forests of *Ulmus*, *Acer*, *Carpinus*, *Corylus*, *Quercus*, and *Fagus*; notably, *Fagus* is the only genus among these not found on the Himalaya. Additionally, fruit trees of the plum tribe are abundant in these areas, and cultivated plants are those of southern Europe.

Conversely, the rainless regions of south-western Asia—which include Afghanistan, Baluchistan, south Persia, and the shores of the Persian Gulf—possess a desert flora continuous with that of northern and eastern Africa, extending from the coast of Senegal to the meridian of 75 deg. E.

## Climate and Geography
In Persia, Afghanistan, Syria, and Asia Minor, the chief seasons of condensation are winter and spring. 

Geographically, the region is defined by significant mountain barriers. A mountain system connects the southern abutment of the Hindu Kush near Bamian with the water-divide north of Herat, while another barrier exists between Herat and Merv, connecting with the Elburz of Persia. 

Scientific geographical mapping of the northern and north-western frontiers of India—including Persia, Baluchistan, and Afghanistan—has progressed remarkably. This effort has produced maps extending from the Euphrates to the Indus and from the Indus to the Oxus. These professional labors involved surveyors such as Major Percy M. Sykes, Captain H.B. Vaughan, and General Sir A. Houtum Schindler in Persia, as well as Cockerill and Sir George Robertson in the Hindu Kush and Kafiristan.

## Political Boundaries and Surveys
The period beginning around 1880 was an era of boundary-making and the definition of politico-geographical limits for Russia, Afghanistan, Persia, India, and Baluchistan. Specific boundary settlements occurred in northern Afghanistan in 1883 and in Seistan in 1870. Other notable geographical efforts include the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1885 and the occupation of Chitral and Gilgit.

Because there has been no general government-directed survey in some areas, researchers rely on compilations. For Afghanistan and Persia, these include a Russian staff map published in 1886, a map of Persia by Colonel Sir T. H. Holdich (1897-1899), and maps published by the geographical division of the general staff.

## Historical Figures and Populations
The region has seen significant political instability and migration. Ayub Khan (born c. 1855), an Afghan prince and son of Shere Ali, took possession of Herat after his father's death in January 1879. Ayub Khan invaded Afghanistan in June 1881 to assert claims to sovereignty and the possession of Kandahar. On July 27, he won a pitched battle against a British force commanded by General Burrows at Maiwand. However, he was later defeated by Sir Frederick Roberts on September 20, 1880. After further conflicts with Abdur Rahman, Ayub Khan took refuge in Persia, where he received an allowance from the Persian government. Following a failed attempt to seize the throne in 1887, he surrendered to a British agent at Meshed in November and was sent to India as a state prisoner.

Regarding population movements, the Kizilbashes (meaning "Red-Heads") are Shiitic Turkish immigrants from Persia. While the Turkish sultans settled them in districts from Angora to Kara-Hissar, many Kizilbashes are also found in Afghanistan, having immigrated during the time of Nadir Shah in 1737. Described as honest and industrious, they serve as scribes, physicians, and traders, and they constitute the bulk of the amir's cavalry.
