# European Imperialism and the Americas

## Early Colonial Relations and Treaties
During the 17th century, tensions between European powers over territories in the New World led to diplomatic resolutions. In 1670, England and Spain concluded a treaty that proclaimed peace and friendship among the subjects of their respective sovereigns. Under this agreement, hostilities of every kind were formally renounced. Spain made a remarkable concession by allowing Great Britain to hold all its New World possessions as its own property. In return, Great Britain consented that its subjects would refrain from trading with any Spanish port unless they obtained a license.

## The Decline of the Spanish Empire
By the early 19th century, the Spanish monarchy exhibited a disposition to return to past conditions, which included the restoration of the Inquisition. This monarchist tendency coincided with instability in the Americas. In 1810, Napoleon placed his brother Joseph on the Spanish throne, prompting Spanish colonies to revolt against the European Great Power System, following the example of the United States. General Bolivar is described as the George Washington of South America during this period.

Spain was unable to suppress these revolts, which persisted in a manner similar to the United States War of Independence. In response, Austria suggested, in accordance with the spirit of the Holy Alliance, that European monarchs should assist Spain. While Britain opposed this in Europe, the project was conclusively halted in 1823 by President Monroe of the United States.

## The Monroe Doctrine
President Monroe announced that the United States would view any extension of the European system in the Western Hemisphere as a hostile act. This policy, known as the Monroe Doctrine, established that there should be no extension of extra-American government in America. This doctrine kept the Great Power system out of the region for nearly a hundred years, allowing the new states of Spanish America to determine their own destinies.

Despite the loss of its colonies, Spanish monarchism continued to operate within Europe under the protection of the Concert of Europe. In 1823, a French army, acting on a mandate from a European congress, crushed a popular insurrection in Spain. Simultaneously, Austria suppressed a revolution in Naples. These actions were driven by the Austrian statesman Metternich.

## The Scramble for Africa
With the Monroe Doctrine barring European adventures in the American region, European powers turned their attention toward Africa. In 1850, much of the continent remained a "black mystery," with only the coast and Egypt being known. Over the following decades, explorers, traders, scientific men, and political agents pierced this "cloud of darkness," discovering diverse races such as the pigmies, animals like the okapi, and the remains of a vanished civilization at Zimbabwe.

By 1900, the European powers had mapped, explored, and divided the entire continent among themselves. This division was characterized by disputation and "snarling," with little regard for the welfare of the native populations. While Arab slave-traders were curbed, the period was marked by horrible atrocities. These included the forced collection of rubber in the Belgian Congo, driven by the "pitiless avarice" of the King of the Belgians, as well as clashes between inexperienced European administrators and native populations.

## The Transatlantic Slave Trade
The revival of gang slavery in mines, plantations, and public works was driven by the immediate advantages it offered the ruling and owning classes. This revival faced significant opposition as it countered the evolving conscience of mankind. The process was particularly brutal due to the trade of man hunts and slave wars in Western Africa and the conditions of the transatlantic voyage, where captives were packed into ships with insufficient food, water, medicines, and sanitation.

Three European nations were the primary participants in this business: Britain, Spain, and Portugal. Their involvement was largely due to their positions as the chief owners of new lands in America.
