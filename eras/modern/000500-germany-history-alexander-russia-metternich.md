# GERMANY: History, Alexander I, and Metternich

## The Post-Napoleonic Settlement and the Congress of Vienna
Following the conquests of Napoleon, which had upset the European balance of power, an international congress was summoned in September 1814 in Vienna. The choice of Vienna reflected the decisive role played by Austria in the preceding events. The diplomacy of Count Metternich achieved a triumphant vindication during these proceedings. While Metternich could not prevent Alexander I of Russia from retaining the grand-duchy of Warsaw, he successfully used a secret treaty with France and Great Britain on January 3, 1815, to stop Prussia from absorbing all of Saxony. 

Under this settlement, Bavaria was forced to return territories it had gained from Austria under Napoleon, and Austria regained Dalmatia and Illyria. Additionally, Lombardy was joined with Venetia to form a kingdom under the Habsburg crown, effectively replacing French influence with Austrian influence across the Italian peninsula.

## The German Confederation and the Policy of Stability
In Germany, the settlement avoided the restoration of the Holy Empire, despite protests from the Holy See. Instead, Austria established a loose confederation of sovereign states (*Staatenbund*) under its own presidency. Metternich believed this body would be "powerless for offence" but "powerful for defence," ensuring the peace of central Europe and maintaining Austrian preponderance. By utilizing the weight of Habsburg power beyond German borders, Austrian diplomacy exerted more influence than the "venerable title" of the old Empire would have provided.

However, this arrangement had long-term consequences. By relinquishing claims to other outlying territories in western Germany and allowing Prussia to establish itself in the Rhine provinces, Austria abdicated its role as the bulwark of Germany against France. 

Metternich subsequently imposed a system of "stability" upon Europe for thirty years. This policy was designed to prevent the success of national aspirations or Liberal principles, which Metternich viewed as threats that could shatter the "rotten structure" of the Habsburg monarchy. To keep Germany quiet and protect Austrian interests, Metternich utilized the Vienna Final Act and the Carlsbad Decrees. He also persuaded Frederick William III that a popular constitution would be fatal to the Prussian monarchy. In the Eastern Question, Austria supported the "legitimate" authority of the sultan over "rebel" Greeks solely to maintain the integrity of Turkey.

## Alexander I and the Holy Alliance
Alexander I of Russia possessed a complex relationship with Napoleon, viewing him as both a rival to be envied and an "underbred upstart" to be despised. Driven by a mystical religiosity and a sense of mission to bring peace to the world by destroying Napoleon, Alexander found this goal compatible with the annexation of Finland, most of Poland, and parts of the Turkish empire. He also sought to resume trade with Britain, as the Napoleonic "Continental System"—which excluded British goods from Europe—had embittered the mercantile classes and dislocated trade in Germany and Russia.

On September 26, a proclamation establishing the Holy Alliance was signed by the sovereigns of Prussia, Austria, and Russia. The idea germinated in a "religious forcing-house," and Madame de Krüdener claimed to have suggested the idea and approved the draft, though Alexander later reproved her for her indiscretion.

## Later Alliances and the Road to Conflict
In 1879, the position of the monarchy was secured through a formal alliance with Germany. Arranged by Bismarck and Andrassy, this treaty bound Germany to support Austria against Russian attacks, while Austria-Hungary pledged to help Germany against a combined attack by France and Russia. This alliance was strengthened in 1882 by the addition of Italy, which sought support following the French occupation of Tunis. 

While some German Austrians viewed this as a "national alliance of the Teutonic races" to be used in internal struggles against Slavs, the governments of Germany and Austria-Hungary rejected this theory. Bismarck, in particular, avoided influencing the internal affairs of his ally to prevent provoking the Poles and Czechs, who were inclined toward an alliance with France.

By the early 20th century, tensions rose as Germany and Austria plotted to extend German influence eastward toward Asia Minor, a goal summarized as "Berlin to Bagdad." This conflicted with Russian ambitions to extend Slav ascendancy to the Adriatic via Serbia and Constantinople. This rivalry created a "feverish state of affairs" in the Balkans, with Turkey turning to Germany for support and Serbia turning to Russia.

## The Outbreak of War and Treaty Abrogation
The geopolitical tension was exacerbated by the fact that France, having lost provinces to Germany in 1871, became an ally of Russia. This created a situation where a war between Germany and Russia would likely trigger a French attack. While the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria, and Italy) initially seemed stronger than France and Russia, the potential entry of Britain into the conflict shifted the balance of power.

Following the conflict, treaties entered into by Germany since August 1, 1914, with enemy States—including Russia and Rumania—were abrogated. Concessions granted to German subjects under pressure from Russia were likewise annulled.
