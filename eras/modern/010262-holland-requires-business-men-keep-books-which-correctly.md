# Book-keeping and Legislative Requirements

## Definition and Purpose of Book-keeping
Book-keeping is defined as a systematic record of business transactions. These records are maintained by corporations or individuals engaged in financial or commercial operations in a form that is conveniently available for reference. The primary objectives of this practice are to allow the business to ascertain, with minimum dislocation and trouble, the detailed particulars of undertaken transactions and the cumulative effect of these transactions on the business and its financial relations with others.

While sometimes described as an art or a science, book-keeping is viewed as a growth rather than a discovery, evolving over time to meet the changing requirements of business. The ideal system seeks to combine the maximum amount of record with the minimum amount of labor. However, to guard against dishonesty, an adequate system must allow records to be verified as a complete and true statement of transactions. This verification process is known as an audit. In large or public concerns, professional accountants typically perform audits, though a "staff audit" or "internal check" may be performed by the book-keeping staff itself when the staff is large enough to check each other's work.

## General Principles and Methods
All book-keeping methods are based on the same fundamental principles, though their practical development varies by country based on legislative requirements and the manner in which business is conducted. Modern records are generally kept in three distinct columns: the date of the transaction, its nature, and its money value.

The center of all systems is the ledger. Other books, often called "subsidiary books," "books of entry," or "books of first entry," are kept for practical convenience. Transactions are first recorded in these subsidiary books before being classified into the ledger. Books used solely for statistical purposes are referred to as "auxiliary" or "statistical" books.

One prominent method is double-entry book-keeping, which provides a complete record of every business transaction. Because transactions are cross-dealings—where one party receives a benefit and another imparts one—an entry of equal amount is made on both the debit and credit sides of the ledger. This allows for the creation of a "trial balance," where the aggregate of the debit side equals the aggregate of the credit side. However, a trial balance is a mechanical test and does not guarantee absolute accuracy; it cannot detect omitted transactions, records entered into the wrong accounts, incorrect money values, or fictitious entries.

Another variation is "tabular book-keeping," which uses books ruled with numerous columns to reduce clerical work and increase the speed of results. When applied to books of first entry, it allows one book to handle multiple classes of transactions (such as separate columns for different trading departments). When applied to ledgers, it allows a large number of personal accounts to be recorded on a single opening if the entries for each individual are few.

## Legislative Requirements in Europe
Different states impose various statutory requirements on how business records must be maintained:

*   **Holland:** The law requires business men to prepare an annual balance sheet and keep books that correctly record their letters received, copies of letters sent, and commercial transactions.
*   **France:** The Code of Commerce requires traders to keep a letter book, an inventory, and a journal. The compulsory nature of the journal can lead to an undesirable amount of duplication if numerous books of first-entry are used, which may leave book-keeping methods in a comparatively backward state. The inventory book contains the balance sheet and periodical lists of ledger balances.
*   **Germany:** Requirements are similar to France, but the journal is not compulsory. Instead, accounts must be kept in bound books with consecutively numbered pages, which creates legal uncertainty regarding the use of loose-leaf or card ledgers. A balance sheet is required annually, though inventories may be taken every two years if the stock-in-trade is difficult to take due to its size or nature.
*   **Belgium:** Merchants must keep a balance book and a journal recording daily transactions, both of which must be initialed by a prescribed officer. Additionally, copies of all sent letters and telegrams, as well as those received, must be preserved for ten years.
*   **Spain:** The Commercial Code mandates the keeping of a journal, ledger, invoice book, letter book, and an inventory.
*   **Portugal:** The law prescribes the use of a journal, ledger, copy-letter book, and a balance book.

## Legal Implications of Record Keeping
In certain jurisdictions, the failure to maintain proper books can have legal consequences. For instance, a committee recommended that if a debtor becomes bankrupt and has failed to keep proper books of account within the two years preceding the bankruptcy without excuse, it should be a punishable offence resulting in imprisonment upon summary conviction. This recommendation included specific limitations: the law must define the required books (following the examples of Germany and France), the liquidated debts must exceed L200, and the debtor must not be punished if the omission was honest, excusable, and did not contribute to the insolvency.
