# Copenhagen

Copenhagen serves as a primary center for academic study, scientific discovery, and literary production in Denmark. The city is home to the university of Copenhagen and various national statistical and meteorological institutions.

## Academic and Scientific Contributions
The university of Copenhagen has been a site of significant scholarly activity across several centuries. In the 17th century, the university hosted prominent figures such as Thomas (1616-1680), who served as professor of mathematics in 1647 and anatomy in 1648, and later became the university's librarian. His son, Erasmus (1625-1698), also held chairs in medicine and mathematics at the institution; while in Copenhagen, Erasmus discovered double refraction in Iceland spar, publishing his findings in 1669. The third generation continued this academic tradition with Caspar Thomeson (1655-1738), who taught anatomy at Copenhagen and is noted for his descriptions of the *glandulae Bartholini* and a duct of the sublingual gland.

In the 19th century, the university continued to produce influential figures, including the American socialist Laurence Gronlund (1846-1899), who graduated from the university of Copenhagen in 1865 before moving to the United States to study law and lecture on socialism.

## Literary and Philosophical Figures
Copenhagen has been the residence and professional hub for numerous poets, dramatists, and thinkers. 

### Poets and Dramatists
Jens Immanuel Baggesen (1764-1826) entered the university of Copenhagen in 1782. He achieved early popularity at age twenty-one with his *Comical Tales* in verse. After a period of travel and a temporary decline in popularity following the poor reception of his opera *Holge Danske*, he returned to the city in 1806. He resided there until 1820, during which time he engaged in frequent literary feuds, specifically attempting to ensure that the poet Oehlenschlager was not regarded as greater than himself.

Holger Henrik Herboldt Drachmann (1846-1908), the son of a Copenhagen physician, was born in the city on October 9, 1846. Though he initially studied to be a marine painter at the Academy of Fine Arts from 1866 to 1870, he later turned to literature under the influence of Georg Brandes.

Other notable literary figures associated with the city include the dramatist Thomas Overskou (1798-1873), who authored a five-volume history of the Danish theatre published in Copenhagen between 1854 and 1864.

### Philosophy and Prose
The city is associated with Sören Aaby Kierkegaard (1813-1855), described as one of Scandinavia's most subtle thinkers and a philosopher for the romanticists. Other prose writers and historians have also operated within the city; Peter Frederik Suhm (1728-1798) published his eleven-volume *History of Denmark* in Copenhagen between 1782 and 1812.

## Institutional and Administrative Publications
Copenhagen is the site of several major administrative and statistical bureaus. The Statens Statistiske Bureau publishes the *Statistisk Aarbog* (beginning in 1896), and the city is the base for the Danish Meteorological Institution, which produces the *Annuaire météorologique*. Additionally, various historical and statistical works on the Kingdom of Denmark have been published in the city, including J. P. Trap's *Statistisk Topographisk Beskrivelse af Kongeriget Danmark* (1859-1860) and *Le Danemark* (1900), which was published in connection with the Paris Exhibition.
