# Finance

## General Concepts of Budgeting
A budget is an account used to balance income and expenditure for a specific period. The term is derived from a Gallic word meaning sack, which was latinized as *bulga* (leather wallet or bag) and later became *bougette* in Old French before entering English around 1760. Generally, a finance minister prepares this account for a state, though the concept is applied by analogy to smaller bodies.

In the United Kingdom, the chancellor of the exchequer presents a statement to the House of Commons, typically in April, detailing the actual results of the previous finance year ending March 31. This statement compares realized estimates against actual revenue and expenditure to identify a surplus or deficit. Simultaneously, the chancellor provides estimates for the current year's revenue and expenditure, assuming taxes and duties remain unchanged. If estimated revenue is insufficient to cover expenditures, new taxes may be imposed or existing ones increased. Conversely, a large surplus may allow for the reduction or remission of taxes, usually limited to the amount of the previous year's surplus.

The UK administration divides estimates into two categories:
*   **Consolidated fund services:** Including the debt charge, pensions, courts of justice, and the civil list.
*   **Supply services:** Including the navy, army, revenue departments, civil service, and telegraph and post-office services.

## National Finance in Bolivia
In Bolivia, information regarding finance is drawn exclusively from estimates presented to congress by cabinet ministers, as itemized returns of expenditures and receipts are not published. Taxation is not considered heavy, and expenditures are not large.

For the years 1904 and 1905, calculated at 21 pence per boliviano, the figures were:
*   **1904:** Revenue of L632,773:15s. and expenditure of L748,571:10s.
*   **1905:** Revenue of L693,763:17:6 and expenditure of L828,937:19:9.

Revenues are primarily sourced from mining taxes, general taxes, duties and fees on imports, export duties on rubber, coca, and minerals (excluding silver), excise taxes on sugar, tobacco, wines, and spirits, stamp taxes, postal and telegraph revenues, and fees for patents and licences. While Bolivia is sometimes called a "free-trade country," this is considered a misnomer. In addition to *ad valorem* import rates of 10% to 40%, the state levies a 1-1/2% consular fee for invoices over 200 bolivianos, a consumption tax of 10 centavos per quintal (46 kilogrammes), fees for viseing certificates for merchandise in transit, and "octroi" taxes on monopoly-controlled goods like tobacco and spirits. Municipalities and departments also levy consumption and import taxes.

Expenditures are mainly directed toward public works, official salaries, interest on public debt, military expenses, justice, church and mission support, and public instruction. 1905 appropriations included:
*   Government and fomento: 2,021,428 bolivianos
*   War: 2,081,119 bolivianos
*   Justice and public instruction: 1,878,941 bolivianos
*   Finance and industry: 1,462,259 bolivianos

## National Finance in Bulgaria
Prior to its 1908 declaration of independence, the Bulgarian principality began with almost no internal or external debt. Although the Berlin Treaty stipulated a tribute to the sultan and a proportion of the Ottoman Debt, these were not carried out. However, other liabilities emerged:
*   **1883:** A claim from Russia for occupation expenses was set at 26,545,625 fr. (£1,061,820), payable in annual instalments of 2,100,000 fr. (£84,000).
*   **1885:** Union with Eastern Rumelia added a loan of 3,375,000 fr. (£135,000) from the Imperial Ottoman Bank and an annual tribute to Turkey of 2,951,000 fr. (£118,040).

Bulgaria contracted several loans: a 6% loan in 1889 for 30,000,000 fr. (£1,200,000) from the Bankverein and Vienna Länderbank; a 6% loan in 1892 for 142,780,000 fr. (£5,711,200) from the Länderbank; and a 5% loan in 1902 for 106,000,000 fr. (£4,200,000) secured by stamp-tax and tobacco dues. In 1904, another 5% loan of 99,980,000 fr. (£3,999,200) was taken for railway construction and French war material. By January 1906, the national debt included 363,070,500 fr. (£14,522,820) in consolidated loans, 15,603,774 fr. (£624,151) in internal debt, and 1,910,208 (£76,408) in Eastern Rumelian debt.

## Other Regional Financial Data
### Austria and Hungary
The Dual Monarchy maintains a general debt borne jointly by Austria and Hungary, which decreased from 232.41 million sterling in 1875 to 224.31 million sterling in 1905. Joint budget expenditure for 1905 totaled L20,430.3 thousand, with the Ministry of War (Army and Navy) accounting for L18,087.7 thousand. Other 1905 expenditures included the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (L493.8 thousand), the Ministry of Finance (L177.1 thousand), and the Supreme Court of Accounts (L13.3 thousand). Revenue for these departments in 1905 came from proportional contributions (L15,650.4 thousand), customs (L4,799.7 thousand), and other departmental revenue (L331.9 thousand).

### Burma
In the year ending March 31, 1900, Burma's total revenue was Rs.7,04,36,240 with an expenditure of Rs.4,30,81,000. By 1905, revenue rose to Rs.9,65,62,298 and expenditure to Rs.5,66,60,047. Primary revenue sources include excise, forests, customs, railways, and land revenue.

### Other State Finances
In another recorded system, the minister of finance submits the budget to the chambers for passage. In 1903, this state's revenue was 632,416,810 francs and expenditure was 627,975,568 francs. Revenue was derived from loans, return of capital, tolls (including railway traffic), and taxes. Major 1903 expenditures included railways (165,086,019 francs), the service of debt (143,065,352 francs), and war (63,972,473 francs).
