# Pardon and Political Reconciliation in Historical Context

The concept of the pardon has appeared throughout history as a tool for political stabilization, the resolution of rebellion, and the management of personal and state rivalries. From the ancient Mediterranean to the early modern English state, the granting of a pardon often served as a mechanism to transition individuals from a state of enmity or exile back into the fold of government service or civic life.

## Pardons in Ancient Greece and Macedonia

In the ancient world, pardons were frequently used by military and political leaders to consolidate power or demonstrate moderation. During the expansion of Macedonian influence, Alexander, upon succeeding to the throne at age twenty, faced a perilous inheritance characterized by barbarian uprisings and an unstable Greek government. When he marched against Thebes, he offered a free pardon to the citizens if they would join him, provided they surrendered Phœnix and Prothytes.

Similarly, in the Bœotian confederacy, the leader Epaminondas utilized pardon as a strategic tool for long-term ascendency. Following a victory at Leuktra, the Thebans were disposed to destroy the city of Orchomenus and enslave its people. Epaminondas intervened, arguing that for Thebes to achieve ascendency in Greece, it was essential to establish a reputation for moderation. Consequently, the Orchomenians were pardoned upon their submission and readmitted to the Bœotian confederacy.

## The Use of Pardon in the English Reformation

During the 16th century, the English crown used the promise of pardon to quell internal insurrections. Robert Aske, a country gentleman from Yorkshire, led the "Pilgrimage of Grace" in 1536. Aske’s forces occupied York and captured Pontefract Castle, seeking the restitution of the church and the expulsion of low-born counsellors. Despite possessing a force of 30,000 to 40,000 men that threatened the royal forces under the duke of Norfolk, Aske persuaded his followers to disband after accepting the king's pardon and a promise of a parliament at York. Aske himself later traveled to London under a safe-conduct guarantee to provide Henry with a full account of the rising.

## Political Exile and Pardon in the 18th Century

In the 18th century, the process of obtaining a pardon often involved complex negotiations and the influence of court intermediaries. One notable instance involved an individual who, in March 1716, declared his final abandonment of the Pretender to the English government in hopes of a pardon. While he refused to betray secrets or individuals, he promised to use his influence to secure the withdrawal of his friends. 

During his subsequent exile, he resided at the estate of La Source near Orleans, where he studied philosophy and criticized the chronology of the Bible. He eventually received his pardon in 1723 through the mediation of the duchess of Kendal, the king's mistress. Upon returning to London in June or July of that year, he offered his services to Walpole. Although Walpole received his offers to bring Tories into the administration coldly, the individual remained active, gaining information for the government in France during the following winter. By 1725, a peremptory order from the king enabled him to hold real estate, though Walpole successfully maintained his exclusion from the House of Lords.

## Pardons in Other Historical Contexts

Pardons also appeared in the governance of Idumea and Gaza. Costobarus, an Idumean governor appointed by Herod, attempted to transfer his friendship to Cleopatra in hopes of obtaining entire government over the Idumeans. When Herod learned of this attempt, he initially intended to kill Costobarus. However, following the entreaties of his mother and sister, Herod forgave him and granted him a full pardon, although he remained suspicious of him thereafter.
