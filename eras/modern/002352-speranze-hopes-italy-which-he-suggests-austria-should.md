# Speranze (Hopes of Italy)

*Speranze*, or *Hopes of Italy*, is a literary work written by Count Cesare Balbo (1789–1853), an Italian statesman and writer. The text was produced during a period when Balbo, having been denied public service following the revolution of 1821, turned to literature as his primary means of influencing the destiny of his country.

## Purpose and Political Vision
The primary objective of Balbo's writings was to assist in securing Italian independence from foreign control. Balbo did not desire or expect true Italian unity; instead, he was devoted to the house of Savoy, which he believed was destined to change the fate of Italy. His genuine ideal for the region was a confederation of separate states existing under the supremacy of the pope.

## Proposals in Speranze
In *Speranze*, Balbo addressed what he considered the first essential of independence—a point he believed was neglected by Gioberti in the work *Primato*. To achieve this independence, Balbo suggested that Austria should seek compensation in the Balkans to offset the inevitable loss of its Italian provinces. He advocated for a strategy based on patience, alertness, and both moral and military preparation.

## Historical Context of Austrian Influence in Italy
The necessity for the independence Balbo sought stemmed from a long period of Austrian dominance. Following the Treaty of Utrecht, the house of Austria had received Naples and Sardinia, eventually establishing Austrian supremacy in Italy. This dominance continued until the mid-19th century. 

By the time of the congress of Paris, the Sardinian plenipotentiary Cavour had exposed the "scandal" of Austrian rule in Italy to Europe. This diplomatic pressure, combined with a 1859 campaign in which Sardinia allied with France, resulted in Austria losing all its Italian possessions with the exception of Venice. Later, in 1866, following the battle of Sadow where the Prussian army crushed the Austrian army, Italy gained the province of Venetia.

## Post-War Settlements and Relations
Following later conflicts, specific treaties dictated the restitution of materials from Austria to Italy. Austria agreed to deliver various livestock, including 4,000 milch cows, 1,000 heifers, 50 bulls, 1,000 calves, 1,000 bullocks, and 2,000 sows. Additionally, Austria renounced cables touching territories assigned to Italy and agreed to restore official records, documents, and objects of art and antiquity taken from ceded territories, with the period for Italy dating from 1861.

Despite these conflicts, the two nations eventually entered a formal diplomatic relationship. In 1882, Italy adhered to a treaty between Germany and Austria-Hungary, a move prompted by the French occupation of Tunis.
