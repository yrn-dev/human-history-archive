# The Silent Engine: The Role of the Peasantry in the Russian Revolution

While traditional narratives of the 1917 Russian Revolution often focus on the urban proletariat of Petrograd and the ideological maneuvers of Vladimir Lenin, the revolution's success was inextricably linked to the rural landscape. The Russian Empire was an agrarian society; the "workers" were a small minority compared to the millions of peasants who tilled the soil. To understand the rise of communism in Russia, one must look beyond the factory gates and into the village communes (*mirs*).

The peasantry did not necessarily embrace Marxist theory—which viewed the peasantry as a reactionary class—but they were driven by a singular, desperate desire: *Zemlya* (Land).

## The Land Question and the Bolshevik Pivot
For decades, the peasantry had simmered with resentment over the incomplete emancipation of the serfs in 1861, which left many burdened by redemption payments and land-hungry. While the Provisional Government of 1917 hesitated to implement land reform, fearing it would undermine legal stability, the Bolsheviks took a pragmatic, if opportunistic, approach.

Lenin recognized that the urban workers alone could not seize power. By adopting the Socialist Revolutionary (SR) party's slogan of "Land to the Peasants," the Bolsheviks gained a tactical alliance with the rural masses. This was not a marriage of ideological conviction, but one of convenience. The peasants supported the October Revolution not because they wanted a vanguard state, but because they believed it was the only vehicle capable of legitimizing the spontaneous seizure of noble estates.

## The Paradox of War Communism
The relationship between the new communist regime and the peasantry soured rapidly during the Russian Civil War (1917–1922). To feed the cities and the Red Army, the Bolsheviks implemented "War Communism," which included *prodrazvyorstka*—the forced requisitioning of grain.

This policy transformed the peasantry from allies into enemies, sparking widespread rural uprisings and the "Green Armies" (peasant insurgents). This tension highlights a central scholarly debate: whether the subsequent New Economic Policy (NEP) of 1921 was a genuine ideological shift toward a "mixed economy" or a desperate, temporary retreat to prevent the total collapse of the Soviet state.

## Key Facts
* **Demographics:** In 1917, approximately 80% of the Russian population lived in rural areas.
* **The Decree on Land:** Issued shortly after the October Revolution, this abolished private land ownership and redistributed it among peasants.
* **The Green Armies:** Independent peasant militias that fought both the "Red" (Bolshevik) and "White" (Anti-Bolshevik) forces.
* **The NEP:** Introduced by Lenin in 1921, it allowed peasants to sell surplus grain for profit to stabilize the economy.
* **Collectivization:** The later, violent reversal of peasant autonomy under Joseph Stalin in the late 1920s.

## Did You Know?
* **The SRs were more popular:** In the 1917 Constituent Assembly elections, the Socialist Revolutionaries (representing the peasantry) won a landslide victory over the Bolsheviks.
* **Spontaneous Seizures:** Much of the land redistribution happened "from below"; peasants burned manor houses and divided land before the Bolsheviks even issued official decrees.
* **The "Kulak" Label:** The term *Kulak* (meaning "fist") originally referred to wealthy peasants, but was later used by the state as a broad political label to justify mass deportations.
