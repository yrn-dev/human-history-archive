# Christendom and the English Gentleman of Buckinghamshire

## Renegades and the Barbary Pirates
Between 1518 and 1587, the beylerbeys served as admirals to the sultan, commanding large fleets to conduct war for political objectives. During this period, they engaged in ferocious slave-hunting, though the archive notes that their Christian enemies were neither more chivalrous nor more humane. After 1587, the objective of these operations shifted exclusively to plunder, targeting both the sea and native tribes on land.

This maritime brigandage was managed by a class or corporation of captains known as *reises*. Capitalists fitted out cruisers, and ten per cent of the value of captured prizes was paid to the treasury of the pasha, or those holding the titles of Bey, Dey, or Agha. While Bougie served as the primary shipbuilding port, utilizing timber from the surrounding country, the technology of the pirates evolved in the 17th century. A Flemish renegade named Simon Danser introduced the use of sailing ships, replacing the galleys that had previously been used.

The introduction of sailing vessels expanded the reach of the pirates; while galleys were confined to the coast and the Mediterranean, sailing ships ventured into the Atlantic, reaching as far as Iceland or the Canaries. During the 17th century, the primary strength of these pirates was provided by renegades from all parts of Christendom. Among these was an English gentleman from the distinguished Buckinghamshire family of Verney, who spent time with them at Algiers. Algiers was the most formidable port of this trade, to the extent that the term "Algerine" became synonymous with Barbary pirate, though similar activities occurred in Tunis, Tripoli, and Moroccan towns such as Salli. In 1631, a Flemish renegade known as Murad Reis sacked Baltimore in Ireland, taking captives who were later observed in the Algiers slave-market by the French historian Pierre Dan.

## History of Buckinghamshire
At the time of the Norman invasion, Buckinghamshire was likely part of the earldom of Leofwine, son of Godwin. Following the Conquest, the county suffered sweeping confiscations as punishment for the support it provided Leofwine at the battle of Hastings. Due to its proximity to London, the county was involved in many major national events.

During the conflict between King John and his barons, William Mauduit defended Hanslape Castle against the king until it was demolished and captured by Falkes de Bréauté in 1216. The county was later severely impacted by the Black Death, which almost entirely depopulated several districts, including Winslow. During the civil war, Buckinghamshire was among the first counties to join a mutual defence association supporting the parliament, maintaining garrisons at Brill and Aylesbury. Conversely, royalist troops briefly garrisoned Newport Pagnell, and the king established his headquarters at Buckingham in 1644.

## The Earls of Buckinghamshire
The first earl of Buckinghamshire was John Hobart (c. 1694-1756), a descendant of Sir James Hobart (d. 1507) and Sir Henry Hobart (d. 1625). John Hobart was created Baron Hobart of Blickling in 1728 and became earl of Buckinghamshire in 1746; his sister, Henrietta Howard, was the mistress of George II. He was succeeded by his son, John (1723-1793), the 2nd earl, who served as the comptroller of the royal household, ambassador to Russia (1762-1766), and lord-lieutenant of Ireland (1776-1780). Until 1784, the 2nd earl signed himself "Buckingham," leading to some historical confusion.

The 3rd earl was George (c. 1730-1804), the half-brother of the 2nd earl. He was succeeded by his eldest son, Robert Hobart, the 4th earl (1760-1816). Robert Hobart was a soldier and member of both the Irish and English Houses of Commons. He served as chief secretary to the lord-lieutenant of Ireland from 1789 to 1793, where he worked to prevent concessions to Roman Catholics. He later served as governor of Madras (1793-1798) before returning to British politics. He held the position of secretary for war and the colonies from 1801 to 1804—his family name being the source for Hobart Town in Tasmania—and was chancellor of the duchy of Lancaster in 1805. He died in 1816.

The titles then passed to his nephew, George Robert Hobart (1789-1849), the 5th earl. In 1824, he inherited the Hampden family estates and adopted the name Hampden. He was succeeded by his brother, Augustus Edward Hobart (1793-1884), the 6th earl, who took the name Hobart-Hampden in 1878. The 6th earl was succeeded by his grandson, Sidney Carr Hobart-Hampden (b. 1860), the 7th earl, who also added the name Mercer-Henderson.
