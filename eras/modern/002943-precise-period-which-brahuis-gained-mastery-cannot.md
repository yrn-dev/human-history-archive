# The Rise of the Brahuis

## Accession to Power
The precise period during which the Brahuis gained mastery cannot be accurately ascertained, though it is believed to have occurred approximately two and a half centuries ago. The transition of power began when the final raja of the Hindu dynasty was forced to seek assistance from mountain shepherds to defend against a horde of depredators. These attackers were led by an Afghan chief who infested the region and posed a threat to the seat of government.

The leader of the mountain shepherds, Kambar, was engaged to check these encroachments and successfully performed the service. After several years of quelling the robbers, Kambar found himself leading the only military tribe in the country. Consequently, he formally deposed the raja and assumed the government.

## Subsequent Governance and Expansion
The history of the region following Kambar's accession is described as obscure, similar to the period of the Hindu dynasty. Eventually, the sceptre was passed to Abdulla Khan, who was the fourth in descent from Kambar. Characterized as an ambitious and intrepid soldier, Abdulla Khan sought the conquest of Kach Gandava. At that time, Kach Gandava was held by various petty chiefs who operated under the authority of the nawabs of Sind.

## Geography and Demographics of Baluchistan
The total area of Baluchistan is 132,315 square miles. As of 1901, the population was 914,551, with a largely nomadic character. The scale of nomadic movement is illustrated by the annual Brahui migration, during which as many as 15,000 camels have been counted in the Bolan Pass in a single month.

Regarding literacy and religion, 280 out of every thousand inhabitants are literates. The population is overwhelmingly Mahommedan; out of every 100,000 inhabitants, 94,403 are Mussulman and 4,706 are Hindus, with the remainder consisting of Sikhs, Christians, and other denominations.

## Language and Philology
The chief languages spoken in the region include Urdu, Panjabi, Pushtu, Sindhi, and the vernaculars of Baluchistan. The Baluchi language is part of the Iranian branch of the Aryan subfamily of the Indo-European family. It is split into two main dialects that are so distinct that speakers of one are almost unintelligible to speakers of the other. These dialects are separated by Las Bela and the Jalawan and Sarawan hills, which are occupied by Sindhi and Brahui speakers.

The western dialect, also known as Makrani, contains more Persian words and expressions because Makran was under the rule of Persian kings for many generations. In the eastern dialect, words for common acts and objects are nearly all pure Baluchi, while other parts of the language are borrowed from Panjabi, Sindhi, and Persian.

The Brahui dialect's philological classification has been a subject of dispute. However, recent enquiries by Dr. G. A. Grierson place it among the Dravidian languages. This is considered remarkable because the tongue is surrounded by Aryan languages, and its nearest family branch is located far away in the Gond hills of central India.

## Cultural Records
There is no indigenous literature for the Baluchi or Brahui languages. Knowledge of the Brahui language is derived from European scholars, such as Bell. While there is no formal literature, many specimens of poetry exist that commemorate brave deeds and heroes, some of which have been collected over time.
