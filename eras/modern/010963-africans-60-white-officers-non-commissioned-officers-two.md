# West African Military Operations and the Relief of Kumasi

## The Siege and Relief of Kumasi Fort
In a military operation involving the Kumasi fort, a garrison consisting of 100 Hausa soldiers and three white men—Dr. Hay, Mr. Ralph, and Captain Bishop—was left behind with rations intended to last for three weeks. To rescue this garrison, Colonel James Willcocks organized a relief expedition at Cape Coast. Colonel Willcocks, who had arrived at Cape Coast from Nigeria on May 26, faced severe obstacles, including a lack of local food supplies and difficulty obtaining carriers. The environment was further complicated by the height of the rainy season, which left roads in a state of "deep mire" and rendered the bush almost impenetrable. Additionally, the enemy utilized concealed stockades and were described as both cunning and brave.

Colonel Willcocks advanced to Fumsu on July 2. Upon learning of the governor's escape and the dire situation of the Kumasi garrison, he moved to relieve the fort. On July 9, he reached Bekwai, where the local king had remained loyal. To divert the enemy, the colonel spread a report that he would attack Kokofu, east of Bekwai, on July 13. This maneuver successfully drew several thousand enemy forces away from Kumasi. After a feint toward Kokofu, Colonel Willcocks marched west. Following "smart fighting" on July 14 and a march through roads in "indescribably bad condition," the relieving force encountered the main rebel stockade.

## Composition of the Relieving Force
The main rebel stockade was captured at the point of the bayonet by Yoruba troops, who were noted as being fully equal to the Hausa in their performance, with a charge that could not have been beaten in *elan* by any soldiers. The force that entered Kumasi on the evening of July 15 consisted of 1,000 fighting men, all of whom were West Africans. This force was supported by two 75-millimetre guns, six Maxims, four seven-pounder guns, and 60 white non-commissioned officers and officers.

Upon entering the city, a bugler from the war-worn garrison sounded the "general salute." The scene outside the fort was characterized by putrid bodies and houses that had been burnt down, while most of the defenders were too weak to stand.

## Suppression of the Rebellion
Following the relief of Kumasi, the objective shifted to crushing the rebellion. The force under Colonel Willcocks was expanded to a total of 3,368 natives, which included a few Sikhs and Yaos from Central Africa. This force was accompanied by 35 British non-commissioned officers and 134 British officers, as well as Ashanti levies. 

The Kumasi forces were completely defeated at Obassa on September 30. This victory led many rebel chiefs to surrender; the final two remaining in the field were captured on December 28. Peace was restored by the opening of 1901. The campaign resulted in 1,007 total casualties, including those who died from disease. Among the British officers, six died of disease, 43 were wounded, and nine were killed in action. For his leadership, Colonel Willcocks was promoted and created a K.C.M.G.

## Linguistic and Cultural Notes on the Fula
Within the broader African context, the Fula language is noted for its unique characteristics. It is akin to the Hamito-Semitic group in its rudiments and has not yet been placed in any African linguistic family. The language utilizes two grammatical genders—non-human and human—rather than feminine and masculine. It employs suffixes, which suggests non-Negroid influences, and resembles Negro dialects in some respects. 

The people are known by various names depending on the source: the Hausa call them Follani, the Kanuri call them Fellatah, the Arab call them Fullan, and those on the Benue call them Fulde. The Mandingan name is Fulbe or Fula (with the singular being Peul or Pullo). In Kororofa, they are given the name Abate, meaning "white," which, along with other names, appears to refer to their light reddish hue. Reports from 1907 indicate an exodus of thousands of Fulani, primarily Mellawa, from the French Middle Niger, with the majority settling in the Nile valley.
