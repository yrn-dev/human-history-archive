# John Ashburnham

John Ashburnham (c. 1603–1671) was an English Royalist who served as a chief adviser to King Charles I and held several high-ranking positions within the royal administration during the English Civil War.

## Early Life and Career
The son of Sir John Ashburnham of Ashburnham in Sussex, Ashburnham entered the service of the king early in his life. In 1627, he was sent to Paris by a relative, the duke of Buckingham, to make overtures for peace. The following year, he prepared to join an expedition to Rochelle, though this was interrupted by the assassination of the duke. In 1628, Ashburnham was elected as a member of parliament for Hastings—a borough he would later represent in the Long Parliament of 1640—and was appointed groom of the bedchamber. While serving in parliament, he provided services to the king by reporting on the proceedings. During this period, he recovered the Ashburnham estates that had been alienated by his father and acquired a considerable fortune.

## Role in the Civil War
As the conflict with Scotland broke out, Ashburnham attended Charles I at York. During the subsequent Civil War, he was appointed treasurer of the royal army. In this role, he clashed with Hyde, the chancellor of the exchequer, who expressed jealousy and remonstrances because Ashburnham infringed upon his province. In 1644, Ashburnham served as a commissioner at Uxbridge.

Ashburnham maintained the full confidence of the king and became one of his primary advisers. In April 1646, he accompanied Charles I in his flight from Oxford to the Scots. Following this, Ashburnham escaped abroad to join the queen in Paris. He later resided at Rouen and was dispatched to the Hague to seek aid from the prince of Orange.

## Later Service and Controversy
In 1647, after the army seized Charles I, Ashburnham joined the king at Hampton Court. While there, he participated in several conferences with other army officers and Oliver Cromwell. On November 11, when Charles escaped from Hampton Court, Ashburnham provided advice that opposed that of Sir John Berkeley, who had urged the king to go abroad. Following Ashburnham's counsel, the king took refuge in the Isle of Wight, where Ashburnham placed him in the care of Robert Hammond, the governor.

Upon realizing the situation, the king exclaimed, "Oh, Jack, thou hast undone me!" In response, Ashburnham fell into a "great passion of weeping" and offered to kill Hammond. This sequence of events led to Ashburnham facing charges of disloyalty and treachery. While Clarendon censured his conduct, he absolved Ashburnham of any crime other than excessive self-confidence and folly. Ashburnham was ultimately acquitted by both Charles I and Charles II.
