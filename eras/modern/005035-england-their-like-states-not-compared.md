# England and the United States

The historical and systemic developments of England and the United States are characterized by distinct trajectories in governance, economics, arts, and social organization. While England developed through a long tradition of parliamentary rule and specific financial institutions, the United States emerged as a unique community defined by rapid growth and technological integration.

## Governance and Political History
England possesses a tradition of parliamentary rule and the Magna Carta, elements that were absent in other European nations such as France. Following the establishment of the Hanoverians, the country was ruled by the House of Lords and the subservient Commons. This political structure was shaped by internal conflict, including a civil war from 1643 to 1649. This conflict was essentially a struggle between the Crown—which was either Catholic or inclined toward Catholicism—and recalcitrant nobles and rich merchants who favored a Protestant formula. In England, these Protestant nobles and merchants ultimately triumphed.

In contrast, the United States is described as a process without precedent in world history. Its existence as a unified community was made possible by the advent of the telegraph and railways; without these, the administration of distant territories like California from Washington would have been more difficult than if they were administered from Pekin. These technologies have woven the United States into a vast unity, promoting a level of assimilation where citizens of disparate regions, such as San Francisco and New York, have become increasingly uniform in their thinking, speaking, and acting.

## Financial and Economic Systems
The Bank of England employs specific measures to maintain the integrity of its currency. Its notes are printed from surface-plates in black, lacking color or special protection aside from a watermark in the paper. To prevent forgery, the bank never reissues notes once they are returned, resulting in a short average lifespan of approximately six weeks. The comparative immunity from forgery is further attributed to the difficulty of negotiating forged notes, as the lowest denomination is L5. In financial circles, the "bank rate" is used to designate the rate of discount charged by leading banks or the state, as opposed to the open-market rate.

Historically, by the opening of the eighteenth century, English writers noted a comparative prosperity among the English poor when compared to the misery of the lower classes in France.

## Arts and Illustration
In the United States, the field of illustration was long dominated by the excellence of the engraver over the artist. A shift occurred only after the black-and-white artists of England were influenced by the brilliant pen-drawing of E. A. Abbey. Following this, several American illustrators gained prominence, including Howard Pyle, a brilliant imitator of Dürer, and C. S. Reinhart, who utilized styles based on German and French examples. Other notable figures include Alfred Brennan, W. T. Smedley, F. S. Church, R. Blum, Wenzell, and A. B. Frost. C. Dana Gibson, in particular, achieved a reputation in England as an American du Maurier. Many of these artists' works appeared in publications such as the *Century Magazine*, *Harper's*, and *Scribner's*.

## Sports and Recreation
Horse racing highlights a technical divergence between the two nations. In the United States, the majority of flat racing is conducted on "dirt-tracks"—courses with specially prepared soil—which are a legacy of harness-racing. While these tracks allow for great speed, they place extreme strain on the horses' legs. Turf courses exist, such as at Sheepshead Bay, but are used for a minority of races.

Steeplechases in the United States are run on turf. Under the administration of the National Steeplechase and Hunt Association, regulation courses have been insisted upon in the east that are practically similar in every respect to those used in England, including the "Liverpool" open ditch.

## International Relations and Influence
England has historically positioned itself as a supporter of Christian States in the Balkans. During periods of struggle, England has used its wealth and power to promote the union of Balkan peoples, such as the union that triumphed in the first Balkan War, asserting that it has no interests of its own to seek in the Balkan Peninsula.

In the realm of literature, the influence of Chaucer was wide and lasting in England and Scotland throughout the century. His work was imitated by court-poetry and writers seeking refinement. In England, his three primary followers were Hawes, Hoccleve, and Lydgate. While Lydgate was frequently praised as Chaucer's equal or superior, Hoccleve is noted for being more original and humorous, though he received less attention.
