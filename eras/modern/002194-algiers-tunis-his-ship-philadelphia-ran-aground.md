# Algiers and Tunis

Algiers and Tunis are North African cities that historically served as centers of maritime activity, governance, and piracy. In antiquity, both cities, along with Morocco, Sardinia, Corsica, and most of Sicily and Spain, were under the dominion of Carthage during its period as a republican empire.

## Rise of the Barbary States
The modern history of these cities was significantly shaped by the events following the 1492 conquest of Granada by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain. This event drove many Moors into exile, who subsequently launched piratical attacks against the Spanish coast. These efforts were bolstered by Muslim adventurers from the Levant, most notably the brothers Arouj and Khair-ed-Din, known to Christians as "Barbarossa" or "Redbeard."

Arouj and Khair-ed-Din initially operated as mercenaries among Berber princes and conducted raids on Christian trade. After Arouj fell in battle against the Spaniards in 1518, Khair-ed-Din sought assistance from the sultan of Turkey, Selim. The sultan provided troops, enabling Khair-ed-Din to drive the Spaniards from a fort on a rocky island in front of Algiers in 1529, thereby establishing Turkish power in the region.

## Governance and Administration
From 1518 until 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. During this initial period, the beylerbeys acted as admirals for the sultan, commanding large fleets for political warfare. While they were ferocious slave-hunters, the archive notes that their Christian enemies were neither more chivalrous nor more humane.

Following 1587, the administrative structure changed. Until 1659, the region was governed by Turkish pashas sent from Constantinople for three-year terms. However, a military revolt in Algiers in 1659 reduced the pashas to nonentities. From that point forward, Algiers and Tunis became nominally part of the Turkish empire but functioned in reality as anarchical military republics. These republics chose their own rulers—who bore titles such as Bey, Dey, or Agha—and sustained themselves through plunder.

## Maritime Piracy and Trade
The maritime activities of Algiers and Tunis were conducted by a class or corporation of captains known as *reises*. These cruisers were funded by capitalists, and the treasury of the ruling official received ten percent of the value of all prizes. Bougie served as the primary shipbuilding port, utilizing timber from the surrounding countryside.

The nature of their naval warfare evolved in the 17th century when a Flemish renegade named Simon Danser introduced sailing ships, replacing the traditional galleys. This transition allowed the pirates to move beyond the Mediterranean and the coast, ranging into the Atlantic as far as Iceland or the Canaries. In 1631, a Flemish renegade called Murad Reis sacked Baltimore in Ireland, and captives from this raid were later observed in the slave market of Algiers.

Because Algiers was the most formidable of these ports, the term "Algerine" became synonymous with Barbary pirate, although Tunis and Tripoli—as well as towns in the empire of Morocco, such as Salli—carried on similar trade.

## International Conflict and Suppression
By the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the "pirate Barbary states" continued to commit outrages against merchant shipping. The United States initially paid tribute to the dey of Algiers to secure exemption from capture. When bribery failed to stop these attacks, the United States employed force. During this conflict, Commodore William Bainbridge served against Tunis and Algiers; his ship, the *Philadelphia*, ran aground on the Tunisian coast, leading to his temporary imprisonment.

Following the general pacification of 1815, European powers viewed the suppression of African piracy and the large-scale slave trade practiced by the Barbary states as a necessity. This objective was included in the Congress of Vienna. In 1816, Lord Exmouth was dispatched by Great Britain to obtain treaties from Algiers and Tunis. After British subjects were brutally ill-treated at Bona, Lord Exmouth, combined with a Dutch squadron under Admiral Van de Capellen, conducted a "smashing bombardment" of Algiers on August 27.

This action forced the pirates of Tunis and Algiers to release over 3,000 prisoners. However, Algiers renewed its slave-taking and piracy, leading to further discussions at the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818 and another British bombardment under Admiral Sir Harry Neal in 1824. Algiers was not fully tamed until its conquest by France in 1830.
