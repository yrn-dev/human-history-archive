# C sec [eta] [I(U) - {A(U) - A(u)}/{S(U) - S(u)}]

The expression $C \sec [\eta] [I(U) - \{A(U) - A(u)\}/\{S(U) - S(u)\}]$ is a mathematical formula used in the calculation of trajectories, specifically within the framework of Siacci's method for high angle fire. It is used to determine the ratio $y/x$ (the altitude divided by the horizontal distance) of a trajectory.

## Theoretical Context and Siacci's Method
In the study of trajectories, equations connecting variables such as $q$ and $i$ often result in integrations that are considered intractable, even when simple mathematical assumptions are made for the function $f(v)$ (such as quadratic or cubic laws where $f(v) = v^2/k$ or $v^3/k$). To resolve this, a method originally pointed out by Euler is employed: in ordinary trajectories, the quantities $i$, $\cos i$, and $\sec i$ vary slowly enough that they can be replaced by their mean values, denoted as $[\eta]$, $\cos [\eta]$, and $\sec [\eta]$.

This substitution is particularly effective if a considerable trajectory is divided into arcs of small curvature. The curvature of such an arc is defined as the angle between the normals or tangents at the ends of the arc. By replacing the angle $i$ with the mean value $[\eta]$, Siacci's pseudo-velocity $u$ is introduced, defined as $u = q \sec [\eta]$. This pseudo-velocity acts as a quasi-component parallel to the mean direction of the tangent, such as the direction of the arc's chord.

## The Altitude-Function and Trajectory Calculation
The mean value $[\eta]$ represents the inclination of the chord of the trajectory arc. While Niven's method also uses $[\eta]$, it requires high accuracy because a 1% variation in $[\eta]$ can cause more than a 1% variation in $\tan [\eta]$. Siacci's method avoids this difficulty through the use of the altitude-function $A$ or $A(u)$. 

In this system, $y/x$ can be calculated without introducing $\sin [\eta]$ or $\tan [\eta]$. Instead, $[\eta]$ appears only as $\cos [\eta]$ or $\sec [\eta]$, which vary slowly for moderate values of $[\eta]$. Consequently, $[\eta]$ does not need to be calculated with extreme accuracy; the arithmetic mean of the angles $[\phi]$ and $[\theta]$ (where $[\eta] = 1/2([\phi] + [\theta])$) is sufficient for any arc of moderate extent.

The altitude-function $A$ is calculated either by integration—when it is legitimate to assume $f(v) = v^m/k$ over a velocity interval where $m$ is constant—or by summation from the finite difference $\Delta A$, where $\Delta A = I(u) u \Delta u / gp = I(u) \Delta S$.

## Application to High Angle Fire
When applying this method to high angle fire, the trajectory is calculated using successive arcs of small curvature. Starting with an initial angle $[\phi]$ and velocity $v_{[\phi]}$, the following steps are taken:
1. The curvature of the arc $[\phi] - [\theta]$ is determined.
2. A first approximation for $[\eta]$ is established as $1/2([\phi] + [\theta])$.
3. The initial pseudo-velocity $u_{[\phi]}$ is calculated using $u_{[\phi]} = v_{[\phi]} \cos [\phi] \sec [\eta]$.
4. The final pseudo-velocity $u_{[\theta]}$ is calculated using formulas involving $I(u)$ or $D(u)$.

The specific formula $\tan [\phi] - y/x = C \sec [\eta] [I(U) - \{A(U) - A(u)\}/\{S(U) - S(u)\}]$ allows for the calculation of $y/x$, and subsequently $y$.

## Considerations for Long Range and Direct Fire
In long range high angle fire, the tenuity of the air becomes a significant factor as the shot reaches greater heights. To account for this, the curvature of an arc is chosen so that the height ascended ($\Delta y$) is limited to approximately 1000 ft, which corresponds to a 3% diminution in the tenuity factor $[\tau]$ or a 1-inch fall in the barometer. Captain James M. Ingalls provided a rule for approximating high angle trajectories in a single arc by assuming the mean density of air is the density at two-thirds of the estimated height of the vertex.

In contrast, for direct fire, the pseudo-velocities $U$ and $u$ are undistinguishable from the real velocities $V$ and $v$. In these instances, $\sec [\eta]$ is replaced by unity. When $y = 0$, the formula simplifies to $\tan [\phi] = C [I(V) - \Delta A / \Delta S]$.
