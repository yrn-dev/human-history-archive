# Karl Friedrich Bahrdt

Karl Friedrich Bahrdt (1741–1792) was a German adventurer and theologian known for his fluctuating religious views, literary activity, and a career marked by professional instability and personal scandal.

## Early Life and Education
Born on August 25, 1741, in Bischofswerda, Bahrdt was the son of a pastor who later served as a canon, professor, and general superintendent at Leipzig. Described as a precocious youth whose formal training had been grossly neglected, Bahrdt began studying theology at the age of sixteen under the orthodox mystic Christian August Crusius, who had become the first professor in the theological faculty in 1757. During this period, Bahrdt exhibited an unbalanced character, engaging in pranks such as an attempt to raise spirits using *Dr Faust's Hoellenzwang*.

## Academic Career and Professional Decline
Despite his personal conduct, Bahrdt's talent was conspicuous and his orthodoxy was considered unimpeachable. This led to his appointment in 1761 as a preacher (*Katechet*) at the church of St Peter and a lecturer on biblical exegesis. His reputation for eloquence resulted in his appointment as professor extraordinarius of biblical philology in 1766. However, he was dismissed two years later due to scandals in his private life.

Bahrdt subsequently obtained a chair of biblical antiquities in the philosophical faculty at Erfurt. Because this position was unpaid, he supported himself and his wife by keeping an inn and taking pupils. During this time, he earned a doctor of theology degree from Erlangen and persuaded the authorities at Erfurt to name him professor designate of theology. His tenure there was short-lived; his truculent and coarse character, combined with financial difficulties, led him to leave for Giessen in 1771, where he served as a preacher and professor of theology.

## Religious Shift and Expulsion
While at Giessen, Bahrdt's behavior remained objectionable. He eventually abandoned his previous orthodoxy, changing his public attitude toward religion. This shift culminated in the publication of *Neueste Offenbarungen Gottes in Briefen und Erzaehlungen* (1773–1775), which he presented as a "model version" of the New Testament translated into modern German with regard to enlightenment. The work was attacked by Goethe for its lack of taste and resulted in Bahrdt's expulsion from Giessen.

Following his expulsion, Bahrdt became the principal of an educational institution in his chateau at Marschlins. The school had been established by the Swiss statesman Ulysses von Salis (1728–1800) and had languished after the death of its first head, Martin Planta (1727–1772). Von Salis attempted to revive the school as a "Philanthropin" under Bahrdt's management. The experiment failed because Bahrdt was uncomfortable with the strict discipline maintained by von Salis; Bahrdt resigned in 1777, and the school closed.

## Later Years and Literary Activity
At the invitation of the count of Leiningen-Dachsburg, Bahrdt moved to Duerkheim on the Hardt as general superintendent. However, his translation of the Testament continued to cause issues, and in 1778, the high court of the Empire suspended him.

In 1779, facing dire poverty, Bahrdt fled to Halle. Although the theologians and the senate opposed him, the Prussian minister von Zedlitz secured him permission to lecture on non-theological subjects. To earn a living, Bahrdt turned to prolific writing. Having completely discarded his orthodoxy, he dedicated his efforts to promoting a "moral system" intended to replace supernatural Christianity.

Bahrdt continued to maintain himself until the death of Frederick the Great, after which a religious reaction occurred at the Berlin court. Due to the strain of his writing, he stopped lecturing and opened another inn on the Weinberg near Halle. He lived there in "disreputable peace" with his daughters and a mistress, having repudiated his wife. In 1789, he was sentenced to one year of imprisonment for writing a lampoon regarding the Prussian religious edict of 1788. During his imprisonment, he wrote coarse polemics, indecent stories, and an autobiography characterized as a mixture of hypocrisy, lies, and "self-prostitution."

Karl Friedrich Bahrdt died on April 23, 1792.
