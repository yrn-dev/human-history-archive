# Venezuela and the British-German Crisis

## Overview of the Crisis
During the early 20th century, a diplomatic and political difficulty arose involving Venezuela, described as a refractory republic. This situation led to a period of co-operation between Great Britain and Germany, as both powers sought to coerce the republic. The crisis had significant repercussions within England, where it triggered an explosion of anti-German sentiment, and caused a degree of restlessness in the United States.

## Resolution and Political Context
The British government managed to bring the crisis to a conclusion through a combination of tactful handling and an ultimate recourse to arbitration. This event occurred during the early tenure of a new prime minister who took power nearly simultaneously with the coronation of King Edward VII and the conclusion of the South African War. 

At the time, the prime minister enjoyed the enthusiastic support of a large parliamentary majority and maintained cordial relations with Mr. Chamberlain. While the Venezuela crisis created temporary tension, the government's primary legislative focus remained on other priorities, including the extension of the new Education Act to London and Mr. Wyndham's Irish Land Purchase Act, the latter of which utilized the British exchequer to provide capital for Irish tenants to buy out their landlords.

## Historical and Geographic Context of Venezuela
The region of Venezuela—a name meaning "little Venice" derived from an Indian village built on piles over shallow water—includes the state of Falcon and its capital, Coro. Founded in 1527 by Juan de Ampues as Santa Ana de Coriana, Coro is situated on a sandy plain between the Gulf of Venezuela and the Caribbean. 

Historically, Coro served as a significant center for German interests; it was the chief factory of the Welsers, a German banking house to which Charles V mortgaged this portion of his colonial possessions. From this location, many colonizing and exploring expeditions were launched into the interior. By 1904, Coro remained a commercial center for a district on the east side of the Gulf of Venezuela and Lake Maracaibo, exporting commodities such as tobacco, goat-skins, coffee, cacao, timber, dyewoods, and castor beans.
