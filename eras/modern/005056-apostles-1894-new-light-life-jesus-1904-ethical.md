# Apostles and Early Christian Development

The study of the apostles and the early Christian era involves an analysis of the transition from the original teachings of Jesus to the established theological systems of the early Church. This development is characterized by a shift from the practices of the original disciples to the intellectual and theological frameworks later developed by leaders such as Paul.

## The Original Apostles and the Judaistic Period
According to the reconstructions of Ferdinand Christian Baur, a professor of theology at Tuebingen from 1826 to 1860, the earliest form of Christianity—associated with Jesus and the original apostles—was entirely Judaistic in its practice and tone. Baur identified John, the son of Zebedee, as one of the leaders of this Judaistic party and attributed the Apocalypse to him as a genuine work. 

In this early period, the faith of the Nazarenes was characterized as a "doctrine of motive" and a "way of living." However, historical analysis of the gospels suggests a gap between these early practices and later Christian doctrines. There is little support in the gospels for the theological assertions generally deemed necessary for salvation by various Christian denominations. Specifically, the texts provide no clear evidence that Jesus claimed to be part of the godhead or the Jewish Messiah (the Christ), nor did he explain the doctrine of the Atonement or mandate sacraments and sacrifices. Furthermore, there is no evidence that the apostles ever heard of the Trinity from Jesus.

## The Role and Mission of Paul
Paul never met Jesus personally; his understanding of Jesus' teachings was derived from the hearsay of the original disciples. While Paul apprehended the spirit of Jesus and the doctrine of a new birth, he transformed these elements into a subtle and ingenious theological system. Through this process, the "doctrine of motive" practiced by the Nazarenes was converted into a "doctrine of belief," and the spirit and hope of the early followers were replaced by the beginnings of a creed.

Paul was a figure of enormous energy who conducted his mission and teaching in several major cities, including Jerusalem, Antioch, Athens, Corinth, Ephesus, and Rome. Baur’s research, based on the genuine letters of Paul to his converts (specifically Galatians and I Corinthians, as well as Romans and 2 Corinthians), suggests that Paul was the first to realize that Judaism was no longer binding for Christians.

## Conflict and the Formation of the Catholic Church
The divergence between Paul's vision and the Judaistic approach of the original apostles led to a long and fierce controversy. This conflict between the "Judaizing disciples" of the apostles and the followers of Paul persisted until the early 2nd century. The dispute was eventually resolved when the two factions coalesced to form the Catholic Church, at which point the strife was forgotten.

This historical conflict serves as a primary tool for dating New Testament documents. For instance, Baur posited that the Acts of the Apostles was written around A.D. 140, after the memory of the controversy had faded. Similarly, the four Gospels are placed in the 2nd century, although the Gospel of Matthew is noted for retaining many unaltered features from its Judaistic original.

## Divergence in Worship and Practice
The transition from the teachings of Jesus to later Christian cults involved significant changes in usage and worship. While many Christian cults adopted the observance of the Sabbath (later transferred to the Mithraic Sun-day), the records indicate that Jesus deliberately broke the Sabbath, stating it was made for man rather than man for the Sabbath. Additionally, Jesus did not mention the worship of his mother, Mary, in the form of Isis, the Queen of Heaven. Much of what became characteristic of Christian worship and usage was ignored by Jesus himself.
