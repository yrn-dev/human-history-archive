## French Diplomacy and the First Consul
In March 1802, France concluded the treaty of Amiens on March 25, following lengthy discussions between the British envoy, Lord Cornwallis, and French representatives Maret and an unnamed diplomat who had also presided over the treaty of Luneville with Austria in 1801. This diplomatic success contributed to the consolidation of Napoleon's power. On August 1, 1802, Napoleon became First Consul for life, a position that granted him the chief voice in selecting his successor.

This power led to a disagreement between Napoleon and his eldest brother, Joseph. Because neither had a male heir, Joseph claimed the succession based on strict ideas of primogeniture. Napoleon, however, preferred to recognize the son of Louis Bonaparte. This friction intensified by May 1804 with the proclamation of the French empire; Napoleon offered Joseph the kingship of Lombardy in exchange for waiving his claim to the French throne, but Joseph refused.

## British Legal and Political Figures
Several notable British figures died in 1802. Lloyd Kenyon, 1st Baron, died on April 4, 1802, in Bath. Kenyon had served as the lord chief-justice of England, a position he assumed in 1788 as the successor to Lord Mansfield. Though described as having a capricious and choleric temper and a lack of tact as an advocate, he was regarded on the bench as a judge of rare and inflexible impartiality. He had previously served as attorney-general in Lord Buckingham's administration and as master of the rolls.

Isaac Barre, a British politician and soldier, died on July 20, 1802. Barre had been a member of parliament since 1761 and was a vigorous champion of the American cause, coining the term "Sons of Liberty" to describe American organizations opposing the Stamp Act. He had served as treasurer of the navy in 1782 before retiring in 1790 due to blindness.
