# Early College Base-Ball Leagues

## Formation of the First College League
Organized amateur base-ball was primarily played by athletic associations as well as university and school clubs. In 1879, the first college league was established. This initial organization consisted of Brown, Amherst, Dartmouth, Princeton, and Harvard. Yale joined this league one year later.

## The Eastern College League
In 1887, a subsequent organization known as the Eastern College League was formed. This league included Yale, Princeton, Harvard, and Columbia. This organization was later dissolved. Currently, while organizations exist in different parts of the south, New England, and the west, the most important universities located in the eastern states are not members of any league.

## Rules and Development
The progression of amateur base-ball followed similar lines to those of professional base-ball. However, there were previously certain minor points where the rules for college play differed from the rules used in professional leagues.

## Associated Institutions
Several of the institutions involved in these early leagues have distinct histories. Dartmouth College, for example, originated from Moor's Indian charity school, founded around 1750 by Eleazer Wheelock in Lebanon, Connecticut. The school was named in 1755 for Joshua Moor, who provided buildings and lands. After Samson Occom, a former student and Indian preacher, raised £10,000 in Scotland and England in 1765, the school planned for enlargement and a move to Hanover. It was incorporated as Dartmouth College in 1769 by a charter from George III, named for the president of the trustees of the British funds, the earl of Dartmouth.

The institution's first building, Dartmouth Hall, was constructed between 1784 and 1791; it closely resembles the University Hall of Brown University and Nassau Hall at Princetown. Other standing structures include Moor Hall (called the Chandler building since 1852) and a college church built in 1796.

## Yale University
Yale also underwent significant development during the late 19th century. Under the administration of Timothy Dwight, who served as president from 1886 to 1899, the institution prospered and became a university in both name and fact. During this era, Yale was home to various distinguished academics, including James Dwight Dana, who served as the Silliman Professor of Natural History and Geology from 1850 to 1892, and Charles Emerson Beecher, who was appointed professor of palaeontology in 1892.
