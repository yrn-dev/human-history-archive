# Pacific Islands

The Pacific Ocean is bordered by a diverse array of island groups, ranging from large archipelagos and remnants of mountain ranges to small, hilly island clusters. These islands play significant roles in global geography, biodiversity, and the strategic control of maritime routes.

## Geography and Physical Features

In the North Pacific, the coast of British Columbia is characterized by the Coast Range (also known as the Cascade Range), which borders the Pacific for 900 miles. This region features a coastline of 7,000 miles in aggregate, defined by deep sounds, inlets, and fjords. This coast is fringed by an archipelago of numerous islands, the largest of which are the Queen Charlotte Islands and Vancouver Island. These two islands are remnants of a mountain range that runs parallel to the coast but is now almost entirely submerged.

In the South Pacific, the Kermadec Islands form a small group of hilly islands located at approximately 30° S., 178° W. The largest island in this group is Sunday Island (also called Raoul), which is 1,600 feet high, thickly wooded, and has a circumference of 20 miles.

## Strategic Maritime Access

In the Far East, the Japanese empire controls the primary avenues from the Pacific Ocean to the Sea of Japan. The Sea of Japan has only two routes communicating with the Pacific: the Tsugaru Strait in the north and a channel in the south between the Japanese island of Kyushu and the Korean peninsula. While Japan maintains complete control of the Tsugaru Strait, the southern channel—which is 102 miles wide—is narrowed by the islands of Iki and the twin islands of Tsushima. Because Tsushima and Iki have belonged to Japan from time immemorial, access to the Pacific from Russia's Maritime Province and the eastern and southern coasts of Korea depends upon Japanese good-will.

## Flora, Fauna, and Natural Resources

The Pacific islands support various species of wildlife, most notably sea-bears and sea-lions. These gregarious and polygamous animals are widely distributed in temperate regions. In the North Pacific, the northern sea-lion (_O. stelleri_) can reach 10 feet in length, while the sea-bear or fur-seal (_O. ursina_) is found in the North Pacific, with skins imported in large numbers from the Pribiloff Islands. Other species, such as _O. forsteri_, are found in Australia and various southern hemisphere islands.

The fur-seal skins from the North Pacific—specifically from Japan, Robben Island, the north-west coast of America, the Pribilof Islands, and the Copper Island of the Aleutian group—are considered the best sort. In contrast, the fur of southern sea seals, found around the South Pacific and South Atlantic, is generally poor and suited only for the cheapest markets, with the exception of the pick of the Lobos Island seals.

Regarding flora and fauna, the Kermadec Islands possess species that largely belong to those of New Zealand.

## Political Administration and Possessions

Many islands in the Pacific have been under British administration or possession. The Kermadec Islands, named in 1791 after Captain Huon Kermadec, were annexed in 1887 and are politically dependent on the colony of New Zealand. Fiji was acquired via cession in 1874. In 1877, a High Commission was created to provide jurisdiction over the Western Pacific Islands, including the Gilbert, Ellice, and Southern Solomon groups, as well as other islands not under the jurisdiction of other colonial governments or civilized powers. By 1900, protectorates were established over all these islands.
