# The Great Depression: The Decade of Economic Despair

## Overview
The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world, lasting roughly from 1929 to 1939. While the 1929 stock market crash is often cited as the catalyst, historians and economists argue that the collapse was the result of a "perfect storm" of systemic vulnerabilities. These included agricultural overproduction, unsustainable consumer debt fueled by installment buying, and a fragile international banking system still reeling from the aftermath of World War I.

The crisis was characterized by a catastrophic spiral: as banks failed, the money supply contracted, leading to massive unemployment (reaching nearly 25% in the United States) and a collapse in global trade. In the U.S., President Franklin D. Roosevelt attempted to stabilize the economy through the "New Deal," a series of federal programs aimed at relief, recovery, and reform. However, a central debate persists among historians regarding the New Deal's efficacy; some argue it saved American capitalism from total collapse, while others, such as those from the Austrian School of economics, suggest that government intervention actually prolonged the depression by preventing natural market corrections.

## Key Facts
*   **Banking Collapse:** Thousands of banks failed during the early 1930s because they had invested depositors' money in the stock market or issued loans that could not be repaid.
*   **The Dust Bowl:** An environmental disaster in the Great Plains, caused by severe drought and poor farming practices, displaced millions of "Okies" and "Arkies."
*   **Deflationary Spiral:** Prices for goods dropped sharply, which sounds positive but actually led businesses to cut wages and fire workers because their products were worth less.
*   **Global Reach:** The crisis was not limited to the U.S.; Germany was hit particularly hard, which contributed to the political instability that allowed the rise of the Nazi Party.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Gold Standard:** Many economists, including Ben Bernanke, argue that the adherence to the Gold Standard forced central banks to keep interest rates high, which strangled economic growth.
2. **Hoovervilles:** Shanty towns built by homeless people were named "Hoovervilles" after President Herbert Hoover, whom the public blamed for the crisis.
3. **The GDP Drop:** Between 1929 and 1933, the U.S. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) fell by approximately 30%.
