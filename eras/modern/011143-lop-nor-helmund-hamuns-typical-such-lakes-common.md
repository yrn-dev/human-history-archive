# Lop Nor and the Helmund Hamuns

The lakes of Asia vary significantly in scale, ranging from highland lochs and the expansive swamps of Persia to inland seas such as Lakes Balkash and Baikal. Among these, Lop Nor and the Helmund *hamuns* serve as typical examples of lakes that are relics of an earlier geological period. These specific bodies of water formed when land areas, recently upheaved from the sea, were spread at low levels and experienced alternating inundations of fresh and salt water.

## Characteristics of Hamuns and Salt Depressions
The Helmund *hamuns*, along with all plateau *hamuns* found in Persia and south-west Baluchistan, are characterized by their instability; they change their extent and form from season to season. Many of these lakes are impregnated with saline deposits originating from the underlying strata. In the Persian desert, these features are often accompanied by *kavirs*, or salt depressions, which more frequently consist of widespread deposits of salt and mud rather than areas covered by water.

## Regional Geography and Hydrography
The Asian continent can be divided into three sections based on altitude and drainage. The northern section, averaging less than 5,000 ft. above sea level, drains toward the Arctic Sea, the Caspian, or the Mediterranean. The southern section consists of lowlands in the Chinese, Siamese, Indian, and Arabian peninsulas, as well as highly elevated table-lands reaching 15,000 to 16,000 ft. 

Between these lies a central section of depression with an average level likely not exceeding 2,000 ft. above sea level, where drainage is lost in *hamuns* or swamps. This great central depression extends from the Tarim desert and Lop Nor through to the Gobi and the foot of the Pamir plateau. A significant geographic feature of this region is the divide separating the Helmund *hamun* basin from the valley of the Hari Rud; this divide, represented by an altitude of approximately 4,000 ft. above sea level, is the only orographic obstacle between Karachi and the Caspian.

## The Lop Nor System
Lop Nor is located within the great central depression of Asia. Research by Sven Hedin revealed the "remarkable phenomenon" of the periodic shifting of the Lop Nor system. The region has also been the subject of study by Dr. M.A. Stein, who demonstrated that the sand-strewn wastes of the Turkestan desert once housed highly civilized centers of industry and Buddhist art.

The exact location and nature of Lop Nor have been subjects of historical and geographical controversy:
* **Exploration:** The Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky visited Lop Nor in 1876 and later crossed the Altyn Tagh to reach it during a journey between 1883 and 1885. Prjevalsky identified the lake's water as sweet, though he placed the lake one degree further south than Chinese topographers did.
* **Geographical Debates:** Baron von Richthofen disagreed with Prjevalsky, arguing that Prjevalsky had followed a "new" river course to sweet-water lakes (one of which corresponds to the ancient Khas-omo) and had missed the true Lop-noor. 
* **Formation of New Lakes:** Grenard discussed the formation of four new lakes created by the Koncheh-daria: Tokum Kul, Sugut Kul, Tayek Kul, and Kara Kul.

## Historical and Urban Context of Lop
While maps based on Chinese authority identify the lake, information regarding a town named Lop is less consistent. However, the *Táríkh-i-Rashídí* (History of Mirza Haidar) notes that the plain of Eastern Turkestan formerly contained several large cities, including Lob and Kank, though most are now buried under sand. Other accounts describe Lopp as being situated a great distance from Yarkand, inhabited primarily by Chinese and some Uzbeks, and noted for a nearby salt-water lake.

Historically, Lop was considered a meeting point for roads leading to Khotan, L’Hasa, Shachau, Urumtsi, and Kashgar. This position gave the town relative importance despite being situated in a poor country. Some theories suggest Lop may have been built on the site of Charkalyk, while others link it to the *Navapa* (or *Napopo*) mentioned by Hiuen Tsang, also known as the country of *Leulan*.

## Political and Transport Boundaries
In the region of the Helmund, political boundaries have been shaped by the terrain. The boundary between Afghanistan and Baluchistan runs through the desert south of the Gaud-i-Zirreh salt swamps. This line passes 50 miles south of the Helmund, effectively shutting off the approach to Seistan from Baluchistan during seasons of flood, except for the valley between the Helmund and the Gaud-i-Zirreh. Consequently, a desert route between Nushki and Seistan has been developed with the digging of wells and the establishment of *serais* (posts) to support *khafila* traffic.
