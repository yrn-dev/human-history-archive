# The Struggle Within the Unionist Party (1903)

During the latter part of 1903, the Unionist party was characterized by an intense internal struggle centered on the issue of fiscal policy. This conflict pitted the advocates of "Tariff Reform" against the proponents of free trade, creating a deep division that threatened the stability of the government.

## The Campaign for Tariff Reform
The movement for tariff reform was initiated in 1903 by Mr. Chamberlain. This initiative was viewed as a natural evolution of the imperialist idea, driven by trade statistics showing that commerce with the colonies constituted an increasingly large proportion of the United Kingdom's total trade. Mr. Chamberlain's primary objectives were to protect imperial trade and provide a preference for colonial goods through the imposition of retaliative duties on foreign goods in specific instances.

Mr. Chamberlain campaigned extensively across the country, advocating for a reorganization of the budget. His proposed scheme involved increasing taxes on imports, including food. However, he suggested that this taxation be adjusted to stimulate employment and improve the economic position of the working classes. By December 1903, this movement led to the appointment of Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Commission.

## The Free Food League and Opposition
In direct opposition to Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Reform league, a group of free-trade Unionists established the Free Food league. The chief representatives of this faction included Lord Goschen, Lord James, Lord Hugh Cecil, and the Duke of Devonshire. 

The opposition's position was formally articulated during a large meeting at Queen's Hall in London on November 24, 1903. At this gathering, the free-trade Unionists rejected the proposal for food taxes and discredited the statistics provided by Mr. Chamberlain. While they admitted that retaliation was a theoretical orthodoxy, they repudiated the assumption that it would be desirable in practice and criticized the attitude of Mr. Balfour.

## The Position of Mr. Balfour
As Prime Minister, Mr. Balfour found himself the target of efforts from both factions, as both sought to capture the party and its leader. Mr. Balfour's approach was marked by "dialectical dexterity," as he avoided expressing definitive fiscal opinions beyond what he had already stated. This evasion drew contemptuous criticism from the Liberal press.

Mr. Balfour refused to go "the whole hog" with Mr. Chamberlain, yet he did not take steps to discourage Chamberlain's campaign. He maintained that no definite action could be taken until the next parliament. Despite the internal strife, Mr. Balfour's personal influence remained potent, and the government held together through 1903, successfully carrying the Licensing Bill in 1904.

## Consequences of the Fiscal Dissension
The struggle over Protection and Tariff Reform eventually split the Conservatives. The division resulted in a significant loss of support for the government, with fifty Conservative or Unionist members withdrawing their support and six Ministers resigning. This loss included many younger members essential for the party's future driving power. The actions of the Free Trade Unionists were actively sustained by pillars of the party such as the Duke of Devonshire and Sir Michael Hicks-Beach, and were indirectly endorsed by Lord Salisbury from his retirement. This period of instability was described as the most formidable loss sustained by the party since the expulsion of the Peelites.

Ultimately, the dissensions over the fiscal question wrecked Mr. Balfour's administration. While the Unionist party was later defeated in England in 1906, the movement for colonial preference continued to gain ground and was eventually adopted by the Unionist party.
