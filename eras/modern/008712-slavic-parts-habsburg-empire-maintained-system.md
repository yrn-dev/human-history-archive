# Slavic Parts of the Habsburg Empire

The Slavic territories within the Habsburg monarchy, most notably Bohemia, were characterized by a long-standing struggle between the desire for national independence and the imperial drive toward centralization and German influence. This tension manifested in repeated constitutional conflicts, electoral disputes, and movements for linguistic and political recognition.

## Constitutional Struggles and Centralization
Following the Italian campaign of 1859, the continuation of absolutism in the Austrian empire became impossible. An attempt was made to establish a constitutional system that would recognize the ancient constitutional rights of various countries under Habsburg rule while maintaining imperial unity. A decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, modeled partly on the ancient diets of Bohemia. While initially received favorably in Bohemia, these hopes were undermined by a subsequent decree on February 26, 1861. This new decree established a central parliament in Vienna with extensive powers and an electoral system that was grossly partial to the Germans.

Although Bohemians initially sent representatives to Vienna, they withdrew in 1863, asserting that the central assembly had encroached upon the constitutional powers of the diet of Prague. After the central parliament was suspended in 1865 and the Austro-Prussian war occurred in 1866, the constitutional position of Bohemia changed. A treaty signed at Prague on August 23, 1866, excluded all lands ruled by the house of Habsburg from Germany, leading to a decline in German influence in the empire and specifically in Bohemia.

## The Constitution of 1867 and National Rights
In 1867, a new constitution was introduced. While Hungary obtained complete independence, this new constitution applied only to the German and Slavic parts of the Habsburg empire. It maintained a system of centralization and attempted to preserve the waning influence of the Germans. The Bohemians energetically opposed this constitution and refused to send representatives.

The title of "emperor of Austria," established in 1804 by Francis II (Francis I), was often viewed as a symbol of this tension. Magyars and Slavs never willingly recognized a style that implied the superiority of German elements and ignored their national rights. For those in the Slavic parts of the empire, such as the Bohemians, the title was resented as they sought the same recognition of national independence that had been granted to Hungary.

## National Aspirations and the Bohemian Movement
National aspirations in Bohemia began to revive after 1815, initially through the revival of literature due to the absolutist government of Prince Metternich, which made political action impossible. By 1845, the estates of Bohemia began opposing the government in Vienna, claiming the right to vote on taxes based on the constitution of 1627. By 1847, they proposed expanding town representation in the diet, tightening financial control, and introducing the Bohemian language into higher schools.

The revolutionary outbreak of 1848 accelerated these demands. On March 11, a public meeting in Prague petitioned Vienna for the Bohemian language to have equal rights with German in government offices, the convocation of a general diet elected on extensive suffrage, and various liberal reforms. On April 8, Emperor Ferdinand declared that equality of rights would be secured for both nationalities in Bohemia.

## Later Reconciliation Efforts
In 1871, Count Karl Hohenwart, the Austrian prime minister, attempted to reach an agreement between Bohemia and the rest of the empire. He sought a constitutional system that would satisfy Bohemian claims while keeping them connected to the Habsburg lands. On September 14, 1871, an imperial message to the diet of Prague recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. However, this attempt failed when a government with a pronounced German tendency took office in 1872, leading Bohemians to again refuse to attend parliamentary assemblies.

A period of relative reconciliation occurred under Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe, who took office in 1879. Taaffe persuaded Bohemian representatives to return to the parliament in Vienna. The Bohemians agreed, provided it was understood that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. In return, the Taaffe government removed some of the grossest anomalies in the Bohemian electoral system—which had previously favored the German minority—and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.
