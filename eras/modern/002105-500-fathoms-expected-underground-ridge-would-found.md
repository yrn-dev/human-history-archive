# Hydrographic Surveys and Submarine Ridges

The study of underwater topography involves the measurement of depths and the identification of submarine structures, such as ridges and troughs, which define the basins of lakes and seas. These surveys reveal the relationship between surface geography and the configuration of the bottom.

## Lake Basin Topography and Ridges
In certain lake basins, the steepness of surrounding mountains continues beneath the water's surface. For example, in a lake surveyed by Drizhenko between 1897 and 1902, the 100-fathom and 250-fathom lines run close to the shores. This lake is divided into two basins by the peninsula of Svyatoi Nos and the elongated hilly island of Olkhon. 

In the south-east of this lake, at the foot of the high plateau's Khamar-daban border-ridge, lies the deepest part. This area contains an elongated trough 66 m. long that reaches depths over 600 fathoms, with a maximum depth of 880 fathoms (approximately 5280 ft. below ocean level). While the depth of the middle portion of the lake has not been measured, it is expected to exceed 500 fathoms. 

Regarding the connection between Olkhon and Svyatoi Nos, it was expected that an underground ridge would be found connecting the two. However, soundings along that line have revealed depths exceeding 622 fathoms. In the northern basin, most of the area exceeds 400 fathoms in depth, with maximum soundings recorded at 491, 485, and 476 fathoms.

## Temperature and Depth
Depth significantly influences water temperature. In the aforementioned lake, surface layers can reach 55-1/2° to 57° F. during the summer. However, these warmer layers are shallow; a uniform temperature of nearly 39° F. is typically found at a depth of 20 fathoms. At a depth of 500 fathoms, the temperature remains nearly uniform at 38°.

## Marine Ridges and Basins
Submarine ridges also serve as boundaries or connectors between larger landmasses and sea basins:

*   **The Baltic:** An unbroken ridge extending from Hango in Finland to Stockholm separates the Baltic basin proper from the Aland Haf. Further north, a ridge defines the southern edge of the Bothnian Sea, which is separated from the Gulf of Bothnia by a ridge across the Kvarken (or Quarken) Strait.
*   **Greenland:** This continental island is connected to other regions only by suboceanic ridges. A submarine ridge, reaching a maximum depth of about 300 fathoms, unites Greenland with Scotland, the Faeroes, and Iceland across the Denmark Strait. Additionally, a similar submarine ridge connects Greenland to the Cumberland Peninsula of Baffin Land across Davis Strait.

## Other Deep-Water Observations
Various other bodies of water exhibit significant depth variations. The Sea of Marmora has a greatest depth of about 700 fathoms, with three depressions in its northern portion—one near the centre, one at the mouth of the Gulf of Ismid, and one south of Rodosto under the European shore—all exceeding 500 fathoms.

In the Caspian Sea, the basin is divided into three sections. The northern section is the shallowest, barely reaching 20 fathoms. In the middle section, over half the area is 50 fathoms deep, with a maximum depth of 437 fathoms.

## Biological Distribution by Depth
The distribution of deep-sea fishes is often categorized by depth. The 100-fathom line is frequently used as an arbitrary limit for bathybial fauna. Fishes have been procured from depths as low as 2750 fathoms. Evidence shows a gradual transition from surface to bathybial forms, as many surface fishes have been found in deep water ranging from 100 to 500 fathoms.
