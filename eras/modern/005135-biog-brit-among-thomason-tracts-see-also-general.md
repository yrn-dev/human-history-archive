# Bristol

Bristol was a prominent and conspicuous figure of his era, recognized for his brilliant abilities and skill as a great orator. He was noted for his capacity to distinguish himself without effort in any field of activity he entered. However, these natural gifts were undermined by an instability of character and a restless ambition that proved fatal to his achievement of real greatness.

## Character and Reputation
The historical record presents Bristol as a man of contradictions. Clarendon described him as the only person he knew of such "incomparable parts" who failed to gain wisdom from experience or the misfortunes that befell him. Clarendon further noted Bristol's extraordinary ability to simultaneously make friends and enemies.

Horace Walpole characterized Bristol's life as "one contradiction," noting several antitheses in his behavior:
* He embraced popery after writing against it.
* He served as a sacrifice for the court despite being a zealous opposer of it.
* He was a persecutor of Lord Clarendon, despite having been conscientiously converted during his prosecution of Lord Strafford.
* He spoke in favor of the Test Act while being a Roman Catholic.
* He practiced astrology on the birthday of true philosophy.

Additionally, while he possessed "romantic bravery," he was consistently an unsuccessful commander, and his great parts often resulted in harm to himself and his friends.

## Literary and Intellectual Works
Bristol was an author of various works across different genres. In 1643, he wrote an *Apologie* (found in the Thomason Tracts, E. 34 (32)) to justify his support of the king's cause. His dramatic works include the comedy *Elvira* (1667) and a Spanish adaptation titled *Worse and Worse*, which was acted but not printed. He is also credited as a co-author with Sir Samuel Tuke of *The Adventures of Five Hours* (1663).

Beyond these works, Bristol engaged in youthful correspondence with Sir K. Digby regarding religion. He delivered eloquent and pointed speeches, many of which were printed and are preserved in the *Biographia Britannica* and the *Thomason Tracts*. A catalogue of his library was published in 1680.

## Personal Life and Succession
Bristol married Lady Anne Russell, the daughter of Francis, 4th earl of Bedford. The couple had two daughters and two sons. Their son Francis predeceased him without marrying. He was succeeded as the 3rd earl of Bristol by his other son, John.

## Archival Sources and Bibliography
Extensive records of Bristol's life and career are maintained across several archives and publications:
* **Biographical Accounts:** Detailed notices are found in S. R. Gardiner's *Hist. of England* and *Hist. of the Civil War*, as well as a short sketch in the *Dict. of Nat. Biog.* Lives are also documented in *Biographia Britannica* (Kippis), Wood's *Ath. Oxon.*, D. Lloyd's *Memoires* (1668), Collins's *Peerage* (1812), Fuller's *Worthies* (1811), and H. Walpole's *Royal and Noble Authors* (1806).
* **State and Parliamentary Papers:** His activities are recorded in Clarendon's *Hist of the Rebellion*, the *Clarendon State Papers*, the *Cal. of Cl. State Papers*, the *Old Parliamentary History*, and the *Hardwicke State Papers*.
* **Manuscripts and Collections:** The *Thomason Tracts* and the general catalogue in the British Museum contain his speeches and writings. The *Harleian MSS.* (Brit. Mus. 1580, art. 31-48) and *Add. MSS.* indexes provide further references. Additionally, manuscripts at Sherborne Castle were transcribed and deposited in the Public Record Office, with calendars provided by the Hist. MSS. Commission.
* **Other Works:** His *Defence of his Spanish Negotiations* (edited by S.R. Gardiner), the *Somers Tracts* (1809), and the *Cabala* (1691) containing letters further document his career.
