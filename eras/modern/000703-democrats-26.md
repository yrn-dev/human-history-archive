# Democrats

The Democratic Party is the oldest existing political party in the United States. Its foundations were rooted in the principles of American colonial democracy, specifically a repugnance to social and political aristocracy and a commitment to local self-government. These tenets formed the basis of the nation's political institutions following the War of Independence, which is described as an essentially democratic movement.

## Origins and Early Development
The party originated as the Democratic-Republican Party, organized by Thomas Jefferson in opposition to the Federalists, who were dominated by Alexander Hamilton. By 1792, it had become a formal party. The Democratic-Republicans, along with the Anti-Federalists, were responsible for attaching a democratic bill of rights to the constitution. Jefferson's followers viewed the Federalists as monarchists and a "court party," leading them to adopt the name "Republicans."

The party gained full control of the government, with the exception of the judiciary, in 1801 and maintained this control until 1825. During this era, formal political platforms did not exist; instead, the party was guided by the writings of Thomas Jefferson. His 1801 inaugural address is noted as a famous statement of democratic principles. While the prefix "Democratic" was not used by Jefferson himself, it eventually became official.

## Ideology and Constitutional Interpretation
A central feature of the party's history is the struggle over "strict construction" of the constitution. This led to significant conflicts regarding the United States Bank, internal improvements, and tariffs—specifically whether tariffs should be for "revenue" only or for protection. The party's doctrines have shown a "progressive latitudinarianism" and have occasionally been inconsistent.

The party has been described as the political champion of the most democratic elements of American democracy, standing nearest to the people. Historically, the party contributed to the concept of a strong presidential veto, a legacy associated with the administrations of Andrew Jackson and Grover Cleveland. Additionally, the popular election of United States senators is a definite Democratic tenet.

## Evolution and Internal Shifts
Some historians date the origin of the modern Democratic Party to 1828–1829, noting that the party of Andrew Jackson was not strictly identical to the Democratic-Republican party of Jefferson. The first formal Democratic platform was established in 1840.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the party faced several challenges:
* **The Solid South:** From 1877, the Southern states were almost solidly Democratic, though this unanimity among whites was often factitious, except regarding the "negro question."
* **Economic Issues:** Between 1878 and 1896, the party attempted to "straddle" the silver issue. The national campaigns of 1896 and 1900 were fought and lost primarily on the issue of "free silver," which was abandoned before 1904.
* **Radicalism and Populism:** After 1896, the party sought to harmonize conservatism and radicalism, attracting the "masses" and radical elements of society. During the years 1896–1900, the party absorbed many members and principles of the "Populists," a third-party movement, while facing challenges from the growing strength of Socialism.
* **Imperialism:** After 1898, the Democrats became hostile to "imperialism."

## Electoral History and Governance
From 1860 to 1908, the party elected only one president, Grover Cleveland, who served from 1885–1889 and 1893–1897. The party controlled the House of Representatives from 1874 to 1894, with exceptions from 1880–1882 and 1888–1890. However, there was rarely a simultaneous Democratic president and Democratic majority in Congress, except for a period during Cleveland's second term.

In various states, the party held significant executive power. In Louisiana, Democrats held the governorship for much of the mid-to-late 19th century, including terms by Isaac Johnson (1846–1850), Robert C. Wickliffe (1856–1860), and Francis T. Nicholls (1877–1880 and 1888–1892). In Kentucky, the party held the governorship under figures such as John Breathitt (1832–1834), Beriah Magoffin (1859–1862), and J. C. W. Beckham (1900–1907).
