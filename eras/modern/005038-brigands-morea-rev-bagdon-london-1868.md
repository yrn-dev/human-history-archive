# The Brigands of the Morea

*The Brigands of the Morea* is a work translated into English by the Rev. J.O. Bagdon and published in London in 1868. The original text was written by S. Soteropoulos, an ex-minister who provided an authentic and interesting account of Greek brigands based on his own experiences after falling into their hands.

## Context of Greek Brigandage
Brigandage in Greece, along with similar activity in Spain, Corsica, and southern Italy, is characterized by deep roots and a history of resistance to total suppression. This persistence is attributed to the availability of hiding places in mountains and forests, as well as poor administration. In these regions, law officers were often viewed as enemies or dangers, resulting in a lack of native assistance for the authorities. Furthermore, local potentates frequently protected brigands because it served their own interests.

Under Turkish rule, the situation in Greece was such that individuals who were not *klephts* (the Greek term for brigands) often became victims of official extortion. Certain clans, such as the Mainotes, lived by the sword under their own laws as an alternative to being flayed by the Turks; the archive notes it would be unfair to label such groups as brigands.

## Political Influence and State Administration
Following the achievement of independence, Greece suffered from extreme maladministration. The country was governed by a nominal parliamentary system where politicians utilized brigands for their own specific purposes. 

The state of affairs eventually led to the adoption of strong measures to clear the Morea. These efforts were successful when the peasantry, recognizing that the government was earnest in its intentions, provided active support to the troops.

## Nature and Characteristics of Brigands
General characteristics of brigandage, as applied to European contexts, suggest that bands were frequently recruited from men who had committed homicide due to gambling quarrels or jealousy. Such men often remained in these bands not out of a love for the lifestyle, but because they feared the gallows. Internal dynamics within these groups could be brutal; for example, a reformed brigand known as Passo di Lupo (Wolf's Step) confessed around 1820 that bullies within the bands terrorized and robbed weaker members, and that murderous conflicts were constant.

Technically, a brigand is an outlaw who conducts warfare like a partisan or irregular soldier, utilizing surprises and skirmishes. They sustain their operations through plunder, the extortion of blackmail, and the capture of prisoners for ransom, killing those who are unable to pay.

## Later Incidents of Brigandage
Despite the clearing of the Morea, brigandage persisted in other parts of Greece. In 1870, an English party was captured at Oropos, near Marathon. The group included Count de Boyl, Mr. Herbert, Mr. Lloyd, Mr. Vyner, and Lord and Lady Muncaster. The brigands demanded a ransom of £25,000. While Lord and Lady Muncaster were released to secure the funds, the Greek government dispatched troops to pursue the captors. Consequently, the remaining prisoners were murdered. The perpetrators were eventually hunted down, captured, and executed, after which Greece became relatively free of such occurrences.
