# George Smith and the British Expedition to Nineveh

## Death of George Smith and Subsequent Expedition
George Smith was a scholar who authored the *History of Assurbanipal* in 1871. Following his death at Aleppo in 1876, the British Museum organized an expedition from 1877 to 1879 to continue the work he had begun. This expedition was conducted under the leadership of Hormuzd Rassam and focused on Nineveh and its surrounding areas.

## Excavations at Balawat and Nimrud
During the 1877–1879 expedition, Hormuzd Rassam conducted excavations at the mounds of Balawat, which the Assyrians referred to as Imgur-Bel. Located 15 meters east of Mosul, these excavations revealed a palace that had been restored by Shalmaneser II in 858 B.C. after being destroyed by the Babylonians. From this palace, bronze gates featuring hammered reliefs were recovered and transported to the British Museum.

Additionally, Rassam discovered a small temple at Balawat dedicated to the god of dreams by Assur-nazir-pal III in 883 B.C. This temple contained a stone ark or coffer holding two rectangular inscribed tables made of alabaster. Further work was carried out at Nimrud (Calah), where the remains of a palace belonging to Assur-nazir-pal III were excavated, resulting in the discovery of hundreds of enamelled tiles.

## Exploration of Babylonia
Following the work at Nineveh, Hormuzd Rassam was sent to Babylonia between 1880 and 1881. During this period, he discovered the site of the temple of the sun-god of Sippara at Abu-Habba. This discovery allowed for the fixing of the position of the two Sepharvaim or Sipparas. Abu-Habba is situated south-west of Bagdad, positioned midway between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers on the south side of a canal. This canal may have once served as the main stream of the Euphrates. The Sippara of the goddess Anunit is located on the opposite bank, in a place now known as Der.

## Context of Modern Discovery
The work of George Smith and Hormuzd Rassam was part of a broader era of modern discovery in the region. Earlier excavations by A. H. Layard and P. E. Botta at Nineveh had opened a new world of understanding, aided by the successful decipherment of cuneiform writing. Layard's discovery of the library of Assur-bani-pal provided scholars with the necessary materials to reconstruct the history and ancient life of Babylonia and Assyria. Layard was also the first to excavate in Babylonia, following topographical work by C. J. Rich. Other contributors to the exploration of the region included W. K. Loftus, who opened trenches at Susa and continued Layard's work in Babylonia, and J. Oppert, who worked on behalf of the French government. Despite these efforts, systematic exploration was not attempted until the last quarter of the 19th century.
