# Southern Baptist Convention

## Origins and Formation
The Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845. Its creation was the result of growing tensions within the Triennial Convention, a national organization for foreign missions established in May 1814. Prior to 1844, sessions of the Triennial Convention had been marked by occasional unpleasantness, characterized by harsh anti-slavery statements made by Northern members toward their Southern brethren, who responded with acrimonious rejoinders.

The decision to establish a separate organization for missionary work was ultimately solidified following a controversy regarding the question of slavery between Richard Fuller (1804-1876) and Francis Wayland. Upon its formation in 1845, the Southern Baptist Convention established a Sunday-school Board as well as Home and Foreign Missionary Boards.

## Organizational Relationship with Northern Baptists
Following the split in 1845, Northern and Southern Baptists continued to maintain perfect fellowship with one another. However, they determined that it was most effective to maintain separate annual meetings and to conduct their foreign and home missionary work through separate boards.

In 1905, a General Baptist Convention for America was created. The purpose of this organization was to promote comity, fellowship, and denominational *esprit de corps*. The General Baptist Convention was designed specifically so as not to undertake administrative work or interfere with the existing sectional organizations.

## Growth and Statistics
Since 1845, both Southern and Northern Baptists have experienced significant growth in their numbers, educational institutions, literary activities, and missionary work. Between 1812 and 1906, the total number of American Baptists grew from fewer than 200,000 to more than 5,000,000.

According to archive records, the Southern Baptist Convention consisted of 20,431 churches and 1,832,638 members.

## Denominational Infrastructure
The broader Baptist denomination in America saw a vast increase in its equipment and organization between 1812 and 1906. While there were no theological seminaries in 1812, by 1906 there were 11, featuring over 100 instructors and 1,300 students, with equipment and endowments valued at approximately $7,000,000.

Educational expansion also included the growth of colleges and universities; from a single degree-conferring college with almost no endowment in 1812, the denomination grew to have more than 100 colleges and universities by 1906. These institutions had an annual income of about $3,000,000 and endowments and equipment valued at roughly $30,000,000.

Additionally, the value of church property was estimated at $100,000,000 in 1906. The denomination's literary output expanded from a single monthly magazine with a circulation of a few hundred in 1812 to hundreds of weeklies, monthlies, and quarterlies by 1906. The American Baptist Publication Society serves as the single publishing concern for the denomination, possessing assets of $1,750,000 and an annual business of nearly $1,000,000.
