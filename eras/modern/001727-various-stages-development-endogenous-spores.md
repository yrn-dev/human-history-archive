# Endogenous Spore Development

Endogenous spores are spores formed from protoplasm within the interior of a cell. This process is observed in various organisms, including certain fungi and Schizomycetes (bacteria).

## Development in Schizomycetes
In Schizomycetes, the formation of endogenous spores typically occurs within a bacillar segment. While usually only one spore is formed per cell, some instances exist where a single cell contains two spores, as seen in *Clostridium*. The spore may develop in either long or short segments, and the cell-wall may change its form to accommodate the internal contents. In some cases, the protoplasm responsible for spore formation produces a blue reaction when treated with iodine solutions.

The process of spore development can occur even in cells that are actively swarming, as the movement of the cell is not interfered with by the process. Certain forms, referred to by German authors as "*Koepfchenbacterien*," are identified as bacterioid segments featuring a spore at one end, where the mother cell-wall has adapted to the outline of the spore.

Specific examples of development stages include:
*   ***Bacillus ramosus***: The process begins with the appearance of brilliant granules. These granules increase and gradually ball together to form spores. One spore forms in each segment, eventually acquiring a membrane and ripening.
*   ***Bacillus megaterium***: Development progresses through successive stages from growing rodlets to the formation of ripe spores. In some instances, a rodlet may be segmented into four, with each segment containing one ripe spore.
*   ***Bacillus anthracis***: In certain culture conditions, such as a drop of aqueous humour, these rodlets grow into long filaments resembling *leptothrix*. These filaments later become septate, and spores develop within the segments.

## Characteristics of Ripe Spores
Ripe spores of Schizomycetes are extremely minute, colorless, or very dark—the latter likely due to their minute size and high index of refraction. They are highly refractive and can be spherical, ovoid, or long-ovoid in shape. For example, spores of *Bacillus subtilis* measure 0.0006 mm in breadth and 0.0012 mm in length. The membrane of the spore may be relatively thick and can exhibit strata or shells.

## Germination Processes
The germination of endogenous spores varies by species:
*   ***Bacillus subtilis***: The germinal rodlet grows along an axis that is at right angles to the long axis of the spore.
*   ***Clostridium butyricum***: The axis of growth for the germinal rodlet coincides with the long axis of the spore.
*   ***Bacillus ramosus***: Observation in hanging drops shows that a spore may swell and germinate within a few hours. The resulting filament elongates and segments into bacilli, eventually breaking up into individual rodlets.

## Endogenous Spores in Other Organisms
Endogenous spores are also a characteristic of the *Saccharomycetaceae*, which include yeasts such as *Saccharomyces*. In these unicellular organisms, the formation of endogenous spores occurs under specific conditions, such as changes in temperature. During this process, the nucleus in the yeast-cell divides, and each daughter-nucleus divides again, resulting in the formation of four spores within the mother cell. This process has been compared to the development of ascospores in an ascus.

## Comparison with Other Spore Types
In the broader category of fungi, spores are classified by their origin. While endogenous spores form from internal protoplasm, other types include acrogenous spores, which are abstricted free from the ends of hyphae, and chlamydospores, which are formed from segments along the course of the hyphae.
