# The Shan States

The Shan States are a region in Burma that was declared part of British India by notification in 1886. The territory is characterized by a diverse geography, ranging from rugged hills and narrow gorges to a few fertile, well-populated open plateaus.

## Boundaries and Geography
The eastern boundaries of Burma, including the Shan States, are defined by several geographical markers. At a latitude level with Mogaung, the boundary is marked by the eastern watershed of the Nmaikha, one of the two great northern affluents of the Irrawaddy. The line follows an irregular course southwards to a position south-east of Bhamo at 24 degrees latitude, where it defines the northern edge of the Shan States. 

The boundary continues to the Mekong river, striking it at approximately 21 degrees 45' latitude. For roughly 200 miles southwards, the Mekong river serves as the boundary between the Shan States and Tongking, marking the limits of occupation between British and French subjects. Around 20 degrees north latitude, the boundary leaves the Mekong to run westwards toward the Salween. It then follows the eastern watershed of the Salween basin, dividing the Lower Burma provinces from Siam.

## Administration and Law
Under the Shan States Act of 1888, the administration of revenue, criminal, and civil matters is vested in the chief of the state. This authority is subject to restrictions specified in a patent or *sanad* granted to the chief. The legal system in each state is based on customary law, provided it does not oppose the spirit of the law in the rest of British India and aligns with equity, justice, and good conscience.

General control over the administration of criminal justice is exercised by superintendents, who possess wide revisionary powers and the authority to call for cases. However, criminal jurisdiction is withdrawn from the chiefs and vested in assistant superintendents or superintendents if the defendant or complainant is a British subject (non-native of a Shan State), a government servant, an American, or a European. Neither superintendents nor assistant superintendents have the power to try civil suits.

In the Myelat division of the southern Shan States, the criminal law is practically identical to that of Upper Burma, and petty chiefs known as *ngwegunhmus* are appointed as magistrates of the second class.

## Political Structure and States
The chiefs of the Shan States are categorized into three classes:
1. **Sawbwas**
2. **Myosas**
3. **Ngwegunhmus** (found only in the Myelat border country between Burma and the southern Shan States).

In the Shan States proper, there are sixteen myosas, fifteen sawbwas, and thirteen ngwegunhmus. Supervision is divided among various officials; for example, two sawbwas are supervised by the commissioner of the Sagaing division and two by the commissioner of the Mandalay division. Additionally, five sawbwaships are under the supervision of the superintendent of the northern Shan States.

The states vary significantly in size. The Trans-Salween State of Kêng Tung covers 12,000 square miles, while Nam Hkôm in the Myelat measured only 3.95 square miles. In 1897, Nam Hkôm had only 210 inhabitants and 41 houses before being merged into an adjoining state.

## Regional Features and Trade
The northern Shan States include Kunlong, a district and ferry on the Salween. While considered insignificant, Kunlong gained notoriety as the nominal terminus in British territory for the railway extending from Lashio to the borders of Yunnan. The actual terminus is located 7 miles below the ferry. 

Historically, trade in this area was considerable, and the Burmese maintained a customs station on the island of Kunlong. However, trade was diverted to the northern route to Bhamo due to several factors: the southward movement of the Kachins, a rebellion in the state of Theinni, and a Mahommedan rebellion in Yunnan. Furthermore, the Wa people, most of whom live in unadministered British territory in the hills overlooking the Nam Ting valley, have made the route dangerous for traders.
