# Bacteriological Classification and Research

The study of bacteria involves complex systems of classification based on morphology and motility, as well as extensive research into cellular structure and environmental interactions. Much of the foundational literature in this field is documented in comprehensive texts and specialized journals.

## Classification of Haplobacterinae
The order Haplobacterinae consists of organisms with a unicellular vegetative body. These bodies may be cylindrical, spheroidal, or spirally twisted, and can exist as isolated cells or connected in other growth series or filamentous forms. This order is divided into several families:

### Coccaseae
The vegetative cells of this family are spheroidal. It is further divided into two sub-families:
*   **Allococcaceae**: These exhibit division in any or all planes. Their colonies are indefinite in size and shape, appearing as isolated cells, pairs, irregular clumps, or short chains. This group includes the non-motile *Micrococcus* and the motile *Planococcus*.
*   **Homococcaceae**: These possess regular and definite division planes. Examples include *Sarcina* (non-motile), which divides in three successive planes at right angles to form packet-like groups; *Planosarcina*, which is motile but follows the same division pattern; *Pediococcus*, which divides in two successive planes at right angles to form tablets of four or more cells; and *Streptococcus*, which divides in only one plane, resulting in chains.

### Bacillaceae
The vegetative cells of this family are straight and may be ovoid, ellipsoid, or cylindric (rodlets). Division planes are always perpendicular to the long axis. The sub-family Bacilleae contains sporogenous rodlets that maintain a cylindric shape. This group is categorized by motility and cilia:
*   **Bacillus**: Non-motile.
*   **Bactrinium**: Motile, featuring one polar flagellum (monotrichous).
*   **Bactrillum**: Motile, featuring a terminal tuft of cilia (lophotrichous).
*   **Bactridium**: Motile, with cilia distributed over the entire surface (peritrichous).

## Critiques of Classification Systems
Certain classification systems, such as that proposed by Fischer, face significant objections. One primary issue is the difficulty in obtaining satisfactory preparations to show cilia. It has been discovered that these motile organs are not formed on all substrata and may only develop during short periods of activity when the organism is young and vigorous. For example, *B. subtilis* and *B. megatherium* transition from a motile stage with peritrichous cilia to a stage of filamentous growth within a few hours, casting off the cilia in the process.

Additionally, critics note that a vast majority of described species—over 1,000—fall into only three genera: *Micrococcus* (approximately 400), *Bacillus* (approximately 200), and *Bactridium* (approximately 150). Furthermore, motility conditions are not always constant; *Pseudomonas rosea* (Mig.) may possess one, two, or three cilia at either end, which would lead to inconsistent classification between *Bactrinium* and *Bactrillum* depending on the state observed.

## Cellular Structure and Biology
Research into the physical makeup of bacteria covers several specialized areas:
*   **Cell Membrane and Cilia**: Studies have been conducted by Butschli, Fischer, and Rowland. Fischer's work specifically includes investigations into the structure of bacteria and cyanophyceen, as well as research on cilia published in *Pringsh. Jahrb.*
*   **Nucleus**: The nucleus and its division have been examined by Wager, Vejdovsky, and Mencl. Vejdovsky's research includes cytological studies on bacteria found in the Prager water supply.
*   **Spores**: Research on spores includes work by Marshall Ward on *B. ramosus*, Sturgis on a soil bacillus of the type of de Bary's *B. megatherium*, and studies by L. Klein.

## Environmental and Pathogenic Research
Bacteria have been studied in various environments and their interactions with other organisms:
*   **Water Bacteriology**: Frankland and Marshall Ward have reported on the bacteriology of water, including specific studies on *B. ramosus* and the bacteria of the river Thames.
*   **Plant Interaction**: Bacteria are known to invade leguminous roots. In peas, "infection threads" (zoogloea) push through cell walls, and bacteria move up root-hairs in thin streams. In lupin, bacteria form root nodules containing bacterial tissue.
*   **Pathogenic Bacteria**: Extensive literature exists on pathogenic microorganisms, notably the *Handbuch der pathogenen Mikroorganismen* by Kolle and Wassermann, which is cited as the most important work on the subject. Other significant texts include works by McFarland, Park, and the *System of Medicine* edited by Clifford Allbutt.
