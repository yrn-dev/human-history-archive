# Earls of Buckinghamshire

The earldom of Buckinghamshire was first held by John Hobart (c. 1694–1756). He was a descendant of Sir Henry Hobart (d. 1625), who served as chief justice of the common pleas and attorney-general under James I and was made a baronet in 1611. Sir Henry was the great-grandson of Sir James Hobart (d. 1507), who served as attorney-general to Henry VII. The Hobart family had been established in Suffolk and Norfolk for many years prior to the creation of the title.

## The First and Second Earls
John Hobart, a son of the 4th baronet Sir Henry Hobart (d. 1698), was created Baron Hobart of Blickling in 1728. He later became the lord-lieutenant of Norfolk in 1740 and was created earl of Buckinghamshire in 1746. His sister, Henrietta Howard, countess of Suffolk, was the mistress of George II. Following the first earl's death on September 22, 1756, he was succeeded by his eldest son, John (1723–1793), as the 2nd earl.

Before inheriting the title, the 2nd earl served as the comptroller of the royal household and was a member of parliament for Norwich. His later career included serving as ambassador to Russia from 1762 to 1766 and as lord-lieutenant of Ireland from 1776 to 1780, though he was considered hardly equal to the exceptional difficulties of the latter role. He died without sons at Blickling Hall, Norfolk, on August 3, 1793. Consequently, his Norfolk estates and Blickling Hall passed to his daughter, Henrietta (1762–1805), who married William Kerr, the 6th marquess of Lothian.

## The Third and Fourth Earls
Upon the death of the 2nd earl, the title passed to his half-brother, George (c. 1730–1804), who became the 3rd earl. He was succeeded by his eldest son, Robert Hobart (1760–1816), the 4th earl.

Robert Hobart had a varied career as a soldier and a member of the Irish and English Houses of Commons. From 1789 to 1793, he served as chief secretary to the lord-lieutenant of Ireland, where he worked to prevent concessions to Roman Catholics. In 1793, while using the courtesy title of Lord Hobart, he was appointed governor of Madras, though he was recalled in 1798 following serious differences with the governor-general of India, Sir John Shore.

Returning to British politics, Robert Hobart entered the House of Lords in 1798 and succeeded to the earldom in 1804. He supported the union between Ireland and England and served as secretary for war and the colonies from March 1801 to May 1804; his family name was used for Hobart Town in Tasmania. He later became chancellor of the duchy of Lancaster under Pitt and served briefly as joint postmaster-general. From 1812 until his death on February 4, 1816, he was president of the Board of Control.

## Later Earls and the Hobart-Hampden Line
Because the 4th earl left no sons, the title passed to his nephew, George Robert Hobart (1789–1849), the 5th earl. He was the son of George Vere Hobart (1761–1802), who had been the lieutenant-governor of Grenada. In 1824, the 5th earl inherited the Buckinghamshire estates of the Hampden family, as his ancestor, Sir John Hobart (3rd baronet), had married Mary Hampden around 1655.

The 6th earl was Augustus Edward Hobart (1793–1884), the brother of the 5th earl. He adopted the name Hobart-Hampden in 1878. He was succeeded by his grandson, Sidney Carr Hobart-Hampden (b. 1860), the 7th earl, who also added the name Mercer-Henderson to his own. The 6th earl's sons, Frederick John Hobart (1821–1875) and Vere Henry, Lord Hobart (1818–1875)—who served as governor of Madras starting in 1872—both predeceased their father. Another son of the 6th earl was Augustus Charles Hobart-Hampden, known as Hobart Pasha.

## Historical Notes
A point of historical confusion exists regarding the 2nd earl of Buckinghamshire, who always signed himself as "Buckingham" and was known by that name by his contemporaries. This practice continued until 1784, when George Grenville, Earl Temple, was created marquess of Buckingham.
