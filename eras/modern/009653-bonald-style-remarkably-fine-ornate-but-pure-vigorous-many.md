## Literary and Philosophical Styles

In the realm of thought and writing, style is often contrasted with the cogency of reasoning. Bonald is noted for a style that is remarkably fine, characterized as being pure and vigorous, yet ornate. While his works contain many fruitful thoughts, his strength is attributed to the sincerity and vigour of his statements rather than the reasoning of his system, which is described as scarcely deserving the name of a philosophy.

In English historical literature, the evolution of style is evident in the works of the 18th century. Edward Gibbon's style is described as possessing a "superhuman dignity," combining monumental industry and co-ordinative vigour. In contrast, the work of William Robertson, who adapted pulpit periphrases for historical generalization, was eventually supplanted by the "ornate pages" of W.H. Prescott. Other notable figures in English literature who gave the fine arts a significant place include John Ruskin, William Morris, Walter Pater, and John Addington Symonds. Ruskin, in particular, is said to have transcended most prose writers of his period through a style containing elements of emotional power.

## Architectural Ornamentation

The term "ornate" is frequently applied to the architectural achievements of Europe, particularly in the Gothic and Renaissance traditions.

### Louvain and Avallon
In Louvain, the Hôtel de Ville stands as one of the richest and most ornate examples of pointed Gothic architecture in the country. Designed by master mason Mathieu de Layens between 1448 and 1463, the building features a façade with ten pointed windows on each of its three storeys, a graceful balustrade, and a lofty roof. Its three sides are lavishly decorated with statuary. Nearby, the church of St Pierre, built between 1425 and 1497, contains fine pictures by Dierich Bouts.

In Avallon, France, the 12th-century church of St Lazare features two western portals adorned with sculpture in the ornate Romanesque style.

## Sumptuous Craftsmanship

The 18th century saw a rapid development in the manufacture of state carriages, which were often sumptuous and ornate to meet the demands of European courts. An example of this style is the 1696 carriage of the imperial family of Vienna, which utilizes curves familiar to the style of Louis XIV. This carriage features panels painted with nymphs in the style of Rubens and an unusual amount of plate glass, topped with a large imperial crown on the roof. Other elaborate examples include the state coach of the city of London (1757) and the royal state coach of England (1761), built for King George III.
