# Fractures

In medical terms, a fracture occurs when a bone is broken. This can happen in several ways: through direct violence, where the bone is broken at the part where it is struck; through indirect violence, where the break is a consequence of a strain applied to the bone; or through muscular action, such as when a violent cough causes a rib to break. Generally, fractures resulting from direct violence are transverse, while those from indirect violence are more or less oblique.

## Types and Classifications
Fractures are categorized as either simple or compound. A simple fracture is analogous to a subcutaneous laceration in the soft parts. A compound fracture is analogous to an open wound in the soft parts. In compound fractures, the wound may be caused by the force that fractured the bone (such as a cart-wheel going over a limb) or by the sharp point of the fractured bone piercing through the skin. 

Because compound fractures create a communication between the injured bone and the external air, there is a risk that germs of suppuration may reach the site. This increases the risks of the case, potentially leading to blood-poisoning, osteomyelitis, necrosis (the death of large pieces of bone), and delayed union.

Other specific classifications include:
*   **Green stick or willow fracture:** Occurs in the soft bones of young people, where the bone may simply be bent rather than broken across.
*   **Impacted fracture:** A fracture where the broken extremities are driven into one another.
*   **Diastasis:** A variety of fracture in children and growing persons where the end of the bone becomes detached from the shaft.

## Diagnosis and Complications
In most cases, a fracture is identified by *crepitus*, which is the feeling elicited when two rough osseous surfaces are rubbed together. Crepitus is absent in impacted fractures and in cases where the bone is merely bent.

Several complications can arise during the healing process:
*   **Delayed union:** This occurs when consolidation takes place very slowly or not at all, due to constitutional or local causes.
*   **Ununited fracture:** A condition where nature makes no attempt to consolidate the parts after many weeks or months. This may be caused by constitutional defects, the interposition of a tendon or muscle between the broken ends, the constant disturbance of the fracture site, or the failure to bring the ends close enough together.
*   **Stiffness:** In old people, splintering of the bone into a joint or injured tendons can cause effusion into the tendon sheaths and joint, which may organize into fibrous tissue and lead to permanent stiffness. In children, diastasis may lead to stiffness of the neighboring joint or an arrest of the bone's growth.

## Treatment and Recovery
The primary goal of treatment is to secure accurate apposition of the fragments to ensure firm bony union. This process of putting the broken ends together is called "setting the fracture." Rest is typically obtained through the use of splints, and it is generally advisable to fix the joint above or below the fracture with the splint.

Modern improvements in treatment emphasize the use of X-rays to ensure fractured surfaces are well adjusted. If ordinary methods cannot achieve close adjustment, surgeons may cut down to the broken ends and fix them with a strong wire suture that remains permanently in the tissues. In compound fractures, immediate wiring is considered a necessary part of primary treatment if the ends cannot be easily adjusted.

Recent practices have moved away from keeping simple fractures at rest in splints for long periods. Instead, they are frequently "taken down" to allow for gentle movements and massage, which helps swelling and pain disappear and ensures a more rapid recovery. For fractures near the wrist, splints are arranged to allow the movement of the thumb and fingers, and are removed daily so the wrist and fingers can be exercised. In cases of joint splintering, passive movement and gentle exercises are used to keep tendons and joints free.

## Non-Medical Applications of the Term
The term "fracture" is also used in other contexts within the archive:
*   **Materials Science:** In bridge designing, Wöhler's law is used to determine the breaking strength of bars. This includes "statical breaking strength" (loaded once gradually up to fracture), "primitive strength" (loaded and unloaded an indefinitely great number of times), and "vibration strength" (subjected to repetitions of stresses equal and opposite in sign).
*   **Food Science:** Well-made butter is characterized by a "granular fracture" similar to that of cast-iron, allowing it to cut clean with a knife.
