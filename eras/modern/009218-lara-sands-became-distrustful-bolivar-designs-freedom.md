# Simon Bolivar and the Administration of Freed Provinces

Simon Bolivar (1783–1830) was a central figure in the independence of South America. Born in Caracas, Venezuela, to noble parents, Bolivar was educated in Europe and resided in Madrid for several years. During his travels, he visited Paris and witnessed the final scenes of the French Revolution. In 1809, he traveled through the United States, where he observed the operation of free institutions. Upon returning to Venezuela, he joined the cause of independence and was one of the promoters of the April 1810 insurrection in Caracas.

## Military Campaigns and the Republic of Colombia
Bolivar’s military career involved several critical campaigns to expel Spanish forces. After an unsuccessful expedition in 1816, he landed in Margarita and Barcelona, where he established a provisional government. Following the defeat of royalist forces under Morillo, Bolivar fixed his headquarters at Angostura on the Orinoco. 

In February 1819, Bolivar submitted his views on government to a congress at Angostura, though he was requested to retain power until independence was fully established. He led his forces across the Cordilleras to join General Santander in New Granada, achieving a victory at Boyaca on August 7, 1819, which secured Bogota. On December 17, 1819, a fundamental law was enacted uniting the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar as president and Santander as vice-president. Bolivar later secured the end of the war in Venezuela through a decisive victory at Carabobo, leading to the surrender of Spanish forces at Puerto Cabello.

## Expansion into Ecuador and Peru
Bolivar sought to complete the liberation of the region by targeting the provinces of Ecuador and Peru. In June 1822, after a battle at Pichincha led by Sucre, the republicans entered Quito. Bolivar then marched on Lima, where he was invested with absolute power as dictator. Despite temporary setbacks caused by republican factions in Peru, Bolivar organized a liberating army that defeated Canterac at the plains of Junin on August 6, 1824. Sucre subsequently gained a decisive victory at Ayacucho, effectively dispersing the Spanish forces in Peru.

## The State of Bolivia and Constitutional Conflict
In June 1825, Bolivar visited Upper Peru, which had detached itself from the government of Buenos Aires to form a separate state named Bolivia in his honor. The first congress of this new republic declared Bolivar its perpetual protector and requested that he prepare a constitution.

Bolivar presented his project for a constitution to the Bolivian congress on May 25, 1826. This code contained a highly controversial provision: it proposed that executive authority be held by a president for life who would be without responsibility and possess the power to nominate his own successor. This proposal alarmed republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires and those who supported liberty. In Peru, Bolivar faced accusations that he intended to unite Bolivia, Peru, and Colombia into a single state under his own rule as a perpetual dictator of the confederacy.

## Political Instability and Resignation
By 1826, Bolivar faced significant challenges to his power. In September 1826, he left Lima, entrusting the government to a council led by Santa Cruz, and arrived in Bogota on November 14. He assumed extraordinary powers authorized by the constitution for cases of rebellion to address disturbances in Venezuela. After meeting with Paez and establishing headquarters in Caracas, Bolivar and Santander were re-elected as president and vice-president, respectively.

However, in February 1827, Bolivar formally resigned the presidency. He expressed a desire to retire to his patrimonial estate to refute accusations of ambition. Santander opposed this resignation, arguing that only Bolivar's personal influence and authority could appease the country's agitations. This view was confirmed by a resolution of congress, leading Bolivar to resume his functions. Before returning to Bogota to take his oaths, Bolivar issued three decrees: one granting general amnesty, one establishing constitutional order throughout Colombia, and one convoking a national convention at Ocana.
