# Kumasi

Kumasi (also spelled Coomassie) is the capital of Ashanti in British West Africa. It is situated on a low rocky eminence, extending across a valley to an opposite hill. The town is located in a clearing within the dense forest that covers most of Ashanti. Its name, more correctly Kum-ase, means "under the okum tree," referring to the abundance of such trees in its streets. As of 1906, the population was 6,280, increasing to over 12,000 when including the suburbs.

## Geography and Climate
The town occupies an area approximately 1.5 miles in length and over 3 miles in circumference. The land immediately surrounding Kumasi was once marshy but has since been drained. To the north-west lies the river Dah, a headstream of the Prah. Approximately 25 miles south-east of the town is Lake Busumchwi, the sacred lake of the Ashanti, which is nearly circular with a maximum diameter of 6 miles and is surrounded by forest-clad hills reaching 800 feet.

The elevation of the region is generally below 2,000 feet, rising toward the north. The climate is characterized by a mean temperature of 76 degrees F and a mean annual rainfall of 40 inches. The rainy season occurs from the end of May until October and is marked by frequent, violent storms. While the water supply is abundant and good, the climate is considered unsuited for the prolonged residence of Europeans, though it is less unhealthy than the coast towns of West Africa.

## Urban Development and Infrastructure
Historically, Kumasi was a center of high culture. Before its destruction by the British in 1874, the city featured broad, regular streets—the main avenue being 70 yards wide—and a king's palace built of red sandstone modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina. A large market-place and a "Spirit House," used for executions, were also located in the south-east. Following the 1874 destruction, the town was only partially rebuilt, and a new royal palace was constructed of clay rather than brick.

By the early 20th century, the most imposing structure was the fort built in 1896, which served as the residence of the chief commissioner and could house several hundred men. The town also included officers' quarters, cantonments, European and native hospitals, and stations for the Wesleyan and Basel missions. Native houses are constructed of red clay, often richly ornamented, with higher-class homes enclosed in compounds. Leading mercantile houses are located near the railway station, while principal Ashanti chiefs own European-style houses which they lease to strangers.

Communications are facilitated by a railway from Sekondi, completed in September 1903, and a 141-mile wide road from Cape Coast. While telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service connect the town to the coast and Northern Territories, travel through the bush often relies on tortuous paths or palanquins borne by four carriers.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Following the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was appointed to the Ashanti throne. Kumasi was administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart. Although outwardly submissive, the Kumasi chiefs remained unreconciled to British rule.

Tensions escalated on March 28, 1900, when the governor of the Gold Coast, Sir F. Hodgson, announced during a public palaver that Ashanti chiefs must pay the British government 4,000 oz. of gold annually. He further reproached the chiefs for hiding the golden stool since 1896. Three days later, Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa sent to find the stool.

The Kumasi, seeking to erase the dishonor of Prempeh's deportation, threatened the Kumasi fort. Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, along with Basel missionaries including Mr. and Mrs. Ramseyer, took refuge inside. The garrison was gradually reinforced, growing from 100 Gold Coast constabulary on April 18 to 700 men by May 15, following the arrival of Lagos constabulary and forces under Major A. Morris.

## Relief and Suppression
A relief expedition was organized at Cape Coast by Colonel James Willcocks. Facing impenetrable bush, deep mire, and a lack of local food and carriers, Willcocks advanced through the rainy season. After a feint toward Kokofu to draw away enemy forces, he marched west. On July 15, Yoruba troops carried the main rebel stockade at the point of the bayonet, and the relieving force entered Kumasi that evening. The force consisted of 1,000 West African fighting men, 60 white officers and non-commissioned officers, and various artillery, including Maxims and seven-pounder guns.

The rebellion was eventually crushed after the Kumasi were beaten at Obassa on September 30. By December 28, the remaining rebel chiefs were captured. Total casualties for the campaign, including disease, numbered 1,007.

## Economy and Trade
Kumasi serves as the distributing center for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti. The region's economy is based on:
*   **Agriculture:** Rubber trees (*Funtumia elastica*), kola nuts, and tobacco (in northern regions). Since 1905, cocoa plantations have been established, particularly in the east.
*   **Mining:** Gold is found in western districts, with the Obuassi mines providing the majority of gold exported from the Gold Coast in 1902 and following years. In 1905, gold output from Ashanti was 68,259 oz., valued at L254,790.
*   **Trade:** Revenue is generated through liquor licenses, rents from government land, contributions from gold-mining companies, and caravan taxes.
