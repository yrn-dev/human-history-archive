# Sachu (Saitu)

Sachu, also referred to as Saitu, is a location in Mongolia that serves as an obligatory point in the construction of modern maps. Its geographical significance is highlighted by its role as a point of junction between the explorations of different researchers, specifically those of the Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky and the native surveyor Krishna.

## Geographical Position and Longitude
The precise longitude of Sachu has been a subject of various scientific determinations. The currently adopted value is 94 deg. 54' E. of Greenwich. This figure is a revised value provided by Prjevalsky in the map that accompanied the account of his fourth exploration into central Asia.

Other recorded longitude values for Sachu include:
*   **Prjevalsky (second and third explorations):** 94 deg. 26'
*   **Krishna:** 94 deg. 23'
*   **Carey and Dalgleish:** 94 deg. 48'
*   **Littledale:** 94 deg. 49'
*   **Kreitner (with Szecheny's expedition):** 94 deg. 58'

## Exploration and Discovery
Sachu has been a key destination for several major geographical expeditions in Asia. Between 1879 and 1882, the surveyor Krishna undertook a remarkable journey that extended from Lhasa northwards through Tsaidam to Sachu. Following this, Krishna passed through eastern Tibet to the town of Darchendo (or Tachienlu), located on the borders of China on the high road between Peking and Lhasa.

The Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky also reached Sachu during his travels. After visiting the sources of the Salween and the Hwang-ho (Yellow river), Prjevalsky traversed the Gobi desert from Hami to Sachu. This intersection of routes established Sachu as a critical reference point for mapping the central region.

## Regional Context
Sachu is situated within the broader geography of Mongolia and the central Asiatic region. This area is characterized by a vast highland plateau stretching from the Himalaya northwards to Chinese Turkestan and eastwards to China. 

To the south and north-west of this general region lies the Gobi, a great desert bounded by the Tian-shan and Yablonoi Mountains to the north and north-west, the northern face of the Tibetan plateau to the south, and the Khingan range to the east. The eastern portion of the Gobi is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia. The elevation of this central Asiatic desert is believed to be no less than 2,000 ft. above sea level, with the more northern parts of Mongolia ranging between 4,000 and 6,000 ft.
