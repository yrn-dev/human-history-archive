# Miniature Painting

Miniature painting encompasses a diverse range of artistic practices, from the illumination of medieval manuscripts to the specialized portraiture of later centuries. The art form has evolved from the cloistered work of monks to a professional trade and a recognized society of artists.

## Manuscript Illumination and the Medieval Miniature
In the context of illuminated manuscripts (MSS), the "miniature" is the technical term for a picture. In the middle ages, book decoration developed along two primary lines: the illustrative picture, which preceded the medieval miniature, and independent simple ornaments that expanded into borders and brilliant initial letters.

Byzantine art reached a mature style in the 9th and 10th centuries. Examples include a 10th-century psalter containing a miniature of David as the psalmist, which adapted a classical scene of Orpheus and the Muses, and the *Homilies of Chrysostom* (belonging to emperor Nicephorus III, 1078-1081), which displays a fine type of features and head. Byzantine miniatures often featured brilliant gilded backgrounds and rectangular head-pieces with geometric, flowered, or tesselated patterns of Oriental design.

Between the late 12th and early 13th centuries, illumination became more conventional. Gold began to be applied in leaf and highly burnished, and the "large, bold style" was superseded by a smaller, more decorated approach. This period saw the establishment of the "miniature initial"—an initial letter containing a miniature that illustrated the immediate text. While miniatures remained prominent, the borders grew more luxuriant throughout the 13th, 14th, and 15th centuries, eventually enveloping the entire page of text.

By the 15th century, the art of illumination entered a new phase. In France, the school of Paris produced magnificent volumes such as the *Sobieski Hours* and the Book of Hours of John Plantagenet, duke of Bedford. The breviary illuminated for John the Fearless is noted for its refinement and minuteness of detail. During this time, the professional miniaturist working for personal fame replaced the nameless monk. Artistically, the flat treatment of miniatures gave way to figure-drawing, true laws of perspective, and atmospheric effects.

## European Miniature Portraiture
Beyond manuscripts, miniature painting developed as a distinct genre of portraiture. In France, Francois Dumont (1751-1831) was considered one of the three greatest miniature painters of his country. He painted portraits of Marie Antoinette, Louis XVI, Charles X, and Louis XVIII. Dumont's work, as well as that of his younger brother Tony Dumont—who specialized in portraits of violinists—is characterized by precision, breadth, and a charming coloring scheme.

In Britain, Nathaniel Hone (1718-1784) achieved his primary success through enamels and miniatures, which he executed with masterly capacity. Although he was a foundation member of the Royal Academy, he experienced disagreements with fellow members who rejected two of his pictures.

Other notable figures include Philip Fruytiers (1627-1666), a Flemish painter and engraver described as an "illuminator." While no undoubted productions of his hand are preserved, some miniatures on vellum found in European collections, which recall the work of Rubens, may be his. Additionally, various artists produced miniatures of prominent figures; for example, James Holmes and George Sanders both produced miniatures of Lord Byron.

## The American Revival and the Society of Painters in Miniature
In the United States, the art of miniature painting experienced a revival in the late 19th century. William Jacob Baer (born January 29, 1860, in Cincinnati, Ohio) was a significant figure in this movement. After studying in Munich from 1880 to 1884, Baer turned to miniature painting in 1892. He served as the first president of the Society of Painters in Miniature, New York. His known miniatures include "Madonna with the Auburn Hair," "In Arcadia," "Daphne," and "The Golden Hour."
