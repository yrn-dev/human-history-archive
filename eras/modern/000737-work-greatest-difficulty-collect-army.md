## The Silesian Campaign of 1741
In February 1741, the Austrians organized a field army under the command of Count Neipperg (1684-1774) with the intent of reconquering Silesia. While Austrian forces continued to hold out in Brieg and Neisse, the Prussians, led by Prince Leopold (the younger) of Anhalt-Dessau, successfully stormed Glogau on the night of March 9, completing the operation in one hour with mathematical precision.

As the Austrian army in Moravia entered the field, Frederick's cantonments were spread across Upper Silesia. Collecting the army became a work of the greatest difficulty because the ground was deep in snow. Before the assembly was completed, the march of Neipperg from Neisse to Brieg relieved Neisse and cut the Prussians off from their own country. This period of slow maneuvering culminated in the battle of Mollwitz on April 10, 1741. Although the Prussian cavalry right wing was quickly routed, the infantry's tenacity and discipline secured the field. Frederick, who had participated in the cavalry melee, was persuaded by Field Marshal Schwerin to ride away when the battle appeared lost, narrowly avoiding capture by Austrian hussars. The victory allowed the king to secure Brieg, while Neipperg retreated to Neisse.

## The Campaigns of Sertorius
In Spain, Sertorius faced significant difficulties regarding the nature of his campaigns and the soldiers under his command. After a battle in the plains near Seguntum, where he was victorious but saw the victory shift as Romans rescued the wounded Metellus, Sertorius retired to a strong city in the mountains. His objective was not to endure a siege, but to deceive the enemy so he could collect a fresh army without interruption.

Sertorius achieved this by sending officers to various cities with orders to gather a body of men and then dispatch a messenger. These messengers were able to break through the besiegers to join his troops. Once his force was reconstituted, Sertorius utilized expeditionary attacks and ambuscades to cut off land supplies. He further isolated the enemy by using piratical vessels to occupy the coast and cut off maritime supplies, eventually forcing the opposing generals to separate.

## Macedonian Advance into India
The Macedonian army encountered extreme difficulty during their campaign in India. After overcoming King Porus—who had deployed twenty thousand infantry and two thousand cavalry—the Macedonians were reluctant to advance further. Alexander faced violent opposition when attempting to cross the river Ganges, which was reported to be a hundred cubits deep and thirty-two furlongs wide. 

The Macedonian forces were faced with the prospect of an army consisting of two hundred thousand foot soldiers, eighty thousand horsemen, six thousand elephants, and eight thousand war chariots, belonging to the kings of the Præsiæ and Gandaritæ. The scale of military power in the region was further evidenced by Androkottus, the king of the country, who later subdued all of India with an army of six hundred thousand men and presented five hundred elephants to Seleukus.

## The Siege of Jerusalem
During the conflict involving Titus and the Israelites, the Roman command faced difficulties in managing a large army against a city of great magnitude. Titus's commanders were divided; some advocated for a full-scale storming of the walls, while others suggested a cautious approach of guarding against the exit of the Israelites and waiting for famine to take hold.

Titus rejected these options, noting that it was impracticable to cast up more banks due to a lack of materials. He also argued that encompassing the entire city was difficult because of the situation and the magnitude of the city, which made the army vulnerable to sallies. Furthermore, he feared that the Israelites, being well acquainted with the area, would contrive secret passages to bring in provisions, thereby delaying the siege and diminishing the glory of his success.

## The Campaign of 1743
The 1743 campaign began with difficulties for the emperor due to quarrels between Seckendorf and Broglie, which hindered the cooperation of the French and Bavarian armies. This lack of connected resistance allowed Prince Charles's army, along with forces from Prince Lobkowitz and Khevenhueller, to advance. 

As the French and Bavarians were driven toward the Rhine, King George II led an Anglo-allied army southward. Marshal Noailles collected a French army on the middle Rhine to oppose them. In the defile between the river Main and the Spessart Hills, George found himself in a position of the greatest danger, blocked by Noailles. However, the allied troops forced their way through, resulting in the battle of Dettingen on June 27, a notable British victory. Following this, the French were forced onto the defensive behind the Rhine.
