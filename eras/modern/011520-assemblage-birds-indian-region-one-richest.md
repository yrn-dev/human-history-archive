# The Indian Zoogeographical Region

The Indian region is one of the primary zoogeographical divisions of the world. The concept of these great regions was first introduced by P.L. Sclater, who divided the globe into the "Old World" (_Palaeogaea_)—comprising the Palaearctic, Ethiopian, Indian, and Australian regions—and the "New World" (_Neogaea_), consisting of the Nearctic and Neotropical regions. These divisions were based largely on the distribution of birds, specifically the arboreal birds known as the order Insessores or Passeres (including Passeriformes, Coraciiformes, and Cuculiformes), as birds are uniquely adapted for rapid and extended locomotion.

## Avian Diversity and Characteristics
The assemblage of birds in the Indian region is among the most varied and richest in the world, surpassed only by the birds of tropical America. Nearly every order is well represented, with the exception of the Struthiones (ostriches). The region is characterized by several peculiar genera not found elsewhere, including _Buceros_, _Harpactes_, _Lophophorus_, _Euplocamus_, _Pajo_, and _Ceriornis_. 

Certain groups are highly characteristic of this region, such as the _Phasianidae_ (excluding true _Phasianus_), specific genera of crows (_Cissa_, _Urocissa_, and _Dendrocitta_), parrots (_Palaeornis_), and barbets (_Megalaema_). The family _Eurylaemidae_ is entirely confined to this part of Asia. Other prevalent forms include vultures (_Vulturidae_) and bulbuls (_Ixidae_), the latter of which includes the subfamily _Phyllornithinae_ that is peculiar to the Indian region. While some naturalists include finches (_Fringillidae_) in this group, this assumes that weavers (_Ploceidae_) are a separate family. In contrast, woodpeckers (_Picidae_) and thrushes (_Turdidae_) are weakly represented; of 400 woodpecker species, only a few cross the boundary into the Moluccas, Celebes, or Lombok.

## Mammals and Marine Life
The boundaries of the Indian region are clearly demarcated by the distribution of mammals. The margin of the Australian region is reached at Wallace's line; for example, Marsupials are found in Celebes and the Molucca Islands but are absent from the Indian region and the adjacent islands of Borneo and Java. Similarly, mammals such as Ruminants, Insectivora, Carnivora, and Quadrumana abound in the western Archipelago but are absent from Celebes and the Moluccas. In Celebes, the ruminant _Anoa_ and a peculiar baboon, _Cynopithecus_, appear allied to African animals rather than Asiatic ones.

In the Indian Ocean, the Dugong (_Halicore_) is found exclusively, while the dolphin _Platanista_ is peculiar to the Ganges river, where it ascends a great distance from the sea.

## Regional Affinities and Boundaries
The Indian region shares various affinities with neighboring zones. There are 71 genera common to the Indian region that do not occur in the Ethiopian region, though 51 genera are shared between the Indian, Ethiopian, and Palaearctic regions. 

The Ethiopian fauna occupies a subordinate role in Asia, primarily intruding into the desert districts of Syria and Arabia and the south-western corner of the continent. Some Ethiopian species extend into Sind, Persia, and western India, including the hunting leopard and the lion. In birds, the Ethiopian influence is marked by the prevalence of stone chats and larks and the absence of the peculiar genera typical of the Indian region.

## Ichthyology and Flora
In terms of fish diversity, the Indian region ranks third globally with approximately 800 species, following the Tropical American (1300) and African (1000) regions. There is a noted resemblance between the Indian and African fish regions, which is far greater than the resemblance between these and other equatorial regions.

The flora of south-eastern Asia—including the Malayan peninsula, islands, and India from the 65th to the 105th meridian—is estimated at 12,000 to 15,000 indigenous species. The dominant orders include Leguminosae, Rubiaceae, Orchidaceae, Compositae, Gramineae, Euphorbiaceae, Acanthaceae, Cyperaceae, and Labiatae. The vegetation varies significantly by environment:
* **Tropical Flora:** Found in hotter, wetter regions of eastern India and the Malayan peninsula.
* **Himalayan Ranges:** Higher, cooler elevations show a closer approach to European forms, with some alpine plants identical to those in north Arctic regions.
* **Tibetan Plateau:** Due to increased dryness, the flora assumes Siberian features, including many Siberian genera and species. Additionally, marine plants are found at elevations of 14,000 to 15,000 feet near salt lakes.
