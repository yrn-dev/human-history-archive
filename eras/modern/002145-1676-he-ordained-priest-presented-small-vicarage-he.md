# Ordination and Ecclesiastical Appointments

The process of ordination to the priesthood and the subsequent presentation to ecclesiastical benefices, such as vicarages and rectories, are recurring themes in the historical records of various religious and scholarly figures. These appointments often coincided with academic achievements or the patronage of influential figures.

## Early Modern and Modern Clerical Appointments

In the 17th century, Adrien Baillet (1649–1706), a French scholar and critic, followed a path from education to the priesthood. After receiving a thorough education at a theological seminary through the kindness of the bishop of Beauvais and serving as a teacher at the college of Beauvais, Baillet was ordained priest in 1676. Following his ordination, he was presented to a small vicarage. Baillet later transitioned into scholarship, becoming the librarian to M. de Lamoignon in 1680. He was known for his intense devotion to study, allowing himself only five hours of rest per day. His works include *Jugemens des savans*, which earned him the hatred of the Jesuits due to his praise of the Jansenists, and *Vie des saints*, which caused scandal because of his critical approach to miracles.

In England, several clergymen combined their religious duties with literary or academic pursuits. Henry Francis Cary (1772–1844), an author and translator, took holy orders and was presented to the vicarage of Abbott's Bromley in Staffordshire in 1797, a benefice he held until his death. He was also presented to the vicarage of Kingsbury in Warwickshire in 1800. Cary is noted for his translations of Dante's *Divina Commedia*, as well as works by Pindar and Aristophanes.

Similarly, Charles Kingsley (1819–1875) was ordained in 1842 to the curacy of Eversley in Hampshire. He was shortly thereafter presented to the rectory of Eversley, which remained his home for thirty-three years. Kingsley's career extended into high-level appointments, including serving as chaplain to Queen Victoria in 1859 and holding a professorship of modern history at Cambridge from 1860 to 1869, before becoming a canon at Chester and later at Westminster.

## Academic and Institutional Paths to Priesthood

The transition from university study to the priesthood is documented in several instances. One such individual entered Oriel College, Oxford, in March 1715. After taking his degree in 1718, he was ordained as both deacon and priest. Through the recommendations of Clarke and Edward Talbot (the bishop of Oxford, and later of Salisbury and Durham), he was nominated as a preacher at the chapel of the Rolls until 1726. He was granted a prebend at Salisbury in 1721 and later presented to the rectory of Stanhope in 1725.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, Robert Hugh Benson (b. 1871) took orders after studying at Eton and Trinity College, Cambridge. After becoming a member of the Community of the Resurrection at Mirfield in 1898, he converted to Roman Catholicism in 1903. He was ordained priest in Rome the following year and subsequently served as the assistant priest of the Roman Catholic church in Cambridge.

## Medieval and Early Catholic Ordinations

Historical records also detail the ordination of statesmen and religious founders. John Kirkby (d. 1290) served as a clerk of the chancery and vice-chancellor under Edward I. Despite holding several valuable church benefices before being ordained, he was not ordained priest until after he was elected bishop of Ely in 1286, at which point he was ordained and consecrated by John Peckham, the archbishop of Canterbury.

In the 16th century, Ignatius and his companions sought to preach the Gospel in Palestine. After receiving leave and alms from Pope Paul III, those who were not yet priests were granted license to be ordained by any catholic bishop on the title of poverty. Ignatius and his companions were ordained priests on June 24, 1537, in Venice, after renewing vows of chastity and poverty to the legate Verallo. Ignatius waited eighteen months after his ordination before celebrating his first Mass on December 25, 1538, at the church of Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome.

## Ancient Israelite and Roman Contexts

In the records of the Israelites, King David organized the priesthood and the tribe of Levi to serve God. David numbered the Levites and appointed twenty-three thousand to care for the temple building, while others served as judges, scribes, porters, and singers. David separated the priests into twenty-four courses—sixteen from the house of Eleazar and eight from the house of Ithamar. He ordained that each course would minister to God for eight days, from sabbath to sabbath.

During the Roman period, Caesar Augustus issued a decree recognizing the customs of the Israelites. The decree mentioned Hyrcanus the high priest and granted the people liberty to follow the laws of their forefathers. This included the protection of their sacred money and the right not to appear before a judge on the sabbath or the day of preparation after the ninth hour.
