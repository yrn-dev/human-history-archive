# The Settlement with Hungary

The settlement with Hungary, often referred to as the "Ausgleich" (compact or compromise), established the system of dualism within the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy. This arrangement transformed the former Austrian Empire into a Dual Monarchy consisting of two independent states: the "lands of St Stephen's Crown" (unofficially called Hungary or Transleithania) and the kingdoms and lands represented in the council of the empire (unofficially called Austria or Cisleithania).

## Structure of the Settlement
The settlement was divided into three distinct parts:
1. **The Political Settlement**: This was designed to be permanent and became part of the monarchy's fundamental constitution.
2. **The Periodical Financial Settlement**: This determined the partition of common expenses, which were arranged by Quota-Deputations and ratified by the respective parliaments.
3. **The Customs Union and Currency Agreement**: This was a voluntary and terminable arrangement made between the two governments and parliaments.

The general settlement between Austria and Hungary typically took place every ten years. Because these reforms were dealt with as a general bargain, concessions in one area—such as commercial policy—were often set off against gains in another, such as the financial agreement. For example, in 1877, Austria provided Hungary a share in the management of the bank, while Austria benefited from the arrangement for paying the bonus on exported sugar.

## Common Affairs and Administration
Common affairs were managed by common ministries and focused exclusively on the army and the empire's foreign policy. These matters were the sole occupation of the Delegations. The annual meetings of these Delegations served as a venue for the foreign minister to justify his policy, often utilizing "red books" containing important despatches. Generally, the army budget was passed with few unimportant alterations.

The common budget covered expenditures for the army, foreign affairs, and the ministry of finance. Revenue for this budget was derived from the joint ministries, net proceeds from customs, and the "quota"—the proportional contributions of the two states. Until 1897, Austria contributed 70% and Hungary 30% of the joint expenditure remaining after common revenues were deducted. From 1897 to July 1907, the quota was adjusted to 66-46/49 for Austria and 33-2/49 for Hungary. In 1907, Hungary's contribution rose to 36.4%. Additionally, 2% of total charges were debited to Hungary due to the incorporation of the former military frontier.

## Terminology and Dualism
The principle of Dualism was reflected in the official titles of the sovereign. By a letter dated November 14, 1868, the emperor ordered his style to be changed to "Emperor of Austria, King of Bohemia, &c., and Apostolic King of Hungary," signifying the separation of the two districts. The realm became known as "The Austrian-Hungarian Monarchy" or "The Austrian-Hungarian Realm."

This distinction extended to public service terminology. The label "Imperial and Royal" (*Kaiserlich und Koeniglich*) was applied to branches belonging to common ministries, starting with the diplomatic service, followed by the army in 1889, and the ministry of the imperial house in 1895. The Austrian half of the monarchy retained the style *Kaiserlich-Koeniglich* ("K.K."). Consequently, the monarchy maintained three ministries of finance: the *Kaiserlich und Koeniglich* for joint affairs, the *Kaiserlich-Koeniglich* for Austrian affairs, and the *Kiralye* for Hungary. In 1871, the title of *Reichskanzler* for the minister of foreign affairs was abandoned to avoid implying the existence of a single state.

## Historical Negotiations and Challenges
The main principles of the agreement were decided in the spring of 1867, with negotiations for the court of Vienna entrusted to Beust. In the summer of 1867, deputations of fifteen members from each parliament arranged the financial settlement. A primary point of contention was a debt exceeding 3,000 million gulden. Hungary refused responsibility for debts contracted while they were deprived of constitutional liberties, agreeing only to pay 29 1/2 million gulden annually toward the interest. Austria assumed responsibility for the remaining interest (over 100 million gulden annually) and the debt's management.

The first full settlement took two years, from 1876 to June 1878. Negotiations were conducted under serious difficulties due to national jealousy and race hatred within the two parliaments. The process required the two ministries to agree on bill drafts, followed by review by elected committees and parliamentary debates.

Political instability occasionally threatened the monarchy. In 1877, constitutional parties in Austria voted against essential duties on coffee and petroleum and opposed provisions of the Treaty of Berlin. This lack of party unity forced Austrian ministers and Andrassy to seek support from the Poles, who subsequently became a government party.

## The 1907 Agreement
In October 1907, a new customs and commercial treaty was reached following an economic crisis in Hungary and the realization in Austria that separation would increase the cost of living in Cisleithania. Key features included:
* Separate but identical customs tariffs for each state.
* Mutual facilitation of railway communications (Vienna to Dalmatia by 1911, and Hungary to Prussia).
* Hungarian reform of produce and Stock Exchange laws to prevent agrarian speculation.
* The establishment of a court of arbitration with four judges from each state and a presidency decided by lot.
* The practical abolition of impediments to free trade in sugar.
* Provisions for Hungary to redeem its share of the old Austrian debt.
