# Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, and South Carolina

The territories of Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, and South Carolina represent a diverse cross-section of early American colonial development, spanning from the Middle Colonies to the South. These regions were characterized by varied religious foundations, complex political loyalties during the War of Independence, and their roles in the later struggle against slavery.

## Colonial Foundations and Boundaries
Pennsylvania was established by the Quaker Penn, a friend of Charles II, who founded the colony at Philadelphia. Its boundaries with Virginia and Maryland were defined by the "Mason and Dixon line," a demarcation that later became significant in United States affairs. To the south, the region of Carolina had a complex origin; while some sources suggest it was named for Charles II or Charles I of England, others state it was originally an unsuccessful French Protestant establishment named after Charles IX of France. Eventually, Carolina fell into English hands and was settled at various points.

By 1732, the town of Savannah was settled by Oglethorpe, an English philanthropist who sought to provide a home for people imprisoned for debt in England. Through these various developments, the entire coast from Maine to Carolina became British possessions, though the Spanish maintained headquarters at Fort St. Augustine in Florida to the south.

## Political Development and the War of Independence
By 1774, these colonies were involved in the growing conflict with the British Parliament. In September of that year, Philadelphia hosted the first colonial Congress, where Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, and South Carolina were among the twelve colonies represented. This body acted as an insurrectionary government and produced a "Declaration of Rights."

During this era, voting practices varied. By 1775, Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, and South Carolina had already provided for the use of written or printed ballots, whereas viva voce voting remained common in other colonies like Maryland and Virginia.

The War of Independence created deep divisions within these colonies. In the southern colonies, including North and South Carolina, the division between Loyalists (Tories) and Whigs did not follow religious lines as closely as it did in the north. While Anglicanism predominated, many Whig leaders in South Carolina were members of the established church; only five of twenty Episcopal ministers in South Carolina were Loyalists. Tory support in the south was primarily found among non-Anglican Scotch and Germans in the upper country. These frontier settlers often felt snubbed by the low-country aristocracy and were denied adequate representation in the colonial assembly and access to courts of justice.

## Military Engagements in the Carolinas
The Carolinas were the site of significant military action. On October 7, 1780, the Battle of King's Mountain took place on a ridge spanning Gaston County, North Carolina, and York County, South Carolina. An American force of approximately 900 backwoodsmen defeated about 1,000 Loyalist militia and 100 Provincial Rangers under Major Patrick Ferguson. This victory aided General Nathanael Greene's campaign against Lord Cornwallis.

Francis Marion, a soldier of Huguenot ancestry born near Georgetown, South Carolina, became a key figure in partisan warfare. After the capture of Charleston in May 1780, Marion organized a small troop of 20 to 70 men. Known as the "old swamp fox" for his ability to elude Colonel Banastre Tarleton via swamp paths, Marion led irregular troops to capture Maryland prisoners and surprise British and Loyalist forces. In 1781, Marion and Colonel Henry Lee successfully took Fort Motte and Fort Watson, breaking British communications between the Carolinas.

## The Underground Railroad and Social Issues
In the 19th century, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and North Carolina were linked by the Underground Railroad, a system used to move slaves to freedom in Canada and New England. The Quakers of Pennsylvania are credited with possibly beginning this work. Thomas Garrett, a Pennsylvania native who moved to Wilmington, Delaware, in 1822, is said to have helped 2,700 slaves; he was fined $8,000 in 1848 under the Fugitive Slave Law.

Levi Coffin, a native of North Carolina, also played a major role. His cousin, Vestal Coffin, had established a "station" near what is now Guilford College, North Carolina, before 1819. Levi Coffin later settled in Ohio, where he successfully brought slaves out of the South. Harriet Tubman, referred to as "Moses" by fellow negroes and "General" by John Brown, made approximately twenty trips into the South, rescuing 300 negroes.

Additionally, the term "lynch law"—referring to summary justice administered without legal procedure—has an obscure origin. Some theories suggest the name originated from Lynch's Creek, South Carolina, while others point to individuals named Lynch in Pennsylvania and Virginia.
