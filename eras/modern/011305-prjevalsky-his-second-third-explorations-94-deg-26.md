# Nicolai Prjevalsky

Nicolai Prjevalsky was a prominent Russian explorer of the 19th century whose scientific expeditions significantly modified existing geographical understanding of the vast central regions of Asia, specifically Tibet and Mongolia. His work contributed to the precise definition of the great highland plateau that extends from the frontier of Kashmir eastwards to China, and from the Himalaya northwards to Chinese Turkestan.

## Major Explorations and Discoveries

Between 1871 and 1873, Prjevalsky crossed the Gobi desert from the north to Kansu in western China. During this period, he mapped the hydrography of the Tsaidam region, which serves as the source for the great rivers of Burma, Siam, and China. He also traveled south, reaching a point within a month's march of Lhasa.

In 1876, Prjevalsky visited Lop Nor and discovered the Altyn Tagh range. This was followed by an expedition in 1879, during which he followed the Urangi river to the Altai Mountains. During this journey, he traversed the Gobi from Hami to Sachu and crossed and named the Dzungarian extension of the Gobi desert. He also visited the sources of the Salween and the Hwang-ho (Yellow river). His findings from this period demonstrated the extraordinary physical changes that had occurred in the heart of the Asiatic continent since the time of Jenghiz Khan.

Prjevalsky's fourth journey, conducted between 1883 and 1885, took him to Sining, a major trade center of the Chinese borderland. From there, he traveled through northern Tibet, crossing the Altyn Tagh to reach Lop Nor. He then utilized the Cherchen-Keriya trade route to reach Khotan, from where he followed the Tarim to Aksu.

## Geographical Contributions and Longitude

Prjevalsky's explorations provided critical data for modern map construction, particularly regarding the position of Sachu (or Saitu) in Mongolia. In the map accompanying the account of his fourth exploration, Prjevalsky provided a revised longitude value for Sachu of 94 deg. 54' E. of Greenwich, which has since been adopted. This revised figure differed from the value he established during his second and third explorations, which was 94 deg. 26'.

## Scientific Controversy and Lob-nor

Prjevalsky's work on the situation of Lob-nor led to a controversy with Baron von Richthofen. Prjevalsky identified the water of the lake as sweet and placed its location one degree further south than Chinese topographers had. 

Baron von Richthofen disagreed, arguing that Prjevalsky had traveled by an ancient road to a point south of the true Lop-noor. Richthofen suggested that Prjevalsky had followed a "new" river course—relative to the time of Kien-long—to reach two sweet-water lakes, one of which corresponded to the ancient Khas-omo. Prjevalsky provided replies to these criticisms in the volume *From Kulja across the Tian Shan to Lob-nor* (1879). Other observers have noted that there may have been several co-existent lakes in the region, and that the lack of fixity in the soil and water spreads of this part of Asia often leads to discrepancies between the observations of different travelers.

## Legacy and Influence

Prjevalsky is regarded as one of the greatest modern Asiatic explorers. His scientific principles influenced subsequent Russian explorers, such as Roborovski and Pevtsov, who in 1889-1890 (and again in 1894) expanded the knowledge of the northern borders of Tibet and the topography of western Chinese Turkestan. He is mentioned alongside other distinguished 19th-century Russian explorers in Asia, including Potanin, P.K. Kozlov, Annentkov, and Lessar.
