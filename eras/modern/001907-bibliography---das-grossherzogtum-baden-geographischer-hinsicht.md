# Das Grossherzogtum Baden in geographischer ... Hinsicht

*Das Grossherzogtum Baden in geographischer ... Hinsicht dargestellt* is a work published in Karlsruhe in 1885. It is listed as a bibliographical resource for the study of the Grand Duchy of Baden.

## Historical Context of Baden
The history of the region that now forms the Grand Duchy of Baden began in 1112 with Hermann, the son of the margrave of Verona and grandson of Bertold, count of Zaehringen and duke of Carinthia. Hermann assumed the title of margrave of Baden, a title likely chosen due to his family's connection to the margraviate of Verona. 

By approximately 1200, the territories of Hermann's son and grandson (both also named Hermann) were divided, leading to the establishment of the Baden-Baden and Baden-Hochberg lines. The Baden-Hochberg branch further split about a century later into the Baden-Hochberg and Baden-Sausenberg lines. The Baden-Baden family expanded its holdings, and in 1391, Margrave Bernard I united several divisions. Bernard also acquired other districts, including Baden-Hochberg after its ruling family became extinct in 1418.

## Territorial Shifts and Dynastic Divisions
In the 15th century, Margrave Charles I (who died in 1475) lost part of his territories during a war with the count palatine of the Rhine, though these were later recovered by his son, Christopher I. In 1503, the Baden-Sausenberg family became extinct, allowing Christopher to unite the whole of Baden. Before his death in 1527, Christopher divided the territory among his three sons. 

Following the death of one son without children in 1533, the remaining sons, Ernest and Bernard, shared the territories in 1535, founding the lines of Baden-Baden and Baden-Pforzheim. The latter was renamed Baden-Durlach after 1565. These partitions led to weakness and rivalry between the two main branches, resulting in open warfare from 1584 to 1622, during which Baden-Baden was held by a prince of Baden-Durlach.

## Religious Conflict and Reunification
Rivalries within the house of Baden were exacerbated by the Reformation, as different rulers adopted either the newer faith or adhered to the older one. This division persisted through the Thirty Years' War, a struggle that caused severe suffering and the alternating exile of both family branches. The *status quo* was restored by the treaty of Westphalia in 1648, and the family rivalry eventually ceased.

During the wars of Louis XIV, French troops ravaged the margraviate. Louis William, the margrave of Baden-Baden (d. 1707), was a prominent soldier in the resistance against French aggressions. The territories were eventually reunified in 1771 when Augustus George of Baden-Baden died without sons, and his lands passed to Charles Frederick of Baden-Durlach. Frederick later assumed the title of grand-duke on September 5, 1856.
