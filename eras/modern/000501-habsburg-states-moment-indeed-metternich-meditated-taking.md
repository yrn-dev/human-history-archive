# Habsburg States

The Habsburg states comprised a complex monarchy of diverse territories in Central Europe, characterized by a long-standing tension between autocratic rule and the national aspirations of the populations it governed. The history of the empire is marked by periods of territorial expansion, diplomatic efforts to maintain European stability, and internal constitutional crises.

## Early Challenges and the Reign of Maria Theresa
The accession of Maria Theresa marked a critical epoch for the Habsburgs. The monarchy faced potential dissolution in 1740 when Frederick the Great invaded Silesia, exploiting the disorganized state of Austrian forces following the Turkish wars. This conflict brought several powers—including France, Spain, Bavaria, Saxony, and Sardinia—into the field against Austria. Internally, the dynasty suffered from unpopularity due to financial ruin, administrative corruption, and the discontent of Slavs and Magyars. 

However, Maria Theresa's resolution and judgment provided unexpected strength. Through personal appeals and timely concessions, she secured the support of the Hungarians, who developed a feeling of loyalty to the Habsburg monarch. Despite these efforts, the treaty of Dresden resulted in the transfer of Silesia to Prussia, and the treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) involved cessions in Italy.

## The Policy of Stability and Metternich
Following the reign of Leopold II—who restored the supremacy of the Church and traditional diets to counter the influence of the French Revolution—the Habsburgs adopted a policy of "stability." This approach was most prominently associated with Metternich, whose diplomacy aimed to prevent any liberal or national movements that might shatter the "rotten structure" of the monarchy.

Metternich's influence was solidified at the Congress of Vienna (September 1814), where Austria sought to re-establish the European balance of power. Under his guidance, Austria regained Illyria and Dalmatia, and added Lombardy to Venetia to create a kingdom under the Habsburg crown. In Germany, Austria opted for a loose confederation of sovereign states (*Staatenbund*) under its presidency rather than restoring the Holy Empire. Metternich believed this structure would guarantee the preponderance of Austria and the peace of Central Europe.

For thirty years, Metternich imposed this system of stability. He influenced other monarchs, such as Frederick William III of Prussia, against granting popular constitutions, and supported the "legitimate" authority of the sultan over the Greeks to maintain the integrity of Turkey. His diplomacy was largely successful for fifteen years after the Congress of Vienna; even the 1830 revolution in Paris and the insurrection in Poland failed to provoke actual movements within the Habsburg states, though Metternich briefly considered supporting the Poles to re-establish a barrier against the Russian Empire.

## Internal Governance and the 1848 Revolutions
The internal conditions of the monarchy under Francis II were characterized by autocratic temper and intellectual obscurantism. Francis II was viewed by the Viennese with a certain *bonhomie*, but he lacked the qualities required for the critical period of the monarchy's history.

The fall of Metternich signaled a widespread outburst of revolution across Central Europe in 1848. In Hungary, the government was forced on March 31 to consent to a new constitution that virtually made Hungary an independent state. On April 8, a separate constitution was promised to Bohemia. The Austrian government's impotence during this crisis was exacerbated by the need to keep the bulk of its forces in Italy, where a concerted rising against Habsburg rule had occurred.

## Constitutional Evolution and Bohemia
Following the Italian campaign of 1859, absolutism became untenable. The empire attempted to establish a constitutional system that recognized the ancient rights of various countries. A decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, which was welcomed in Bohemia. However, a subsequent decree on February 26, 1861, established a central parliament in Vienna with extensive powers and an electoral system biased toward Germans. This led the Bohemians to leave the parliament in 1863.

The Austro-Prussian war of 1866 further altered the landscape. The treaty signed at Prague on August 23, 1866, excluded all Habsburg-ruled lands from Germany. While Hungary obtained complete independence, the new constitution of 1867 for the German and Slavic parts of the empire maintained centralization and waning German influence, which the Bohemians energetically opposed.

In 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the empire by recognizing the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. This effort failed, and by 1872, a government with a pronounced German tendency took office. Later, under Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe (1879), Bohemian representatives returned to the parliament in Vienna. In exchange, the government removed some electoral anomalies that favored the German minority and permitted the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.
