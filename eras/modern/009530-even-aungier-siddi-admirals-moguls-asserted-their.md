# The Settlement of Bombay under Gerald Aungier

## Foundation and Administration
The modern foundation of the city of Bombay was the work of Gerald Aungier (also spelled Angier), the brother of Francis Aungier, the 1st earl of Longford and 3rd Lord Aungier of Longford in Ireland. Aungier succeeded Sir George Oxenden as the president of Surat in 1669 and served until his death on June 30, 1677. In 1672, Aungier transferred his headquarters to Bombay, where he began to organize the settlement.

Aungier operated under the constraints of a "niggardly policy" from the court of directors, who refused to fund the employment of skilled engineers. Despite this, he succeeded in fortifying the shore and the town. To ensure security, he raised a force of regulars and militia; the regulars consisted primarily of Germans, who were considered more trustworthy than the individuals recruited by the Company's crimps in London.

## Social and Fiscal Reforms
Aungier's administration was guided by a belief in the traditions of English liberty and a desire to move away from the oppressive nature of previous Portuguese rule, which had been characterized by religious intolerance, cruel treatment of natives, and oppressive fiscal policies. 

With the consent of a general assembly of the people's chief representatives, Aungier replaced the burdensome land tax with a fixed money payment. He also forbade the forced carrying of burdens by natives and protected all castes in the celebration of their religious ceremonies. These reforms led to a rapid increase in population, which grew from 10,000 to 60,000 within eight years. This growth attracted Armenians and Parsees, as well as the Hindu capitalist class known as the banya, for whom a special quarter was established. However, this concentration of people in an unwholesome location resulted in the prevalence of disease due to appalling sanitary conditions.

## Strategic Threats and Naval Relations
During Aungier's tenure, Bombay's position was precarious, isolated between the Mogul empire and the Mahrattas. The settlement faced threats from the Mahrattas inland, as well as from the Dutch and Malabar pirates at sea. The Portuguese further complicated the situation by occupying the island of Salsette and establishing a customs-barrier in the channel between the shore and Bombay. In 1670, Aungier successfully frightened off an imposing Dutch fleet that had attempted to surprise the island.

Regarding the Mogul empire, the Siddi admirals asserted their right to use the Bombay harbour as winter quarters for their fleet, even during Aungier's leadership. However, the Siddis failed to secure the harbour as a base for operations against the Mahrattas.

## Decline and Political Instability
Following Aungier's death in 1677, the situation in Bombay became more precarious under his successor, Rolt (1677–1682), who is described as weak. During this period, the English waters became a battle-ground for rival navies, leaving Bombay at the mercy of both.

Internal instability also plagued the Company's rule. The board of directors' focus on peaceful dividends over "warlike rule" caused friction with soldiers of fortune. This tension led to a mutiny in 1674, which was suppressed by executing the ringleader. A more significant movement occurred in 1683 led by Richard Keigwin, a naval officer and former governor of St Helena. Elected governor of Bombay by popular vote, Keigwin declared his government to be under the immediate authority of the crown, citing the "exactions, oppressions and intolerable extortions" of the Company.

Keigwin's rule was characterized by moderation; he reformed taxation, increased port trade by admitting "interlopers," and secured notable concessions from the Mahrattas. However, he was unable to extend his rebellion beyond Bombay. Upon receiving a letter under the royal sign manual ordering him to surrender the fort to Sir John Child—the appointed captain-general and admiral of the Company's forces—Keigwin obeyed.
