# Boston and Athletic Traditions

## Boston in History and Development
Boston, Massachusetts, has been a center for professional development, architecture, and finance in the United States. In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the city was shaped by figures such as Charles Bulfinch, an architect born in Boston in 1763. Bulfinch was the first professional architect to practice in the city, designing the "new" State House in 1798 and the old Federal Street theatre in 1793. Serving as chairman of the board of selectmen from 1797 to 1818, Bulfinch modernized the city by reorganizing fire and police departments and implementing new street-lighting and drainage systems.

The city also served as a hub for commerce and religion. Francis Cabot Lowell, a merchant in Boston and founder of cotton manufacturing in the U.S., died there in 1817. His brother, Charles Lowell, served as the pastor of Boston's West Congregational (Unitarian) Church from 1806 until his death in 1861. In the realm of art, the sculptor Thomas Ball produced several works for the city, including statues of Josiah Quincy in City Hall Square and Charles Sumner and George Washington in the Boston public gardens.

## Financial Role of Boston
Boston played a significant role in stabilizing the United States economy during the panic of 1893. When the treasury experienced heavy gold losses, a combination of leading bankers from several cities, including Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, and Chicago, provided a large portion of their holdings to replenish government reserves.

## Base-Ball in Boston
Base-ball, a national summer sport of the United States also popular in Japan and Canada, saw the organization of several famous clubs as its popularity spread. Among these were the Lowell and the Beacon clubs of Boston. The sport's origins are debated; some authorities suggest it derived from the English game of rounders, while others believe it originated in Cooperstown, New York, in 1839 via Abner Doubleday. Earlier variations of the game, such as "One Old Cat," were popular among schoolboys in the North Atlantic states at the start of the 19th century.

### Ancient Athleticism
In ancient times, athletic victors were granted high honors. In Sparta, the king would take victors with him on military expeditions to serve as his guard. Victors were often maintained for the rest of their lives by their country through pensions. Solon regulated these pensions, setting them at five hundred drachmas for Olympic game victors and one hundred for those of the Isthmian games. These champions were also exempted from civil employments and offices. Historians such as Pausanias, Diodorus Siculus, Thucydides, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus often dated occurrences by Olympiads, using the name and country of the chariot-race victors to identify them.
