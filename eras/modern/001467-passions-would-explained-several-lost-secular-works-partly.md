# Passions and Related Concepts in History and Philosophy

The term "passions" appears across various historical and philosophical contexts within the archive, ranging from the psychological theories of René Descartes to the dramatic works of Euripides and the musical compositions of J.S. Bach.

## Psychological Theory of Descartes
In the psychology of René Descartes, passions or emotions are defined as perceptions or sentiments of the mind. These are caused and maintained by movements of the vital spirits, though they refer specifically to the mind. Descartes reduces these passions to six primitive forms:
*   Admiration
*   Love
*   Hatred
*   Desire
*   Joy
*   Sadness

According to Descartes, the presentation of an object—such as something causing dread—can have a double effect. One effect is a reflex mechanical movement independent of the mind, where animal spirits reflected from the image on the pineal gland move through nervous tubes to trigger physical escape. The second effect involves vital spirits causing a movement in the gland, allowing the mind to perceive the affection of the organs and learn whether an object should be shunned, admired, hated, or loved. These perceptions are intended to dispose the mind to pursue what nature dictates as useful, although the resulting estimates of goods and evils are indistinct.

## Passions in Greek Drama
In the works of the Athenian playwright Euripides, the depiction of passion was a point of significant critical contention. Euripides depicted "illicit and fatal passion" in several of his female characters, specifically Phædra and Sthenobœa. While his opponents admitted that the stories themselves were true, they argued that such passions should be kept back and not produced on the stage. This criticism was viewed as evidence of the sensitive ethical criticism and the continued mythical faith of the age.

Furthermore, Euripides was accused by critics and the playwright Aristophanês of vulgarizing mythical heroes. His compositions were seen as being influenced by practical eloquence and physical philosophy learned under Anaxagoras. Critics derided the "effeminate pathos" he brought to the foreground, which was considered unworthy of the majesty associated with legendary heroes.

## Musical Passions of J.S. Bach
In the context of music history, "Passions" refers to a specific genre of ecclesiastical work. J.S. Bach composed the great Matthew and John Passions. There is also a Passion according to St Luke preserved in an indisputable Bach autograph, though responsible critics have described it as "worthless," suggesting Bach may have copied it due to personal interest.

Regarding other works, J.N. Forkel reported that Bach wrote five Passions. If a genuine Lucas-Passion existed, this report would be explained. Additionally, a lost Passion according to St Mark is known to have existed; movements from this work were preserved in the *Trauer-Ode* (a funeral ode for the Duchess Eberhardine). Based on these preserved movements, the Passion according to St Mark is judged to have been larger than the Passion according to St John.
