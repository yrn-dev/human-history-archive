# Scientific Geographical Exploration in Asia

The modern period of scientific geographical exploration in Asia has been characterized by a prolific series of expeditions, particularly those directed toward the central depressions and the elevated table-lands of Tibet and Mongolia. These efforts have significantly modified previous understandings of the orography of the vast central region, allowing for the definition of the great highland plateau—which extends from the Himalaya northwards to Chinese Turkestan and from the frontier of Kashmir eastwards to China—with comparative geographical exactness.

## Central Asia and the Tibetan Plateau

Much of the knowledge regarding the great plateau in southern Tibet was provided by native surveyors attached to the Indian survey, specifically Pundits Nain Singh and Krishna. Between 1865 and 1867, Nain Singh explored the sources of the Upper Brahmaputra and the Indus. Later, from 1874 to 1875, he traveled from the eastern frontiers of Kashmir to Lhasa, passing through the Tengri Nor lake.

Pundit Krishna conducted a remarkable journey between 1879 and 1882, traveling north from Lhasa through Tsaidam to Sachu (or Saitu) in Mongolia. He also traversed eastern Tibet to Darchendo (or Tachienlu), located on the borders of China on the high road to Peking. Although he failed to reach India via Upper Assam, he eventually crossed the Himalayas through a more westerly route.

Modern map construction relies on "obligatory points" for accuracy in this region. The longitude of Sachu in Mongolia is currently adopted as 94 deg. 54' E. of Greenwich, a revised value provided by Prjevalsky during his fourth exploration. Other recorded values for Sachu include 94 deg. 26' (Prjevalsky's second and third explorations), 94 deg. 23' (Krishna), 94 deg. 48' (Carey and Dalgleish), 94 deg. 49' (Littledale), and 94 deg. 58' (Kreitner). Similarly, the longitude of Darchendo is an obligatory point; the Royal Geographical Society adopts 102 deg. 12", while other values include 102 deg. 15" (Krishna), 102 deg. 5" (Kreitner), and 102 deg. 18" (Baber).

## Russian Explorations

Russian scientific enterprise has contributed high-value results to the study of Asia. Following Prjevalsky, explorers Pevtsov and Roborovski added significantly to the knowledge of the northern borders of Tibet and the topography of western Chinese Turkestan during expeditions in 1889-1890 and 1894. Other prominent 19th-century Russian explorers include Potanin, P.K. Kozlov, Lessar, and Annentkov, the latter of whom bridged the Trans-Caspian deserts via a railway.

## Western Asia and the Indian Frontiers

The northern and north-western frontiers of India have seen a remarkable extension of scientific geographical mapping rather than simple exploration. This progress was driven by the occupation of Chitral and Gilgit, the Afghan war of 1878-80, the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1885, and the systematic survey of Baluchistan beginning in 1880. These efforts produced maps extending from the Oxus to the Indus and from the Indus to the Euphrates. Key contributors to these professional labors included Major Percy M. Sykes, Captain H.B. Vaughan, and General Sir A. Houtum Schindler in Persia, as well as Cockerill and Sir George Robertson in the Hindu Kush and Kafiristan.

In Persia, steady scientific inquiry has resulted in a fairly complete geographical map. This includes detailed information on the harbors and coast of the Persian Gulf and interior passes. Such knowledge has highlighted the possibility of commercial development through trade roads linking the Karun, the Caspian, India (via Seistan), and the Persian Gulf.

## Arabia and the Near East

Progress in Arabia has been slower. While Colonel Wahab provided exact geographical knowledge of a limited area in the Aden hinterland, knowledge of the interior deserts remains largely based on the works of foreign travelers and Englishmen, including Sir Lewis Pelly, Wm. Gifford Palgrave, Sir Richard F. Burton, John Lewis Burckhardt, Karsten Niebuhr, and Visconte Joseph Halevy. Charles Doughty and Wilfrid S. Blunt described the district of Nejd and the waning glories of the Wahabi empire. However, these findings have not led to great practical issues, and commercial relations remained largely unchanged from their 1875 status.

In Mesopotamia, Syria, and Asia Minor, material development has been limited, though a German company holds a railway concession for the Euphrates Valley. In Palestine and regions beyond Jordan, researches by English surveyors and other explorers have served primarily to elucidate history rather than to increase material wealth or commercial enterprise.

## Status of Asiatic Surveys

By the close of the 19th century, western Asia (excluding Arabia) was considered largely free of geographical perplexity. Indian triangulation extensions have reached the eastern districts of Persia, the coast of Arabia via Makran, the mountain peaks of Nepal, and have branched into Siam and western China. These extensions have raised the standard of accuracy in Asiatic surveys to levels previously deemed unattainable. Despite this, enormous areas of eastern Siberia, Mongolia, and China remain in a state of "nebulous uncertainty" and primitive geographical knowledge.
