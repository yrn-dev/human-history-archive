# Insolvency and Bankruptcy

In legal and commercial usage, the terms "insolvency" and "bankruptcy" possess distinct significations, although they are sometimes used indiscriminately. Insolvency occurs when a person's financial liabilities exceed their means of meeting them. A person in this state may still continue their business affairs by utilizing credit—paying old debts by incurring new ones—and may potentially regain solvency without creditors becoming aware of their true condition.

Bankruptcy, derived from the Latin *bancus* (table or counter) and *ruptus* (broken), is the status of a debtor who has been declared unable to pay his debts through a judicial process. While a debtor may be insolvent without becoming bankrupt, it is impossible to be a bankrupt without being insolvent. Bankruptcy serves as a legal declaration of insolvency and establishes a statutory system for the administration of the debtor's property, removing it from their personal control. A debtor who is insolvent may still avoid bankruptcy if they can reach a voluntary arrangement with their creditors.

## Practical Construction of Insolvency
Insolvency is construed in a practical sense. A person is considered insolvent when the aggregate of their property, based on a fair valuation, is insufficient to pay their debts. However, a person is not necessarily insolvent simply because their realized assets are insufficient to meet their liabilities.

## Legal Administration and Proceedings
The administration of bankruptcy law is entrusted to district courts and carried out via court-appointed officers known as referees. Creditors are responsible for appointing a trustee or trustees of the estate. Following a judicial examination, a bankrupt may offer a composition to their creditors; however, this composition must be approved by the court after any objections have been heard.

Involuntary proceedings may be initiated against debtors owing $1,000 or more, subject to certain exceptions. For a creditor to petition, their debt must amount to $500. Regarding the eligibility of entities, any person, whether a trader or non-trader, may avail themselves of the act. Corporations possess a peculiarity in this regard: they may be petitioned against, but they cannot petition.

Furthermore, an insolvent estate may be administered in bankruptcy, where the law of bankruptcy dictates the order in which assets are divided among creditors. Under the Judicature Act 1875, an insolvent estate may also be administered in the chancery division. In such instances, the rules of bankruptcy regarding the valuation of future and contingent liabilities, the rights of secured and unsecured creditors, and provable debts and liabilities are observed.

## Historical and Regional Variations

### Scotland and England
In both Scotland and England, bankruptcy law developed as a remedy against the frauds of insolvent debtors. In Scotland, an act of parliament in 1621 declared that no debtor should fraudulently diminish the fund belonging to creditors after insolvency. Fraudulent dealing was presumed if a deed of assignment was executed gratuitously in favor of a confidential friend or near relation after debt was contracted.

The act of 1696 defined a "notour" or "notorious" bankrupt as a debtor who, while under diligence by caption or horning at the instance of creditors, fled, absconded for personal security, retired to a privileged place or abbey, was imprisoned, or defended their person by force, and was subsequently found insolvent by the lords of session. This definition was intended to remedy fraudulent preference. Later legislation removed exceptions for those exempted by special privileges or those absent from Scotland. Additionally, Scottish law does not recognize the English distinction between traders and non-traders. Under the statute, all assignations, voluntary dispositions, and other deeds made within sixty days before bankruptcy or after it were rendered null and void.

### International Law
The legal framework for insolvency varies by region:
*   **Canada:** The Dominion parliament has exclusive legislative power over bankruptcy and insolvency under the British North America Act 1867, though no existing Dominion act is in place (an 1875 act was repealed in 1880). Provincial legislatures handle matters such as imprisonment of debtors, fraudulent preferences, and the administration of deceased insolvents' estates. Quebec follows a French code based on *cessio bonorum*, while Ontario follows English law.
*   **Africa:** English law is substantially followed in Natal and Cape Colony. In the Transvaal, the Insolvency Law No. 13 of 1895 (Roman-Dutch law) provides for compulsory sequestration and voluntary surrender. The Orange River Colony follows a similar law.
*   **Other Regions:** The law is modeled on the English pattern in Jamaica, Hong Kong, Mauritius, Gambia, British Guiana, Trinidad, Tobago, Grenada, and the Straits Settlements. In India, the Indian Insolvency Act 1848, extended by the Act XI of 1889, regulates insolvency.

## Ancient Perspectives on Debt
Historical records indicate varying treatments of insolvent debtors. In the Teutonic codes, an insolvent debtor fell under the power of the creditor and was subject to chastisement and personal fetters. This is assimilated to the old Roman law of personal execution and addiction. In Rome, during the time of Columella, wealthy Romans used adjudged debtors to cultivate possessions. Caesar noted that some individuals, pressed by the magnitude of tributes, *ære alieno* (debt), or the injustice of the powerful, would declare themselves servants to nobles.
