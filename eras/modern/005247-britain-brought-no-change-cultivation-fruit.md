# Fruit Cultivation in Great Britain

## Historical Origins
In the early history of Britain, the invasion of the island brought no change to the management of ordinary domestic animals. Bee-keeping was practiced, and cattle and sheep were pastured on common lands belonging to the village, while pigs were kept in the woods, particularly in Kent. However, the cultivation of vegetables and fruit was likely almost entirely new. By the 7th century, most of the names for these crops were known, and they were almost all derived from Latin.

## Scale and Distribution of the Industry
The extent of the fruit industry in Great Britain is measured by the acreage of small fruit plantations and orchards. Official returns from the Board of Agriculture indicate a general expansion of orchard areas from 199,178 acres in 1888 to 234,660 acres in 1901. While there was a decline in 1892, this is attributed to a change in data collection; after 1891, returns were only collected from holdings larger than one acre, whereas previously they included holdings of a quarter of an acre or more. Because many holdings are smaller than one acre and fruit is also grown in other gardens and suburban areas, the actual land devoted to fruit culture exceeds official figures. By June 1908, official returns stated that 308,000 acres were devoted to all kinds of fruit cultivation.

The distribution of orchards is determined by climate and soil conditions. The twelve counties with the largest extent of orchard land are located in the west or south of the island. Expansion has been mainly confined to England, while Scotland and Wales have seen slight decreases.

## Orchard Fruits
Apples are the primary fruit grown in the south-western and western counties. In Middlesex, apples, pears, and plums predominate. Kent produces large quantities of pears, cherries, plums, apples, and damsons for the market. In Cambridgeshire, plums are grown almost as extensively as apples. Other areas with plum orchards include the Pershore and Evesham districts of Worcestershire and parts of Gloucestershire. In many counties, damsons are planted around fruit plantations to provide shelter from the wind.

According to 1908 returns, the acreage for specific orchard fruits was as follows:
*   **Apples:** 172,751 acres
*   **Plums:** 15,683 acres
*   **Cherries:** 11,868 acres
*   **Pears:** 9,604 acres
*   **Other kinds:** 40,391 acres

## Small Fruit Cultivation
Small fruit cultivation—including raspberries, strawberries, currants, and gooseberries—has seen significant growth. The acreage for small fruit in Great Britain was 36,724 acres in 1888 and rose to 84,880 acres by 1908.

Kent serves as the primary center for strawberries and raspberries. Strawberries are also grown extensively in several other counties: Cambridgeshire (2,411 acres), Hampshire (2,327 acres), Norfolk (2,067 acres), and Worcestershire (1,273 acres). Additionally, Essex, Cornwall, Middlesex, Cheshire, and Lincolnshire each devote more than 500 acres to strawberries. Gooseberries are grown in most small fruit districts, while red currants are more widely cultivated than black currants, as the latter have been affected by the black currant mite.

As of 1908, the acreage for small fruits was distributed as:
*   **Strawberries:** 28,815 acres
*   **Currants and Gooseberries:** 26,241 acres
*   **Raspberries:** 9,323 acres
*   **Other kinds:** 20,501 acres

## Botanical Context and Other Plants
Botanically, fruit develops from the flower following the fertilization of the ovule. The ovary enlarges to form the pericarp, and the ovules become seeds. Some fruits, termed pseudocarps, include parts of the flower other than the ovary; for example, the fruit of the pear and apple consists of the floral receptacle and the ovary. In some cases, such as bananas and pineapples, cultivation can produce seedless fruit.

Other plants introduced to Britain include the dahlia, a genus of herbaceous plants from the high sandy plains of Mexico. Introduced from Spain in 1789 by the marchioness of Bute, the dahlia was likely the species *D. variabilis*. While originally featuring single flowers with dull scarlet rays and yellow disks, cultivation in England and France since the early 19th century produced brilliant hues and various shapes, including globular, anemone-like, and ranunculus-like forms.
