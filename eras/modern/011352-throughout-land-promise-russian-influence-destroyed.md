# Russian Influence and Territorial Retraction in Asia

## Territorial Boundaries and Expansion
Russian expansion toward the Oxus and the Turkoman deserts necessitated the establishment of formal borders with Afghanistan. Between 1884 and 1886, the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission determined a boundary that was largely artificial in construction. This line was fixed between the Oxus and the Hari Rud (the river of Herat). Specifically, the boundary leaves the Hari Rud at Zulfikar, located approximately 70 miles south of Sarakhs, and reaches its most southerly point at the crossing of the Kushk, about 60 miles north of Herat.

From Khamiab, located roughly 150 miles above the Russian Trans-Caspian railway crossing at Charjui, the main channel of the Oxus river serves as the northern boundary of Afghanistan, separating it from Russia up to the river's source in Victoria Lake of the Great Pamir. In 1895, an Anglo-Russian Commission demarcated a further line extending to the glaciers and snowfields overlooking the Chinese border. 

Further east, the southern boundary of Siberia follows an irregular path defined partly by the Alatau and Tian-shan mountains, reaching a northerly point at approximately 53 deg. N. lat. marked by the Sayan range west of Irkutsk. The boundary then moves south-east to the Kerulen affluent of the Amur river (approximately 117 deg. 30' E. long. and 49 deg. 20' N. lat.), following that affluent to the Amur river, and then the Amur to its junction with the Usuri. The boundary follows the Usuri to its head and finally reaches the Pacific coast at the mouth of the Tumen river, 100 miles south of the Amur bay, where the Russian port of Vladivostok is situated.

## Conflict with Japan
The period of European arrogance in Asia reached a turning point during a struggle between Russia and Japan. This conflict was driven by a group of financial adventurers, including the Tsar's cousins, the Grand Dukes, who gambled on the prospective looting of China and Manchuria and refused to withdraw. This led to the deployment of Russian peasants via the Siberian railway to distant battlefields and the positioning of Russian forces at the Yalu and Port Arthur. The Yalu forces were intended to resist advances from Korea, while those at Port Arthur defended the Liaotung peninsula.

The Russian forces, described as dishonestly provided and badly led, suffered defeats on both land and sea. Japanese forces landed approximately three thousand troops at Chemulpo, which allowed them to advance upon and take possession of Seoul. Additionally, the Russian Baltic Fleet, after sailing around Africa, was utterly destroyed in the Straits of Tshu-shima.

## The End of the War and Territorial Loss
The "remote and reasonless slaughter" of Russian soldiers sparked a revolutionary movement among the common people. This internal pressure obliged the Tsar to end the war in 1905. As a result of the defeat, Russia was forced to:
* Evacuate Manchuria.
* Resign Korea to Japan.
* Return the southern half of Saghalien, which Russia had previously seized in 1875.

This sequence of events marked the beginning of the retraction of Europe's "tentacles" in eastern Asia.

## Internal Collapse and the Russian Republic
By 1916 and 1917, the Russian state faced profound internal instability. Following the murder of the monk Rasputin on December 29, 1916, food riots in Petrograd escalated into a revolutionary insurrection by March 1917. This period saw the attempted suppression of the Duma and the arrest of liberal leaders, leading to the formation of a provisional government under Prince Lvoff and the abdication of the Tsar on March 15.

The Russian people, exhausted by wars, Great Powers, and the old order, sought relief from "unendurable miseries." A new Russian republic emerged under the leadership of Kerensky. However, Kerensky was assailed by a deeper revolutionary movement known as the "social revolution" and was largely ignored by Allied governments. The Allies, whose diplomatists were often ignorant of the Russian language and focused on the Russian Court rather than the reality of the country, showed little goodwill toward republicanism.

Despite the naval predominance of the Allies, the British Admiralty and other Allies—except for some submarine attacks—left the Germans in complete mastery of the Baltic during the war. This left the new Russian Republic to fight unsupported, even as the French and British press pressured them for fresh offensives.
