# Bankruptcy and Insolvency

## Definitions and Legal Status
Bankruptcy is the status of a debtor who has been declared by judicial process to be unable to pay his debts. While the terms "bankruptcy" and "insolvency" are sometimes used interchangeably, they have distinct legal and commercial meanings. Insolvency occurs when a person's financial liabilities exceed their means of meeting them. An insolvent person may still conduct business affairs through credit—paying old debts by incurring new ones—and may potentially regain solvency without creditors becoming aware of the situation. 

A debtor may be insolvent without becoming bankrupt; however, a person cannot be a bankrupt without first being insolvent. Bankruptcy is the legal declaration of insolvency and serves as a statutory system for administering the debtor's property, which is removed from their personal control.

## Administration and Process
In certain jurisdictions, the administration of bankruptcy law is entrusted to district courts and carried out via court-appointed officers known as referees. Creditors are responsible for appointing a trustee or trustees of the estate. 

Involuntary proceedings may be initiated against debtors owing $1000 or more, with some exceptions, though a petitioning creditor's debt must amount to at least $500. Insolvency is interpreted practically: a person is insolvent if the fair valuation of their aggregate property is insufficient to pay their debts, regardless of whether their realized assets are sufficient to meet liabilities.

The process involves a public or judicial examination of the bankrupt. Once this judicial examination is complete, the bankrupt is permitted to offer their creditors a composition. For such a composition to take effect, it must be approved by the court after any objections have been heard. If no composition or scheme of arrangement is proposed or approved, or if the debtor fails to provide a proper statement of affairs or their public examination is adjourned *sine die*, the court adjudicates the debtor bankrupt. Consequently, the property vests in a trustee and is divided among creditors (after administration costs) until debts are paid in full with 4% annual interest.

## Discharge and Qualifications
The court may grant a bankrupt a discharge, either absolutely or under specific conditions, following the conclusion of the public examination and after hearing the trustee, the official receiver, and any creditor. However, several qualifications apply:

*   **Criminal Offenses:** If the bankrupt committed a criminal offense related to the bankruptcy, the application for discharge must be refused unless the court determines otherwise for special reasons.
*   **Financial and Behavioral Grounds:** The court may refuse or suspend the discharge (for at least two years, or until a dividend of ten shillings in the pound is paid), or qualify the order by entering judgment for unpaid balances, if any of the following are proven:
    *   Assets are not equal to ten shillings in the pound of unsecured liabilities (unless the bankrupt is not responsible).
    *   Proper books were not kept.
    *   The debtor traded after knowing they were insolvent or contracted debts without a reasonable probability of payment.
    *   The debtor contributed to bankruptcy through gambling, rash speculation, culpable neglect, or unjustifiable expenses.
    *   The debtor failed to account for deficiency or defended legal proceedings on frivolous grounds.
    *   An undue preference was given within three months before the receiving order.
    *   Liabilities were increased to make assets equal to ten shillings in the pound.
    *   The debtor has a history of bankruptcy, fraudulent breach of trust, or arrangements with creditors.

A discharge releases the debtor from most obligations, except for debts incurred by fraud, debts due to the crown, and public obligations that require Treasury consent.

## Regional Variations and Legislation
### Scotland
In Scotland, sequestration may be awarded by the sheriff or the court of session. It can be recalled by a majority in number and four-fifths in value of creditors. If it proceeds, creditors elect a trustee and three commissioners. The bankrupt must provide all estate information under pain of imprisonment and undergo a public examination on oath. Discharge may occur via composition or without it, with timelines ranging from immediate (with all creditors' concurrence) to two years (without concurrence). The Debtors (Scotland) Act 1880 abolished imprisonment for debt but maintained punishments for fraudulent bankrupts.

### Italy
Under the Commercial Code of 1883, only merchants—defined as those engaging in commercial business as a habitual profession, including companies—can enter bankruptcy court. Bankruptcy is declared only where there is insolvency, regardless of the debt amount. The judgment removes the bankrupt's right to administer their affairs and appoints a trustee under a judge and commission of creditors. All property is sold by auction. While arrangements are recognized if they have the assent of creditors representing three-fourths of the indebtedness, composition before bankruptcy is not recognized.

## Fraudulent Preference and Summary Administration
A fraudulent preference occurs when a creditor is preferred; unlike English law, it is not necessary to prove the payment was made "with a view to prefer." 

For smaller estates, summary administration may be ordered if the official receiver reports that the property is unlikely to realize more than L300. This simplifies the procedure: the Board of Trade acts as the committee of inspection, local paper advertisements are removed, and the official receiver typically remains the trustee. However, the procedures for the public examination and discharge of the bankrupt remain unmodified.
