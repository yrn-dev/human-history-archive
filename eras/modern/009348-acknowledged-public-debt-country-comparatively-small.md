# Public Debt and Financial Obligations

Public debt and the management of state liabilities vary significantly across different nations and historical periods, ranging from the liquidation of war indemnities to the issuance of bonds for national reconstruction and public works.

## National Debts of Bolivia and Japan

In Bolivia, the acknowledged public debt has been described as comparatively small. Following a war with Chile, the country held an indemnity debt of 6,550,830 bolivianos owed to citizens of the Chilean republic. This debt was nearly liquidated in 1904 when Chile assumed the unpaid balance, leaving Bolivia with no other foreign debt. By 1905, Bolivia's internal debt totaled 6,243,270 bolivianos (L546,286), which included 1,998,500 bolivianos in treasury bills. Additionally, in 1903, the government authorized the issuance of one million bolivianos (L87,500) in treasury notes for the National Territory and the department of Beni; 10% of customs receipts from these two districts were designated for their redemption.

In Japan, the national debt originated from the commutation of samurai pensions and the incomes of feudatories. While a small portion of these costs was paid in ready money, the majority took the form of public loan-bonds, which comprised the bulk of State liabilities during the first half-cycle of the Meiji era. The total national debt of Japan did not exceed 305 million yen by the close of the twenty-eighth year of the new era. This total included the debts of the fiefs taken over by the Central Government, two small foreign loans, various debts for naval construction, public works, and minor purposes, as well as the costs associated with quelling the Satsuma rebellion.

## German Reparations and Obligations

Following the World War, Germany was required to acknowledge its debt through the issuance of bonds or other obligations as guarantees for claims. The Reparations Commission required bond issues in the following amounts:
* 20,000,000,000 marks gold, payable without interest by May 1, 1921.
* 40,000,000,000 marks gold, bearing 2-1/2 per cent interest between 1921 and 1926, increasing to 5 per cent thereafter, with a 1 per cent sinking fund payment starting in 1926.
* An undertaking to deliver 40,000,000,000 marks gold bonds with 5 per cent interest under terms fixed by the Commission.

Interest on Germany's debt was set at 5 per cent unless otherwise determined. Payments not made in gold could be accepted in the form of businesses, rights, concessions, commodities, or properties. As bonds were distributed, the equivalent par value was considered liquidated. 

Regarding pre-war debt, powers receiving ceded German territory were to assume a portion of that debt based on the ratio of the ceded territory's revenue to Germany's total revenues for the three years preceding the war. However, France would not assume any pre-war debt in Alsace-Lorraine because Germany had refused to accept any part of the French public debt when the territory was separated in 1871. Similarly, Poland would not share in debts incurred for the oppression of Poland. Mandatory powers were not to assume any German debts. Furthermore, Germany was required to pay the total cost of the armies of occupation as a first charge on its resources, followed by the cost of reparation.

## Debt in Ancient Greece and Early America

In Sparta around 250 B.C., the city faced severe degradation, with land concentrated in a few hands and many citizens becoming miserably poor. King Agis attempted to reconstruct the state by proposing the cancellation of all debts, the redivision of lands, and the restoration of military training and the public mess. While Agis failed to realize this scheme through persuasion, his successor, Kleomenês, eventually achieved a similar change through violence.

In the early United States, specifically during the Territorial period, land claims were a primary concern. By 1818, many citizens were in debt for their lands. To address this and the invasion of settlers' rights by "squatters," Congress reduced the price of land from $2 to $1.25 per acre and implemented a policy of pre-emption to favor existing settlers.

## Commercial Instruments and Debt

A bill of exchange serves as a substitute for money and a device to avoid transmitting cash to settle trade debts. While they constitute commercial currency, they are not the same as money; a person is not obligated to accept a bill in payment of a debt unless previously agreed. If a bill is dishonoured, the original debt revives. In England, the obligation to pay a bill depends on actual agreement, whereas in some continental countries, a creditor may draw on a debtor for the amount of the debt.
