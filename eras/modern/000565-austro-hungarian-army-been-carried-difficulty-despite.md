# The Austro-Hungarian Army

The military system of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy, established on the principle of universal and personal obligation for citizens to bear arms since 1868, was a complex structure divided between joint and national institutions. The force consisted of the common army (*K. und K.*), the *Landsturm* (levy-in-mass), and two separate national armies: the Austrian *Landwehr* (*K.K.*) and the Hungarian *Honveds*.

## Administration and Recruitment
The administration of the military was split by jurisdiction. The common army was managed by the joint minister of war, while the *Landwehr* and *Honveds* were administered by their respective national ministries of defence. 

Recruitment was determined by military bills voted upon by the Austrian and Hungarian parliaments, generally based on population figures from the most recent census. In 1905, the yearly contingent of recruits totaled 103,100 men, with Austria providing 59,211 and Hungary providing 43,889. Additionally, 10,000 men were annually allotted to the Austrian *Landwehr* and 12,500 to the Hungarian *Honveds*.

The term of service varied by branch:
* **Colours:** 2 years (extended to 3 years for the cavalry).
* **Reserve:** 7 or 8 years.
* **Landwehr:** 2 years.
Men not drafted into the active army spent an equivalent total period of service in various special reserves.

## Strength and Composition (1905)
As of 1905, the total strength of the Austro-Hungarian army on a peace footing consisted of 20,564 officers and 336,818 men, supported by 64,887 horses and 1,048 guns. The distribution was as follows:

* **Common Army:** 15,863 officers and 285,733 men. This included 376 officers and 6,372 men stationed in Bosnia and Herzegovina.
* **Austrian Landwehr:** 2,053 officers and 25,766 men.
* **Hungarian Honveds:** 2,648 officers and 25,319 men.

Within the common army, the infantry comprised 10,801 officers and 187,604 men, while the cavalry consisted of 1,890 officers and 45,486 men. Other specialized units included Field Artillery (1,630 officers, 27,612 men), Fortress Artillery (408 officers, 7,722 men), Technical troops such as pioneers and railway/telegraph regiments (588 officers, 9,935 men), the Transport Service (461 officers, 4,312 men), and the Sanitary Service (85 officers, 3,062 men).

## Political Conflict and the Language Crisis
The joint army became a focal point for nationalist tensions, particularly regarding the "Magyarization" of the force. In 1889, Army bills intended to increase the peace footing of the joint army were carried with difficulty, despite the efforts of Count Julius Andrassy the Elder and Koloman Tisza. Count Andrassy warned the country of the dangers of nationalist chauvinism and reminded it of the obligations established under the Compact of 1867.

A significant constitutional crisis emerged when a section of the Hungarian independence party demanded that German words of command in the joint army be replaced by Magyar words in regiments recruited from Hungary. This demand was viewed by the crown as an encroachment on royal military prerogatives.

The legal dispute centered on two clauses of the Hungarian Fundamental Law XII. of 1867:
* **Clause 11:** Recognized that everything relating to the inner organization, leadership, and unitary direction of the whole army (including the Hungarian army as a complementary part) was subject to the disposal of His Majesty.
* **Clause 12:** Reserved the country's right to periodically complete the Hungarian army and to fix the conditions on which recruits were granted.

Partisans for Magyar words of command argued that since Hungary could fix the conditions for granting recruits, they could make the abolition of German commands a condition of those grants. The crown, however, viewed this as a demand to differentiate the Hungarian part of the army from the Austrian part, which would effectively make it impossible for non-Magyar officers to command Hungarian regiments—even though less than half of those regiments had a majority of Magyar recruits.
