# Human Emotions and Social Relations

The archives record a wide array of human experiences regarding love, dislike, and intense emotional attachments, ranging from personal relationships and familial bonds to national patriotism and religious teachings.

## Patriotism and National Identity
In the Greek city-states, patriotism was characterized as a personal passion of "inspiring and dangerous intensity." This feeling was reinforced by a man's love for his native town, his home, and his religion, as these elements were considered one. Because the privileged class of citizens monopolized the state, their patriotism took a narrow form; they formed alliances but avoided coalescing with other city-states to preserve their specific fees and privileges. 

This intensity of feeling could be volatile; like rejected love, Greek patriotism was apt to turn into something resembling hatred. This was particularly evident in Greek exiles, who might treat their own country roughly in an effort to save it from those they viewed as "devils in human form" who had ousted them. In oligarchic states, the excluded class often overcame their dislike of foreigners through a greater dislike of the oppressive class at home.

## Royal Figures and Public Sentiment
The archives detail several instances where the love or dislike of the public was tied to the status of royal women. Anne Boleyn was characterized by "universal unpopularity," a factor that, along with the despotic power of King Henry, hindered the administration of pure justice during her trial. Despite this, she maintained her innocence to Sir William Kingston and, while on the scaffold, described the king as a "gentle and sovereign lord."

In contrast, Caroline Amelia Augusta, queen of George IV, often enjoyed the sympathies of the English people. This support grew because she was perceived to have been badly treated by her "profligate husband." When the government attempted to prevent her recognition as queen at foreign courts and omitted her name from the liturgy, it stirred strong feelings in her favor among the general public.

In China, the Divine Empress (who later styled herself God Almighty) exhibited an overbearing arrogance in her later years. She demanded that the beauty of lilies and roses be compared to her, rather than vice versa. Her erratic behavior included ordering all peonies in the capital to be pulled up and burnt because they failed to bloom instantly upon her command.

## Family and Intimacy
The nature of family affection varies across different cultural and historical contexts. In the account of Jacob, he was overcome by his love for a damsel due to her flourishing beauty. This connection was strengthened by their mutual kindred, as Jacob's mother, Rebeka, was the sister of the damsel's father, Laban.

In other contexts, traditional family loyalties were challenged. Jesus sought to replace narrow and restrictive family affections with the "love of God," proposing that the kingdom of Heaven serve as the family for his followers. He defined his mother and brethren as those who do the will of his Father in heaven.

In certain societies described by Marco Polo, marriage customs allowed a man to take up to thirty wives depending on his wealth. While the first wife was held in the highest consideration, a man could simply turn off any wife he disliked and take another.

## Literary and Religious Perspectives on Emotion
The Brontë sisters' works are noted for their emotional intensity. Emily Brontë's *Wuthering Heights* is described as a monument of intensity, characterized by passion and a "grey melancholy." Similarly, Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* and *Villette* are recognized for their independence and intensity.

The sacred texts of the Qur'an present a vision of life where human likes and dislikes may not align with divine wisdom. The text teaches that fighting is prescribed even though people dislike it, noting that it is possible for people to dislike something that is good for them, or love something that is bad for them, because "Allah knoweth, and ye know not." 

Furthermore, the texts warn believers against taking intimates from outside their own ranks, stating that such individuals may desire their ruin and that "vehement hatred" or "rank hatred" may be revealed through their mouths, while what their hearts or breasts conceal is even worse.
