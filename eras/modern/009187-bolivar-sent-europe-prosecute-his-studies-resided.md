# Simon Bolivar

## Early Life and Education
Simon Bolivar (1783–1830), recognized as the hero of South American independence, was born on July 24, 1783, in Caracas, Venezuela. He was born to Maria Concepcion Palacios y Sojo and Juan Vicente Bolivar y Ponte, both of whom came from noble Venezuelan families. To pursue his studies, Bolivar was sent to Europe, where he resided in Madrid for several years. Following the completion of his education, he traveled extensively, primarily through the south of Europe. During this period, he visited Paris, where he witnessed some of the final scenes of the Revolution.

## Return to Venezuela and Early Struggles
Bolivar returned to Madrid and married the daughter of Don N. Toro—the uncle of the marquis of Toro in Caracas—in 1801. The couple then traveled to Venezuela, where Bolivar intended to focus on the improvement of his large estate. However, his young wife died prematurely from yellow fever, an event that prompted Bolivar to return to Europe.

He returned home in 1809, traveling through the United States, which provided him with his first opportunity to observe the operations of free institutions. Shortly after arriving in Venezuela, he aligned himself with the cause of independence that had been affecting the Spanish colonies for several years. In April 1810, Bolivar was one of the promoters of the insurrection at Caracas. The revolutionary junta granted him a colonel's commission, and he was subsequently associated with Louis Lopez Mendez on a mission to the court of Great Britain.

## Military Campaigns and the War to the Death
Following Venezuela's declaration of independence on July 5, 1811, war began in earnest the next year with the advance of Spanish troops under Monteverde. Bolivar was given command of the post of Puerto Cabello, but he was forced to evacuate due to a lack of support. Following the recovery of the country by the Spaniards—aided by the inaction of Miranda—Bolivar fled to Curacao.

In September 1812, Bolivar traveled to Cartagena after hearing of movements in New Granada. He received a commission to operate against Spanish troops on the Magdalena river, a campaign in which he was eminently successful. He pushed into Venezuela with no more than 500 men, reaching Truxillo and Merida and gaining the support of the population. He divided his forces into two divisions, placing one under the charge of his colleague Rivas, and issued a decree of "war to the death" as he advanced toward Caracas. 

On August 4, 1813, Bolivar entered Caracas in triumph after the Spanish troops under Monteverde suffered a crushing defeat at Lastoguanes. By January 1814, patriots held entire possession of the country, following the liberation of the eastern district by General Marino. This success was short-lived; royalists concentrated their forces and defeated Bolivar near Cura, in the plains of La Puerta. By the end of 1814, the royalists were again the undisputed masters of Venezuela.

## The Republic of Colombia and Final Victories
Bolivar traveled from Cumana to Cartagena and then to Tunja to meet with the revolutionary congress of New Granada. Despite personal enemies, he was treated with consideration and appointed to lead an expedition against Santa Fe de Bogota. Later, on December 17, 1819, a fundamental law was enacted uniting the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state called the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar serving as president.

Bolivar continued to fight the Spaniards under Morillo, concluding a six-month armistice at Truxillo on November 25, 1820. When the armistice expired, Bolivar struck a decisive blow at Carabobo, routing the troops of General Torre. This battle effectively ended the war in Venezuela. Bolivar entered Caracas on June 29, 1821, and the constitution of Colombia was adopted on August 30, 1821, with Bolivar as president and Santander as vice-president.

## Liberation of Ecuador and Peru
Bolivar sought to complete the liberation of the region by expelling Spaniards from Peru and Ecuador. He marched on Quito in Ecuador, where his colleague Sucre routed the Spaniards at the battle of Pichincha in June 1822. Bolivar then marched on Lima; the royalists evacuated the city, and Bolivar was invested with absolute power as dictator. 

Though forced to withdraw to Truxillo temporarily due to republican intrigues in Peru, Bolivar organized a liberating army. On August 6, 1824, he defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin. Sucre followed the retreating royalists into Upper Peru, achieving a decisive victory at Ayacucho. The remaining Spanish forces were confined to the castles of Callao for over a year. In June 1825, Bolivar visited Upper Peru, which became a separate state named Bolivia in his honor. In August 1825, the first congress of Bolivia declared Bolivar its perpetual protector and requested he prepare a constitution.

## Final Years and Retirement
In September 1826, Bolivar left Lima for Bogota, arriving on November 14. He assumed extraordinary presidential powers to address rebellions and traveled to Puerto Cabello on December 31 to issue a general amnesty and meet with Paez. 

Although re-elected as president in January 1827, Bolivar formally resigned the presidency in February. He expressed a desire to retire to his patrimonial estate to refute claims of ambition. However, Santander and a resolution of congress urged him to resume his functions to appease the country's agitations. Bolivar agreed to return to Bogota to take the oaths, issuing decrees for a general amnesty, a national convention at Ocana, and the establishment of constitutional order throughout Colombia.
