## Political Figures and Governance in England
In the early 18th century, the English political landscape featured figures such as St John, who was returned to parliament in 1701 for the family borough of Wootton Bassett in Wiltshire. A declared Tory, St John aligned himself with the speaker Harley (later Lord Oxford), whom he referred to as "dear master." St John gained significant ascendancy over the House of Commons through his eloquence in debate, surpassing his schoolfellow, Walpole.

During his tenure, St John held several key roles and supported various legislative efforts. In May, he was responsible for the bill to secure the Protestant succession. He opposed the oath abjuring the Pretender and participated in the impeachment of Whig lords regarding their conduct with the Partition treaties. Following the accession of Anne, he supported bills in 1702 and 1704 against occasional conformity and was involved in disputes between the two Houses. 

In 1704, St John became secretary at war alongside Harley, establishing a relationship with Marlborough, who treated him with paternal partiality. After leaving office in 1708, he returned in 1710 as a privy councillor and secretary of state in a new ministry under Harley, representing Berkshire in parliament. His later legislative contributions included supporting a real property qualification for parliamentary seats and authoring a bill in 1712 that taxed newspapers. In 1711, he founded the Brothers' Club, a society composed of men of letters and Tory politicians. He also promoted two expeditions to Canada and the West Indies in 1711, both of which failed.

Regarding foreign policy, the government sought peace with France. The ministry, supported by the people, parliament, and the queen, aimed to terminate hostilities, arguing that the Whigs' refusal to grant terms in 1706 and 1709 indicated the war was not being pursued for English national interests.

## Transportation and Social Customs in Medieval England
Records from the middle ages indicate the use of various vehicles in England. Early carriages were known as caroches, chariots, cars, whirlicotes, and chares. In the poem *The Squyr of Low Degree*, a sumptuous carriage is described as being covered with red velvet, azure blue and white damask diapered with lilies, and fine gold cloth. Chaucer also described a chare as being "pierrie" and "with gold wrought."

By the end of the 14th century, during the reign of Richard II, most followers of the king traveled on horseback when he fled his rebellious subjects, though his mother utilized a carriage. The popularity of these vehicles declined when Ann, the wife of Richard II, introduced the side-saddle to English ladies. Consequently, the use of chariots and whirlicotes was largely forsaken, except for specific spectacles such as coronations.

The text states that on a future day, Allah will raise a witness from every people against them, and the Book has been revealed as a mercy, a guide, and glad tidings for those who submit or have surrendered to Allah.
