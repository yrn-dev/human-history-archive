# Languages of the Bombay Presidency: Cutchi, Gujarati, Hindustani, and Mahratti

The Bombay Presidency, a province of British India, is characterized by a diverse linguistic landscape. The territory, which extends from 13 deg. 53' to 28 deg. 45' N. and from 66 deg. 40' to 76 deg. 30' E., is divided into four commissionerships: Sind, the northern division (Gujarat), the central division (Deccan), and the southern division (Carnatic). The distribution of languages across these divisions reflects the region's complex geography and ethnic composition.

## Languages of Gujarat and Cutch
In the northern division of the presidency, the primary languages are Gujarati, Hindustani, and Cutchi. Gujarati and Hindustani are the chief languages spoken in Gujarat. The Gujarati language is noted for its close agreement with languages to its north, a result of the Gujarati people originating from that direction. Because of this, speakers of Gujarati find little difficulty in understanding natives from places such as Jaipur, Marwar, Mewar, Ajmere, and Delhi.

Linguistically, the language in Gujarat and Rajputana is considered mixed. While there are few traces of the "Outer language" near the original Midland, these traces become more evident as one moves further from that center. Consequently, Gujarati contains several important survivals of the old language spoken by earlier Aryan inhabitants.

In the territory of Cutch, the chief language is Cutchi. Cutch is geographically distinct, featuring the Runn or Lake of Cutch—a salt marsh, inland lake, or arm of the sea depending on the season—which can convert the territory into an island during the rains.

## Mahratti in Thana and the Deccan
Mahratti (Marathi) is the chief language of Thana and the central division. It is also spoken in Khandesh, where it exists alongside Gujarati, and in the southern division, where it is found alongside Kanarese. The Mahrattas are a dominating race in the region, numbering 3,650,000 as of 1901; this population includes 1,900,000 Kunbis, 350,000 Konkanis, and 1,400,000 Mahrattas not otherwise specified.

Unlike other Aryan languages that gradually fade into one another, Marathi possesses a distinct linguistic frontier. It does not merge into cognate neighboring forms of speech. This isolation is attributed partly to the physical barrier of the Vindhya range to the north and partly to the fact that northern languages belong to the Midland rather than the Outer Band. This creates a sharp divide; for example, Gujarati speakers generally do not comprehend those speaking Marathi from Maharashtra or the Konkan.

Within the Marathi language, the only real dialect is Konkani, spoken near Goa. There are also local varieties, including those of the Deccan, the northern and central Konkan, and the Central Provinces (including Berar). In the southern part of the Ratnagiri district, the Konkani variety of Marathi gradually merges into the true Konkani dialect. Additionally, aboriginal tribes in the hill country employ several broken jargones based upon Marathi.

## Hindustani and Related Tongues
Hindustani is one of the chief languages of Gujarat. It is identified as one of two forms of the same language—the other being Hindi—spoken by Mahommedans and Hindus, respectively. This serves as an example of how religion can deeply affect culture. As a language of a Moslem nation, Hindustani, like Persian, Turkish, and Malay, borrows a large proportion of its vocabulary from Arabic and is usually accompanied by the use of the Arabic alphabet.

## Other Regional Dialects and Vernaculars
Beyond the primary languages, the presidency contains other linguistic groups. These include dialects spoken by the Bhil (120,000 speakers) and Gipsy (30,000 speakers). The region's history of administration and exploration also saw individuals, such as Sir Richard Francis Burton, laboring to achieve proficiency in these vernaculars, including Gujarati, Marathi, and Hindustani, alongside Persian and Arabic.
