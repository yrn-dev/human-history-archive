# Dalmatia (Austria)

## Geography and Demographics
As of 1909, Dalmatia (Austria) encompassed an area of 4,923 square miles. The population recorded for that year was 591,597, resulting in a population density of 120.1 people per square mile. Between 1869 and 1900, the population grew steadily, reaching the 591,597 mark by the turn of the century, with a noted constant excess of male children over female children.

The population is composed of several distinct groups. Foreigners make up 1% of the total, while Italians account for approximately 3%, a figure that has shown a slow decline. The remaining 96% are the Morlachs, who belong to the Serbo-Croatian branch of the Slavonic race. This group absorbed other elements over time, including Albanians, Latinized Illyrians, and other alien groups.

## The Morlachs and Vlachs
The term "Morlachs" (also written as "Morlaks" or "Morlacks") is commonly used by English writers to describe Dalmatian Slavs, though it is sometimes specifically applied to the peasantry living in the hills. The name is an abbreviated form of *Mavrovlachi*, which likely means "Black Vlachs" or, less probably, "Sea Vlachs." 

Originally, the term applied to Latin or Latinized inhabitants of central Illyria who fled to the mountains during the 7th century to escape barbarian invaders. Throughout the middle ages, the Mavrovlachi functioned as muleteers, cattle-drovers, or nomadic shepherds. In the 14th century, they migrated into maritime Croatia and northern Dalmatia; consequently, these areas were known as *Morlacchia* until the 18th century. Over time, the Mavrovlachi adopted the manners and language of the Slavs and became identified with them. In northern Dalmatia, the Slavs of the interior are still referred to as *Morlacchi*, although in the south, the term is used to express contempt. 

Very few "Vlachs" in the proper sense remain in the country, though the Slavs frequently use the name "Vlachs" to refer to town-dwellers and Italians.

## Language and Culture
The spoken language in Dalmatia is modified by various dialect forms. However, the literary languages used in the region are Serbo-Croatian and Italian.

## Political History and Administration
The political status of Dalmatia shifted frequently between the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Following the fall of the Venetian republic in 1797, the treaty of Campo Formio transferred Dalmatia to Austria. During this period, the republics of Poglizza and Ragusa remained independent, with Ragusa benefiting from its neutrality during the early Napoleonic wars.

In 1805, the peace of Pressburg handed the country over to France. This occupation was contested by a Russian force that seized the Bocche di Cattaro and received aid from the Montenegrins. Napoleon deprived Poglizza of its independence in 1807 and Ragusa in 1808. While French troops withdrew in 1809, the treaty of Vienna restored Dalmatia to France and united it with the Illyrian kingdom. Between 1812 and 1815, a British naval force under Captain Hoste occupied the islands of Lagosta, Lesina, and Curzola after an engagement with a French squadron off Lissa. This occupation helped establish a significant overland trade route through Germany, Austria, and Dalmatia.

By 1814, allied Austrian and British forces expelled the final French garrison. In 1815, Dalmatia was incorporated into the Austro-Hungarian empire. Its subsequent history was largely tranquil, interrupted only by ineffectual risings near Cattaro in 1869 and between 1881 and 1882.

## Administrative Context
Within the broader administrative structure of the territories associated with the Austrian sovereign, Dalmatia was one of the lands represented in the Reichsrath. While the term "Austria" was frequently used to describe these territories, official documents referred to them as "the kingdoms and lands represented in the Reichsrath" because an official "Austrian empire" did not technically exist.

In the aftermath of later conflicts, property of predominant historic interest to Dalmatia, along with other regions such as Croatia, Slavonia, Bosnia, Herzegovina, and the Republic of Ragusa, could be transferred without payment to states arising from the dismemberment of Austria.
