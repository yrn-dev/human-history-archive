# The Reichsrath: From Diet Elections to Direct Popular Vote

The Reichsrath served as the parliament for the territories of the Habsburg possessions not included in the 1867 settlement with the Hungarian crown. Established under the February Constitution of 1861, the Reichsrath was designed as a central legislative body for the "kingdoms and lands represented in the Reichsrath." It consisted of two houses: the *Herrenhaus* (House of Lords), comprising members of the imperial family, archbishops, prince bishops, and other life or hereditary members; and a Chamber of Deputies.

## The System of Indirect Election
Originally, members of the Chamber of Deputies were not chosen by direct election. Instead, they were elected by the *Landtage*, or diets, of each territory. These diets were elected every six years through a system of four electoral bodies, known as *curiae*:
1. Great proprietors (owners of estates with feudal privileges prior to 1848).
2. Chambers of commerce.
3. Towns.
4. Rural districts.

In the categories of towns and rural districts, suffrage was granted to those paying at least ten gulden in direct taxes. This indirect system gave the diets significant power over the composition of the central parliament.

## Political Conflict and the Federalist Weapon
The method of electing the Reichsrath became a central point of contention between German Liberals and the Federalists (including Slav races). The Federalists sought greater autonomy for individual territories. Because the diets elected the deputies, the Federalists could use the diets as a "weapon" to obstruct the central government.

For example, in 1867, diets in Bohemia, Moravia, Carinthia, and Tirol—which held Federalist majorities—refused to elect deputies to the Reichsrath unless the government accepted terms it could not meet. Similarly, following the 1868 "declaration" in Bohemia, Czechs supported by Clericals and Feudals gained a majority in the Bohemian diet and refused to elect delegates for the Reichsrath entirely.

By 1870, the situation reached a crisis where the Polish club, followed by Slovenes and Tirolese, left the House. This left a rump parliament of only 110 members, consisting primarily of Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia, making the country nearly impossible to govern.

## Transition to Direct Election
To neutralize the power of the Federalists and the obstructive potential of the diets, the government introduced a new electoral law in 1873. This measure transferred the right of electing members of the Reichsrath from the diets to the direct vote of the people.

The result of this change was a significant weakening of the Federalist opposition. Under the previous system, a territory could formally refuse to appoint deputies, effectively blocking the legal representation of an entire region. Under direct election, if a Czech or Slovene member refused to take their seat, the only consequence was that a single constituency remained unrepresented, rather than the entire territory.

The transition was met with strong resistance. A petition containing 250,000 names was presented from Bohemia, and the Poles withdrew from the Reichsrath in protest during the introduction of the law. Despite this, the measure passed with a vote of 120 to 2, as enough members remained to provide a legal quorum. Simultaneously, the total number of members in the Reichsrath was increased to 353.

## Impact on Governance and Representation
The shift to direct elections, combined with other government policies, allowed for a more stable German majority in the parliament. In Bohemia, the government used vigorous measures to ensure this majority, including the suppression of opposition newspapers and the transfer of Czech journalists' cases to German districts to be tried by hostile juries. 

Furthermore, to secure the votes of feudal proprietors, German capitalists from Vienna formed a society (popularly called *Chabrus*) to purchase estates that carried voting rights. This resulted in Viennese individuals replacing traditional figures like the Thuns and Schwarzenbergs in the political structure. Because the Czechs refused to sit in the diet for many years following these events, the government was able to operate without significant difficulty.
