# Brokers

A broker is an agent whose primary function is to bring a principal and a third party into a privity of contract. Unlike a factor, a broker does not have possession of the principal's goods and, unless expressly authorized, cannot buy or sell in his own name. Once the contract is established, the broker ordinarily drops out of the transaction.

## Types of Brokers

The role of a broker varies across different commercial sectors:

*   **Ship-brokers:** These agents act as commission agents and often serve as ships' managers. They procure purchasers for ships or ships for purchasers, similar to how house-agents operate. They also find charterers for ship-owners or ships for charterers, effecting the charter-party.
*   **Chartering brokers:** Customarily paid by the ship-owner upon the effectuation of a charter-party, these brokers may be employed by either the owner or the charterer. If a charter-party includes a provision for payment to a specific broker (e.g., 2½% on estimated freight) and stipulates that the ship be consigned to that broker for business at a specific port, it prevents the ship-owner from disputing the broker's right to payment. In such cases, the broker acts as "ship's husband," which includes the duty to clear the vessel at customs.
*   **Insurance brokers:** These agents effect marine insurance policies. They are employed by the person with the interest to insure, but their commission is paid by the underwriter. The broker pays premiums to the underwriter and receives payments in the event of a loss. By trade custom, the underwriter looks only to the broker for premium payments and has no right of action against the assured. If an underwriter refuses to pay a loss, the broker drops out and the assured sues the underwriter directly.
*   **Stockbrokers:** These brokers contract for the sale of shares and stocks. Because stocks and shares are not classified as "goods," the memorandum in writing required by the Sale of Goods Act 1893 does not apply.
*   **Bill-brokers:** Originally agents who procured the discounting of bills for country bankers in London for a commission, the role evolved. Modern bill-brokers typically buy bills on their own account at a discount and borrow money on their own security at interest, profiting from the difference. In this capacity, they deal as principals rather than agents and are not brokers in the strict sense.

## Relations with Principals and Third Parties

A broker is obligated to carry out a principal's instructions with skill, diligence, and perfect good faith. This includes ensuring the bargain matches the principal's orders regarding price, quantity, and quality, and ensuring the contract is legally enforceable. A broker must not accept commissions from third parties or allow his own interests to oppose those of his principal.

Regarding third parties, a broker who signs a contract note "as broker" on behalf of a principal is not personally liable. However, if the broker makes the contract in a way that makes himself a party to it, the third party may sue either the broker or the principal. While brokers generally lack authority to receive payment, if a trade custom allows it and a buyer pays the seller's broker, the buyer may use this as a defense if the seller later sues for the price because the broker absconded or became insolvent. Additionally, brokers may be liable for damages in tort for conversion if they negotiate a sale for a principal who lacks title to the goods.

## Contract Memoranda and Formalities

Historically, from the reign of James I until 1884, London brokers were licensed by the corporation and regulated by statute. Following the Statute of Frauds in the reign of Charles II, brokers acting for both parties maintained a formal book to record contracts, issuing "bought notes" to buyers and "sold notes" to sellers.

In modern practice, brokers are no longer regulated by statute in London or elsewhere and do not keep formal books. The written memorandum of a sale is usually found in a "contract note." When two brokers are involved (one for the buyer and one for the seller), they may exchange notes—such as "I, acting on account of A. B... have this day bought from you, acting on account of C. D..."—to ensure both parties have a signed memorandum upon which they can sue.

## Remuneration and Liability

A broker is entitled to "brokerage," usually a percentage of the total price of the goods. A higher rate of remuneration is paid if the broker is employed on *del credere* terms, meaning he guarantees the solvency of the other party. Brokers are also entitled to reimbursement for money properly expended or indemnity for liabilities incurred on the principal's behalf. Because they do not possess the goods, brokers have no lien to enforce these rights. If a broker fails in his duty, he loses his right to remuneration and indemnity and may be sued for damages for breach of the employment contract.

## Influence of Exchanges

Since the mid-19th century, "Exchanges" have modified broker relations. Members of these associations agree to be bound by specific rules and a governing body. Disputes between members are settled by an arbitration tribunal of members guided by a printed book of rules. To simplify accounts, particularly for "futures" (contracts for future delivery), a clearing-house is used to settle weekly "differences" between contract prices and market prices.
