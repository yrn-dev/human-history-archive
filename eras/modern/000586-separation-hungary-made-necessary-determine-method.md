# The Governance of the Dual Monarchy

The separation of Hungary from the other territories of the Habsburg possessions necessitated the establishment of a new method of governance for the remaining lands. This process was formalized through the "Ausgleich" (Compromise or compact) concluded on February 8, 1867, which regulated the relations between Austria and Hungary. Under this arrangement, the two states became completely independent of one another, each maintaining its own government and parliament.

## The Austrian Administration
The territories designated as "Austria" consisted of those Habsburg possessions not included in the lands of the Hungarian crown by the 1867 settlement. Because these territories were separate political units with diverse histories and varying claims to self-government, there was no clear legal basis for new institutions. 

The ultimate basis for government became the February Constitution of 1861, which established a parliament known as the *Reichsrath*. This body consisted of a Chamber of Deputies and a House of Lords (*Herrenhaus*). The House of Lords included members of the imperial family, archbishops, prince bishops, and other members appointed for life or hereditary members. The Chamber of Deputies was composed of members chosen by *Landtage* (diets) in each territory. These diets were elected every six years through four electoral bodies or *curiae*: the towns, the rural districts, the chambers of commerce, and the "great proprietors" (owners of estates with feudal privileges prior to 1848). In the rural and town classes, suffrage was granted to those paying at least ten gulden in direct taxes.

## Common Institutions and Dualism
Despite their independence, the unity of the Dual Monarchy was maintained through a common head of state—the Emperor of Austria and Apostolic King of Hungary—and the joint administration of three specific areas: finance regarding joint expenditure, the army (including the navy), and foreign affairs (including consular and diplomatic representation).

To administer these common institutions, a system of dualism was established to ensure complete parity between Hungary and the other territories of the king. A *Delegation* of sixty members was elected from both the Austrian and Hungarian parliaments. These Delegations were responsible for the common ministers and the estimates for joint services. To prevent the Delegations from overshadowing the parliaments, they did not sit together; instead, they communicated in writing with translations in German or Magyar. A common session was only convened if three exchanges of notes failed to produce an agreement, in which case a vote was taken in silence by simple majority.

## Common Ministries and Military Organization
Three ministers were appointed for common purposes: finance, war, and foreign affairs. While these ministers were responsible to the Delegations, the Delegations possessed no actual legislative power. 

The minister of war controlled the common army, though the laws regarding recruitment had to be voted on separately in each parliament. In 1868, compulsory military service was introduced, fixing the available war army at 800,000 men. In addition to the joint army, each state maintained a separate minister for national defence and a separate militia. In Hungary, this force was the *honved*; in Austria, it was the *Landwehr* (though the *Landwehr* was organized as part of the standing army, except in Tirol and Dalmatia). In 1886, a *Landsturm* was created to organize the male population up to age forty-two for emergencies. German remained the common language of the army, serving as a primary point of unity for the monarchy.

## The Ausgleich and Financial Settlements
The financial and economic reforms between Austria and Hungary were managed through a general settlement known as the *Ausgleich*, which occurred every ten years. This bargain included the determination of the "Quota," or the proportion of common expenses each half of the monarchy would contribute. 

These negotiations were often fraught with difficulty due to national jealousy and race hatred within the two parliaments. Concessions in one area, such as commercial policy, were often traded for gains in financial agreements. For example, in 1877, Austria granted Hungary a share in the management of the bank, while Austria benefited from arrangements regarding bonuses on exported sugar. Because any amendment could lead to the rejection of the entire package, the process was an anxious period of negotiation. The first settlement took two years (1876 to June 1878) to successfully pass through both parliaments.

## Political Conflict and Instability
Relations between the two halves of the monarchy remained critical due to the clash between Habsburg imperialism, which sought military and economic unity, and Magyar nationalism, which sought ascendancy in an independent Hungary. 

Tensions were exacerbated by issues such as the "Magyar word of command" and the renewal of the Austro-Hungarian State Bank charter. In March 1908, a proposal was made in the Hungarian parliament that the bank's charter should not be renewed after 1910, suggesting the establishment of a separate Hungarian Bank if negotiations failed. Additionally, while universal suffrage had been granted in the Austrian half of the monarchy, the Hungarian government remained hesitant to redeem pledges for franchise reform, as Magyar ascendancy relied on a narrow and artificial franchise.
