# American Ornithology and Mammals of North America

## Ornithological Regions and Distribution
From an ornithological perspective, North America is part of the Holarctic Region, which also includes the extratropical landmass of the Old World. This region is characterized by the presence of Oscines, which have reached their highest development here, as well as the *Colymbi*, *Alcidae*, and *Gallidae* (or Alectoropodous Galli). Conversely, the region lacks Psittaci, Tinami, Ratitae, and non-Oscine Passeres, with the exceptions of *Conurus carolinensis* and *Tyrannidae*, the latter of which extend into North America.

The Nearctic Subregion shows a close affinity with the Palaearctic avifauna. This relationship is most apparent when excluding bird groups believed to have originated in the Neotropical region, specifically turkey-buzzards, humming-birds, and numerous *Tyrannidae*. In Greenland, the distribution of breeding birds is diverse; of the sixty-one species found there, four are American, eight are European-Asiatic, and the remainder are North Pacific, North Atlantic, or circumpolar.

## Spencer Fullerton Baird
Spencer Fullerton Baird (1823–1887) was a prominent American naturalist born in Reading, Pennsylvania. A graduate of Dickinson College (1840), Baird's interest in ornithology was solidified after meeting J. J. Audubon in 1838, who provided him with a portion of his own bird collection. Baird's early field experience included an ornithological excursion through the mountains of Pennsylvania, during which he walked 400 miles in twenty-one days.

Baird held several significant scientific and governmental positions. He served as a professor of natural history, chemistry, physiology, and mathematics at Dickinson College starting in 1845. From 1850 to 1878, he was the assistant-secretary of the Smithsonian Institution, eventually becoming its secretary following the death of Joseph Henry. He also served as the U.S. Commissioner of Fish and Fisheries from 1871 until his death. Between 1850 and 1860, Baird supervised several government scientific expeditions to the western territories of the United States and authored a manual titled *Instructions to Collectors*. His extensive bibliography includes 1,063 entries, with one of his most important volumes being *Birds*, part of a series of reports on explorations and surveys for a railway route.

## Land Mammals of North America
The land mammals of Greenland are described as being decidedly more American than European. Species that have come from America include the dog, the Eskimo, the musk-ox, the reindeer (probably), and the banded lemming (*Cuniculus torquatus*), as well as the white polar wolf, which appears to have recently invaded the country via the northern part to the east coast. Other mammals in the region, such as the Arctic hare, the polar fox, the stoat (*Mustela erminea*), and the polar bear, are considered circumpolar forms.

Within North America, various rodent mammals are noted. The marmot, a large, burrowing rodent allied to squirrels, occurs in North America, where it is known as the "wood-chuck" (the most common species being *A. monax*). Marmots are characterized by a stout build, long thickly haired tails, the absence of cheek-pouches, and a rudimentary first front-toe with a flat nail. In contrast, the "prairie-dog" is a separate genus (*Cynomys*) of smaller, more slender North American rodents that possess small cheek-pouches. Additionally, "pouched-marmots" (*Spermophilus* or *Citillus*), which are common to both hemispheres, are distinguished by having large cheek-pouches.
