# Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke

Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke (1678–1751), was a prominent Tory chieftain and statesman whose career and writings left a significant, though debated, mark on 18th-century England. While his style was once the cynosure of statesmen, his lasting influence on posterity is viewed as weaker than the impression he made on his contemporaries.

## Political Career and the Treaty of Utrecht
Bolingbroke played a primary role in the diplomatic negotiations between England and France. On July 7, he was created Baron St John of Lydiard Tregoze and Viscount Bolingbroke. In August, he traveled to France to conduct negotiations, signing a four-month armistice on the 19th. This process culminated in the signing of the Treaty of Utrecht on March 31, 1713, by all allies except the emperor.

Although the terms granted to England were perhaps not highly critical, the method of peacemaking—characterized by underhand conspiracies and the violation of solemn international assurances—is regarded as a lasting stain on national honor and credit. Bolingbroke is held primarily responsible for this manner of diplomacy, as well as the abandonment of the Catalans. Additionally, a commercial treaty he established for free trade with France was rejected in June.

## Relations with Harley and the Tory Administration
The Tory administration was based on the friendship between Bolingbroke and Harley. This relationship gradually dissolved over time. In March 1711, an attempt on Bolingbroke's life by Guiscard resulted in Harley receiving the wound instead, which earned Harley the credit. By May, Harley became lord treasurer and obtained the earldom of Oxford. Bolingbroke expressed disappointment in July upon receiving only his viscountcy rather than the extinct earldom of his family, and for being passed over for the Garter. Despite an attempt by Jonathan Swift in September 1713 to reconcile the two, their differences persisted. As the health of the queen declined, both men maintained secret communications with James, fearing their own downfall upon the accession of the elector of Hanover.

## Eloquence and Literary Style
Bolingbroke's most brilliant gift was his eloquence, which Jonathan Swift noted was acknowledged by men of all factions to be unrivalled. Lord Chesterfield described his conversation as possessing a "flowing happiness of expression" that would require no correction if printed. However, none of his great orations survived, a loss that Pitt regretted more than the loss of the missing books of Tacitus and Livy.

His written works are characterized by a dignified, balanced, and decorated style. His political writings are often spirited and splendidly eloquent, though they are frequently criticized as being exceedingly rhetorical. His philosophical essays were largely undertaken to display his eloquence, a quality that renders them less readable for posterity.

## Philosophical and Political Writings
Bolingbroke's works include essays on "History" and "a Patriot King," the latter of which provided political ideas for George III and Bute. These works reacted against a partisan conception of politics and a narrowing view of history. His philosophical essays—including *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge*, *On the Folly and Presumption of Philosophers*, *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*, and *On Authority in Matters of Religion*—were composed as amusements in retirement. These texts are described as deficient in solidity and permanent interest, presenting a feeble deism and lacking originality in their attacks on theology and metaphysics.

His influence on figures such as Gibbon, Burke, and Voltaire is considered problematical. Edmund Burke wrote *A Vindication of Natural Society* as an imitation of Bolingbroke's fine style, but did so to refute his principles and reduce his political rationalism to an absurdity. Burke later questioned who still read Bolingbroke in full, denying that Bolingbroke's words left a permanent impression on his mind.

## Legacy and Biographies
Bolingbroke's genius and character are described as superficial, with his abilities exercised on ephemeral objects rather than universal ideas. He was succeeded in his title by his nephew Frederick, the 3rd Viscount St John.

Various biographies of Bolingbroke have been published over the centuries. An *Authentic Memoirs* appeared around 1740, followed by accounts in 1752 and 1754, and a life by Goldsmith in 1770. Modern biographies include works by Sir Leslie Stephen (1897), C. de Remusat (1856), T. Macknight (1863), J. Churton Collins (1886), A. Hassall (1889), and Walter Sichel (1901-1902), the latter of which is noted as being brilliant but unduly eulogistic.
