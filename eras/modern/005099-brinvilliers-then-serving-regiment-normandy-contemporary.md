# Marie Madeleine Marguerite d'Aubray, Marquise de Brinvilliers

Marie Madeleine Marguerite d'Aubray, known as the Marquise de Brinvilliers, was a French poisoner born in Paris around 1630. She was the daughter of Dreux d'Aubray, who served as the civil lieutenant of Paris.

## Early Life and Marriage
In 1651, Marie Madeleine married the marquis de Brinvilliers, who was serving in the regiment of Normandy at the time. Contemporary accounts from this period describe the marquise as a pretty woman who was much-courted and possessed a fascinating air of childlike innocence.

## Relationship with Godin de Sainte-Croix
In 1659, the marquis de Brinvilliers introduced his wife to a friend, Godin de Sainte-Croix. Sainte-Croix was a handsome young cavalry officer known for his bad reputation and extravagant tastes. The marquise became his mistress, a relationship that eventually resulted in a public scandal. Because the marquis de Brinvilliers had left France to avoid his creditors and made no effort to end the affair, M. d'Aubray secured the arrest of Sainte-Croix via a *lettre de cachet*.

## Acquisition of Poisons
Godin de Sainte-Croix was imprisoned in the Bastille for one year. It is popularly believed that during his incarceration, he learned the art of poisoning from a fellow prisoner, an Italian poisoner named Exili. Upon his release from the Bastille, Sainte-Croix plotted revenge against the marquise's father. 

The marquise willingly participated in this plot, experimenting with poisons prepared by Sainte-Croix, who may have had the assistance of a chemist named Christopher Glaser. To test these substances, she used the sick people she visited in hospitals and the poor who sought her charity.

## The Poisoning Plot and Murders
As Sainte-Croix faced complete financial ruin, he expanded the scope of his plan. He determined that the marquise and himself should come into possession of the large family fortune by poisoning not only M. Dreux d'Aubray but also his other daughter and two sons.

By February 1666, the marquise was satisfied that the preparations were efficient and could be administered without detection. She subsequently poisoned her father. In 1670, with the connivance of their valet, La Chaussée, she poisoned her two brothers. Although a post-mortem examination suggested the actual cause of death, no suspicion was initially directed toward the murderers.

The plot to kill Mlle Théresè d'Aubray was not carried out because Godin de Sainte-Croix died suddenly. Because he left no heirs, the police were called in.
