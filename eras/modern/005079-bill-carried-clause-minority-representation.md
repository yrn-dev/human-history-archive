# Bill

The term "bill" in the English language possesses three distinct meanings and derivations. In a legal and commercial context, it refers to a written document that was originally sealed; the word is derived from the Latin *bulla* (meaning "seal"), the Anglo-Latin *billa*, and the Early English *bille*. In this sense, it is a doublet of the word "bull." In a biological sense, it refers to the beak of a bird. Finally, as a Teutonic word, it describes a long-handled cutting weapon, derived from the Old English *bil* or *billes* (sword or falchion) and Old Saxon *bill*.

## Legislative Bills
In legislative bodies such as the English parliament, a bill is a form of statute submitted to either house. Once a bill is finally passed, it becomes an act. This system of legislating via bill and statute appears to have been introduced during the reign of Henry VI, replacing an older process involving petitions from the Commons that were assented to by the king and enrolled by judges. A legislative bill is composed of clauses containing the enactments and a preamble that recites the necessity for the legislation.

Various historical bills have shaped English and Irish governance:
*   **Reform Bills:** The first Reform Bill's second reading was initially carried in the Commons by a majority of one, which was viewed as a defeat and led to a dissolution. In the subsequent parliament, the second reading passed by a majority of 136, though the House of Lords later rejected the bill by a majority of 41. A later Reform Bill was carried with a clause for minority representation.
*   **Irish Legislation:** Stanley introduced and carried the first national education act for Ireland, which resulted in an education scheme administered harmoniously by Presbyterians, Episcopalians, and Catholics. Stanley was also primarily responsible for the Irish Church Temporalities Act, which abolished eight bishoprics and two archbishoprics to remedy abuses regarding church revenues. While the original bill included a clause to appropriate surplus revenues for non-ecclesiastical purposes, this was withdrawn before reaching the Lords due to opposition from Stanley and other cabinet members.
*   **Other Measures:** In 1614, a bill was introduced to secure representation for the borough of Barnard Castle and the city and county of Durham; however, opposition from the bishop delayed the county's return of members until 1654. Other notable legislative efforts include the Conspiracy Bill of February 1858 and a bill to remove Jewish disabilities.

## Commercial and Legal Bills
In commerce, the term "bill" is used in several specific capacities:
*   **Bill-Brokers:** Originally, a bill-broker acted as an agent who procured the discounting of bills in London for country bankers for a commission. Over time, the practice evolved so that the broker guaranteed the financier. In modern practice, a bill-broker typically buys bills on his own account at a discount and borrows money on his own security at interest, profiting from the difference between the interest and the discount. In this capacity, the bill-broker acts as a principal rather than an agent.
*   **Construction Contracts:** In building, a "bill of quantities" is used alongside drawings and specifications. The builder signs a contract agreeing to carry out work for a stated sum of money according to these documents and the instructions of the architect.

## The Bill as a Weapon
As a weapon, the bill began as a hedge-bill or bill-hook—essentially a short scythe-blade mounted on a tall staff—used for lopping branches and pruning hedges. These were used by the English before the Conquest. By the 15th century, the weapon was reinforced with a pike head above the curved blade and another jutting from the blade's back, becoming the "brown bill" featured in ballads.

While not named in Henry VII's troop contracts and absent from the king's host at Calais and Crecy, the bill was used by private men. For example, a Westmorland squire named Walter Strickland contracted armed men in the mid-15th century where seventy-six billmen marched with seventy-one bowmen, and over half of his horsemen used the bill as their primary arm. By the 16th century, the bill and halbert were largely replaced by the pike in regular troops because the pike's longer staff provided better defense against cavalry. During the 17th century, the bill remained in use as a weapon for constables or watchmen.
