# Arrests and Repudiations in Political Conspiracies

The history of political plots often involves the arrest of suspected conspirators, subsequent interrogations by sovereign authorities, and the repudiation of charges. These events frequently occur within the context of high treason or attempts to overthrow established administrations.

## The Case of Robert Baillie
Robert Baillie, also known as Baillie of Jerviswood, was a Scottish conspirator and the son of George Baillie of St. John's Kirk. Baillie had previously incurred the resentment of the Scottish government in June 1676 after rescuing his brother-in-law, a Presbyterian minister named Kirkton, who had been illegally confined by an informer named Carstairs. This action resulted in Baillie being imprisoned for four months and fined L500.

In 1683, after a failed attempt to emigrate to South Carolina, Baillie traveled to London to communicate with Russell, Monmouth, and their party to seek redress. Following the discovery of the Rye House Plot, Baillie was arrested. During an interrogation conducted by the king himself, Baillie repudiated any knowledge of the conspiracy. However, he admitted with truthfulness that he had been consulted regarding a potential insurrection in Scotland. Despite a lack of evidence connecting him to the plot, he was loaded with irons, returned to Scotland as a prisoner, and fined L6000. On December 24, 1684, he was brought before the high court on treason charges, found guilty the next day, and hanged at the market cross in Edinburgh.

## Macedonian and Spartan Conspiracies
In ancient Greece and Macedonia, the discovery of plots led to swift arrests and violent outcomes. Alexander the Great faced a conspiracy involving a man named Limnus; when Limnus was arrested, he defended himself desperately and was killed during the scuffle. Alexander subsequently suspected that Limnus was merely a tool for a more powerful person. This led to the arrest of Philotas, who was tortured in the presence of Macedonian army officers while Alexander listened from behind a curtain. After Philotas begged Hephæstion for mercy, Alexander questioned how someone so cowardly could contrive such daring plots. Philotas was put to death, and Parmenio was later assassinated in Media.

In Sparta, a conspiracy led by Kinadon was thwarted by the ephors. Kinadon was seized on the road by guards who had ostensibly accompanied him on a mission. After being interrogated and tortured, Kinadon revealed the names of his accomplices, including the prophet Tisamenus. When the ephors questioned Kinadon about his purpose for the conspiracy, he replied that he "wanted to be inferior to no man at Sparta." Kinadon and his accomplices were manacled with clogs around their necks, conducted through the city while being pricked and scourged, and eventually put to death.

## Other Notable Arrests and Plots
Throughout history, various figures have been arrested in connection with treason or political instability:

*   **Edward Marcus Despard:** An Irish conspirator and former navy captain, Despard was arrested in 1798 following complaints. Later, he engaged in a plot to assassinate George III and seize the Bank of England and the Tower of London. He was found guilty of high treason and, in 1803, became one of the last men in England to be sentenced to be hanged, drawn, and quartered.
*   **Charles I and the Five Members:** In a conflict with the Long Parliament, King Charles I plotted a sudden seizure of London to save the Earl of Strafford. Later, the King attempted to arrest five of his most active opponents by entering the House of Commons. However, he found their seats vacant as they had taken refuge in the city of London.
*   **Masabates:** In a personal plot devised by Parysatis, the king's eunuch Masabates was arrested after Parysatis won him in a game of dice played with King Artaxerxes. Masabates was subsequently flayed alive and impaled.
