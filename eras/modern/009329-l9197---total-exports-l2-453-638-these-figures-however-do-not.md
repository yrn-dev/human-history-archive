# Global Trade and Commerce (Selected Regions)

## Bolivian Foreign Trade
The foreign trade of Bolivia is described as comparatively unimportant, though statistical returns are considered unsatisfactory and incomplete. In 1904, imports were valued at £1,734,551, consisting of clothing, provisions, hardware, machinery, spirits, wines, woollens, cottons, and live-stock. Exports for the same year totaled £1,851,758. 

The principal exports recorded in 1903 included:
* Tin and its ores (£1,039,298)
* Silver and its ores (£636,743)
* Rubber (£260,559)
* Copper ores (£157,609)
* Coca (£28,907)
* Other minerals (£20,948)
* Bismuth (£16,354)
* Cinchona (£9,197)

The total exports were listed as £2,453,638. However, these figures do not accurately represent the total aggregates of Bolivian trade because exports and imports passing through Mollendo, Arica, and Antofagasta are largely credited to Peru and Chile. Trade is further restricted by the poverty of the population and a geographical position that limits exports to pastoral, forest, and mineral products due to tariffs from neighboring countries and transportation costs.

## Danish Commerce
Danish commerce is primarily based on home consumption and production, though Copenhagen provides bonded warehouses and a free port for goods imported for re-exportation. In the last twenty years of the 19th century, the value of Danish commerce doubled, exceeding fifty million sterling. Imports generally exceed exports in a ratio of roughly 1.35 to 1.

The most significant exports are food products of animal origin, specifically dairy produce. Butter is the most famous export, accounting for nearly 40% of the total export value. Other major exports include eggs and bacon. Less valuable exports include wool, vegetables, tallow, bones, cement, and dairy machinery. Home-bred horned cattle are the most important export among living animals; previously important trades in live swine and sheep have mostly shifted to a dead-meat trade. Conversely, Denmark imports a large amount of timber because native plantations of pines and firs are insufficient in quality and quantity for building purposes.

## Bulgarian Commerce
Internal commerce in Bulgaria has been modified by the development of communications, leading to the gradual disappearance of the primitive barter system in rural districts. While ninety-seven gatherings of the great fairs (*panaïri*) were held in 1898, these have lost much of their former importance, though business is still transacted at towns such as Dobritch and Eski-Jumaia.

The export trade is centered at Baltchik, Burgas, and Varna on the Black Sea, as well as Vidin, Rakhovo, Silistria, Nikopolis, Rustchuk, and Svishtov on the Danube. Imports are primarily distributed through Burgas, Philippopolis, Rustchuk, Sofia, and Varna. The government has worked to divert trade toward Varna and Burgas, opening new ports in 1906 and 1904, respectively. In 1887, the total value of Bulgarian foreign commerce was £4,419,589. Approximately 10% of exports cross the Turkish frontier.

## Trade in Other Regions
### The Protectorate and Rhodesian Provinces
In the year 1899-1900, the total trade of the protectorate was £255,384 (an increase of 75% over the previous year), with imports at £176,035 and exports at £79,449. By 1905-1906, imports rose to £222,581 and exports were £56,778. During the same 1905-1906 period, the Rhodesian provinces had exports of £18,000 and imports of about £50,000 (excluding railway material). Principal exports include rubber, ivory, tobacco, cotton, coffee, and minerals. Approximately 200 Scotsmen and Englishmen are settled as coffee planters, mainly in the Shiré Highlands.

### Taganrock and the Archipelago
Commerce between the Archipelago and Taganrock involves the export of corn, leather, and salt-fish in exchange for fruit and wines. Taganrock serves as a major deposit for Siberian productions, receiving iron, furs, and tallow from Orenburg.

## United Kingdom Brewing Industry
The brewing trade in the United Kingdom reached vast proportions, with a maximum production of 37,090,986 barrels in 1900. Production in 1905-1906 was 34,109,263 barrels. The firm Messrs Guinness owns the world's largest brewery, producing over two million barrels and paying roughly one million sterling to the revenue.

In 1905-1906, the industry used:
* Hops: 62,360,817 lb
* Malt: 51,818,697 bushels
* Sugar: 2,746,615 cwt.
* Rice, flaked maize, and similar materials: 1,348,558 cwt.
* Unmalted corn: 125,671 bushels
* Hop substitutes: 49,202 lb

The beer duty received by the exchequer in the year ending September 30, 1905, was £13,156,053. While 520,826 barrels of beer were exported and 57,194 imported, the majority is consumed domestically. In 1905-1906, consumption was 28,590,563 barrels in England, 3,265,084 in Ireland, and 1,648,463 in Scotland.
