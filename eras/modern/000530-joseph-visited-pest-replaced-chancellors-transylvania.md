# Francis Joseph and the Reorganization of the Austrian Empire

## Political Shifts and the Visit to Pest
In June 1865, Emperor Francis Joseph visited Pest as part of a broader effort to address the necessity of coming to terms with the Hungarians. During this visit, the emperor replaced the chancellors of Hungary and Transylvania. Counts Nadasdy and Francis Zichy, who were supporters of the February constitution, were removed from their positions and replaced by Count Majlath, a leader of the old conservative magnates.

This administrative change was followed by the resignation of Schmerling, who was succeeded by Count Richard Belcredi. Subsequently, on September 20, the Reichsrath was prorogued, an action equivalent to the suspension of the constitution. In December, the emperor personally opened the Hungarian diet, delivering a speech from the throne that recognized the validity of the laws of 1848.

## The War of 1866 and its Aftermath
The war of 1866 marked the beginning of a new era for the Austrian empire. Through the treaty of Prague, signed on August 23, 1866, the emperor surrendered the position in Germany held by his ancestors for centuries. Consequently, Salzburg, Bohemia, Tirol, and Austria ceased to be German, separating eight million Germans from political union with their fellow-countrymen. Additionally, the surrender of Venetia ended the long-established Habsburg domination in Italy.

Following defeats on the field of battle, the Hungarian diet was able to dictate its own terms. They revoked all attempts made over the previous century to absorb Hungary into a greater Austria and recognized no union between their country and other parts of the monarchy except that based on the Pragmatic Sanction. An agreement was reached stating that the emperor would be crowned at Pest and take the ancient oath to the Golden Bull. Under this arrangement, Hungary—including Croatia and Transylvania—was to have its own parliament.

## The Development of Budapest
The city of Pest, which was essentially a German settlement in its early history, grew rapidly during the 19th century. While it was eclipsed for a time by the sister town of Buda—which became the capital of Hungary in 1361—Pest eventually outstripped it in size. In 1723, Pest became the seat of the highest Hungarian officials, and its importance was further increased by the efforts of Joseph II and Maria Theresa.

By 1848, Pest served as the seat of the revolutionary diet. In 1872, the towns of Buda and Pest were united into one municipality. By 1900, the civil population of Budapest reached 716,476 inhabitants. The city became divided into ten municipal districts: Vár, Viziváros, and Ó-Buda on the right bank (Buda), and Belváros, Lipótváros, Terézváros, Erzsébetváros, Józsefváros, Ferenczváros, and Köbánya on the left bank (Pest). While Buda remained the official center housing government offices and the royal palace, Pest became the industrial and commercial center, as well as the hub for intellectual and nationalistic life.

## Diplomatic Relations and Reconciliation
Following the war of 1866, Austrian foreign policy was influenced by the conflict for several years while Beust served as chancellor. This period saw sharp disagreements with the Prussian government over the South German states, though a close friendship was maintained with France, including meetings between the emperor and Napoleon in Paris and Salzburg.

The war of 1870 ended these tensions, as the rapid German successes led Austria to abandon hopes of regaining its position in Germany. Following the retirement of Beust in 1871, his successor, Count Andrassy (a Hungarian), established a good understanding with Bismarck. This reconciliation was signaled in 1872 when Emperor Francis Joseph and his minister visited Berlin. A full reconciliation with Italy followed three years later when Francis Joseph visited Victor Emmanuel in Venice.
