# Anaerobic Bacteria

Anaerobic bacteria are a category of minute vegetable organisms—classified as schizomycetes—that are characterized by their ability to grow only in the absence of oxygen. Because they are strictly anaerobic, specific cultivation methods must be employed to exclude oxygen. These include "deep cultures," where inoculation occurs deep within a solid medium (particularly those containing reducing agents like glucose), or the use of the plate and fluid methods where air is displaced by an indifferent gas, typically hydrogen.

## Symbiotic Relationships
Anaerobic bacteria frequently engage in symbiosis, a form of life in common where different organisms unite. Examples of such associations include the kephir and ginger-beer plants, where anaerobic bacteria are associated with yeasts. In the ginger-beer plant, the yeast *Saccharomyces pyriformis* is found entangled in the meshes of the bacterium *B. vermiforme*.

Symbiosis also occurs among bacteria to achieve chemical results that a single species cannot produce alone. For instance, *Bacillus chauvaei* ferments cane-sugar solutions to produce hydrogen, carbon dioxide, inactive lactic acid, and normal butyric acid, while *Micrococcus acidi-paralactici* ferments the same solutions into optically active paralactic acid. However, when these two organisms occur together, they produce large quantities of normal butyl alcohol. Similarly, *B. coli* and *B. denitrificans* cannot reduce nitrates individually, but acting together, they can undo the structure of sodium nitrate so that nitrogen is released in a free state.

In soil and aquatic environments, aerobic species often protect anaerobic ones from oxygen. Winogradsky's *Clostridium pasteurianum* is an anaerobic bacterium that can only fix nitrogen if aerobic species protect it from oxygen. This cooperation is likely responsible for numerous symbiotic fermentations in the soil, including those involving *Tetanus*.

## Role in Carbon and Sulphur Circulation
Anaerobic bacteria play a critical role in the natural circulation of carbon and sulphur. While some anaerobic bacteria decompose the pectin compounds of the middle lamella to isolate cellulose fibres (a process seen when flax is "retted"), they cannot attack the cellulose itself. Other anaerobic bacteria found in the mud of rivers, marshes, and cloacae decompose cellulose, hydrolysing it and splitting the products into marsh gas and carbon dioxide. In the presence of calcium sulphate, this nascent methane leads to the formation of water, sulphuretted hydrogen ($\text{SH}_2$), and calcium carbonate. This process explains the presence of these gases in bogs and the intestines of herbivorous animals.

In the circulation of sulphur, certain bacteria utilize $\text{SH}_2$. Mud bacteria evolve sulphuretted hydrogen, which is then utilized by sulphur bacteria located above them. In marshes and estuaries, cellulose bacteria set free marsh gas that reduces sulphates (such as gypsum) to liberate $\text{SH}_2$. Sulphur bacteria thrive by oxidizing this $\text{SH}_2$ and storing sulphur in their protoplasm. If $\text{SH}_2$ becomes scarce, they oxidize the stored sulphur into sulphuric acid. 

Some partially anaerobic sulphur bacteria exist only at specific depths in quiet waters, where they meet the $\text{SH}_2$ rising from decomposing vegetable mud and the atmospheric oxygen descending from above. These bacteria use $\text{SH}_2$ as a respiratory substance to liberate energy, similar to how higher plants use carbohydrates. Conversely, the anaerobic form *Spirillum desulphuricans* attacks and reduces sulphates, reversing the work of the sulphur bacteria.

## Interaction with Higher Plants
The relationship between anaerobic bacteria and higher plants is complex. While it was once believed that the acid content of plants provided immunity to bacterial diseases, it is now known that many bacteria can flourish in acid media. Some anaerobic forms are capable of fermenting cellulose, and others can penetrate the cell-walls of living cells, such as those in Leguminosae.

In certain plant diseases, bacteria act as primary agents, causing the vessels to stop and shoots to wilt. In these cases, transverse sections of the shoots often appear brown and choked with a dark yellowish slime containing bacteria; this is observed in potatoes, cucumbers, and cabbages. However, in other cases, such as the slime fluxes of oaks or the Sereh disease of sugar-cane, bacteria are likely saprophytic forms that entered rotting tissues already injured by other agents, such as fungi or insects.
