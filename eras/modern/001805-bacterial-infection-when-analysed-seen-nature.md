# Bacterial Infection

## Nature and Mechanism of Infection
When analyzed, a bacterial infection is seen to be of the nature of an intoxication. The process is governed by two essential factors: the intoxication itself and the multiplication of living organisms within the tissues. The latter is essential to, and regulates, the supply of toxins. There is no fixed relationship between the production of toxins and the multiplication of bacteria; some organisms that are highly active as toxin producers possess relatively little power to invade tissues.

The action of toxins is believed to involve the disturbance of the complex molecules of living cells through chemical affinities, leading to varying degrees of disintegration. According to the work of Ehrlich on diphtheria toxin, the toxin molecule contains two primary atom groups: the "haptophorous" group, which attaches the toxin to the cell protoplasm, and the "toxophorous" group, which exerts a ferment-like action on the living molecule to produce toxic symptoms. Susceptibility to a toxin depends on both the chemical affinity of tissues for the toxin molecule and the sensitiveness of those tissues to its actions.

## Factors Influencing Disease Development
The entrance of a virulent bacterium into the tissues of an animal does not result in a disease with fixed characters, but varies based on circumstances. The two chief factors are the virulence of the bacterium and the susceptibility of the host. 

Susceptibility varies naturally across different species, different races of the same species, and among individuals of the same race. It also fluctuates based on the period of life; for example, adults are less susceptible to certain diseases, such as diphtheria, than young subjects. Furthermore, acquired susceptibility can increase due to conditions that lower general resisting powers, including starvation, fatigue, and exposure to cold. Local resistance may also be depressed by vitality loss or injury.

Regarding the bacterium, virulence is the all-important factor. Generally, the virulence of an organism toward an animal increases when it sojourns in that animal's tissues. This increase is particularly marked through the method of "passage," where the organism is inoculated from animal to animal in series, representing an adaptation to surroundings and the elimination of less virulent members of the species. Conversely, virulence tends to diminish when bacteria are cultivated on artificial media outside the body.

## Patterns of Bacterial Invasion
Bacterial behavior upon entering the body varies by disease type:

*   **Septicaemia:** In extreme instances, such as pneumococcus septicaemia in rabbits or anthrax septicaemia in guinea-pigs, bacteria introduced into subcutaneous tissue rapidly enter the blood stream and multiply freely, causing general poisoning via toxins. In humans, often due to streptococci, organisms are found especially in the capillaries of internal organs and may not be detectable in peripheral circulation during life.
*   **Secondary Extension:** Some organisms create a marked local lesion first, then extend via the blood or lymph stream to other parts of the body to form secondary nodules, abscesses, or tubercle glanders. In typhoid fever, this involves secondary invasion of the mesenteric glands and the presence of bacilli clumps in internal organs, particularly the spleen.
*   **Localized Infection:** In some diseases, bacteria are restricted to a specific area, and symptoms result from absorbed toxins. Examples include tetanus (restricted to a wound), diphtheria (restricted to the region of the false membrane), and cholera (practically confined to the intestine). In tetanus, local multiplication requires the presence of other bacteria, as the tetanus bacillus cannot multiply in healthy tissues.

## Bacterial Infections in Plants
Bacteria can act as primary agents in plant diseases, often characterized by the wilting of shoots and the stoppage of vessels. Transverse sections of these shoots typically appear brown and choked with a dark yellowish slime containing bacteria; this is seen in potatoes, cucumbers, and cabbages. 

While it was previously argued that acid contents provided plants immunity, it is now known that many bacteria flourish in acid media. Some anaerobic forms can ferment cellulose, and others can penetrate the cell-walls of living cells, as seen in the bacteria of Leguminosae. However, some attributed plant diseases, such as the slime fluxes of oaks or Sereh disease of sugar-cane, are considered doubtful, as bacteria in these cases are likely saprophytic forms entering tissues already injured by fungi or insects.

## Defense and Treatment
Defense against bacterial disease depends on the ability to either kill the organisms producing toxins or neutralize the toxins themselves. 

**Anti-bacterial Serums and Agglutination**
Some anti-bacterial serums possess the property of agglutination, where bacteria aggregate into clumps. This process requires the presence of salts in the fluid; if the masses are washed salt-free, the clumps resolve. Agglutination occurs when "agglutinogens" (molecules in the bacteria) unite with "agglutinins." This phenomenon has been demonstrated in cases of plague, cholera, Malta fever, infection with *B. coli*, typhoid fever, and "meat-poisoning" due to Gaertner's bacillus.

**Vaccines and Opsonic Action**
Vaccines—injections of dead cultures of the corresponding bacterium—are used to raise the "opsonic index" of the serum to increase immunity. This treatment stimulates the general production of anti-substances throughout the body to aid tissue cells in destroying organisms. The process involves a "negative phase" where the opsonic index falls after an initial rise following the first injection; practitioners aim to avoid additional vaccines during this phase to prevent further diminution of opsonic action.
