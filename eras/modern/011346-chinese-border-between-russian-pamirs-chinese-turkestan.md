# The Chinese Border: Russian Pamirs and Chinese Turkestan

The border regions between the Russian Empire and China, specifically where the Russian Pamirs meet Chinese Turkestan, are characterized by rugged topography and a history of shifting political control and scientific exploration. This frontier encompasses high mountain ranges, vast deserts, and a complex intersection of ethnic groups and imperial interests.

## Geographic Demarcation and Boundaries
The boundary between the Russian Pamirs and Chinese Turkestan is defined by the rugged line of the Sarikol range, although the actual dividing line has remained indefinite. To the north-west, the Russian boundary was influenced by the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886 and the Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895. The latter demarcated a line extending to the glaciers and snowfields that overlook the Chinese border.

Beyond Kashgar, the southern boundary of Siberia follows an irregular course toward the north-east. This line is partially defined by the Alatau and Tian-shan mountains until it reaches a northerly point at approximately 53 deg. N. lat., marked by the Sayan range west of Irkutsk. From there, the boundary deflects south-east to the Kerulen affluent of the Amur river (approximately 117 deg. 30' E. long and 49 deg. 20' N. lat.). It follows the Kerulen to the Amur river, then the Amur to its junction with the Usuri. The boundary continues along the Usuri to its head and finally reaches the Pacific coast at the mouth of the Tumen river (about 42 deg. 30' N. lat.), located 100 miles south of the Amur bay and the Russian port of Vladivostok.

## Exploration and Topography
Knowledge of the topography of western Chinese Turkestan and the northern borders of Tibet was significantly expanded by Russian explorers. Between 1889 and 1894, Pevtsov and Roborovski conducted scientific expeditions in these regions. Other notable 19th-century Russian explorers in Asia include Potanin, P.K. Kozlov, Lessar, and Annentkov, the latter of whom constructed a railway across the Trans-Caspian deserts.

In the region between Arghan and Tikkenlik, known to the Chinese as Lob, there is a significant volume of water flowing from Northern Tibet, the Eastern Pamir, and the Thian Shan into the Tarim basin. Lieutenant K. P. Kozlov argued that the Chinese Lob-nor is the Kara-Koshun (Black district), a second lake formed by the Tarim. Kozlov's research utilized a Chinese map published by Dr. Georg Wegener in 1863 and the Chinese work *Si-yui-shui-dao-tsi* (1823).

European pioneers such as Robert Barkley Shaw and George Hayward entered the central dominion of Kashgar in 1868, followed by Sir Douglas Forsyth's mission to Yarkand in 1870, during which Henry Trotter produced the first maps of Chinese Turkestan. In 1886, Sir Francis Younghusband crossed the Muztagh, the mountain barrier separating China and Kashmir.

## Historical Political Shifts
The region has seen various imperial transitions. The Mongols, under Jengis Khan, swept over the Pamirs into Turkestan in 1218, conquering Kashgar, Bokhara, and Samarkand. By the time of Kublai, the Mongols in China were saturated with Chinese civilization; Kublai is treated in Chinese annals as the founder of the Yuan dynasty (1280-1368). This was followed by the Ming dynasty (1368-1644), which was eventually overthrown by the Manchus.

The Manchu dynasty expanded Chinese civilization and influence northward into Mongolia and Manchuria. By the middle of the 18th century, the Russians and Chinese had established contact in Mongolia. During this period, China ruled Tibet, Nepal, Burmah, Annam, and eastern Turkestan, the latter of which was recovered from the Kalmuks around 1757.

## Ethnic and Cultural Groups
The uplands along the Russo-Chinese frontier, between 35° and 50° N. lat. and 70° and 85° E. long., are occupied by the Kara-Kirghiz (called *Burut* by Mongolians and Chinese). Their ancestral home lay south of the governments of Tomsk and Yeniseisk, extending beyond the Sayan range to the Tannuola hills in Chinese territory. Since the 13th century, their homes have included the Issyk-kul basin, the Tian-shan range, the Ala-tau range, and the valleys of the Chu, Tekes, and Talass rivers, as well as the Pamir table-land.

Additionally, the region of Chinese Turkestan and Northern China is home to the *Tungăni* (or *Dungen*), Mahomedans who have at times revolted against Chinese authority. One tradition suggests they are Uighúrs transferred to the vicinity of the Great Wall during the Thang Dynasty (7th to 10th century), while another traces their origin to Samarkand. Known for their commercial integrity and predilection for mercantile speculations, the Tungani were often selected by the Chinese to serve as police functionaries.
