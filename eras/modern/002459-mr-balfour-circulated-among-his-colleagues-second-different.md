# Arthur James Balfour

Arthur James Balfour (1848–) was a prominent British statesman, the eldest son of James Maitland Balfour of Whittingehame and Lady Blanche Gascoyne Cecil. Educated at Eton and Trinity College, Cambridge, Balfour entered Parliament in 1874 as a Conservative M.P. for Hertford, a seat he held until 1885.

## Early Career and Rise to Prominence
Balfour's early political trajectory included a period as private secretary to his uncle, Lord Salisbury, starting in the spring of 1878. In this role, he attended the Berlin congress and gained experience in international politics regarding the settlement of the Russo-Turkish conflict. During this time, he also established a reputation as a speculative thinker with the publication of *Defence of Philosophic Doubt* (1879).

In the early 1880s, Balfour was associated with the "Fourth Party," a group including Lord Randolph Churchill, though Balfour was viewed as the "odd man" of the group and did not participate directly in the criticisms leveled against the "old gang." Early perceptions of his physique and manner led some to doubt if he possessed the energy required for a political career.

His apprenticeship in departmental responsibilities began under Lord Salisbury's first administration (June 1885–January 1886), where he served as president of the Local Government Board, and later as secretary for Scotland. However, he gained significant recognition as chief secretary for Ireland. In this role, he implemented a policy of "coercion"—the administration of the Crimes Act—alongside remedial legislation. He was noted for his strength of will and his ability to break down the Plan of Campaign in Ireland while resisting the Home Rule party.

## Leadership and Parliamentary Activity
Balfour served as first lord of the treasury and leader of the House of Commons starting in 1891. After leading the opposition for three years, he resumed the leadership of the House upon the Unionists' return to power in 1895. While his management of education proposals in 1896 was initially criticized, he later regained his standing. His activities during the 1895–1900 period included championing voluntary schools, handling the "crisis in the Church" regarding ritualistic practices, and proposing a Roman Catholic university for Ireland.

Between 1898 and 1900, Balfour managed the foreign office during Lord Salisbury's illness and absences, notably conducting critical negotiations with Russia concerning railways in North China.

## Fiscal Disputes and the Unionist Party
As prime minister, Balfour faced significant internal party conflict over the "fiscal question." In September, he published a pamphlet titled "Insular Free Trade," in which he reviewed economic history since Cobden's time and argued for the "freedom to negotiate" to increase exchange. This manifesto led to the resignation of "strait free-traders" including Lord George Hamilton, Mr. Ritchie, Lord Balfour of Burleigh, and Mr. Arthur Elliot. Simultaneously, Mr. Chamberlain resigned.

Correspondence between Balfour and Chamberlain (September 9th and 16th) revealed that while Balfour sympathized with retaliatory duties and fiscal union with the colonies, he believed the country was not yet ready for the taxation of food. This period was marked by confusion; Lord George Hamilton stated that Balfour had circulated a second and different document among colleagues that was in fuller agreement with Mr. Chamberlain.

Following a period where the Unionists were a "miserable minority" in parliament (157 Unionists against 379 Liberals, 83 Nationalists, and 51 Labour members), Balfour and Chamberlain patched up an agreement on February 14th. Balfour declared that fiscal reform must remain the party's first constructive work to secure closer commercial union with the colonies and more equal competition for British trade. He noted that a small duty on foreign corn and a moderate general tariff on manufactured goods were not "in principle objectionable" if necessary for revenue or the party's ends.

## Resignation and 1906 Election
The end of Balfour's premiership came in November 1905. On November 14th, he gave a speech at Newcastle appealing for party unity, which was seen as an appeal to Mr. Chamberlain. However, on November 21st at Bristol, Chamberlain insisted on the adoption of his own programme. Balfour resigned on December 4th.

In the January 1906 polls, the Liberals achieved a "landslide" victory, gaining a majority of more than one hundred over all other parties combined. The Conservatives returned to the House of Commons with barely 150 members. In Manchester, Mr. Balfour and eight Conservative colleagues were defeated and replaced by nine Labour or Liberal men.
