# Baptists

The Baptists are a body of Christians distinguished by their specific view of the ordinance of baptism, maintaining that it should be administered only to believers. While some groups, such as the Mennonites, practice baptism by pouring or sprinkling, "modern" Baptists are characterized by the use of immersion.

## Origins and Theological Divisions
The Baptist movement had a double origin, resulting in two primary sections based on differing views of redemption. Those who held Arminian views and the doctrine of "General Redemption" were known as General Baptists. Those who followed Calvinistic theory and the doctrine of "Particular Redemption" were known as Particular Baptists.

The first Calvinistic or Particular Baptist Church emerged from the Jacob church in Southwark. Originally an Independent church, the Jacob church became Baptist; however, a group of its members had already been dismissed in 1633 to form the first Particular Baptist Church. Notable members of the Jacob church included William Kiffin, Hanserd Knollys, Henry Jessey, Praise-God Barbon, and John Lothropp (or Lathrop).

Over time, theological shifts occurred. In the 18th century, many General Baptists adopted Socinian or Arian theories. This led the orthodox portion of the General Baptists to form the General Baptist New Connection in 1770 under the influence of Dan Taylor. By the start of the 19th century, the New Connection consisted of 3400 members across 40 churches, while the "Old Connection" gradually merged into the Unitarian denomination. Conversely, the Calvinism of the Particular Baptists became more rigid in some churches, approaching Antinomianism, though many later became more moderate due to the writings of Andrew Fuller.

## Organization and Government
Baptists believe that each separate church is complete in itself. Consequently, every church has the power to create its own regulations and choose its own ministers to advance the kingdom of Christ. A small section, known as Scotch Baptists in the north of England and Scotland, maintains that a "plurality of elders" or pastors is required for a church's complete organization. In Scotland, the church at Keiss in Caithness (formed about 1750) was the only Baptist church before the 1765 origin of the largest Scotch Baptist church in Edinburgh, which had approximately 500 members.

Most churches are voluntarily united in associations based on geographical limits. In 1813, the Particular Baptists formed the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland. In 1891, under the influence of General Baptist leader Dr. John Clifford, the General and Particular denominations united into this single body. The Union is purely voluntary and exerts no authoritative action over separate churches, though it performs important work in collecting information. By 1909, the United Kingdom recorded 3,046 Baptist churches, 4,124 chapels, 424,008 members, and 2,078 pastors in charge. In London, a 1904 census showed Baptists as the second-largest group after Anglicans in terms of places of worship (443), aggregate attendances (54,597), and percentage of public worship attendance.

## Strict Baptists
Strict Baptist churches form a separate denomination characterized by an insistence on baptism as a condition for Christian communion and a rigorous adherence to a type of Calvinistic theology that is now generally obsolete. Because of their loose organization, accurate statistics are unavailable, though their numbers are small. They are typically linked in geographical associations, such as the Suffolk and Norfolk Association of Particular Baptist Churches or the Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches.

There is no national Union for Strict Baptists. They are further divided into the "Vessel" and "Standard" parties, named after their respective organs, the *Earthen Vessel* and the *Gospel Standard*. The Strict Baptist Missionary Society, founded in 1860 and refounded in 1897, conducts work in South India; as of 1905, it had 72 pastors and workers, 730 members, and an income of L1146.

## Education and Global Expansion
To provide an educated ministry, Baptists established several voluntary colleges. These include the Bristol Baptist College (erected about 1811), a college in Rawdon near Leeds (formed in 1804 at Bradford), and a college in Regent's Park (formed in 1810 at Stepney). Other institutions include the Pastors' College in connection with the Metropolitan Tabernacle (1856), the Baptist College at Manchester (instituted in 1866 for "Strict" views), the Midland Baptist College in Nottingham (maintained by General Baptists since 1797), and colleges in Glasgow, Ireland, and Wales. Total student enrollment across these institutions is approximately 210.

During the 19th century, the modern Baptist movement spread across Europe. In Roman Catholic countries, it was spread by English or American missionaries: France (1832), Italy (1866 and 1884), and Spain (1888). In Protestant countries and Russia, the movement began without such intervention. J. G. Oncken formed the first church in Hamburg in 1834, leading to the formation of churches in Denmark (1839), Holland and Sweden (1848), Switzerland (1849), Norway (1860), Austria and Rumania (1869), Hungary (1871), and Bulgaria (1884), as well as Finland and Russia in the 1850s and 1860s.

In British colonies, Baptists maintain a presence with unions in New Zealand, Tasmania, Canada (four Unions), South Africa, and the Australian states of New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia, and South Australia. The Baptist South African Missionary and Colonial Aid Society, based in London, assists work in South Africa.
