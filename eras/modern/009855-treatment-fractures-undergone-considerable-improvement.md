# Treatment and Classification of Fractures

The medical approach to the treatment of fractures has seen considerable improvement in recent years, moving toward methods that prioritize mobility and precise alignment to ensure a more rapid and complete recovery.

## Classification and Causes of Fractures
A fracture occurs when a bone is broken, which can happen through several mechanisms:
*   **Direct Violence:** The bone breaks at the point where it is struck, generally resulting in a transverse fracture.
*   **Indirect Violence:** The bone breaks due to a strain applied to it, typically resulting in an oblique fracture.
*   **Muscular Action:** Violent internal movements, such as a violent cough, can cause a bone (such as a rib) to break.

Fractures are further categorized by the condition of the surrounding soft tissue. A **simple fracture** is analogous to a subcutaneous laceration. A **compound fracture** involves an open wound in the soft parts, creating a communication between the injured bone and the external air. This open wound may be caused by the initial force of the injury (such as a cart-wheel passing over a limb) or by a sharp point of the fractured bone piercing the skin. Compound fractures carry significantly higher risks, as germs of suppuration can enter the site, potentially leading to blood-poisoning, osteomyelitis, necrosis (death of large pieces of bone), or delayed union.

In young people, soft bones may simply bend rather than break, a condition known as a willow or green stick fracture. Additionally, children and growing persons may experience **diastasis**, where the end of a bone becomes detached from the shaft. This can lead to stiffness in neighboring joints or an arrest of the bone's growth.

## Diagnostic Indicators and Complications
Surgeons often identify fractures through **crepitus**, the feeling produced when two rough osseous surfaces rub together. However, crepitus is absent in cases where the bone is merely bent or in an **impacted fracture**, where the broken extremities are driven into one another.

Several complications can arise during the healing process:
*   **Delayed Union:** Consolidation occurs very slowly or not at all, sometimes requiring patience, massage, and attention to general health.
*   **Ununited Fracture:** A state where nature makes no attempt to consolidate the parts after many weeks or months. This may be caused by constitutional defects, the interposition of tendon or muscle, constant disturbance of the fracture site, or insufficient closeness of the broken ends.
*   **Joint Stiffness:** In old people or cases involving injured tendons and splintered bone, effusion into the joint or tendon sheaths can organize into fibrous tissue, causing permanent stiffness.

## Modern Treatment Methods
The primary goal of treatment is "setting the fracture," which involves securing the accurate apposition of fragments to ensure firm bony union. While splints are used to provide rest—often fixing the joint above or below the fracture—modern practice has shifted away from keeping simple fractures at rest for extended periods.

Under the influence of the French surgeon Just Lucas-Championniere, it has been demonstrated that the use of massage, discreet movements, and exercises helps swelling and pain disappear more quickly and reduces the frequency of stiff hands and feet. For example, in fractures near the wrist, splints are removed daily so the fingers and wrist can be gently exercised.

The use of X-rays allows surgeons to verify that fractured surfaces are in close apposition. If ordinary methods fail to achieve a satisfactory position, surgeons may cut down to the broken ends and fix them permanently with a strong wire suture. In compound fractures where fragments cannot be easily adjusted, immediate wiring is considered a necessary part of primary treatment. For ununited fractures (excluding those caused by constitutional defects), treatment involves cutting down to the ends, sawing off a thin slice to freshen them, and fixing them with a screw or wire.

## Specialized Applications
Fracture treatment extends to various specialized medical fields:
*   **Dentistry:** In the treatment of jaw fractures, interdental splints made from a vulcanite base (vulcanized caoutchouc) are utilized. Vulcanite is also used for extensive restorations of the jaws following disease or surgical operations.
*   **Neurology:** In cases of Jacksonian epilepsy, seizures may be caused by a depressed fracture of the brain. The surgical elevation of this fracture often results in the complete disappearance of the seizures.
