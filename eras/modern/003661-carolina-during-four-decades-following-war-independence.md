# Carolina during the four decades following the War of Independence

The period following the War of Independence in the Carolinas was characterized by significant religious shifts, the influence of partisan leaders, and ongoing tensions regarding frontier settlement and political sovereignty.

## Religious Developments and the Baptist Movement
In South Carolina, the Baptist denomination experienced a period of notable growth and organizational development. The First Church in Charleston, which had nearly become extinct by 1746 due to Arminianism, saw a return to prosperity starting in 1749 under the leadership of Oliver Hart. This growth was bolstered by the formation of the Charleston Association in 1751, which was modeled after the Philadelphia Association. The association provided denominational strength by raising funds for the education of ministers beginning in 1756 and supporting domestic missionary work from 1755 onward.

During the four decades following the War of Independence, the most prominent leader of the South Carolina Baptists was Richard Furman, who served as the pastor of the First Church in Charleston. However, the most dramatic numerical increase in the Baptist population occurred between 1787 and 1812, when membership grew from 1,620 to 11,325. This expansion was attributed primarily to the "Separate" Baptist movement led by Marshall and Stearns, rather than the efforts of the Charleston Association. While the Charleston Association influenced more cultivated communities, the Separate Baptists evangelized the masses. This religious movement extended into Georgia, affecting cities such as Augusta and Savannah. Additionally, many negro slaves in the Carolinas, Georgia, and Virginia became Baptists; while most joined white Baptist churches, some in towns like Savannah and Richmond were encouraged to establish their own.

## Political Tensions and the Legacy of Loyalism
The social and political landscape of the Carolinas was heavily influenced by the divisions of the War of Independence. In the southern colonies, the divide between Loyalists (Tories) and Whigs did not follow religious lines as strictly as it did in the north. In South Carolina, for instance, Whig leaders were almost exclusively members of the established church, and only five of twenty Episcopal ministers were Loyalists.

The Tory cause in the Carolinas drew its primary support from the Scotch and non-Anglican Germans residing in the upper country. These frontier settlers had been socially and politically snubbed by the low-country aristocracy and were denied adequate representation in the colonial assembly and access to courts of justice. Because they were not engaged in commerce, they had little material interest in the political disputes over stamps and tea that drove the conflict. This disconnect between the frontier population and the low-country leadership contributed to the difficulties faced by British officers during their southern campaign.

## Frontier Conflict and Indigenous Relations
The western frontiers of the Carolinas remained a site of conflict following the war. Alexander McGillivray, a Muscogee Indian chief of Scotch and French-Indian descent, had used his British commission during the War of Independence to incite attacks on the western frontiers of the Carolinas and Georgia. Following the peace of 1783, McGillivray remained hostile to American interests, utilizing a Spanish commission to prevent American settlement in the south-west. 

McGillivray, who commanded between 5,000 and 10,000 armed followers, played British, Spanish, and American interests against one another. In 1790, President Washington sent an agent to New York to induce McGillivray to make peace. McGillivray signed a formal treaty of peace and friendship on August 7, receiving a brigadier-general's commission and payment for property confiscated by Georgia. Despite this, he remained hostile to Americans until his death, though he eventually sought to provide training for the Muscogees in white man's civilization.

## Legal and Social Order
As the population expanded westward, civil institutions often failed to keep pace, leading to the rise of "lynch law" to suppress desperadoes on the frontier. In the South, these extra-legal methods were frequently employed against anti-slavery agitators and individuals, both black and white, who were accused of inciting slaves to commit crimes against their masters or to start insurrections.
