# British India and Afghanistan: Frontiers and Boundaries

The geopolitical relationship between British India and Afghanistan is defined by a complex system of boundaries, buffer zones, and strategic passes designed to maintain a width of neutral or independent territory between the two powers. This demarcation extends from the Pamirs in the north-east to the Arabian Sea in the south.

## The Northern and Eastern Boundaries
The boundary of Afghanistan in the north-east is defined by the water-parting of the Hindu Kush. The Pamir extension of Afghan territory reaches nearly 75 deg. E., following the water-divide to the head of the Taghdumbash Pamir. Moving south-west, the boundary leaves the Hindu Kush near the Dorah Pass, separates Kafiristan from Bajour and Chitral, and crosses the Peshawar-Kabul route at Lundi-Khana.

This arrangement creates a broad belt of independent territory between Afghanistan and the boundaries of British India. This independent zone includes Bajour, Swat, and portions of Mohmand territory located north of the Kabul river. The principle of maintaining an intervening width of neutral territory is applied throughout Afghanistan's eastern borders, continuing to the point where it meets the northern limits of Baluchistan on the Gomal river.

## Baluchistan as a Buffer State
From the Gomal river, Baluchistan serves as an intervening state between Afghanistan and British India. The dividing line between Baluchistan and Afghanistan begins at Domandi, at the junction of the Kundar and Gomal rivers, following the Kundar stream south-west for approximately 40 miles. The boundary then diverges northwards toward Lake Ab-i-Istada before striking the Kadanai river and winding through the open plains west of the Kojak.

The boundary eventually reaches the western edge of the Toba plateau, which stands 8,000 feet high, before descending to the plains near the railway station of New Chaman. From there, it is defined by hill ranges southwards to Nushki. While the "red line" at the foot of the frontier hills defines the border of British India, the area between this line and Afghanistan consists of a buffer system of independent tribes.

The British government maintains a line of posts on the Zhob river, which flanks the Suliman Range. This positioning gives the British command of the hills and control over the doors of communication with Afghanistan, which contributes to a greater degree of peace and order on the southern frontier compared to the north.

## Administration of Baluchistan
Baluchistan is a country within the borders of British India, extending from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea and from the borders of Persia and Afghanistan to those of Sind and the Punjab. It is divided into two primary divisions:

1.  **British Baluchistan:** An official province of British India under a chief commissioner. It covers 9,403 sq. m. and consists mainly of tracts ceded by Afghanistan under the Treaty of Gandamak (1879) and formally incorporated into British India in 1887. This includes the districts of Sibi, Pishin, and Thal-Chotiali.
2.  **Foreign Territories:** These are under the superintendence of the same officer acting as agent to the governor-general. This section includes:
    *   **Directly-administered districts:** Areas including the Bolan Pass and the whole of the Chagai and Zhob political agencies, totaling 36,401 sq. m. Some of these are held on lease from the khan of Kalat.
    *   **Native States:** Territories belonging to Las Bela and the state of Kalat (including Kharan and Makran), covering 79,382 sq. m.
    *   **Tribal Areas:** Lands possessed by the Bugti and Marri tribes, covering 7,129 sq. m.

## Strategic Passes and Districts
The Khyber Pass is the most strategically important gateway from Afghanistan into the plains of India. It is a narrow defile of limestone and shale cliffs ranging from 600 to 1,000 feet high. 

To the east of the Kunar valley lies Bajour, a district of about 45 miles long and 20 miles broad, inhabited primarily by Tarkani Pathans. Bajour is separated from the Kunar valley by rugged frontier hills. Historically, the old road from Kabul to India ran through this barrier before the Khyber Pass became the primary route. The khan of Nawagai, the chief town of Bajour, is under British protection to safeguard the road to Chitral.

## Diplomatic and Military History
In the late 19th century, the advance of Russia in Central Asia led to critical tensions. In 1875, Lord Lytton was nominated governor-general of India with instructions to recover the friendship of the Afghan amir, Shere Ali, or arrange the frontier to be indifferent to his hostility. After the establishment of a Russian agent in Kabul and the denial of entry to the British mission of Sir Neville Chamberlain, war began in November 1878.

Following the flight and death of Shere Ali, the Treaty of Gandamak was signed in May 1879 with Yakub Khan. Under this treaty:
*   The districts of Sibi, Pishin, and Kuram were assigned to British administration.
*   The Khyber and other passes were placed under British control.
*   A permanent British Resident was established at Kabul.
*   The amir was to receive a subsidy.

Further boundary settlements occurred in the north-west via the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission (1884-1886) and the Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895, which demarcated lines reaching the snowfields and glaciers overlooking the Chinese border.
