# The Crown Requiring Advice in the Ordinary and Constitutional Manner

In the British constitutional system, the crown receives advice in an ordinary and constitutional manner to guide the administration of the state and its territories. This process involves specific channels of communication between the sovereign and the ministry, as well as specialized offices for the management of imperial affairs.

## Ministerial Advice and the Cabinet
The process of tendering advice to the crown is primarily managed through the cabinet. Cabinets are typically convened by the prime minister via a summons addressed to "His Majesty's confidential servants." These meetings generally take place at the foreign office or the official residence of the first lord of the treasury in Downing Street, though they may be held in any location.

The deliberations of the cabinet are characterized by strict confidentiality; no other officer or secretary is present, and no official record of the proceedings is maintained. Keeping a private record of these discussions is considered a breach of ministerial confidence. However, on certain important occasions, cabinet minutes are drawn up and submitted to the sovereign. This serves as the most formal method for the advice of the ministry to be tendered to the crown and placed on record. More commonly, the prime minister is tasked with presenting the collective opinion of his colleagues to the sovereign to determine the sovereign's pleasure regarding appointments and public measures.

A fundamental principle of the British constitution, and an established practice, is that the sovereign never presides at a cabinet meeting and is never present during discussions between the crown's advisers. While the sovereign does preside over meetings of the privy council, the business conducted in that forum is purely formal.

## Colonial Administration and Imperial Advice
Regarding colonial administration, the crown receives advice in the ordinary and constitutional manner from the secretaries of state for India and for the colonies. Historically, this system evolved from the "Council of Trade and Plantations," which was suppressed in 1782. Following this, there was a period where an attempt was made to govern colonies simply as dependencies, reflecting a reaction against liberal systems of colonial self-government.

The evolution of imperial governance saw the creation of the Board of Control for India in 1784, followed by the granting of representative institutions to Canada in 1791, and later to South Africa and Australia. While the home government maintained responsible control over these institutions, the growth of these communities in the early 19th century led them to reassert claims for the liberty to manage their own affairs.

## Constitutional Theory of the Empire
Under the constitutional theory of the British empire, it is recognized that the principal sections of the empire enjoy equal rights under the crown and that no section is subordinate to another. While the theory of the direct and exclusive dependence of colonial possessions on the crown has not been fully practicable in operation, it is recognized as far as possible.

The intervention of the imperial parliament in colonial matters is admitted only theoretically, specifically in instances where the constitutional advisers of the crown require the support of parliament. To bring imperial practice into complete harmony with this theory, it has been suggested that a council should be constituted to advise the crown on imperial affairs, featuring representation from all important parts of the empire. The assumption of full self-governing responsibilities by principal colonies has paved the way for movements to bring the supreme headship of the empire into closer accord with modern ideas.

## Prerogatives of the Crown
Beyond the receipt of ministerial advice, the crown possesses specific prerogatives, such as the power to create corporations by charter. In the current state of the constitution, these powers are practically exercised by parliament. A charter granted by the crown is viewed as a contract between the king and the corporation; such documents are construed more strictly against the grantee and more favorably for the crown. If a corporation surrenders its charter and the crown accepts and enrolls this surrender in chancery, the corporation is dissolved.
