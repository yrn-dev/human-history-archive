# India and the Burmo-Siamese Peninsula

## Geography and Climate
The climate of the coasts of India and the Burmo-Siamese peninsula is significantly influenced by the south-west monsoon. This monsoon carries heated air from the Indian Ocean over southern Asia, highly charged with watery vapour. When this wind current is interrupted by continuous ranges of mountains, the mass of air is forced to rise. This process rarefies the air, increases its specific capacity for heat, and reduces its temperature. This reduction in temperature leads to the condensation of the vapour, resulting in the release of a large body of water as rain upon the land.

## Cartography and Surveys
A trigonometrical survey of British India began in 1800, resulting in a survey comparable to those of most European states. These surveys utilize various scales: revenue surveys for land settlement use a scale of 1:4000, while topographical maps typically use 1:63,360. An *Indian Atlas* (scale 1:255,660), begun in 1827, includes the Malay Peninsula and Ceylon, though some sheets remain unpublished. Other official documents include a map of India (1:1,000,000) published in 1903, and maps of India's great provinces, including Burma, at a scale of 1:2,827,520.

In Siam, a regular survey was organized between 1881 and 1883 by Mr. J. McCarthy, a former official of the Indian survey. This work assisted in determining the Franco-Siamese frontier in 1906, utilizing scales of 1:4000, 1:31,680, and 1:63,360. Additionally, the British North Borneo Company published a map of British North Borneo in 1905 at a scale of 1:633,600.

## Ethnology and Population
India is characterized by a diversity of races. This is exemplified by the city of Bombay, a great port and meeting-place of the Eastern world, which exhibits a vast variety of types. While the Mahratta race is the dominant element alongside European rulers, the population also includes Arab traders, Afghans, Sikhs, Bengalis, Rajputs, Chinese, Japanese, Malays, negroes, Tibetans, Sinhalese, and Siamese. The population of Bombay is divided among Hindu, Mahommedan, Parsee, Jain, and Christian groups; the Parsees, though numbering only some 46,000, are highly influential, owning most great business houses and founding many large charities.

The native population of India was recorded at 294,191,379 in the 1901 census, categorized into 118 language groups. These are collected into principal groups including Indo-European (Indo-Aryan sub-family), Dravido-Mu[n.][d.][=a] (Dravidian and Mu[n.][d.][=a]/Kolarian), Semitic, Hamitic, Malayo-Polynesian, and Indo-Chinese (comprising Mon-Khmer, Tibeto-Burman, and Siamese-Chinese).

## Indo-China and Regional Relations
The region comprising Burma, Siam, Cambodia, and Annam is referred to as Indo-China due to its position and civilization between India and China. Indian influence is predominant as far as Cambodia, where Indian alphabets and Sinhalese-type Buddhism are employed. In contrast, Annam and Tongking utilize Chinese script and institutions.

The populations of these areas vary:
*   **The Burmese:** Linguistically allied to Tibetans, they likely entered Burma from the north-west. The kingdom annexed by Britain in 1885 was founded around 1750 by Alompra, who united the Burmese and broke the power of the Talaings.
*   **The Siamese (Thai):** Descending from southern China around the 13th century, they speak a monosyllabic language of the Chinese type but use an Indian alphabet.
*   **The Khmers (Cambodians):** An ancient kingdom now a French protectorate, showing strong Hindu influence in ruins dating from A.D. 800 to 1000.
*   **The Annamese:** A distinct race whose history involved struggles with the Chinese.

Historically, the Burmans and Siamese engaged in frequent conflict. Siamese rebels in 1771 were never subdued by the Burmans, though the Burmans held the sea-coast as far as Mergui. After failed attacks on Junkseylon (1785) and an invasion of Siam by 30,000 men (1786), peace was concluded in 1793. The Siamese ceded the coast of Tenasserim on the Indian Ocean and the seaports of Tavoy and Mergui to the Burmans. In 1795, a dispute arose when 5,000 Burmese troops entered the British district of Chittagong in India to pursue three robbers; the matter was amicably arranged by General Erskine.

## Flora and Fauna
The flora of the Indian peninsula and Ceylon shows a distinct connection to eastern tropical Africa, particularly in the similarity of mountain flora and the identity of certain families and genera. Both regions are notably absent of cycads, conifers, and oaks. While the Balsaminaceae are abundant in the peninsula, the genus *Nepenthes*—developed in the Malayan area—is found in Ceylon and the Khasi hills, but is absent from the peninsula of India and the Himalaya.

Regarding fauna, the distribution of bustards (*Eupodotis*) is described as interrupted, as none are found closer to Australia than India.
