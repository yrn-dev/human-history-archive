# Bolingbroke

Viscount Bolingbroke was an English statesman and writer known for his eloquence, political maneuvers, and philosophical essays. While he exerted a significant influence on his contemporaries, his reputation and the impact of his writings have diminished in the eyes of posterity.

## Political Career and the Treaty of Utrecht
Bolingbroke rose to prominence within the Tory administration. In July, he was created Baron St John of Lydiard Tregoze and Viscount Bolingbroke. In August, he traveled to France to conduct negotiations, signing a four-month armistice on the 19th. This process culminated in the signing of the Treaty of Utrecht on March 31, 1713, by all allies except the emperor.

Bolingbroke is held primarily responsible for the manner of this peacemaking, which was characterized by underhand conspiracies with the enemy and the violation of solemn international assurances, rather than open conferences with allies. This approach, along with the abandonment of the Catalans, is noted as a lasting stain on national honor and credit. Following this, his commercial treaty with France, which sought to establish free trade, was rejected in June.

## Rise and Fall from Power
The relationship between Bolingbroke and Harley, the basis of the Tory administration, gradually dissolved. This tension was highlighted in May when Harley was made lord treasurer and obtained the earldom of Oxford, while Bolingbroke was disappointed to receive only a viscountcy and was passed over for the Garter.

Bolingbroke eventually achieved supreme power, and his trajectory seemed to lead toward a Jacobite restoration. During this period, the Jacobite Sir William Windham was appointed chancellor of the exchequer, and military posts were filled by the faction. However, this ascent was ruined by the sudden death of the queen on August 1, and the subsequent appointment of Shrewsbury to the lord treasurership. 

Bolingbroke's actual intentions during this time remain obscure. While he claimed in *The Patriot King* that there were no designs to place the Pretender on the crown—a statement described as a palpable falsehood—he also wrote to Windham that "none of us had any very settled resolution." His primary objective appeared to be the acquisition and maintenance of supreme power by any means necessary, as evidenced by his dinner with Whig leaders on the day of Oxford's dismissal and his overtures to the elector the day before the queen's death.

## Literary Works and Philosophy
Bolingbroke was celebrated during his life for his wit, social qualities, and especially his eloquence, which Swift claimed was unrivalled among all factions. However, none of his great orations survived. His written works are divided primarily into political and philosophical categories:

*   **Political Works:** These include *The Patriot King* and *A Collection of Political Tracts* (published 1748). While spirited and dignified, they are described as exceedingly rhetorical and focused on party questions without depth or sincerity.
*   **Philosophical Essays:** Written as an amusement during retirement, these include *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge*, *On the Folly and Presumption of Philosophers*, *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*, and *On Authority in Matters of Religion*. These works are characterized as feeble deism lacking originality.

His collected works were first published in a faulty 1754 edition by Mallet, which Samuel Johnson denounced as a "blunderbuss charged against religion and morality." Subsequent editions were published in 1778, 1809, and 1841.

## Influence and Legacy
Bolingbroke's influence on later figures is debated. While George III and Bute derived political ideas from *The Patriot King*, his influence on Gibbon, Voltaire, and Burke is considered problematical. Edmund Burke wrote *A Vindication of Natural Society* (1756) as a satire that imitated Bolingbroke's magnificent style but served to refute his principles. Burke viewed Bolingbroke's rationalism as a threat that could undermine political government just as it sought to overthrow revealed religion. Burke later questioned who still read Bolingbroke in full and denied that the writer left a permanent impression on his mind.

## Biographies
Several accounts of Bolingbroke's life have been published over the centuries:
*   **Contemporary Accounts:** *Authentic Memoirs* appeared around 1740, recounting his escapades. Other accounts followed in 1752 and 1754, and Goldsmith published a life in 1770.
*   **Modern Biographies:** Notable works include those by C. de Remusat (1856), T. Macknight (1863), J. Churton Collins (1886), A. Hassall (1889), and Sir Leslie Stephen in the *Dict. of Nat. Biog.* (1897). Walter Sichel produced an elaborate biography between 1901 and 1902, though it is noted as being unduly eulogistic.

Bolingbroke was succeeded in his title by his nephew Frederick, the 3rd Viscount St John.
