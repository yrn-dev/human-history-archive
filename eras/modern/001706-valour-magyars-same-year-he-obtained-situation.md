# The Valour of the Magyars

The term "Magyars" refers to the modern Hungarians, a Turko-Finnish people whose language belongs to the Finno-Ugrian division of the Ural-Altaic tongues. Historically, the Magyars were located on the Volga around 550 and eventually settled in Hungary approximately 900 years after the era of the Roman God-Cæsars.

## Military Valour and the War of Independence
The valour of the Magyars is exemplified in the career of Janos Damjanich (1804-1849), a Hungarian soldier born in the Banat. Although an orthodox Serb, Damjanich was a devoted adherent of the Magyar liberals. During the Hungarian war of independence, he rose to the rank of major in the third Honved regiment at Szeged and later earned a colonelcy through his ability and valour at the battles of Lagerdorf and Alibunar in 1848.

By early 1849, as commander of the 3rd army corps in the middle Theiss, Damjanich gained a reputation as the bravest man in the Magyar army, utilizing daring and dash to win successive engagements. His most significant exploit occurred in early March 1849, when he annihilated a brigade at Szolnok. He further distinguished himself during the general advance on the Hungarian capital under Gorgei, fighting at Hatvan and Hort, turning the fight of Tapio-Bicsk into a victory, and displaying irresistible *elan* at the battle of Isaszeg. 

Damjanich's military fame peaked on April 19, 1849, with his victory at the battle of Nagysarlo, which enabled the relief of the fortress of Komarom. Even after the Vilagos catastrophe, when summoned to surrender the fortress of Arad, Damjanich maintained his defiant spirit, stating he would defend the fortress to the last drop of his blood against the entire Austrian army.

## Literary and Patriotic Expressions
The theme of Magyar valour also appeared in the arts. Janos Bacsanyi (1763-1845), a Hungarian poet born at Tapolcza, published a patriotic poem titled *The Valour of the Magyars* in 1785. Bacsanyi's life was marked by patriotic activity; he co-edited the *Magyar Museum* until its suppression by the government in 1792 and was later imprisoned in the Spielberg state prison for two years following his involvement in the conspiracy of Bishop Martinovich. In 1809, Bacsanyi translated a proclamation by Napoleon to the Magyars, an act considered anti-Austrian that forced him to seek refuge in Paris.

## Nationalist Spirit and Political Struggle
In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, a growth in extreme nationalist Magyar spirit led to tensions within the Austro-Hungarian dual system. This was evidenced by demands for the "Magyarization" of the joint army. By 1905, a deadlock ensued between the crown and Hungary. The Coalition in the Chamber demanded customs separation from Austria and the introduction of Magyar words of command in the army.

The demand for Magyar words of command in regiments recruited from Hungary was viewed by the crown as an encroachment on royal military prerogatives defined by the Hungarian Fundamental Law XII. of 1867. While the crown maintained that the monarch had the right to fix the language of service and command, partisans of the Magyar words argued that because Hungary reserved the right to fix the conditions under which recruits were granted, the abolition of German words of command could be made a condition of those grants.

## Social and Political Hegemony
The political landscape of Hungary was characterized by the hegemony of the Magyar nobility and gentry. During the constitutional crisis of 1905-1906, the Coalition feared that the introduction of universal suffrage—proposed by General Baron Fejervary and drafted by Kristoffy—would destroy this hegemony. There was a specific concern that admitting non-Magyars to political equality would undermine the supremacy of the Magyar race. Consequently, the Coalition eventually came to terms with the crown to ensure that any introduction of universal suffrage would safeguard Magyar supremacy over other Hungarian races.
