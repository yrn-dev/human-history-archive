# Immunkoerper of Ehrlich and Substance Sensibilisatrice of Bordet

The *Immunkoerper* (as termed by Ehrlich) or *substance sensibilisatrice* (as termed by Bordet) is a specific substance involved in the process of lysogenesis, also known as bacteriolytic or lysogenic action. This process consists of a change in a bacterium that causes it to swell, become granular, and potentially undergo dissolution.

## Role in Lysogenesis
Lysogenesis requires the interaction of two distinct substances: one that is present in normal serum and another that is either developed specifically or developed in excess. The *Immunkoerper* or *substance sensibilisatrice* is the latter. 

While this substance provides the specific character to the reaction, it cannot act alone to produce lysogenic action. It must work in conjunction with a second substance found in normal serum, which is ferment-like and highly labile. In contrast, the *Immunkoerper* is more stable and can resist temperatures of 60°C. Notably, exposure to this temperature does not result in the loss of protective power, a fact that appears to depend upon a specific anti-substance.

## Scope of Action
The lysogenic action associated with these substances is not limited to bacteria. It also occurs with other organized structures. Research by Metchnikoff, Ehrlich, and Bordet has demonstrated that if an animal is treated with injections of leucocytes, spermatozoa, or red corpuscles, its serum acquires the power to produce a disintegrative effect or dissolve these bodies.

## Relation to Anti-Substances and Complement
The development of an immune body with a specific combining affinity is analogous to the production of antitoxins. Within the broader study of anti-substances—bodies appearing in the blood serum of animals treated with antigens—Ehrlich categorized anti-substances into three groups. The *Immunkoerper* falls into the third group. 

In this category, the anti-substance first combines with the antigen. This combination then leads to the union of a third body present in normal serum known as complement (referred to by French writers as *cytase* or *alexine*). The union of these three substances results in a digestive or dissolving action, which is the mechanism observed in bacteriolytic or haemolytic serum.

## Comparison with Bacteriolysis
It is possible for the serum of a treated animal to contain an immune body without exhibiting a bacteriolytic effect. In such instances, the immune body is present and leads to the fixation of complement, but bacteriolysis does not occur.
