# Make a Point

In the context of board games and mathematical or technical analysis, the phrase "make a point" refers to distinct actions depending on the field of study. In gaming, it specifically refers to the positioning of pieces to secure a location or the accumulation of a score, while in technical contexts, it refers to the identification or designation of a specific coordinate or value.

## Game Strategy and Positioning

In certain board games, a player is said to "make a point" when they move their pieces so that two men are placed on the same point. This is contrasted with a "blot," which occurs when only a single man occupies a point. A blot is vulnerable because it may be "hit" (taken up) by an adversary who throws a number allowing them to place a man on that same point. When a man is hit, he is placed on the bar and must restart by entering the adversary's home table.

Strategic play involves two primary principles regarding the movement of men:
1. Making points where there is the best opportunity to obstruct the opponent.
2. Choosing positions for blots that minimize the probability of them being hit.

The most advantageous points to secure are the five-point in both the player's own inner table and the adversary's inner table. Other high-value points include the player's own bar-point and the four-point in their own inner table. 

Specific throws at the start of a game suggest different methods for making points. For example, an Ace and six allows a player to make their bar-point, while a deuce and four allows them to make the four-point in their own table. A trey and five allows for making the trey-point in their own table. If a player has made all the points in their own board, it provides a significant advantage because an adversary who has been hit cannot enter the board until a point is opened.

## Scoring in Card Games

In the context of card games, "making a point" refers to the accumulation of numerical values toward a winning total.

### Twenty-One-Point Casino
In Twenty-One-Point Casino, points are not scored until the end of the deal. Players maintain a mental count of points made during the final deal. The game is claimed once 21 points are scored; however, if the total is more or less than 21 when added to previous deals, the claimant loses. The order of counting is: cards, spades, Big Casino, Little Casino, aces, and sweeps. In the variation known as Spade Casino, the total points increase to 24 because each spade counts as 1, and the knave of spades counts as 2. These are recorded on a cribbage-board, and the overall game is won at 61 points.

### Playing to the Score
In other card play, players aim for specific points to gain an advantage. Principal target points include 6, 18, and 22. This is because the scores of 8, 12, and 24 are the most common for a dealer to make and can take the dealer out of the game. The value of points can fluctuate based on the stage of the rubber; for instance, in the deciding game, a point scored for honours is worth only one-third of a point scored for tricks.

## Technical and Mathematical Applications

In technical and mathematical archives, "making a point" or selecting a point refers to the designation of a specific location within a geometric or numerical set.

### Planimetry
When using a simple instrument as a planimeter, it is necessary to select a point R. While geometrical theory has failed to provide a specific rule for this, analytical theory suggests that if the mass-centre of the area is taken as R, then A may be any point on the curve. To approximate the area, an operator puts a tracer T on point R, guides it along a straight line to a point A on the boundary, and follows the boundary back to R.

### Theory of Aggregates and Analysis
In the "theory of aggregates" (also known as *Mengenlehre* or *Theory of sets of points*), points are treated as elements of an aggregate. For example, the aggregate of all real numbers or points on a line is considered an infinite aggregate. In mathematical analysis, a "neighbourhood of a point a" is defined as the aggregate of all points x where the absolute value of x - a does not exceed a positive number h.

Furthermore, in the study of uniform convergence, the condition of convergence at a point a is determined by whether, after a positive number [epsilon] is specified, a number n can be found such that the difference between sums of a series remains less than [epsilon] for all values exceeding n.
