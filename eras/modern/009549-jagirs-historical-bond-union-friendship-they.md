# Jagirs and Bonds of Union

## Jagirs in British India
In the context of the Bombay Presidency—a province of British India consisting of British districts and native states—the term *jagir* appears in relation to land grants and political settlements. The presidency, which extends from 13 deg. 53' to 28 deg. 45' N. and 66 deg. 40' to 76 deg. 30' E., includes several native states. Among these are the Deccan Satara Jagirs, which comprise the states of Aundh, Bhor, Akalkot, Daphlapur, Jath, and Phaltan.

Historically, *jagirs* were also used as financial instruments or guarantees in political negotiations. For example, Asaf-ud-Dowlah, the nawab wazir of Oudh from 1775 to 1797, obtained a loan of 26 lakhs of rupees from his mother. In exchange, he provided her with a *jagir* valued at four times the amount of the loan. Subsequently, Warren Hastings secured an additional 30 lakhs of rupees for the nawab by providing the begum with a full acquittal and the recognition of her *jagirs* for her lifetime, without interference from the Company. These grants were later confiscated due to documentary evidence of the begum's complicity in the rising of Chai Singh.

## Social and Religious Bonds of Friendship
Various cultures have utilized formal bonds to establish union and friendship. Among the Morlacchi, friendship is treated as a religious point, with a sacred bond tied at the foot of an altar. The Sclavonian ritual includes a specific benediction for the union of two friends of the same sex in front of a congregation. These individuals are termed *Pobratimi* (males) or *Posestreme* (females), meaning half-brothers and half-sisters. The duties of *Pobratimi* include revenging mutual wrongs and assisting one another in times of danger, sometimes to the point of risking their lives.

In early Roman history, the concept of a "bond of union and friendship" was proposed as a means to stabilize the state. When the Romans sought a king to avoid civil war, it was suggested that such a leader could serve as a bond of union and friendship between the Sabine nation and the city, thereby combining two races and directing the people's fury away from internal conflict.

## Political and Legal Unions
Bonds of union have frequently been established through legal oaths, marriage, or strategic alliances. In Norway, King Olaf sought to bring the Farey Islands under his authority in A.D. 1024. He required the Farey people to be subject to his laws and to pay *scat*. To finalize this union, he bound the Farey men by oath to conclude the union and observe the land privileges and laws he established.

In Japan, political unions were often driven by ambition rather than genuine friendship. For instance, the union between the Hosokawa chief, Katsumoto, and Yamana Sozen was formed to break the power of Hatakeyama Tokuhon. This alliance was cemented through the marriage of Katsumoto's daughter to Sozen and the adoption of Sozen's son, Koretoyo, by Katsumoto. Despite these ties, the union was fragile; Sozen distrusted Katsumoto's political strategy and eventually championed rivals such as Shiba Yoshikado and Hatakeyama Yoshinari in 1467.

## Cultural and Pan-Hellenic Bonds
In ancient Greece, various mechanisms served as bonds of union for the dispersed Greek populations. In the Peloponnesus, the friendly power of Ætolian Elis and its Olympic games acted as a bond of union. Between 600-560 B.C., the Olympic games were joined by three other sacred *agônes*—the Pythia, Nemea, and Isthmia. These festivals served as tutelary bonds of collective Hellenism, ensuring that Greek competitors were granted safe and inviolate transit even when passing through hostile Hellenic states.

Additionally, early political unions existed in the Peloponnesus through semi-religious and semi-political bonds. The city of Argos was initially connected to towns such as Epidaurus, Phlius, Sikyôn, and Trœzên by such a bond, granting Argos greater power and consideration than Sparta during the earliest period of historical cognizance.
