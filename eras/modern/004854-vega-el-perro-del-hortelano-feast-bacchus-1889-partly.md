# Vega's El Perro del hortelano and The Feast of Bacchus

## The Literary Influence of Lope de Vega
Lope de Vega was a significant figure in Spanish drama, establishing conventions on the Spanish stage that later dramatists were compelled to accept. His influence extended to contemporaries such as Calderón, who adapted several of Lope's works. For instance, Calderón's *Saber del mal y del bien* was an adaptation of Lope de Vega's *Las Mudanzas de la fortuna y sucesos de Don Beltran de Aragón*, and *Selva confusa* was adapted from a play of the same title by Lope. Additionally, Calderón's *El Alcalde de Zalamea* was transformed from one of Lope de Vega's rapid improvisations into a finished masterpiece.

The work of Lope de Vega also influenced later English poets. Robert Bridges, an English poet born in 1844 and educated at Eton and Corpus Christi College, Oxford, authored a comedy titled *The Humours of the Court* (1893). This play was founded in part on Lope de Vega's *El Perro del hortelano*, as well as on Calderón's *El secreto á voces*.

## The Feast of Bacchus in Antiquity
The worship of Bacchus was brought from Egypt to Athens, leading to the establishment of several feasts. These were divided into two primary categories: the "less feasts" and the "great feasts."

The less feasts, known as Lenea (derived from a Greek word meaning wine-press), served as a preparation for the great feasts. These were celebrated in the open field during the autumn. The great feasts, called Dionysia (named after one of the god's names), were solemnized within the city during the spring. According to historical accounts, these feasts contributed to the production of the theatre of Athens.

Both types of feasts were characterized by great magnificence and attracted vast crowds. The public was entertained with shows, games, and dramatic representations. During these events, poets competed for prizes by submitting comic or tragic pieces to the judgment of specifically chosen arbitrators.

## Rituals and Observances of the Bacchanalians
The feasts of Bacchus lasted for many days and involved elaborate rituals performed by initiates who mimicked the poetical depictions of the god. Participants wore wreaths of vine-branches, ivy, and other trees sacred to Bacchus, and covered themselves in the skins of wild beasts. They carried a thyrsus—a pike with twisted ivy-leaves—and used pipes, horns, and drums to create a great noise.

The masquerades included representations of Pan, the Satyrs, and Silenus. Some participants rode asses, while others dragged goats for the purpose of sacrifice. Both men and women appeared in public day and night, imitating drunkenness and dancing with indecent gestures through forests and mountains. The women were noted as being particularly outrageous, invoking the god with loud cries such as *εὐοῖ Βάκχε*, *ὦ Ἴακχε*, *Ἰόβακχε*, or *Ἰὼ Βάκχε*. This procession was followed by *κανηφόροι*, virgins from the city's noblest families who carried baskets on their heads covered with ivy and vine leaves.

## Geographic and Historical Reach
The celebration of the rites of Bacchus extended beyond Athens. In the land of the Budinoi, the city of Gelonos—a city constructed entirely of wood—maintained temples of Hellenic gods with altars and sacred images. The Gelonians, who were originally Hellenes that had settled among the Budinoi, celebrated the rites of Bacchus and held festivals to Dionysos every other year.

In later history, the Roman Emperor Honorius tolerated these rustic festivals in A.D. 399, provided they were conducted without superstition or sacrifice. He reiterated and enforced this proviso nine years later.

## Robert Bridges' The Feast of Bacchus
In the 19th century, the poet Robert Bridges produced a work titled *The Feast of Bacchus* (1889). This piece was partly translated from the *Heauton-Timoroumenos* of Terence. Bridges, who had a career as a physician at the Great Northern hospital and the Children's hospital in Great Ormond Street before retiring in 1882, was known for a theory of prosody emphasizing that poetry should follow the rules of natural speech and depend on the number of stresses in a line rather than the number of syllables.
