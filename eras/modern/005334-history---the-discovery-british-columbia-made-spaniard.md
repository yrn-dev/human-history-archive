# British Columbia

British Columbia is the western province of the Dominion of Canada. Its geography is defined by the Pacific Ocean to the west, where it fronts for approximately 600 miles, and the coast strip of Alaska for an additional 400 miles. To the north, the boundary is 60° N, and to the south, it is formed by 49° N and the strait separating Vancouver Island from the state of Washington. The eastern boundary follows the continental watershed in the Rocky Mountains until it intersects 120° W, which then runs north to 60° N, incorporating part of the Peace river country. The province includes the Queen Charlotte Islands, Vancouver Island, and various smaller islands off the western coast of Canada.

## Early Exploration and Colonial History
The discovery of British Columbia was made by the Spaniard Perez in 1774. Geographical exploration of the coast began in 1778 with a visit from Cook. Between 1792 and 1794, Vancouver surveyed almost the entire coast of the province, as well as significant portions to the north and south, on behalf of the British government. During this same period, Mackenzie and traders from the N.W. Company entered the interior. In 1821, the N.W. Company amalgamated with the Hudson's Bay Company, which then ruled the immense territory for twenty-eight years with "beneficent despotism."

The region transitioned into formal colonial status in 1849 when Vancouver Island was proclaimed a British colony. In 1858, following the discovery of gold and a resulting large influx of miners, the mainland territory was established as a colony named British Columbia. This mainland colony was later united with the colony of Vancouver Island in 1866 under the name British Columbia.

## Confederation and Governance
British Columbia entered the confederation and became part of the Dominion of Canada in 1871. A primary condition of this entry was the promise of the speedy construction of the Canadian Pacific railway. Relations between the province and the government at Ottawa became strained by 1876 due to the non-fulfilment of this promise and the government's apparent indifference to the province's representations; these relations were only ameliorated once the construction of a transcontinental road began.

Under the British North America Act (1867), the provincial legislature is granted jurisdiction for its own governance, though its acts may be disallowed by the governor-general in council within one year of passage. Legality may be challenged in the supreme court of Canada or via appeal to the juridical committee of the privy council of the United Kingdom. The province sends seven members to the lower house of the federal parliament and three senators to the federal parliament in Ottawa.

The provincial judicial system includes a supreme court presided over by a chief justice and five puisne judges, along with several county courts. Notably, the supreme court of British Columbia retains jurisdiction in divorce cases, a right invested in the colony prior to confederation.

## Population and Demographics
As of the 1901 census, the province had an area of 357,600 square miles and a population of 190,000. This population increased following the discovery of precious metals in the Atlin, Boundary, and Kootenay districts. While much of this growth consisted of a floating population of miners, the permanent population grew due to new steamboat lines, railway expansion into the valleys, and settlements near the Canadian Pacific railway.

The population is diverse, comprising men of many nationalities. The 1901 census recorded a Chinese population of 14,201, though the influx of Chinese immigrants practically ceased due to a $500 per head tax imposed by the dominion government. Japanese immigrants, primarily engaged in fishing and lumbering, also arrived. Friction occurred between 1907 and 1908 between the province and the Dominion government over an anti-Japanese attitude in British Columbia, which manifested in serious riots. White populations expressed objection to both groups based on labor market competition, though the Japanese were perceived as adapting better to the ways of white men.

The Indian population was recorded at 25,488 in 1901, with 20,351 professing Christians and 5,137 pagans. These populations are divided into many tribes. In the northern interior, the Tinneh or Athapackan people reside, while the Salish and Kootenay occupy the southern interior. Coastal tribes include the Tsimshian, Haida, Nootka, and Kwakiatl. Various tribes related to the Salish proper are located around the Gulf of Georgia. The Bellabella (a corruption of Milbank) are a tribe of Kwakiutl Indians including the Ocalitk, Oetlitk, and Kokaitk subtribes, numbering about 300 and converted to Christianity by Protestant missionaries. The Bellacoola (or Bilqula) are a Salishan stock tribe numbering some 300. The Kutenai (Kutonaga) are a distinct stock of Kitunahan who ranged along the Kootenay river and lake; noted for their honesty and friendliness to whites, there were some 550 of them in British Columbia in 1904. Unlike the Indians of the plains, there is no treaty with the Indians of British Columbia for the relinquishment of their land title, though the government provides assistance.

## Society and Infrastructure
In 1901, the religious distribution of the population included 40,687 members of the Church of England, 34,081 Presbyterians, 33,639 Roman Catholics, and 25,047 Methodists, with 40,197 identifying as others.

The educational system is government-maintained, free, and undenominational, consisting of high, graded, and common schools. These are controlled by trustees selected by ratepayers in each district, overseen by a superintendent of education under the provincial secretary. The province's sole college is the McGill University College of British Columbia in Vancouver, affiliated with McGill University in Montreal.

Key historical developments following confederation include the founding of the city of Vancouver by the C.P.R., the establishment of Canadian steamship lines to Australia and to China and Japan, the discovery of the Boundary and Kootenay mining districts, and disputes with the United States regarding pelagic sealing.
