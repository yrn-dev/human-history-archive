# The Fur-Seal Fishery

The fur-seal fishery is an industry of significant importance, although it has shown signs of decline. This decline is attributed to the scarcity of seals and international difficulties regarding pelagic sealing in the Bering Sea, which is the region where the greatest number of seals have been taken.

## Biological Classifications and Characteristics
Within the fur trade, supplies of furs are dependent upon several classes of animals, including the Carnivora, which encompasses fur seals and hair seals. There are two distinct classes of sealskins: fur seals and hair seals. Hair seals lack a growth of fur beneath their stiff top hair and are generally hunted for their leather and oil, with the exception of some marbled seals.

Sea-bears and sea-lions are also widely distributed in the temperate regions of both hemispheres, though they are absent from the North Atlantic coasts. These animals are gregarious and polygamous, with males typically being much larger than females. They spend more time on shore and range further inland than true seals, particularly during breeding times, although they must return to the water for food. Some of these species possess a fine, dense, woolly under-fur beneath a stiff, hairy covering. When this under-fur is dressed and the longer outer hairs are removed, it becomes the "sealskin" used in commerce.

Notable species include:
*   ***O. ursina***: The sea-bear or fur-seal of the North Pacific.
*   ***O. stelleri***: The northern sea-lion of the North Pacific, reaching approximately 10 feet in length.
*   ***O. jubata***: The southern sea-lion, found in Patagonia and the Falkland Islands.
*   ***O. californiana***: Found in California.
*   ***O. antarctica*** (or ***O. pusilla***): Found at the Cape of Good Hope.
*   ***O. forsteri***: Found in Australia and various southern hemisphere islands.

## Geography and Distribution
The highest quality fur seals are located from the Alaska coast down to San Francisco. Environmental factors influence the characteristics of the fur; in exposed regions, the body covering is coarser, whereas in densely wooded districts, the furs are darker in color and the hair and wool are softer and more silky.

The supply of the best skins comes primarily from the North Pacific, specifically from Alaska, the Pribilof Islands, the north-west coast of America, Japan, Robben Island, and the Copper Island of the Aleutian group near Kamschatka. Skins are also harvested from the South Atlantic and South Pacific Oceans, including the Crozet Isles, the Cape of Good Hope, the Falkland Islands, and around Cape Horn up to the Lobos Islands at the entrance of the La Plata river. While skins were formerly obtained in large numbers from Australia and New Zealand, current importations are small and of poor quality. Except for the "pick" of the Lobos Island seals, the fur of southern sea seals is considered very poor and suitable only for the cheapest market.

## Commercial Trade and Preparation
Seal skins vary in size from 24 x 15 inches to 55 x 25 inches. The skins taper at each end, especially at the tail, and are very narrow in the center between the fins. In the trade, the largest skins—reaching up to 8 feet in length—are known as "wigs"; however, these are weak and uneven in the fur, and hunters do not seek them. 

Furriers prefer skins that match the length of the garment to avoid cross or lateral seams. While very small pups are of beautiful quality, they are too tiny for garments. The most useful skins for coats are large pups measuring 42 inches long, as their quality is uniform and very good. The value of these skins ranges from 10s. to 232s. Although the preparation of seal skin takes longer than any other fur skin, it is valued for its durability, warmth, and rich finished effect.

## Legal and Regional Regulations
In England, the Seal Fisheries Act of 1875 was passed following representations by the Swedish government regarding the killing of seals on the east coast of Greenland during breeding time, which left young seals to starve. This act allowed the crown to establish a "close time" for the fishery via order in council, provided that foreign states involved in the fishery made similar provisions. An order in council declaring the season to begin on April 3rd of each year was issued on February 8, 1876; after being rescinded on February 15, 1876, it was re-enacted on November 28, 1876, and remains operative.

Other regional sealing activities include the hunting of seals in Krasnovodsk Bay and sealing operations conducted by the Eskimo.
