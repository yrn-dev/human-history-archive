# Sir Francis Younghusband and the Exploration of Asia

Sir Francis Younghusband was a British explorer and officer who conducted several significant journeys across the Asian continent during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, contributing to the geographical knowledge of Tibet and Central Asia.

## The Mission to Lhasa (1904)
In 1904, Sir Francis Younghusband led a mission to the city of Lhasa. This expedition had two primary geographical results. First, it led to an extension of the Indian system of triangulation, which served to finally determine the geographical position of Lhasa. Second, the mission facilitated a highly valuable reconnaissance of the valleys of the Indus and the Upper Brahmaputra, conducted by Captains C.G. Rawling and C.H.D. Ryder.

## Early Explorations and Central Asia
Prior to his mission to Lhasa, Younghusband engaged in adventurous travel across the heart of the Asian continent. In 1886, he completed a journey that involved crossing the Muztagh, which is the great mountain barrier situated between Kashmir and China.

During his travels in the Altai Mountains, Younghusband collected several heads of *Ovis Poli*, known as Argali by the Mongols. He noted that these specimens differed from those he later saw at Yarkand (which had been brought from the Pamir), observing that the Gobi specimens had a shorter length of horn, a less pronounced curve, and were considerably thicker at the base. In later years, Younghusband frequently mentioned the sport of shooting *Ovis Poli*.

## Context of Tibetan Exploration
Younghusband's work was part of a broader period of prolific scientific geographical exploration in Asia, much of which focused on the central depression and the elevated table-land of Tibet. Before the 1904 mission, several other explorers had attempted to reach or map the region:

*   **Native Surveyors:** Pundits Nain Singh and Krishna provided significant data. Nain Singh explored the Upper Brahmaputra and Indus sources (1865-1867) and visited Lhasa in 1874-1875. Krishna traveled from Lhasa northwards to Sachu in Mongolia (1879-1882) and later passed through eastern Tibet to Darchendo.
*   **European Explorers:** Various travelers attempted to enter the "jealously-guarded portals" of Lhasa with varying success. Pierre G. Bonvalot (1886-1887), W.W. Rockhill (1889-1891), and Hamilton Bower (1891) all failed to enter the city. St George Littledale came almost within sight of the sacred walls of Lhasa during a journey in 1894-1895 but also failed to pass inside.
*   **Other Contributors:** Between 1896 and 1898, Captain H. Deasy and Captain M.S. Wellby provided valuable service to geography. Captain Deasy extended the Pamir triangulation into the sources of the Zarafshan and the plains of Kashgar. Additionally, the Swede Professor Sven Hedin conducted monumental investigations in Tibet and the deserts of Takla Makan between 1894 and 1896, later resuming journeys from 1905-1908 to revolutionize knowledge of the region north of the upper Tsanpo.
