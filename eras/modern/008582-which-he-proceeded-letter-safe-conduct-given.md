# Letters of Safe Conduct and Notification

In historical and legal contexts, formal letters have been utilized for a variety of purposes, ranging from the granting of safe passage for individuals and diplomats to the notification of victims in legal proceedings.

## Safe Conduct in Historical Contexts

Letters of safe conduct have historically served as official guarantees of protection for individuals traveling through potentially hostile or foreign territories.

### Medieval Scotland and Europe
In the 14th century, such letters were used to facilitate academic pursuits. John Barbour, a Scottish poet and archdeacon of Aberdeen, was granted a letter of safe conduct in 1357 to allow him to travel to Oxford for study. He received a similar letter in 1364 and another in 1368, which granted him permission to travel to France, likely for further studies at the university of Paris.

### Norse and Russian Relations
During the early 11th century, safe conduct was employed to facilitate political alliances and the restoration of royalty. In 1034, Einar Tambaskelfer and Kalf Arnason led a retinue from Throndhjem through Jamtaland, Helsingjaland, and Svithjod, eventually reaching Ladoga in Russia. They sought to assist Magnus, the son of King Olaf the Saint, in attaining his father's heritage and the kingdom of Norway. Upon receiving this request, King Jarisleif of Novgorod, after consulting with his queen and chiefs, granted safe conduct to the Northmen to enable their journey to Novgorod.

## Legal Notification and Victim Correspondence

In modern legal frameworks, specifically within the United States, the use of notification letters is governed by statutes and guidelines intended to inform victims of the status of criminal proceedings.

### The CVRA and Victim Notification
The Crime Victims' Rights Act (CVRA), cited as 18 U.S.C. § 3771, governs the notice of proceedings and the results of investigations regarding federal crimes. In the case of Jeffrey Epstein, the USAO (United States Attorney's Office) handled the issuance of victim notification letters. Between July and September 2008, a series of notifications occurred:
*   In July 2008, Villafafia prepared and sent a notification letter to listed victims.
*   Between July and August 2008, the FBI sent notification letters to victims residing outside the United States.
*   Between August and September 2008, the federal court ordered the USAO to disclose the Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA) to victims, resulting in a revised notification letter.

### Disputes Over Notification
The process of notifying victims often involves conflict between prosecutors and defense counsel. In the Epstein case, defense counsel Lefkowitz objected to CVRA notifications, arguing that the Attorney General Guidelines defined a "crime victim" as someone harmed by an offense charged in federal district court, and noting that Epstein had not been charged in federal court. While Lefkowitz did not object to "some form of notice" being given to alleged victims, he insisted that any notices regarding state offenses be sent by the State Attorney rather than the USAO.

Furthermore, defense counsel argued that the actions of Villafafia and First Assistant United States Attorney Sloman constituted professional misconduct. This accusation stemmed from their efforts to send a "victim notification letter" to all listed victims, which the defense characterized as "highly improper and unusual."

### OPR Findings on Conduct
The Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR) reviewed the conduct of Villafafia regarding these notifications. OPR found that the narrative suggesting Villafafia colluded with defense counsel to benefit Epstein at the expense of victims was inaccurate. The evidence indicated that Villafafia cared deeply about the victims; she had raised the issue of consulting victims with her supervisors before the NPA was signed and drafted letters to notify victims of pending state plea proceedings. OPR concluded that any missteps in her interactions with victims were not intended to silence them.

## Intercepted Correspondence and Political Pretexts

Letters have also been used as instruments of political manipulation. In ancient Greece, Dion wrote a letter to Carthaginian commanders in Sicily, inviting them to send any peace propositions for Syracuse through him. Although the letter did not imply treason, Dionysius used the intercepted correspondence as a pretext to remove Dion. After inviting Dion to the acropolis under the guise of healing their differences, Dionysius accused him of treason and ordered him to be carried off to Italy by boat.
