# Amblystoma and the Axolotl

The genus *Amblystoma* consists of approximately twenty species of salamanders found in North America, including the eastern and western United States and cooler regions of Mexico. Established by J. J. Tschudi in 1838, the genus was created to categorize salamanders previously described as *Salamandra* or *Lacerta*. These animals are characterized by smooth, shiny bodies with vertical grooves on their sides and a feebly compressed tail. They possess moderately large eyes with movable lids and a nearly straight upper lip. A distinguishing feature of their anatomy is the dentition of the palate, where small teeth form a long transverse series—either continuous or interrupted—behind the inner nares (choanae), mirroring the single row of teeth found in the jaws.

## The Axolotl and the Larval Form
The axolotl is the Mexican name for larval salamanders of the genus *Amblystoma*. In the early 19th century, when the first specimens of *Gyrinus edulis* (the axolotl) were brought to Cuvier by Humboldt, Cuvier recognized that these creatures were not related to permanent gill-breathers like *Proteus* or *Siren*, but were instead the larval form of an air-breathing salamander. This opinion was largely ignored by systematists for over fifty years. Because axolotls were found to breed while remaining in their branchiate (gill-bearing) condition, they were placed in the genus *Siredon* and classified as permanent gill-breathers.

## Habitat and Distribution near Mexico City
Axolotls occur in abundance in Lakes Chalco and Xochimilco near Mexico City, where they are procured for the market. These lakes are situated approximately 10 feet higher than the Lago de Texcoco and are separated from it by several hills. Lake Xochimilco is characterized by powerful springs, though its water is otherwise dark and muddy, containing decomposing vegetable matter, worms, *Daphniae*, insect larvae, and fish. The surrounding environment includes high mountains sloping toward the southern shores, fertile pastures, ravines, rocks, shrubs, and trees.

Axolotls breed in early February, fastening their eggs to water plants. The larvae grow rapidly, becoming "big, fat creatures" by June. While they may take to the rushes in late summer and become scarce in autumn, no axolotls in these lakes have been known to metamorphose or leave the water. Notably, research by H. Gadow in 1902 confirmed that axolotls are entirely absent from the brackish Lago de Texcoco, contradicting theories by Weismann that salt-incrusted surroundings prevented their metamorphosis.

## Metamorphosis and Amblystoma tigrinum
The axolotl is the larval form of the salamander *Amblystoma*. The "perfect" or imago form is the *Amblystoma tigrinum*, a widely distributed species that rarely exceeds nine inches in length. It is typically brown or blackish with yellow spots, sometimes arranged in transverse bands. The transition from the larval axolotl to the *Amblystoma* state involves a metamorphosis marked by the loss of the gills and dorsal crest, the development of movable eyelids, and changes in dentition.

While it was once stated that axolotls in the lakes near Mexico City never transform, evidence suggests otherwise. Alfred Duges described an *Amblystoma* from mountains near Mexico City, though he remained suspicious of various statements regarding this occurrence and skeptical of theories proposed by biological speculators who had not visited Mexico. H. Gadow later confirmed Duges's statement, noting a second species of *Amblystoma* near Mexico at a higher altitude that undergoes normal metamorphosis.

## History of Study in Paris
In late 1863, thirty-four axolotls (including one albino) were sent to the Jardin d'Acclimatation in Paris by order of Marshal Forey. Six of these were given to Professor A. Dumeril at the Jardin des Plantes. While initial spawning in the Jardin d'Acclimatation failed because the eggs were eaten, the female at the Jardin des Plantes successfully reared young. 

By the second generation, some individuals began to transform, losing their gills and developing the yellow spots and characteristics of *Amblystoma tigrinum*. Between 1865 and 1870, twenty-nine such transformed salamanders were obtained, though they did not breed. It was not until 1876 that Professor E. Blanchard reported the first instance of an axolotl in its *Amblystoma* state spawning in the reptile house of the Jardin des Plantes.
