# Tibet

Tibet is characterized as a great elevated table-land and highland plateau. Geographically, this region stretches from the Himalaya in the south northwards to Chinese Turkestan, and from the frontier of Kashmir in the west eastwards to China. In the modern period, this plateau, along with the central depression located to its north, has been a primary focus of scientific geographical exploration in Asia.

## Geographical Exploration and Mapping
Recent scientific enterprises have worked to modify previous understandings of the orography of Tibet and Mongolia. Through these efforts, the highland plateau has been defined with comparative geographical exactness. 

Modern map construction relies on specific obligatory points to ensure accuracy. One such point is Sachu (also known as Saitu) in Mongolia. While various explorers provided different longitude values for Sachu—including Krishna (94 deg. 23'), Prjevalsky (94 deg. 26' during his second and third explorations), Carey and Dalgleish (94 deg. 48'), Littledale (94 deg. 49'), and Kreitner (94 deg. 58')—the adopted value is 94 deg. 54' E. of Greenwich, as revised by Prjevalsky during his fourth exploration. 

Another obligatory point is Darchendo (or Tachienlu), located on the extreme east on the high road between Lhasa and Peking, on the borders of China. The Royal Geographical Society has adopted a longitude value of 102 deg. 12" for this location, though other values were recorded by Baber (102 deg. 18"), Krishna (102 deg. 15"), and Kreitner (102 deg. 5").

## Russian and Native Expeditions
Russian explorers have contributed significantly to the knowledge of Tibet's northern borders and the topography of western Chinese Turkestan. Between 1889 and 1890, and again in 1894, explorers Pevtsov and Roborovski conducted expeditions based on scientific principles that yielded results of the highest value.

In southern Tibet, knowledge of the great plateau was expanded largely by native surveyors attached to the Indian survey, specifically Pundits Nain Singh and Krishna. 
* **Nain Singh:** Between 1865 and 1867, he explored the sources of the Upper Brahmaputra and the Indus. Later, from 1874 to 1875, he traveled from the eastern frontiers of Kashmir to Lhasa, where he stayed for several months, passing through the Tengri Nor lake.
* **Krishna:** Between 1879 and 1882, Krishna journeyed from Lhasa northwards through Tsaidam to Sachu in Mongolia. He then traveled through eastern Tibet to Darchendo. After failing to reach India via Upper Assam, he returned to the vicinity of Lhasa and crossed the Himalayas using a more westerly route.

Both Nain Singh and Krishna visited the city of Lhasa.

## Political History and Foreign Intervention
By the middle of the eighteenth century, China ruled over several regions, including Tibet, Nepal, Burmah, Annam, and eastern Turkestan. 

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Tibet became entangled in the imperial ambitions of European powers. Various foreign offices sought to carve up civilized Asian countries for exploitation, driven by the imperial successes of the Dutch in the East Indies and the British ruling class in India. This period of geopolitical tension saw the Russians take possession of Port Arthur in 1899 and later seize Manchuria. In 1904, the British invaded Tibet.

## Documentation and Natural Resources
Various scientific works and diaries have documented journeys through the region. Notable publications include Sven Hedin's *Scientific Results of a Journey in Central Asia* (1905-1907), which includes a general map of Tibet at a scale of 1:1,000,000, and W. W. Rockhill's *Diary of a Journey through Mongolia and Tibet in 1891 and 1892*. Other records include the *Izvestia* of the Russian Geog. Soc. and the *Geog. Journal*.

Additionally, the region is noted for the Tibet lamb, an animal with a creamy white, curly, silky, and fine fleece. These skins are traded in London, where they are used for children's muffs, ties, perambulator aprons, and trimmings for evening mantles.
