## Overview of Literary Sources
Within the historical record of Joseph Bonaparte, the eldest brother of Napoleon I, several key works provide analysis of his life and tenure. Among these are the publications by G. Bertin, specifically *Joseph Bonaparte in America* and the anonymously authored *Joseph Bonaparte juge par ses contemporains*. These works exist alongside a broader body of literature, including the ten-volume *Memoires et correspondance politique et militaire du roi Joseph* (1854) and *Les Rois freres de Napoleon* (1883) by Baron A. du Casse, as well as J.S.C. Abbott's *History of Joseph Bonaparte* (1869). Other relevant archival materials include the *Memoirs of Count Miot de Melito* and the *Correspondence of Napoleon with Joseph Bonaparte*.

## Political Career and Reign
Joseph Bonaparte's political trajectory was closely tied to the rise of his brother, Napoleon. In 1808, Napoleon appointed Joseph as the King of Spain. During his time in Naples, Joseph had a minister of war, Dumas, who served him before Dumas rejoined the French army for the 1808 campaign in Spain. 

Prior to his kingship, Joseph's role was heavily diplomatic. He served as a member of the *Corps Legislatif* and the council of state. He was instrumental in several major international agreements, including the treaty of Luneville with Austria (February 9, 1801) and the treaty of Amiens (March 25, 1802), the latter of which was the result of lengthy discussions with Lord Cornwallis, the British envoy. Joseph also concluded a convention with the envoy of the United States at his country-house in Mortfontaine in 1800.

Internal family friction occurred regarding the succession of the French throne. Because neither Napoleon nor Joseph had a male heir, Joseph—holding strict ideas of primogeniture—claimed he should be recognized as the heir. Napoleon, however, preferred the son of Louis Bonaparte. To resolve this, Napoleon offered to make Joseph the king of Lombardy if he waived his claim to the French throne, but Joseph refused.

## Life in the United States
Following his time as the ex-king of Spain, Joseph Bonaparte resided in the United States. He lived in Bordentown, a city in Burlington County, New Jersey, during two separate periods: from 1817 to 1832 and again from 1837 to 1839. 

During his residence in Bordentown, he established a magnificent estate known as "Bonaparte's Park." His presence in the region was significant enough that the New Jersey legislature passed a special law allowing him, as an alien, to own real property. It is noted that the state of New Jersey received the nickname "Spain" in reference to this arrangement. While living at his estate, Joseph entertained various distinguished guests, including Lafayette.

## Legacy and Family
Joseph was the eldest of Napoleon's brothers and sisters. The Bonaparte family legacy continued into later generations in America; for example, Charles Joseph Bonaparte (born 1851 in Baltimore) was the grandson of Jerome Bonaparte, king of Westphalia. Charles Joseph became a prominent American lawyer and politician, serving as the secretary of the navy under President Roosevelt from July 1905 to December 1906, and subsequently as the attorney-general of the United States until March 1909.
