# Stipulations and Legal Obligations

In historical and legal contexts, a stipulation refers to a specific requirement, condition, or agreed-upon sum within a contract, treaty, or legal framework. The nature and enforcement of these stipulations vary significantly across different cultures, legal systems, and political eras.

## Roman Law and the Concept of Stipulation
In ancient Rome, the "stipulation" was a fundamental legal mechanism used to create civil obligations. According to the rigid maxims of the decemvirs and patricians, a simple promise, oath, or naked pact did not constitute a civil obligation unless it was confirmed through the legal form of a stipulation. This process was characterized by a firm and irrevocable contract expressed through a specific mode of question and answer. For example, one party (such as Seius) would ask, "Do you promise to pay me one hundred pieces of gold?" and the other party (such as Sempronius) would reply, "I do promise." 

While the strict theory of stipulation was the baseline, the praetors, acting as guardians of social faith, eventually allowed for rational evidence of voluntary and deliberate acts to produce equitable obligations, providing remedies and actions where a formal stipulation might have been missing.

## Contractual Damages and Liquidated Sums
In modern legal systems, stipulations often concern the amount of damages to be paid if a contract is not fulfilled. In France, under the Civil Code (specifically articles 1152, 1153, and 1780), stipulations regarding damages for a breach of contract—other than for the payment of a sum of money—are binding on the courts. Similarly, in Scotland, these are referred to as "stipulated" or "estimated" damages.

However, in the United States, England, and Scotland, courts may disregard the specific wording of a stipulation to determine the real nature of the transaction. They distinguish between "ascertained damage" and a "penalty" intended to be *in terrorem* (as a threat) over the defaulter. If a court deems a stipulated sum to be a penalty, it will require proof of actual loss. For instance, in the 1829 case of *Kemble v. Farren*, a contract stipulated that £1000 be payable for any breach as liquidated damages; however, the court ruled this was a penalty because the sum could have been triggered by the non-payment of a trifling amount. Other examples of accepted contractual measures of damage include stipulations to complete torpedo boats for a foreign government or to finish sewerage works by a specific date.

## Diplomatic Treaties and International Stipulations
Stipulations are frequently central to international treaties and the subsequent disputes that arise from their breach.

### The Ashanti and the Fomana Treaty
Following the war of 1873-74, the Ashanti placed Mensa on the golden stool, the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's rule was characterized as tyrannous and marked by repeated human sacrifices. During his reign, almost every article of the Fomana treaty was broken. This included the failure to pay a stipulated indemnity; of the 50,000 oz. of gold required, only 4,000 oz. were paid. Later, under King Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III), the British government intervened when Prempeh closed roads to traders and failed to respond to demands to fulfill the 1874 treaty and accept a British protectorate.

### The Treaty of Yandaboo
In the conflict between the British and the army of Ava, peace was proposed under several conditions. These included the cession of Ye, Tavoy, and Arakan, and the payment of a crore of rupees as indemnification for war expenses. A specific stipulation was included stating that British ships would no longer be required to land their guns or unship their rudders in Burmese ports. This was eventually ratified in the treaty known as the treaty of Yandaboo.

### The Delagoa Bay Railway Dispute
A dispute arose between Portugal and the Delagoa Bay and East African Railway Company regarding the construction of a line to the Transvaal. Portugal required the extension to Komati Poort to be completed within eight months, a requirement described as an "impossible stipulation" due to the rainy season. Following the failure to meet this deadline, Portugal seized the line in 1889. This led to an arbitration by three Swiss jurists, resulting in Portugal paying £950,000 (plus an initial £28,000) in damages.

## Other Applications of Stipulated Terms
Stipulations also appear in royal agreements and financial structures:
*   **Royal Marriages:** The peace of the Pyrenees (1659) included an important stipulation regarding the marriage of Louis XIV to his cousin, the Infanta Maria Theresa, to cement the friendship between France and Spain.
*   **Financial Associations:** In building and loan associations, a member typically pays a stipulated minimum sum (such as $1) upon taking membership and buying a share of stock.
