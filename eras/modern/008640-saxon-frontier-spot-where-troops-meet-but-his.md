# Ferdinand and the Bohemian Army of 1546

## Military Mobilization and the Saxon Frontier
In 1546, Ferdinand sought to raise a Bohemian army to provide support for his brother. This objective proved difficult due to a shift in the views of Bohemian reformers since the beginning of the Reformation in Germany. While some advanced Utraquists held views similar to German Lutherans, the Bohemian Brethren—whose influence had grown significantly following the accession of several powerful nobles—strongly sympathized with German Protestants.

To navigate these tensions, Ferdinand employed a "tortuous policy" to secure the necessary forces. Because no armed force could be raised in Bohemia without the consent of the estates of the realm, he persuaded the estates to vote for a general levy. He did so under the "somewhat disingenuous pretext" that the Turks were menacing Bohemia.

Ferdinand designated the town of Kaaden, located on the Saxon frontier, as the meeting point for the troops. However, upon his arrival at Kaaden, he discovered that many nobles and cities had failed to send men, particularly those associated with the community of the Bohemian Brethren. Furthermore, many of the soldiers who did arrive were Protestants who sympathized with their co-religionists in Germany. Consequently, the Bohemian army refused to cross the Saxon frontier, and Ferdinand was forced to disband the forces toward the end of 1546.

## Subsequent Levies and Political Unrest
Early in the following year, Ferdinand again called upon his Bohemian subjects to provide an army to aid his brother. This second attempt saw limited success; only a few retrograde Utraquists and Romanists obeyed the order. 

The majority of Bohemians viewed this period as an opportunity to recover the ancient liberties of Bohemia. They believed Ferdinand had encroached upon these liberties by curtailing the old privileges of the land and claiming a hereditary right to the crown. In March 1547, without waiting for a royal summons, the estates met at Prague. This assembly was considered an unconstitutional proceeding and was characterized by a revolutionary nature, driven largely by the influence of the knights and nobles of the Bohemian Brotherhood and representatives from the town of Prague. This revolutionary sentiment intensified following news that the elector of Saxony had achieved victory at Rochlitz.
