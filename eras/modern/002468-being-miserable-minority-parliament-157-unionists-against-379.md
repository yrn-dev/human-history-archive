# Unionist Minority in Parliament

During a period of significant political instability, the Unionist party found itself in a "miserable minority" within parliament, consisting of 157 members. They faced a substantial opposition composed of 379 Liberals, 83 Nationalists, and 51 Labour members.

## Consolidation and the Fiscal Agreement
Because of their diminished numbers, immediate consolidation among the Unionists was required. This led to negotiations between Mr. Balfour and Mr. Chamberlain, which resulted in an agreement reached through correspondence dated February 14th. This compact was later confirmed during a party meeting at Lansdowne House.

In a letter outlining the new agreement, Mr. Balfour identified "fiscal reform" as the primary constructive work of the Unionist party. The stated goals of this reform were to achieve closer commercial union with the colonies and to secure more equal terms of competition for British trade. While Balfour noted that it was inexpedient to let differences over specific methods divide the party, he stated that the imposition of a small duty on foreign corn and the establishment of a moderate general tariff on manufactured goods were not "in principle objectionable." He specified that such a tariff should not be used to give artificial protection against legitimate competition or to raise prices, but should be adopted if necessary for revenue or to attain the party's ends. Following this, Mr. Balfour's leadership of the party was confirmed, and he secured a seat in the City of London following the retirement of Mr. Gibbs.

## Internal Party Struggles (1903)
Despite the agreement, internal conflict persisted throughout 1903. Mr. Chamberlain campaigned across the country for a definite budget reorganization that included more taxes on imports, including food, arguing that taxation could be adjusted to stimulate employment and improve the position of the working classes.

In opposition to Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Reform league, a "Free Food league" was established by free-trade Unionists, led by Lord James, Lord Hugh Cecil, Lord Goschen, and the duke of Devonshire. At a meeting at Queen's Hall in London on November 24th, this group rejected food taxes, discredited Chamberlain's statistics, and repudiated Balfour's assumption that retaliation would be desirable.

By December, Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Commission was appointed. Both the free-trade and tariff-reform factions fought to capture the party and the prime minister. Mr. Balfour utilized "dialectical dexterity" to avoid expressing definitive fiscal opinions, a trait that drew contemptuous criticism from the Liberal press. While Balfour declined to go the "whole hog" with Chamberlain, he did not discourage Chamberlain's campaign.

## Administration and Eventual Collapse
Despite these internal divisions, the government held together through 1903 and 1904. Minor ministerial changes occurred, with Mr. Arnold-Forster becoming minister for war and Mr. Brodrick moving to the India office. In 1904, the government successfully carried the Licensing Bill.

The Unionist regime eventually collapsed due to several factors. Ministers were viewed as "stale," and income-tax payers were disgusted by the retention of heavy taxation from the war years. Additionally, the government faced challenges regarding labour questions, the Education Act, and the 1904 introduction of Chinese labour into South Africa. 

While the administration was wrecked by dissensions over the fiscal question, Mr. Balfour's personal tenure in foreign affairs was considered conspicuously successful, leaving the country in a position of strength abroad not seen since the Crimean War. His significant reforms included the creation of the new Army Council (1904) and the permanent Committee of Imperial Defence, based on a November 1903 report by a "triumvirate" consisting of Sir George Clarke, Sir John Fisher, and Lord Esher.

## Period of Opposition
The resulting electoral defeat weakened Mr. Balfour's public confidence. However, the archive notes that within a year of being in opposition, Balfour's power in the House of Commons was realized once again, despite the small size of the party behind him. Although the Radical majority initially viewed the leader who had been rejected at Manchester with contempt, this perception shifted by 1907.
