# A Letter from Mr Ashburnham to a Friend

*A Letter from Mr Ashburnham to a Friend* is a published work from 1648 written to defend the conduct of John Ashburnham regarding the king. It serves as one of several documents, including *A Letter to W. Lenthall* (1647), which sought to address charges against the king, specifically repudiating the claim that the king had violated his parole.

## Subject of the Defense: John Ashburnham
The work defends John Ashburnham (c. 1603–1671), an English Royalist and the son of Sir John Ashburnham of Ashburnham in Sussex. Ashburnham entered the king's service early in his career. His diplomatic and political activities included being sent to Paris in 1627 by the duke of Buckingham to make peace overtures and preparing to join an expedition to Rochelle in 1628. 

Ashburnham held several significant positions, including groom of the bedchamber and member of parliament for Hastings, a borough he also represented during the Long Parliament of 1640. In his parliamentary role, he provided services to the king by reporting on proceedings. He eventually became one of the king's chief advisers and earned his full confidence.

## Role in the Civil War and Conflict
During the Civil War, Ashburnham served as the treasurer of the royal army. In this capacity, he caused remonstrances and jealousy from Hyde, as Ashburnham infringed upon Hyde's province as chancellor of the exchequer. In 1644, he served as a commissioner at Uxbridge.

Ashburnham's involvement with the king continued through several critical periods:
* **Flight and Exile:** He accompanied Charles in April 1646 during his flight from Oxford to the Scots. Ashburnham subsequently escaped abroad, joining the queen in Paris, residing at Rouen, and traveling to the Hague to seek aid from the prince of Orange.
* **Hampton Court:** In 1647, after the army seized Charles, Ashburnham joined him at Hampton Court and participated in conferences with other army officers and Cromwell.
* **The Isle of Wight:** On November 11, when Charles escaped from Hampton Court, Ashburnham advised against Sir John Berkeley's suggestion that the king go abroad. Instead, Ashburnham placed the king in the hands of Robert Hammond, the governor of the Isle of Wight.

## Charges of Treachery
The decision to place the king with Robert Hammond led to a crisis. Upon understanding the situation, the king exclaimed, "Oh, Jack, thou hast undone me!" Ashburnham responded with a "great passion of weeping" and offered to kill Hammond.

This sequence of events resulted in Ashburnham facing charges of disloyalty and treachery. While Clarendon censured his conduct, he absolved Ashburnham of any crime other than excessive self-confidence and folly. Ashburnham was ultimately acquitted by both Charles I and Charles II.

## Related Publications and Legacy
Beyond the 1648 letter, other works concerning Ashburnham's life were published later. A longer *Narrative* was published in 1830 by George, the 3rd earl of Ashburnham; however, this championship of the ancestor is described as unconvincing and entirely uncritical.

The Ashburnham family continued in prominence through various titles. John Ashburnham (1656–1710) was created Baron Ashburnham in 1689. His son, also named John (1687–1737), became the 3rd Baron Ashburnham in 1710 and was later created earl of Ashburnham and Viscount St Asaph in 1730. The 4th earl, Bertram (1797–1878), was the collector of the famous Ashburnham library, which was dispersed in 1883 and 1884. This library contained significant works, including a map from 970 (the oldest of its kind) and the Ashburnham Genesis, a 7th-century manuscript of likely Italian origin that serves as a link between classical drawing and medieval art.
