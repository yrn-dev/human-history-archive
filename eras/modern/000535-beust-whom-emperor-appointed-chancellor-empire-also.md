# Beust

Beust served as a high-ranking statesman in the Austrian Empire, holding the dual roles of chancellor of the empire and minister-president of Austria. Appointed by the emperor, Beust was entrusted with negotiations on behalf of the court of Vienna during a period of significant constitutional and financial restructuring.

## Domestic Administration and the System of Dualism
In the spring of 1867, the main principles of a new agreement were decided, though the Austrians were not truly consulted during that phase. Beust, who lacked previous experience in Austrian affairs, was appointed to lead the negotiations. His primary objective was to secure a settlement that would allow the empire to establish a strong position within international politics.

Following the meeting of the Austrian Reichsrath in the summer of 1867, two parliaments each elected fifteen-member deputations to resolve financial settlements. A central issue was the debt, which exceeded 3,000 million gulden, plus a floating debt. The Hungarians argued they were not responsible for debts incurred while they were deprived of constitutional liberties, though they agreed to pay 29.5 million gulden annually toward the interest. The Austrians assumed responsibility for the management of the debt and the remaining annual interest, which exceeded one hundred million gulden. 

To address these financial needs, it was agreed that the two states would form a Customs Union for ten years, with customs paid to a common exchequer. Additional expenses were divided, with Hungary providing 30% and Austria providing 70%. These arrangements were enacted by the two parliaments on December 21 and 24, 1867, establishing the system of dualism.

## Foreign Policy and International Relations
During Beust's four-year tenure as chancellor, foreign policy remained influenced by the aftermath of the war of 1866. Beust's own history suggested an inclination toward recovering the position lost in Germany and avenging Koeniggraetz. This period was marked by sharp disputes with the Prussian government over the status of the South German states.

Conversely, Beust maintained a close friendship with France. This relationship included meetings between the emperor and Napoleon in Paris (1869) and Salzburg (1868). While the death of Maximilian in Mexico cast a shadow over this bond, it did not destroy it. In 1870, discussions took place regarding a formal alliance with France against the North German Confederation, though no agreement was signed. A warlike policy was likely hindered by financial difficulties and opposition from the Hungarians.

The war of 1870 ended these ambitions. The rapid success of Germany meant Austria did not intervene. Following the absorption of South Germany into the German empire, the primary cause for friction vanished, leading to a friendship based on the maintenance of the established order. Austria abandoned hopes of regaining its position in Germany, and Germany disclaimed intentions to acquire Austria's German provinces.

## Final Years in Office and Dismissal
Beust's influence continued into 1871, though the political landscape shifted. In February 1871, the emperor appointed a ministry led by A. E. F. Schaeffle and Count Hohenwart, consisting of Clericals and Federalists. This ministry attempted to grant the Federalists their demands and sought to revise the constitution by recognizing the separate existence of the Bohemian kingdom and promising the emperor's coronation as king at Prague.

This move caused the Germans to secede in Moravia, Carinthia, and Upper Austria, threatening the union with Hungary and the 1867 constitution. Beust and Andrassy warned the emperor of these dangers, and Beust summoned the crown prince of Saxony to remonstrate with the emperor. On October 20, a great council was held in Vienna, where the emperor decided that Bohemian demands could not be accepted. Consequently, Hohenwart resigned, and Beust was dismissed.

Beust's retirement in 1871 finalized the transition to new international relations. He was succeeded by Count Andrassy, a Hungarian, who established a good understanding with Bismarck.
