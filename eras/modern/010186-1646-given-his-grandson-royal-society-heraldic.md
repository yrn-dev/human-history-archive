# History of Private Book Collecting and Public Libraries

## The Evolution of Book Collecting
During the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries, the practice of book-collecting typically existed as an added grace in the formation of fine libraries rather than as a separate pursuit. Most large-scale book buyers of this era operated with a public objective, and their zeal was often rewarded by their collections being deemed worthy of a public resting-place. This trend shifted the ownership of significant literary and scientific treasures from private individuals to public institutions.

## Academic and Institutional Bequests
Many prominent figures bequeathed their private libraries to universities and colleges. Sir Thomas Smith (d. 1577) left his books to Queens' College, Cambridge, while the library of Archbishop Williams was given to St John's College, Cambridge. Archbishop Parker left his collection to Corpus Christi College at the same university, though these were left under severe restrictions. 

At Oxford, Sir Thomas Bodley refounded the university library during his lifetime; this institution also received liberal contributions from Laud and a bequest from Selden. In Dublin, the library of Archbishop Usher was purchased for Trinity College, and Archbishop Marsh founded a library there after having bought the printed books of Stillingfleet.

## Specialized Collections and Scientific Contributions
Certain collections were curated for specific academic or professional purposes. The mathematical and scientific books of Thomas Howard, earl of Norfolk (d. 1646), were given to the Royal Society by his grandson. Ralph Sheldon (d. 1684) left his heraldic collections to Heralds' College. Other notable contributions include the library of Pepys, which went to Magdalene College, Cambridge. Additionally, George I purchased Bishop Moore's books—which included nearly unique Caxton quartos—and presented them to the university library at Cambridge.

## Transition to National Museums
The movement toward public ownership culminated in the foundation of major national institutions. While some collections were dispersed through auctions—such as the immense accumulations of Thomas Rawlinson (d. 1725) and the printed books of Harley, which were sold to the bookseller Osbourne—others were offered to the nation. Richard Rawlinson (d. 1755) bequeathed the best of his books to Oxford, and the Harleian MSS. were offered to the nation at a price far below their value.

The foundation of the British Museum in 1753 was driven by several factors: the offer of the great collections of Sir Hans Sloane (which included approximately 50,000 printed books) and the necessity for better care of the remaining Cotton manuscripts. The Cotton manuscripts had been vested in trustees for public use in 1702, though they were partially destroyed by fire in 1731. The British Museum opened in 1757.

## Related Antiquarian Pursuits
The drive to preserve history extended beyond books to museums and records. Elias Ashmole (1617-1692), an English antiquarian, founded the Ashmolean Museum at Oxford. He presented the museum—the first public museum of curiosities in the kingdom—to the university in 1677, having inherited much of it from John Tradescant. Ashmole required that a suitable building be erected for the collection, which was finally installed in 1683. He also made a subsequent gift of his library to the university.

Other antiquarians focused on the preservation of physical monuments. Sir William Dugdale (1605-1686) was commissioned in 1641 by Sir Christopher Hatton to create exact drafts of monuments in the principal churches of England, including those in York, Norwich, Lincoln, Ely, and Westminster Abbey, to prevent their ruin and spoliation during the war.
