# The Bombay Peninsula

The Bombay Peninsula, also referred to as the Bombay Island, is a landmass extending from a coast characterized by lofty hills. Its harbor is marked by precipices and rocky islands with peaks of great height. The approach from the sea is considered one of the finest panoramas in the world, with the Bay of Naples cited as the only European analogy.

## Geography and Urban Layout
The island consists of a plain approximately 11 miles long and 3 miles broad, flanked by two parallel lines of low hills. A neck of land stretching toward the south-west protects the eastern side of the harbor from the open sea, enclosing water between 5 and 7 miles wide. At the south-west of the island lies Back Bay, a shallow basin roughly 2 miles wide that runs inland for about 3 miles between the two hill ranges.

The nucleus of the city of Bombay is the fort, situated on a slightly raised strip of land between the harbor and the head of Back Bay. From the fort, the land slopes westward toward a low-lying central plain. Before the construction of the Hornby Vellard embankment, this central tract was submerged by the sea during high tide.

The city is characterized by spacious streets for European commerce and handsome native bazaars. In these bazaars, houses reach three or four storeys and feature elaborately carved front work and pillars. European commercial buildings and hotels are described as being on the American scale; the Taj Mahal hotel, built by the Tata family in 1904, is noted as the most modern and palatial hotel in India. European private residences are typically set in large gardens or compounds, separate from mercantile and native quarters. A favorite suburb is Malabar Hill, a high ridge running into the sea with terraced houses.

## Early History and Administration
The name Bombay is derived from Mumba, a form of Parvati, the goddess of the Kolis. The Kolis, a race of fishermen and husbandmen, were the earliest known inhabitants, occupying the island likely around the beginning of the Christian era. Originally, Bombay consisted of seven islands, known as the *Heptanesia* of Ptolemy. It was part of the dominions of several successive dynasties in western India, including the Rashtrakutas, Chalukyas, Mauryas, and Satavahanas. Between 450 and 750, during the Chalukya and Maurya periods, the principal location in the harbor was the city of Puri on Elephanta Island.

The first town built on the island was Mahikavati (Mahim), founded by King Bhima, likely of the house of the Yadavas of Deogiri, following Ala-ud-din Khilji's 1294 raid into the Deccan. Mahikavati remained under Hindu rule until 1348, when it was captured by a Mahommedan force from Gujarat. The islands remained part of the kingdom or province of Gujarat until 1534, when Sultan Bahadur ceded them to the Portuguese.

## English Acquisition and Development
The East India Company established its first settlement in the Bombay presidency at Surat in 1618 under a charter from Emperor Jahangir. After an unsuccessful attempt by the English and Dutch to gain the island in 1626, and subsequent purchase proposals in 1653, the island was ceded to the English crown in 1661 as part of the dower of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. Due to poor administration by crown officers, the island was transferred to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10.

The modern city's foundation is attributed to Gerald Aungier, who succeeded Sir George Oxenden as president of Surat in 1669. Aungier transferred his headquarters to Bombay in 1672. To counter threats from the Dutch, Mahrattas, and Malabar pirates, he fortified the shore and town and raised a force of regulars, primarily Germans. Aungier implemented reforms to attract settlers, including commuting the land tax to a fixed money payment, protecting the religious ceremonies of all castes, and forbidding the forced carrying of burdens by natives. Consequently, Parsees, Armenians, and the Hindu banya (capitalist) class moved to the city, and the population grew from 10,000 to 60,000 within eight years.

## Political Struggles and Expansion
The settlement faced periods of instability. Following Aungier's death in 1677, the region became a battleground between the navies of the Moguls (represented by Siddi admirals) and the Mahrattas. Internal strife also occurred, including a mutiny in 1674 and a more significant movement in 1683 led by Richard Keigwin. Keigwin, elected governor by popular vote, cited the "oppressions and exactions" of the Company and declared government under the crown. He reformed taxation and increased trade by admitting "interlopers" before surrendering the fort to Sir John Child upon royal order.

Bombay became the head of all Company possessions in India in 1687, though it became subordinate to Calcutta in 1753. The governor's seat moved from Surat to Bombay after the amalgamation of the old and new companies in 1708. The city wall was completed in 1718.

Military conflicts with the Mahrattas led to the treaty of Salbai in 1782, ceding Salsette to the British. The second Mahratta war ending in 1803 resulted in the acquisition of districts such as Kaira and Broach, the latter having been handed over to Sindhia.

## Economy and Demographics
Bombay is the chief seaport of western India and the capital of the Bombay Presidency, located at 18 deg. 55' N. and 72 deg. 54' E. It is the second most populous city in the Indian empire as of the 1901 census, with a population of 776,006 (estimated at 977,822 in 1906). The city's growth was driven by its position nearest to Europe, its role as a railway center, and its monopoly of the cotton industry—a trade that expanded significantly with China starting in 1770.

Despite its commercial prosperity, the region lacks the fertility of the Ganges valley and a comparable great waterway. The city also struggled with health crises, including cholera and plague, the latter of which severely affected the nearby Kolaba district.
