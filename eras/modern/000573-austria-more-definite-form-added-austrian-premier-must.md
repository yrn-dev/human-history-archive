# Austria

## Early Diplomatic and Territorial Shifts
Following the conquests of Napoleon, Vienna served as the site of the international congress in September 1814 to re-establish the European balance of power. Through the diplomacy of Metternich, Austria regained Dalmatia and Illyria, and added Lombardy to Venetia to create a kingdom under the Habsburg crown. Austrian influence replaced French influence across the Italian peninsula. In Germany, Austria presided over a loose confederation of sovereign states (_Staatenbund_) rather than restoring the Holy Empire, a move Metternich believed would guarantee the peace of central Europe and maintain Austrian preponderance. However, by relinquishing claims to other outlying territories in western Germany and accepting Prussian establishment in the Rhine provinces, Austria abdicated its role as the bulwark of Germany against France.

In the late 18th century, Austria's involvement in Belgium saw the restoration of Austrian rule in the "Austrian Netherlands" via the Treaty of the Hague (1790), following the collapse of the "République des États Belgiques unis." This restoration was supported by England, Holland, and Prussia to prevent Austrian extension toward Constantinople and maintain the balance of power. This rule was eventually ended by the French Revolution.

## Internal Governance and Political Crisis (1904–1906)
By the end of 1904, the system of government by paragraph 14, perfected by Dr. von Koerber, proved ineffective for long-term needs, particularly regarding the contraction of lasting burdens on the exchequer or the sale of state patrimony. Due to the premier's unpopularity with the Czechs, Baron von Gautsch was appointed premier on January 1, 1905.

During this period, Austrian politics were heavily influenced by crises in Hungary. Public opinion in Austria grew resentful of the belief that the crown could use imperial ordinances under paragraph 14 to force acquiescence to concessions made to Hungary. In August 1905, the crown considered the introduction of universal suffrage into the official programme of the Fejervary cabinet in Hungary, a move the monarch likely intended to mirror in Austria.

## Franchise Reform and Social Unrest
The movement for universal suffrage gained rapid ground in Austria, spurred by a huge socialist and working-class demonstration in Budapest on September 15, 1905. The Austrian Socialist party subsequently pressed for similar reforms, leading to demonstrations and collisions with police in Vienna, Graz, and Prague. 

On November 28, 1905, Premier Baron Gautsch permitted a massive, orderly Socialist demonstration in the Vienna Ringstrasse. He subsequently promised franchise reform by February 1906. The resulting measures proposed the abolition of the electoral class system (_curia_) and the establishment of universal suffrage for every male citizen over 24 years of age with a one-year residential qualification. To protect racial minorities from being overwhelmed by majorities, the Gautsch redistribution bill proposed dividing Austria into racial compartments, increasing constituencies from 425 to 455, and allotting them based on population and tax-paying capacity.

## Relations with Hungary and the Szell-Koerber Compact
Tensions persisted regarding the economic and political relationship between Austria and Hungary. A pact concluded on April 8, 1906, between the crown and the Coalition required the suspension of military questions until universal suffrage was introduced and the ratification of commercial treaties concluded by Tisza. 

While the Coalition government was obliged to carry on a dualist policy, many of its members belonged to the Independence or Kossuth party, which sought political and economic separation from Austria. In May 1906, the Coalition cabinet was authorized to present the Szell-Koerber tariff as a Hungarian autonomous tariff. Because this concession was made without the knowledge of the Austrian government, Austrian premier Prince Konrad Hohenlohe resigned. His successor, Baron Beck, withdrew the Szell-Koerber compact on July 6, stating that the remaining economic ties were limited to the joint state bank, management of excise, commercial treaties with foreign countries, and freedom of trade. Baron Beck asserted that if Hungary wished to regulate its relationship with Austria in a more definite form, a new agreement must be concluded before the end of 1907.

## Foreign Policy and the First World War
In the years leading up to the Great War, Austria-Hungary maintained a peaceful foreign policy and a close connection with Germany, though national enmities at home occasionally threatened this. The government focused on maintaining influence in the Balkan states through commercial treaties with Bulgaria, Servia, and Rumania, as well as the building of railways and the opening of the Danube for navigation. The growth of Russian influence in Servia caused serious anxiety.

During the war, internal pressure caused by hunger and privations grew. In Austria, the situation was reported as worse than in Germany. A letter from the Austrian Emperor to Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma indicated a desperate need for peace, leading Germany to doubt the loyalty of its ally and suspect that Austria might seek a separate peace regardless of the sacrifices required.

## Post-War Settlement and the Republic of Austria
Following the war, Austria was recognized as an independent republic under the name "Republic of Austria." The peace terms imposed severe restrictions:
*   **Territory and Sovereignty:** Austria had to recognize the complete independence of Hungary, Czecho-Slovakia, and Jugoslavia, as well as all territories formerly part of Russia. Boundaries were fixed with Czecho-Slovakia (Bohemia and Moravia) and Jugoslavia (Marburg and Radkersburg).
*   **Military:** Austria was prohibited from having naval or air forces. It had to demobilize all aerial forces within two months, surrender all naval arms and material, and break up all warships under construction. The future use of submarines was prohibited.
*   **International Obligations:** Austria renounced all extra-European rights and accepted economic conditions and freedom of transit similar to those in the German treaty. It was required to recognize the frontiers of Poland, Rumania, Bulgaria, Greece, and Hungary.
*   **Legal and Social:** The treaty mandated that Austrian nationals of all races, languages, and religions be equal before the law.
