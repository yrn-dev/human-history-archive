# Peru and the Political Influence of Simón Bolívar

## Liberation from Spanish Rule
The process of liberating Peru from Spanish dominion was a central component of the broader War of Independence in South America. Following the expulsion of Spaniards from Colombia, Simón Bolívar sought to complete the liberation of the region, including Ecuador and Peru. After the republican victory at Pichincha in June 1822, Bolívar marched on Lima. The royalists evacuated the city upon his approach, and Bolívar entered the capital in triumph, where he was granted absolute power as dictator and authorized to utilize all national resources.

This period of control was interrupted by the intrigues of republican factions in Peru, which forced Bolívar to withdraw to Truxillo. This allowed the Spaniards, led by Canterac, to re-occupy Lima. However, by June 1824, the liberating army was reorganized. Bolívar defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin on August 6, 1824, and returned to Lima. Meanwhile, Sucre pursued the royalists into Upper Peru, achieving a decisive victory at Ayacucho. This victory dispersed the Spanish forces, leaving their remaining possessions limited to the castles of Callao, which were held by Rodil for over a year.

## The Formation of Bolivia
Upper Peru, previously known as the province of Charcas and part of the Spanish vice-royalty of Peru, underwent a period of prolonged conflict between the patriots of Buenos Aires and Spanish royalist forces from July 1809 to August 1825. This warfare included an invasion by General Balcarce in 1811, a total rout of patriot forces between Potosi and Oruro in 1815, and a subsequent revolt by Indians in the southern provinces of Peru and Creoles that was suppressed by royalists. Guerrilla warfare continued in the mountains of the Yungas province for several years.

In June 1825, Bolívar visited Upper Peru. The region had detached itself from the government of Buenos Aires to form a separate state named Bolivia in honor of Bolívar. The first congress of this new republic met in August 1825, declaring Bolívar the perpetual protector and requesting that he draft a constitution.

## Political Conflict and the Bolivian Code
Bolívar's efforts to administer the freed provinces were often met with slander and opposition. In December 1824, he convoked a constituent congress, which invested him with dictatorial power for another year due to the unsettled state of the country. On May 25, 1826, Bolívar presented a project for a constitution for Bolivia. 

The resulting Bolivian code contained a highly controversial provision: the executive authority was to be held by a president for life who would be without responsibility and possessed the power to nominate his own successor. This proposal caused alarm among republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires. In Peru, Bolívar faced accusations that he intended to unite Peru, Bolivia, and Colombia into a single state and establish himself as the perpetual dictator of that confederacy.

## Later Governance and Resignation
In September 1826, Bolívar left Lima, entrusting the government to a council led by Santa Cruz. He traveled to Bogota, arriving on November 14, where he assumed extraordinary powers authorized by the constitution for cases of rebellion. He subsequently moved to Venezuela to address disturbances, reaching Puerto Cabello on December 31 and issuing a general amnesty.

Despite being re-elected as president alongside Santander, Bolívar formally resigned the presidency in February 1827. He expressed a desire to retire to his patrimonial estate to refute accusations of ambition. However, Santander and a resolution of congress urged him to resume his functions, arguing that only his personal influence and authority could appease the country's agitations. Before returning to Bogota to take the oaths of office, Bolívar issued three decrees: one for a general amnesty, one convoking a national convention at Ocana, and one to establish constitutional order throughout Colombia. His return was further accelerated by events in the southern departments and Peru that threatened the foundation of his power.

## International Relations and Conflict
Peru's later history involved diplomatic and military tensions with Chile. Peru offered to act as a mediator in a conflict, but Chile rejected this, citing a secret treaty and a 1875 Peruvian law that created a monopoly of the Tarapaca nitrate beds, which prejudiced Chilean enterprise. When Peru refused to annul the secret treaty or guarantee neutrality, Chile declared war.

Chilean naval superiority allowed them to land troops to face allied forces at Arica. In the south, the Peruvian general Buendia was routed at Pisagua. General Daza, who was meant to cooperate with Buendia, instead led Bolivian troops in a disorderly retreat to Tacna. Following the fall of San Francisco and the evacuation of Iquique, severe fighting occurred before the province of Tarapaca surrendered to the Chileans by the end of 1879.
