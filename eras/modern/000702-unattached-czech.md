# Unattached Czech

In the context of parliamentary representation and political organization within the Austro-Hungarian sphere, the "Unattached Czech" refers to a specific, singular designation within the broader Czech political bloc.

## Parliamentary Representation
According to archival records of parliamentary parties, the Czech political presence was divided into several distinct groups. While the majority of Czech representatives were affiliated with organized parties—such as the Czech Agrarians (28), Young Czechs (18), Czech Clericals (17), Czech National Socialists (9), and Old Czechs (7)—there existed a very small minority of representatives who did not align with these primary factions. Specifically, the records list one individual as an "Unattached Czech." Other minor groupings included the "Realists," who held two seats.

## Political and Social Context in Bohemia and Moravia
The political environment for Czechs was characterized by a vehement contest with Germans in Bohemia and Moravia. During this period, the two races were nearly equal in number, though the last twenty years had seen a constant encroachment by the Czechs upon the Germans. This shift was facilitated by government ordinances:
* **1880 Ordinance:** Determined that business brought before government offices or law courts would be dealt with in the language in which it was introduced throughout Bohemia and Moravia, granting the Czech language a position within the government service.
* **1886 Order:** Mandated that business introduced in Czech be handled in that language within the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid frequent translations.

Educational expansions furthered this trend through the founding of Czech elementary and middle schools, the introduction of Czech classes in German schools, and the 1882 establishment of Czech classes at the university of Prague.

## Electoral Systems and Conflict
The allotment of constituencies was based on tax-paying capacity and racial division. In mixed districts, separate registers were established so that Czechs could only vote for Czech candidates and Germans for German candidates. This system ensured that while internal victories occurred—such as Czech Radicals defeating Young Czechs—there could be no victory of a Czech candidate over a German one.

Political tensions led to significant parliamentary obstruction. Czech representatives utilized "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction to block business, including one sitting that lasted 57 hours without interruption. These actions prompted German parties—including the Christian Socialists, German Populists, German Progressists, and Constitutional Landed Proprietors—to form a supreme committee of four members to protect German racial interests.

Attempts at compromise, such as those proposed by Dr. von Koerber in 1902 and 1903, failed. Koerber suggested dividing Bohemia into ten districts (five Czech, three German, and two mixed) and allocating 133 of 234 district tribunals to the Czechs. However, the Czechs demanded that the Czech and German languages be placed on an equal footing for all official purposes throughout Bohemia. Because this required recognizing Czech as a language of internal service, the Germans refused.

## The Czech Expedition and Democracy
Outside of the parliamentary sphere, the Czech people demonstrated a strong commitment to democracy, particularly within the Czech army during expeditions in Siberia. These forces were characterized by a lack of traditional singular leaders; instead, positions were held by trained men who owed their status to popular choice. 

The expedition was influenced by Professor Masaryk in America and supported by Kenneth Miller of the New York Y.M.C.A., who managed complicated travel arrangements for the trains. While traveling via the Trans-Siberian route, the Czechs engaged in study, athletics, and the distribution of President Wilson's speeches to Russian and Siberian peasants. Their success in Russia and the seizure of Vladivostok were attributed to their status as a Slavic, Russian-speaking people, which allowed them to gain the confidence of the local population and fight oppressive Russian elements without being misunderstood.
