# Modern British Barracks

Modern British barracks are designed according to a systematic evolution of standards intended to prioritize sound sanitary construction and convenience of plan over external ornamentation. The current authorized standard of accommodation is detailed in the *Barrack Synopsis*, which provides particulars on authorized military buildings, fittings, and the required areas and cubic contents of rooms. To ensure consistency and avoid the need for entirely new designs for every building, the government utilizes "Standard Plans" that incorporate the best points of previous designs, which are then modified to suit local conditions.

## Infantry Battalion Accommodation
In a modern barrack for an infantry battalion, unmarried soldiers are housed in barrack-rooms typically planned for twelve men. This specific number is intended to align with company organization and is preferred by soldiers over larger rooms, as it provides greater privacy while maintaining the comradeship essential to soldier training.

Each man is allotted 600 cubic feet of air. The rooms feature windows on both sides, with beds spaced so that no more than two beds are placed between any two windows and only one bed occupies a corner. Air circulation is maintained via inlet ventilators fixed high on the side walls and an extract shaft warmed by the chimney flue. The layout generally places the door at one end of the room and the fireplace at the opposite end. For personal storage, each soldier has a locker and shelf above his bed, with his rifle kept near the head of the bed.

## Sanitary and Dining Facilities
To maintain purity and freshness of air within the sleeping quarters, food is no longer served in the barrack-rooms. Instead, men assemble for meals in lofty, well-ventilated dining rooms warmed by hot water, located adjacent to the cook-house.

Sanitary facilities are organized for efficiency and hygiene:
* **Ablution-rooms:** Containing basins and foot-baths, these are located for convenient access from the barrack-room door.
* **Night facilities:** Water-closets and urinals for night use are disconnected from the main rooms by a lobby.
* **Day facilities:** Provided in separate external blocks.
* **Baths:** Usually grouped in a central bath-house adjacent to the cook-house with hot water laid on.

Additionally, for every two to four barrack-rooms, a small single room is provided for the sergeant in charge. This sergeant is responsible for a small store where soldiers may leave their kit and rifles when going on furlough.

## Historical Development and Sanitary Reform
Historically, the British army relied on fortifications or the "billeting" of soldiers among town populations. Billeting was eventually abandoned because it was burdensome to civilians, prejudicial to military efficiency, and subversive of discipline. The earliest recorded English barracks were for the foot guards in 1660, followed by the Royal Barracks at Dublin in 1700.

A significant shift occurred following a 1857 commission on the sanitary condition of barracks and hospitals. The commission found that while a 1845 order had directed 450 to 500 cubic feet of air per man, some soldiers actually had less than 250 cubic feet. This led to the establishment of the Army Sanitary Committee in 1862, which advises the secretary of state for war on sites, type plans, and sanitary fitments. The minimum scale of 600 cubic feet per man was established by the 1857 royal commission and became the basis for the first *Barrack Synopsis* in 1865.

These improvements in accommodation contributed to a marked decrease in mortality rates among the home army, which dropped from 17.5 per 1,000 men in 1857 to 3.42 per 1,000 in 1897. Construction was managed by the Corps of Royal Engineers until 1904, when a civil department was formed under a "director of barrack construction."

## Specialized Barracks
### Naval Barracks
Naval barracks, such as those at Devonport, Chatham, and Portsmouth, are designed to mimic "board-ship life." Barrack-rooms feature caulked floors resembling ships' decks and rows of hammocks that are stowed during the day, allowing the rooms to serve as mess-rooms. Sanitary and ablution arrangements are located on basement floors. While officers' messes have splendid public rooms, their quarters are smaller than those in military barracks. Married quarters for men are only provided in connection with coastguard stations.

### Indian Barracks
In India, barracks are built by Royal Engineer officers assisted by military foremen and native staff. The accommodation is more liberal than that provided for colonial stations. Rooms are lofty and airy, featuring clerestory windows and verandahs all around, with roofs usually consisting of double tiling. Each man is allowed 90 square feet of space in rooms 16 feet high. These barracks include adjoining day rooms for meals or sitting, separate blocks of married quarters, and schools for children. Recreation facilities are extensive, including cricket grounds, fives courts, plunge baths, and bowling and skittle alleys.
