# BONE

## Medical Conditions and Pathology
Bone diseases can be infective, characterized by micro-organisms that germinate in damaged bone and travel through the blood-stream into other tissues, potentially causing blood-poisoning. In cases of septic osteomyelitis, the disease typically begins in the delicate growing tissue located between the cartilaginous end and the main shaft of the bone (the diaphysis). Consequently, initial pain is often reported just above or below the wrist, elbow, ankle, or knee. If a surgeon operates promptly, the disease may be found limited to that specific spot. In infants, these germs may move into the neighboring joint, resulting in acute arthritis of infants.

Septic ostitis may be mistaken for rheumatism or erysipelas, though it can be distinguished by tenderness and central thickening. This condition may lead to sub-periosteal abscesses that strip the bone bare, potentially causing necrosis. In severe cases, the diaphysis may be found loose within a large abscess cavity, or multiple bones may be killed outright (multiple necrosis) due to devastating abscess and inflammatory engorgement. Treatment involves an incision down to the bone to scrape out the affected area and disinfect it with a solution of corrosive sublimate. Depending on the severity, the shaft of the bone may need to be opened for its length to clear pus and germs, or immediate amputation may be required to save the patient's life.

Additionally, chronic inflammation of tuberculous origin affects the spongy ends of long bones as well as the bones of the feet, hands, and vertebrae. In tuberculous ostitis, bacilli in the spongy tissue cause colorless corpuscles from the blood to collect and form small greyish-white heaps called tubercles. These can replace living tissue, producing a rarefying ostitis. These tubercles may eventually break down into tuberculous abscesses that escape to the surface. Because spontaneous opening of these wounds can lead to infection by pus-producing germs and rapid destruction, surgeons are advised to cut down upon the part, scrape out the foci, and use a strong antiseptic lotion. Other treatments include prescribing iron, cod-liver oil, plenty of fresh air, absolute rest on a splint, and consideration of tuberculin injections.

## Surgical Interventions and History
Treatment for a fracture of the patella (knee-cap) has seen various approaches. Some surgeons utilize a back-splint or plaster of Paris splints, which can result in a useful limb if the fibrous bond of union is protected from stretching. However, this can sometimes leave the patient with an enfeebled limb if fragments remain wide apart. In 1883, Lister demonstrated that the knee-joint could be opened with cleanliness and the fragments secured in close apposition using a stout wire suture, allowing the fragments to unite solidly by bone. While some surgeons feared the risk of septic micro-organisms invading the joint, others adopted compromises, such as Professor A.E.J. Barker's method of passing a strong silver wire vertically around the fragments with a large needle without opening the joint. Other methods involve passing a metal or silk suture circumferentially.

Historically, the practice of surgery was linked to other trades. In England, barbers and surgeons were united by 32 Henry VIII. c. 42, with barbers limited to drawing teeth and blood-letting, while surgeons were forbidden from shaving. These corporations were separated in 1745. Notable figures in surgical history include Charles Bell, who published *Observations on Injuries of the Spine and of the Thigh Bone* in 1824, and Baron Guillaume Dupuytren (1777-1835), a French anatomist and surgeon known for his skill and dexterity.

## Anthropological and Industrial Applications
In the study of the Later Palæolithic Age, skeletal remains are used to define physical types. Two distinct sorts of remains are known: the Grimaldi race and the Cro-Magnon race. The latter are termed Cro-Magnards because complete skeletons of this type were first discovered in the grotto of Cro-Magnon. However, much of the human traces from this period are either without bones or contain insufficient bones to define a physical type. Tools from the Reindeer Age utilized bone and reindeer horn, including bone needles, bone points (pierced for thong), and arrow straighteners.

Industrially, bones are processed to create animal charcoal. Degreased bones are heated in horizontal cast-iron retorts for twelve to thirteen hours, with about half of the gelatinous matter extracted and the residue carbonized. The resulting product consists of approximately 75% calcium phosphate and 10% carbon, used as a clarifying and filtering material. The process evolves vapors that are condensed into two layers: a lower ammoniacal liquor (used to produce ammonium sulphate for manure) and an upper tarry layer. The distillate from the tarry layer is "bone oil," consisting of pyridine derivatives and fatty amines, while the residue, "bone pitch," is used in the manufacture of black varnishes.
