# The Litoral Department and Chilean Control

The Litoral department, formerly a region of Bolivia, passed under Chilean control as a result of the war between the two nations from 1878 to 1881. This transition of territory had significant implications for the region's population, its infrastructure, and the subsequent diplomatic and economic relations between Bolivia, Chile, and Peru.

## Origins of the Conflict
The tensions leading to the loss of the Litoral department were rooted in territorial and economic disputes. Under a treaty from 1866, Bolivia recognized the 24th parallel as the boundary of Chilean territory and granted Chile full trading facilities and a half share of customs on the coast between the 23rd and 24th parallels. Additionally, Chile was permitted to export products from this district without hindrance or tax from Bolivia. In 1870, Bolivia further granted an Anglo-Chilean company the right to work nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel for a sum of $10,000.

These arrangements created discontent within Bolivia. Simultaneously, Peru was dissatisfied with the influence of Chilean capital in the rich Tarapaca district. On February 6, 1873, Bolivia and Peru entered into a secret agreement for mutual defense and the preservation of territorial integrity, specifically contemplating potential further encroachments by Chile.

## Escalation to War
Diplomatic relations deteriorated further under the presidency of General Hilarion Daza, who succeeded Dr. Tomas Frias in 1876. Daza demanded a tax of 10 cents per quintal on all nitrates exported from the country, threatening to seize nitrates held by exporters if the levy was not paid. In response, and to protect the property of Chilean subjects, the Chilean fleet blockaded the ports of Tocapilla, Cobija, and Antofagasta. On February 14, 1879, Colonel Sotomayor of Chile occupied Antofagasta. The Bolivian government declared war on March 1, 1879.

During the conflict, Chilean forces secured the Bolivian ports. Chile also declared war on Peru after Peru refused to annul its secret treaty with Bolivia or guarantee neutrality. Following the rout of Peruvian troops at Pisagua and the evacuation of Iquique, Chilean forces gained complete possession of the province of Tarapaca by the end of 1879.

## Impact on Infrastructure and Population
The war resulted in the transfer of the Litoral department's railways, totaling 282 miles in length, to Chilean control. By 1903, the total railway network in Bolivia was limited to 430 miles, consisting of the Guaqui to Alto de La Paz line (54 miles), a private branch to the Pulacayo mines (26 miles), and the Antofagasta and Oruro line (574 miles total, though only 350 miles were within Bolivian territory).

The population of the Litoral department was also recorded in Bolivian census data following the transfer. A rough census from 1900 listed the population of the Litoral as 49,820, while an official estimate in 1904 increased this figure to 59,784.

## Post-War Communications and Development
Following the loss of its coastal department, Bolivia sought new ways to connect its isolated region with the outside world. In 1903 and 1904, Bolivia signed treaties with Brazil and Chile to facilitate railway construction. Under the 1904 treaty (ratified in 1905), Chile agreed to build a railway from Arica to La Paz spanning 295 miles; the Bolivian section of this line was to become Bolivian property fifteen years after completion. Chile also agreed to pay interest (not exceeding 5%) on capital invested in certain interior railways if constructed within thirty years, with a yearly cap of £100,000 and an aggregate cap of £1,600,000.

By 1907, several railway lines were projected to improve internal and external access, including:
* From La Paz to the navigable waters of the Beni.
* From La Paz to Cochabamba.
* From Viacha to Oruro.
* From Uyuni to Potosi and Sucre.
* From Uyuni to Tupiza.
* From Arica to La Paz via Coro.

Additionally, Bolivia maintained communication with the outside world through telegraph lines via Peru, Chile, and Argentina. By 1904, there were 3,115 miles of telegraph lines in operation, some of which belonged to the Antofagasta and Oruro railway and were located partly within Chilean territory.
