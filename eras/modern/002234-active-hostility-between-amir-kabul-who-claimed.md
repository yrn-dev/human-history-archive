# Conflict and Sovereignty in the Afghan Frontier

The geopolitical landscape of Afghanistan and its frontiers has been historically defined by the struggle for sovereignty between the amirs of Kabul, local tribal chiefs, and the interests of neighboring powers, most notably Great Britain and Russia. These tensions frequently manifested in disputes over territorial control, the administration of strategic passes, and the legitimacy of ruling dynasties.

## The Amirs of Kabul and the British Empire
The relationship between the amirs of Kabul and the British government was often volatile, characterized by shifts between alliance and open warfare. In the early 19th century, Dost Mahommed Khan, founder of the Barakzai dynasty, sought to recover the fortress of Peshawar from Ranjit Singh, the Sikh ruler of the Punjab. Despite attempts to form an alliance with England in 1837, the British governor-general, Lord Auckland, demanded that Dost Mahommed place his foreign policy under British guidance. This led to British military action in 1839, the proclamation of Shah Shuja as amir, and the eventual capture of Dost Mahommed in 1840.

Later, in the 1870s, the advance of Russia in Central Asia pushed Shere Ali, the amir of Afghanistan, to seek safety as a vassal of the tsar. Lord Lytton, nominated governor-general of India in 1875, attempted to conciliate Shere Ali. However, the establishment of a Russian agent in Kabul and the denial of entrance to the mission of Sir Neville Chamberlain led to war in November 1878. Following the flight and death of Shere Ali, the Treaty of Gandamak (May 1879) recognized Yakub Khan, Shere Ali's son, as amir. Under this treaty, the British gained control of the Khyber and other passes, as well as the districts of Sibi, Pishin, and Kuram, and established a permanent British Resident at Kabul.

## Dynastic Struggles and Internal Conflict
The struggle for the Afghan throne continued through various claimants. Ayub Khan, another son of Shere Ali, took possession of Herat after his father's death in 1879. Ayub Khan launched several invasions to assert his claim to sovereignty, notably winning a pitched battle against British forces under General Burrows at Maiwand on July 27, 1880. However, he was later defeated by Sir Frederick Roberts and subsequently by Abdur Rahman, who eventually became the subsidized ally of the Indian government and the amir of Afghanistan.

## The Conflict with Umra Khan and the Bajour District
A significant instance of active hostility occurred between the amir of Kabul and Umra Khan, the chief of Jandol in the Bajour district. Bajour is a high-level district peopled by Pathan races of Afghan origin, primarily Tarkani Pathans. The amir of Kabul claimed sovereignty over the districts that Umra Khan attempted to appropriate for himself, including Bajour, Dir, and a large portion of the Kunar valley.

This specific hostility between the amir and Umra Khan resulted in two major political outcomes:
1. The demarcation agreement of 1893, which established the boundary of Afghanistan in Kunar.
2. The invasion of Chitral by Umra Khan in 1895 and the subsequent siege of the Chitral fort, as Umra Khan was not a party to the 1893 boundary settlement.

## Strategic Geography and the Khyber Pass
Control of the routes between Kabul and India has been a central point of contention. While the old road to India once crossed the divide between the Kunar valley and Bajour, the British adopted the Khyber Pass as the main route to Kabul. This pass was the site of numerous skirmishes during the first Afghan War and was again a flashpoint in 1878 when Sir Neville Chamberlain's mission was stopped at the fortress of Ali Masjid.

Following the Treaty of Gandamak, the Khyber tribes were placed under British control, and the pass was protected by subsidized Afridi jezailchis. However, in 1897, a wave of fanaticism led the Afridis to attack the pass, resulting in its temporary loss to the British and the subsequent Tirah Expedition of 1897.

## Regional Administration and Landmarks
The influence of the amirs extended to various provinces and historical sites. In the district of Kohistan, north of Kabul, officials of the amir discovered the remains of an ancient city believed to be one of those founded by Alexander. To the northwest, in Bamian, the amir utilized the chambers of ancient colossal idols—carved into cliffs and reaching heights of 173 and 120 feet—as store-houses for grain. In the north, the amirs of Kabul also exerted influence over Badakshan; for instance, in 1765, the ruler of Kabul invaded and ravaged the region.
