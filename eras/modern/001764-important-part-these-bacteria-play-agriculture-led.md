# Bacteria in Agriculture and Nature

Bacteria, specifically Schizomycetes, play a role of supreme importance in agriculture and the natural circulation of elements. While many are harmless and the vast majority are not pathogenic, they serve as indispensable aids in various natural operations and useful fermentations.

## Agricultural Applications and Nitrogen Fixation
The ability of certain bacteria to fix free nitrogen has led to significant agricultural developments. In Germany, a commercial product known as "nitragin," consisting of a pure culture of these bacteria, was introduced to be applied to seeds before sowing or sprayed over the soil. Although initially found to have an uncertain effect, later experiments in America and the use of a modified preparation in England, directed by Professor Bottomley, yielded successful results.

A striking characteristic of these bacteria is their apparent specialization to leguminous plants, such as vetch and beans, which allow the plants to provide their own nitrogenous manure. However, in 1907, Professor Bottomley announced to the British Association for the Advancement of Science that he had broken down this specialization. Through a suitable treatment, he caused bacteria from leguminous nodules to infect other plants, including roses, tomatoes, and cereals, with a marked effect on their growth. If these results are confirmed and commercialized, it could lead to larger crops produced at a decreased cost.

## The Nitrogen Cycle and Soil Management
The movement of nitrogen in the soil and rivers involves a complex series of bacterial actions. Some bacteria oxidize ammonia into nitrous acid, and subsequently into nitric acid. Conversely, other bacteria reverse these processes. Warington demonstrated that many species can reduce nitrates to nitrites, while researches by Giltay, Aberson, Dupetit, and Gayon showed that some bacteria can reduce these further, allowing ammonia or free nitrogen to escape.

This process explains a long-standing agricultural puzzle regarding the wastefulness of applying nitrates and manure together on land. Fresh manure contains an abundance of de-nitrifying bacteria. These organisms not only reduce nitrates to nitrites and set free nitrogen and ammonia, but they also undo the work of any nitrifying bacteria present, resulting in great loss.

## Circulation of Sulphur and Carbon
Bacteria are also essential to the circulation of sulphur and carbon in nature. Sulphur bacteria of the genera *Monas*, *Spirillum*, *Chromatium*, and *Thiothrix* operate in estuaries, sulphur springs, and marshes. In these environments, cellulose bacteria release marsh gas, which reduces sulphates (such as gypsum) to liberate $\text{SH}_2$. Sulphur bacteria thrive by oxidizing this $\text{SH}_2$ and storing sulphur in their protoplasm; if $\text{SH}_2$ becomes scarce, they oxidize the stored sulphur into sulphuric acid. Some anaerobic forms, such as *Spirillum desulphuricans*, attack and reduce sulphates, reversing the work of sulphur bacteria.

Regarding carbon, anaerobic bacteria in rivers, cloacae, and marshes decompose cellulose. They likely hydrolyze the substance first, splitting the products into carbon dioxide and marsh gas. This process explains the presence of sulphuretted hydrogen and marsh gas in bogs and the intestines of herbivorous animals.

## Classification by Nutrition
Fischer proposed a classification of bacteria based on their mode of nutrition rather than the traditional division of parasites and saprophytes:

*   **Prototrophic (Monotrophic):** These are primitive forms that require no organic food materials, as they can work up carbon or nitrogen from inorganic sources. This group includes iron-bacteria, sulphur-bacteria, nitrogen-fixing, and nitrifying bacteria.
*   **Metatrophic:** This group includes those ordinarily termed saprophytes. They require organic materials from dead remains. They may be *saprogenic* (bringing material to a putrefactive state), *saprophilous* (living best in putrefying materials), or *zymogenic* (producing metabolic products that may cause toxic effects). These can be *Polytrophic* (utilizing various organic materials) or monotrophic.
*   **Paratrophic:** These are obligate parasites that live and multiply within the tissues, juices, or blood of living organisms.

## Industrial and Daily Life Applications
Beyond field agriculture, bacterial fermentations are used in various industrial processes, including the preparation of tobacco, the soaking of skins for tanning, the preparation of indigo, and the production of ensilage and hay. 

In the dairy industry, pure cultures of specific bacilli are used in American and Danish dairies to produce butters with fine flavors or to prevent rancidity. Bacterial fermentation is also central to the ripening of various cheeses, including Edam cheese, and the Japanese production of cheeses from boiled Soja beans.
