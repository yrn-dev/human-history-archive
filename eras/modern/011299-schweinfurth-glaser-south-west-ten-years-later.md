# Schweinfurth and E. Glaser in the South-West

## Exploration of Arabia
In the late 19th century, research into the physiography of the oases and great deserts of Arabia expanded significantly. Following the work conducted by Wilfrid S. Blunt and Charles Doughty in northern Arabia between 1877 and 1878, further research was carried out in the south-west. Approximately ten years after the northern expeditions, G. Schweinfurth and E. Glaser conducted their labors in this south-western region, contributing additional light to the geographical understanding of the area.

## Related Regional Investigations
The period surrounding the work of Schweinfurth and Glaser saw several other adventurous journeys and investigations in the region. In 1884-1885, Colonel S.B. Miles traveled through Oman. Subsequently, Theodore Bent conducted investigations to explore ancient Semitic history, focusing on Hadramut from 1894 to 1895 and the Bahrein Islands in 1888.

## Other Works of G.A. Schweinfurth
Beyond the explorations in south-west Arabia, G.A. Schweinfurth is noted for his extensive research in Africa, specifically detailed in his work *The Heart of Africa* (published in 1873 and 1874). His studies included a two-year period living among the Bongo, a tribe of Nilotic negroes located in the south-west portion of the Bahr-el-Ghazal province in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. 

Through his observations of the Bongo, Schweinfurth recorded the following:
* **Demographics and History:** He stated that before slave-raiders arrived around 1850, the Bongo numbered at least 300,000. Their population was greatly reduced by these raiders and later by the dervishes.
* **Physical Characteristics:** He described them as a race of medium height and thick-set with black hair and a red-brown complexion. Schweinfurth noted that their heads were nearly round, asserting that no other African race known to him possessed a higher cephalic index. He also observed that women tended toward steatopygia in later life.
* **Culture and Dress:** He described the traditional dress of men as a loin-cloth and dozens of iron rings on the arms for armor, while women wore a girdle with a tuft of grass. He noted the use of nails or plugs in the lower lip, including disks several inches in diameter for women, as well as rings or straw in the septum and nostrils.
* **Lifestyle:** Schweinfurth observed that the Bongo focused on agriculture—cultivating durra, sesame, tobacco, and sorghum—rather than cattle-breeding. He recorded that they ate fungi, tubers, fruits, and almost every type of reptile, insect, beast, and bird, excluding only the dog.
