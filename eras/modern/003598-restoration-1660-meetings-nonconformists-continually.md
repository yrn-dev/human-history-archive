# Restoration (1660)

The Restoration of 1660 marked a period of significant political, social, and legal transition in England and other regions, characterized by the return of the monarchy under Charles II and the subsequent reorganization of state and social institutions.

## Religious Nonconformity and Persecution
Following the Restoration, the period of "breathing time" previously enjoyed by certain religious groups ended. Nonconformists, who held specific beliefs regarding baptism—viewing it as an ordinance of the New Testament to be dispensed only to disciples or those professing faith via dipping or plunging the whole body under water—faced increasing pressure. While these groups declared that they were bound to obey the civil magistracy as an ordinance of God, they maintained that they must obey God rather than men, continuing their Christian communion even amidst trials and afflictions.

Soon after 1660, the meetings of nonconformists were continually disturbed, and their preachers were subjected to fines or imprisonment. This era of persecution led to the ejection of many ministers in 1662. Later historical accounts, such as those by Edmund Calamy, documented the apologies of these ejected ministers, detailing the grounds of their nonconformity and their practices regarding occasional and stated communion with the Church of England. Some nonconformist leaders, such as Bunyan, experienced periods of incarceration; for instance, Bunyan is believed to have written *Pilgrim's Progress* during a short imprisonment in 1675.

## Political and Diplomatic Transitions
The Restoration saw the return of figures who had navigated the interregnum. Downing, an able diplomatist, made his peace with Charles II in April 1660, renouncing principles he had acquired in New England. As a reward, he was knighted in May 1660, confirmed in his tellership of the exchequer, and continued in his embassy in Holland. Downing also represented Morpeth in the convention parliament of April 1660. He strongly supported the Navigation Act of 1660 and later played a role in managing the treasury and the customs.

Other figures also found favor or restoration of status. Fuller, who had argued for a free and full parliament in 1660, was admitted as a Doctor of Divinity at Cambridge by royal letters on August 2, and later served as chaplain in extraordinary to Charles II.

## Legal and Social Changes
The Restoration brought about specific legal reforms, most notably the abolition of tenure by knight-service by law (12 Car. II. c. 24). This ended a long system of military tenure and abolished associated "vexatious exactions," such as fines on alienation and other pecuniary incidents.

Socially, the Restoration initiated changes that became more pronounced during the reign of Anne (1702-1714). This period saw the expansion of reading across all social classes and a development of nationality in letters, politics, and religion. Over time, the intense religious controversies and bickering between nonconformists and episcopalians began to diminish.

## Cultural Revival and Horse-Racing
The return of the monarchy provided a new impetus to horse-racing, which had languished during the civil wars. Races at Newmarket, which had been suspended, were restored and attended by the king. Charles II actively promoted the sport, rebuilding the house at Newmarket originally erected by James I, establishing races at Datchet, and becoming the first monarch to enter and run horses in his own name. To encourage emulation, royal plates were provided at principal courses and "royal mares" were imported from abroad. By 1666, the Round course was established, and racing was regulated systematically regarding weights, courses, and other conditions.

## International Effects: Norway
The revolution of 1660 also had beneficial effects for Norway. With the disappearance of the *Rigsraad*, Norway ceased to be a subject principality and was placed on constitutional equality with Denmark, although it remained united with it. While the Norwegian people had hoped for an administration independent of the Danish government—a hope that was not realized as Copenhagen remained the headquarters—the general condition of the country improved. In 1661, a land commission investigated financial and economical conditions, leading to the transformation of fiefs into counties and the removal of tax immunity for nobles. Additionally, in July 1662, Norwegian towns were granted special privileges, including a monopoly on the timber trade.
