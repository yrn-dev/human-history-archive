# Girondists

The Girondists were a political faction during the French Revolution characterized by their ardent republicanism, sentimental humanitarianism, and a vague deism. They operated within the National Convention and the Legislative Assembly, often finding themselves in direct conflict with the Montagnards and the Jacobin club.

## Political Ideology and Members
The Girondists advocated for republican values and were often opposed to the influence of the Parisian populace. Members such as François Nicolas Léonard Buzot, an advocate from Evreux, sought to protect the National Convention from the people of Paris by proposing the formation of a national guard drawn from the various departments. Although this proposal was carried, it was never put into force, leading to significant bitterness among Parisians toward Buzot and the Girondists.

Other figures associated with the faction included Louvet, a self-styled *philosophe* and editor of *La Sentinelle* and the *Journal des débats*. Louvet shared the Girondists' republican and humanitarian views and used his journalistic platforms to enlighten the public on perceived plots. Additionally, the Spanish author José Marchena Ruiz de Castro avowed his sympathies with the Girondists after escaping the Inquisition and arriving in Paris.

## Conflict with Robespierre and the Montagnards
The Girondists engaged in fierce political struggles with the Montagnards, specifically targeting figures such as Robespierre and Marat. Louvet launched conspicuous attacks on these individuals, labeling Robespierre a "royalist" and Marat "the principal agent of England," while characterizing the Montagnards as Orleanists in masquerade. Similarly, Charles Jean Marie Barbaroux, a successful advocate and deputy, posed as an opponent of the Mountain and accused Robespierre of aiming for a dictatorship on September 25, 1792.

These conflicts extended to the trial of Louis XVI. While many revolutionaries sought the king's death, some Girondists, including Louvet and Buzot, supported an "appeal to the people" or a postponement of the sentence. These positions served to further discredit the Girondists in the eyes of their opponents.

## Downfall and Suppression
The decline of the Girondists was accelerated by the actions of their enemies within the Convention. Bertrand Barere de Vieuzac, a member of the National Convention, proposed a decree of accusation against the Girondists on June 2, 1793. He later delivered a violent speech against them on July 15. Other figures, such as Jean Baptiste Carrier, took prominent roles in the overthrow of the Girondists on May 31.

Following their proscription on June 2, 1793, many Girondists fled. Buzot escaped to Normandy, where he helped organize a federalist insurrection against the Convention. This movement was suppressed, and Buzot eventually committed suicide in the woods of St Émilion on June 18, 1794. Charles Jean Marie Barbaroux also escaped, attempting to organize a civil war in Caen and later writing his *Memoires* in Saint-Emilion. However, he was discovered and guillotined in Bordeaux.

## Regional Influence and the Siege of Lyons
The Girondists maintained significant power in the city of Lyons. In 1793, Girondists and Royalists in the city rose against the Convention. This uprising led to a seven-week siege ending on October 10, when the city was forced to yield to the republican army under General Kellermann. The subsequent chastisement was severe: the city was renamed Ville-affranchie, buildings were demolished on a wholesale scale, and numerous proscribed individuals were killed with grape shot. The city only regained its original name after the fall of Robespierre.
