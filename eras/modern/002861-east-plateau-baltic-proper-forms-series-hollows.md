# The Baltic Sea

The Baltic Sea (known in German and Scandinavian as *Ostsee* and in Russian as *Baltiyskoe More*) is a body of water extending between 54° and 66° N latitude and 9° and 30° E longitude. It is surrounded by the territories of Denmark, Germany, Russia, and Sweden. The sea's central axis runs approximately from the south-west to the north-east, with a maximum length of about 960 m and a maximum breadth of about 400 m. The total length of its coastline is 5,000 m.

## Geographic Boundaries and Connections
The Baltic is connected to the North Sea via a winding channel located between the Cimbrian peninsula and the south of Scandinavia. This channel is generally considered part of the Baltic and is divided into two sections: the Skagerrak (west of a line joining Christiania fjord with the Skaw) and the Kattegat (east of that line). At its southern end, the Kattegat is blocked by Danish islands, communicating with the Baltic proper through narrow channels known as the Little Belt, the Great Belt, and the Sound.

The physical boundary between the North Sea and the Baltic proper is defined by a plateau. This plateau includes the islands of Laaland, Funen, and Zealand, and extends from the islands of Moen and Falster to the coasts of Ruegen and Mecklenburg.

## Bathymetry and Basin Structure
East of the aforementioned plateau, the Baltic proper is characterized by a series of troughs or hollows. The first is the Bornholm deep, located east of the island of Bornholm; it is separated from the Gotland deep by the Middelbank. To the south-east of the Middelbank lies an isolated depression called the Danziger Tiefe. To the north-east is the Gotland basin, the deepest and largest of all the hollows, which extends toward the Gulf of Finland.

Along the Swedish coast, a deep channel runs northward from outside the island of Oland. This channel is cut off to the east and south by a bank that sweeps northward and eastward from near Karlskrona—the bank upon which the island of Gotland stands. This channel connects with the Gotland deep at its northern end; the deepest point in the Baltic, measuring 420 metres (230 fathoms), is located near the junction opposite Landsort.

Further north, an unbroken ridge from Hango in Finland to Stockholm separates the Baltic basin proper from the Aland Haf, a depression between the Aland Isles and Sweden. North of the Aland Haf, another ridge defines the southern edge of the Bothnian Sea. This area is separated from the Gulf of Bothnia, the most northerly division, by a ridge across the Quarken (or Kvarken) Strait.

The Baltic also includes several arms and inlets:
* **The Gulf of Finland:** An arm running eastwards for about 250 m, separating Esthonia from Finland. The Gotland deep extends directly into this gulf.
* **The Gulf of Riga:** A shallow, roughly circular inlet between Courland and Esthonia, measuring about 100 m in diameter and reaching a maximum depth of 27 fathoms.

The total area of the Baltic, including the Kattegat and Skagerrak, is 166,397 sq. m with a volume of 6,907 cub. m. This results in a mean depth of 36 fathoms. The seabed in the southern deep hollows consists of hard clay or soft grey and brown mud, while shallow banks and low coasts typically feature small pebbles and fine sand of yellow, brown, or white colour.

## Water Circulation and Composition
The circulation of water in the Baltic proper is distinct from the circulation in the channels connecting it to the North Sea, with the plateau between Ruegen, Mecklenburg, Falster, and Moen serving as the dividing line. 

In the hollows and basins from Ruegen to the Gulfs of Finland and Bothnia, the upper layers of water (30 to 70 metres thick) maintain almost the same salinity throughout, with vertical circulation driven by convection currents. Beneath these layers lie stagnant masses of salter water that are surcharged with carbonic acid and deficient in oxygen. These salter waters originated from outside and passed over the plateau between Mecklenburg and Falster, though their horizontal extension is limited by ridges separating the deep hollows. Inflow to these deep basins is intermittent, characterized by long periods of reflux and flux.

## Geological History
The Baltic has undergone significant transformations during glacial times. During a period of great subsidence, an arm of the sea extended across Sweden, submerging much of the littoral up to the Gulf of Bothnia and including the present lakes Malar, Hjelmar, and Vener. During this era, the northern Baltic was salty enough for oysters.

A subsequent upheaval restricted communication with the open sea to the Danish channels, causing the waters to become fresher. While oysters disappeared, seals and various crustaceans and cold salt-water fishes became acclimatized. Later, further elevation transformed the Baltic into a vast freshwater lake known as the *Ancylus* lake (named for the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*), which covered approximately 220,000 sq. m, including Lake Ladoga, a large part of Finland, and the current Baltic area.

Another subsidence re-established communication through the Danish channels, allowing the sea to become salty enough for *Littorina littorea* and *Cardium edule*. During this epoch, the Gulf of Bothnia suffered greater depression than the Baltic proper, evidenced by deposits 100 metres thick near Hernosand compared to 25 metres near Gotland. The process of elevation that gave the Baltic its current form followed this subsidence and appears to still be in progress. Dr. R. Sieger's isobasic lines indicate that while movement is nearly nil along the Gulf of Finland and the axial lines of the Baltic, it increases in amplitude toward the main ridge of the southern Sweden *massif* and northwards toward the Gulf of Bothnia.
