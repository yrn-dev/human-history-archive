# The Baptist Churches

The Baptist movement is a global religious tradition characterized by its views on baptism, the independence of individual churches, and a historical commitment to religious liberty. While Anabaptist communities existed in England during the late 16th century—notably in London, Kent, and East Anglia—the rise of modern Baptist churches was wholly independent of them.

## Origins in England and Holland
The modern Baptist movement in England broke away from Brownism during the time of John Smyth (d. 1612). Smyth, an ordained clergyman and preacher in Lincoln in 1600, became a separatist by 1605 or 1606. He emigrated to Amsterdam with the Gainsborough Independents, where he joined the "Ancient" church alongside Morton and Thomas Helwys (ca. 1560–ca. 1616). In 1609, influenced by Mennonite teaching, Smyth separated from the Independents and baptized himself.

In 1611, this group formed the first English Baptist Church and published a declaration of faith. This document taught that baptism should be administered only upon a confession of faith and sins, asserting that baptism is an outward manifestation of walking in newness of life and dying unto sin; consequently, it does not pertain to infants. This confession also established that no church should claim prerogative over another and that the magistrate should not compel any form of religion or meddle with matters of conscience.

## General and Particular Baptists
The movement split into two distinct sets of Baptists with different origins and theological leanings. The "General" Baptists originated when Thomas Helwys returned to England in 1612 and established a church in Newgate Street, London. These Baptists were Arminians who rejected the Calvinistic doctrine of predestination.

The "Particular" Baptists were Calvinists who emerged from the Independents. The first Particular Baptist Church was formed in 1633 by a group dismissed from the Jacob church in Southwark. The Jacob church had originally been Independent before becoming Baptist; its members included William Kiffin, Hanserd Knollys, Henry Jessey, Praise-God Barbon, and John Lothropp. From the Jacob church, six other churches sprang, five of which were Baptist.

Until 1644, these two groups had little intercourse. While both rejected infant baptism, neither was initially committed to immersion. In 1644, the Particular Baptist churches of London published a Confession of Faith, though they were falsely called Anabaptists.

## Expansion in North America
Baptists were attracted to the Quaker colonies due to the presence of religious liberty. In 1684, Thomas Dungan, an Irish Baptist minister, helped found a church at Cold Spring in Bucks county, Pennsylvania. In 1688, Elias Keach (son of the evangelist Benjamin Keach) helped form the Pennepek church. By 1707, the Philadelphia Association was established to maintain sound doctrine, supply ministers, and consult on church needs. This body was influential in educational and missionary work. Seventh-day Baptist churches also formed within this association starting in 1705.

In the American South, the "Separate" Baptist movement, led by figures such as Stearns and Marshall, drove significant numerical growth. In South Carolina, membership grew from 1,620 in 1787 to 11,325 by 1812. In Virginia and the Carolinas, evangelists Samuel Harris and John Waller established churches between 1760 and 1770. For a brief period in 1774, the Virginia association appointed "apostles," including Harris, Waller, and Elijah Craig, though this practice was later abandoned.

The "Regular" Baptists of the Philadelphia type and the "Separate" Baptists eventually harmonized. The Separate Baptists adopted the Calvinistic Philadelphia Confession of Faith to preserve harmony and cooperated with Regular Baptists in struggles for religious equality and the separation of church and state. In the South, many negro slaves became Baptists; while most joined white churches, some were encouraged to establish their own churches in towns like Savannah and Richmond.

## Organizational Evolution and Global Alliance
In Great Britain and Ireland, the General and Particular denominations united in 1891 under the influence of Dr. John Clifford to form "The Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland." This union is voluntary, and some churches remain independent. Additionally, "Strict" Baptist churches exist as a separate denomination, divided into "Vessel" and "Standard" parties based on their respective organs, the *Earthen Vessel* and *Gospel Standard*.

On a global scale, the Baptist World Alliance was formed in 1905 during the first Baptist World Congress in London. Its constitution emphasizes the "essential oneness in the Lord Jesus Christ" among Baptist churches worldwide and promotes fellowship and co-operation while recognizing the independence of each particular church.
