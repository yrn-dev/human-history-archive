# Bombay

## Geography and Topography
The more level parts of Bombay are composed of five well-demarcated tracts: the Carnatic, the Deccan, the Konkan, Gujarat, and Sind. Sind, located in the lower valley of the Indus, is very flat with scanty vegetation and relies entirely on irrigation for productiveness. Gujarat consists primarily of rich alluvial plains watered by the Nerbudda and Tapti, though its northern parts differ. The Konkan is a rugged, difficult region of isolated peaks, detached hill ranges, and creeks, situated between the sea and the Western Ghats. The plains of Khandesh and the Deccan are watered by large rivers, but they remain bleak and devoid of vegetation for most of the year due to uncertain rainfall. The Carnatic plain, found south of the river Kistna, features extensive tracts of cotton or black soil in a high state of cultivation.

The Bombay Peninsula (or Island) is characterized by a harbour with rocky islands and precipices. The island itself consists of a plain approximately 3 miles broad and 11 miles long, flanked by two parallel lines of low hills. A neck of land extending south-west shelters the harbour on its eastern side, enclosing water 5 to 7 miles wide. Back Bay is a shallow basin roughly 2 miles wide that runs inland for 3 miles. The city's nucleus is a fort situated on a raised strip of land between the harbour and the head of Back Bay. Before the construction of the Hornby Vellard embankment, the central low-lying plain was submerged by the sea at high tide.

## History
The name of the city and island is derived from Mumba, a form of Parvati, the goddess of the Kolis. The Kolis, a race of fishermen and husbandmen, were the earliest known inhabitants, likely occupying the island around the beginning of the Christian era. Originally consisting of seven islands (referred to by Ptolemy as the Heptanesia), Bombay was part of the dominions of several successive dynasties in western India, including the Rashtrakutas, Chalukyas, Mauryas, and Satavahanas. Between 450 and 750, during the Chalukya and Maurya periods, the principal place in the harbour was the city of Puri on Elephanta Island.

The first town built on Bombay Island was Mahikavati (Mahim), founded by King Bhima, likely of the house of the Yadavas of Deogiri, following a 1294 raid into the Deccan by Ala-ud-din Khilji. Mahikavati remained under Hindu rule until 1348, when it was captured by a Mahommedan force from Gujarat. The islands remained part of the kingdom or province of Gujarat until 1534, when Sultan Bahadur ceded them to the Portuguese.

## Administration and Government
Bombay is one of the nine great provinces of the Indian empire and is presided over by a governor. The government is administered by a governor in council, which includes the governor as president and two ordinary members appointed by the crown from the Indian civil service. Legislative functions are handled by a legislative council consisting of the governor, his executive council, and between eight and twenty other persons, at least half of whom are non-officials.

Justice is administered by a high court at Bombay, comprising a chief justice and seven puisne judges, supported by assistant and district judges. District administration is managed by assistant collectors, collectors, and supernumerary assistants. In native states, political administration is supervised by British agents.

The city's municipal government, framed by an 1888 act of the Bombay legislative council, consists of a town council and a municipal corporation. The corporation has 72 members: 16 nominated by the government, 36 elected by ratepayers, 16 elected by justices of the peace, 2 by the university senate, and 2 by the chamber of commerce. The corporation includes Parsees, Mahommedans, Hindus, and Europeans. Additionally, a port trust nominated from the commercial community manages the docks, warehouses, and port.

## Education and Society
Established in 1857 as an examining body based on the model of the university of London, the university of Bombay is a corporate body consisting of fellows, a vice-chancellor, and a chancellor (the governor of Bombay *ex officio*). The state's education department is led by a director of public instruction. In 1905, there were 10,194 educational institutions with 593,431 pupils. Art colleges numbered ten, with five under private management, three by native states, and two by the government. However, a 1901 census indicated that nearly 24 million of the 25-1/2 million population were illiterate.

Key institutions in Bombay City include the government law school, the Grant medical college, the government Elphinstone College, the Victoria Jubilee technical institute, the Sir Jamsetjee Jeejeebhoy school of art, and two missionary colleges, St Xavier and Wilson.

## Military and Urban Infrastructure
Following Lord Kitchener's 1904 re-arrangement, the old Bombay command was replaced by the Western army corps under a lieutenant-general, divided into three divisions. The 4th division (headquarters at Quetta) covers the Sind and Quetta districts. The 5th division (headquarters at Mhow) includes the Bombay district north of the Tapti and the districts of Nerbudda, Nagpur, Deesa, Mhow, and Bundelkhand. The 6th division (headquarters at Poona) includes the Bombay district south of the Tapti, the Poona district, the Aurungabad and Dharwar districts, and the Belgaum district north of the Tungabhadra. Belgaum, a town in the southern division of Bombay, serves as a trade center for cotton weaving and houses a brigade headquarters for the 6th division.

The city of Bombay features spacious streets for European commerce and handsome native bazaars with houses three or four storeys high and carved pillars. European residences are typically set in large gardens or compounds, with Malabar hill serving as a favorite suburb. Notable architecture includes the Taj Mahal hotel, built by the Tata family in 1904.
