# Prusse et l'Autriche, Paris, 1870

The title *La Prusse et l'Autriche*, published in Paris in 1870 by Laveleye, is a work cited in historical records regarding the concordat and the legal position of the Church in Austria.

## Church and State in Austria
In 1870, the proclamation of papal infallibility led the Austrian government to declare that the concordat had lapsed. This decision was based on the assertion that there had been a fundamental change in the character of the papacy. This shift was mirrored among the Austrian prelates, nearly all of whom opposed the new doctrine. Many of these prelates remained at the council and voted against the proclamation, eventually declaring their submission only with great reluctance. Following these events, laws regulating the position of the Church were carried in 1874. Despite these tensions, the Old Catholic movement did not achieve significant progress within Austria.

## Diplomatic Relations: Prussia, Austria, and France
The period surrounding 1870 was marked by intense diplomatic activity involving Prussia, Austria, and France. In 1866, Napoleon attempted to intervene between Prussia and Austria. During the subsequent advance on Vienna, the French diplomat Benedetti accompanied the Prussian headquarters and assisted in arranging the preliminaries of the armistice signed at Nikolsburg.

Following this, Benedetti was tasked with presenting French demands for "compensation" to Bismarck. In August of that year, discussions between the two led to a draft treaty in which Prussia promised to support France in the annexation of Belgium. Although this treaty was never concluded, Bismarck retained the draft—written in Benedetti's own hand—and published it in *The Times* in 1870, shortly after the outbreak of war.

## The Outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War
In July 1870, the candidacy of the prince of Hohenzollern for the Spanish throne triggered a diplomatic crisis. The duc de Gramont instructed Benedetti to present demands to the king of Prussia at Ems. These demands required that the king order the prince to withdraw his candidacy and provide a promise that the candidacy would never be renewed.

The final demand was delivered during an informal meeting on the promenade at Ems. Misleading reports of this conversation circulated, leading Germans to believe Benedetti had insulted the king, while the French believed the king had insulted the ambassador. These reports served as the immediate cause of the ensuing war. In the aftermath, Benedetti faced severe criticism in France, with the duc de Gramont attempting to hold him responsible for the failures of French diplomacy. Benedetti later defended his conduct in his 1871 book, *Ma Mission en Prusse*.
