# Chinese Frontier

The frontiers of China encompass a diverse range of geographical boundaries, extending from the mountainous regions of the south and west to the high deserts and plateaus of Central Asia. These boundaries are defined by a combination of natural watersheds, mountain ranges, and high-altitude basins.

## Southern and Eastern Boundaries in Burma
On the eastern boundaries of Burma, the frontier with China is not yet fully demarcated. At a latitude level with Mogaung, situated near the northern termination of the Burmese railway system, the boundary is defined by the eastern watershed of the Nmaikha. The Nmaikha is the eastern of two great northern affluents of the Irrawaddy. From this point, the boundary follows an irregular course southwards until it reaches a position south-east of Bhamo at latitude 24 degrees. It then defines the northern edge of the Shan States before striking the Mekong river at approximately latitude 21 degrees 45'.

## Central Asian and Northern Frontiers
The frontiers of China in Central Asia are characterized by extreme elevations and desert landscapes. The great desert of Central Asia, known as the Gobi, is bounded on the north and north-west by the Tian-shan and the Yablonoi Mountains (and their western extension), and on the east by the Khingan range. The southern boundary of this region is the northern face of the Tibetan plateau. The western portion of the Gobi forms Chinese or eastern Turkestan, while the eastern part is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia.

Elevation in this region is consistently high; no part of the central Asiatic desert is believed to be less than 2,000 ft. above sea level. Specifically, the route across the desert between the Chinese frontier and Kiakhta never drops below 3,000 ft. Other regional elevations include:
*   The plain around Yarkand and Kashgar: 4,000 to 6,000 ft.
*   Northern parts of Mongolia: 4,000 to 6,000 ft.
*   Various mountain ridges: Many points exceed 10,000 or 12,000 ft.

## Western Frontiers and the Tibetan Plateau
The boundaries to the south and west are well-fixed via Indian survey determinations of Himalayan peaks. In the west, explorations of Chinese Turkestan have been connected via survey to India and Kashmir. 

The Kuen-lun mountains, which extend from the frontiers of Kashmir (north of Leh) almost due east to the Chinese province of Kansu, serve as the northern limit of the Chang Tang, the elevated highland steppes of Tibet. From a point at approximately 83 degrees E. longitude, a minor system branches north-east, culminating in the Altyn Tagh and extending as a continuous water-divide to the Nan Shan mountains. This system creates a region of elevated valleys known as Tsaidam, situated 8,000 to 9,000 ft. above sea level, which acts as an intermediate steppe between the highest regions and the low-lying sands of the Gobi east of Lop Nor. This geography explains why the great trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed far to the north.

## Regional Features: Koko-Nor
Located in the extreme north-east of Tibet is Koko-Nor (also known as Kuku-Nor, *Tsing-hai* in Chinese, and *Tso-ngombo* in Tangut). This salt lake is situated at an altitude of 9,975 ft., approximately 30 miles from the western frontier of the Chinese province of Kan-suh (100° E. and 37° N.). It is positioned between the Nan-shan Mountains to the north and the southern Koko-nor range (10,000 ft.) to the south, among the eastern ranges of the Kuen-lun. The lake measures 66 miles by 40 miles and contains several islands, including one with a Lamaist (Buddhist) monastery. The lake is entered from the west by the river Buhain-gol and is surrounded by Tangut nomads.
