# Limits of Investigation, Application to Practice, and Preparations

## Inductive Investigation and the Baconian Method
In the inductive investigation of the form of any nature, the process follows the definition of the form itself. Because the form is necessarily present when the nature is present, absent when it is absent, and fluctuates in accordance with the nature's increase or decrease, specific tables are required for inquiry. These include the table of *Essence and Presence* (instances where the nature is present), the table of *Absence in Proximity* (instances where the nature is absent, limited to those most akin to instances of presence), and the table of *Degrees* or *Comparison* (instances where the nature is present in varying degrees).

The most significant contribution of the Baconian method is the process of exclusion or rejection, which eliminates the non-essential. While this process would be a mechanical "counting out" leading infallibly to the detection of the cause or form if the tables were complete and notions clear, these conditions are never adequately fulfilled. Recognizing that the method was impracticable, Bacon devised *adminicula* (helps), which include:
* Limits of Investigation
* Application to Practice
* Preparations
* Prerogative Instances
* Supports of Induction
* Rectification of Induction
* Prerogative Natures
* Varying the Investigation according to the Nature of the Subject

## Legal and Federal Investigations
In the context of federal investigations, limits may be defined by principles of federalism. In the case of the investigation into Epstein, Acosta viewed the federal role as limited by the need to recognize the independent authority of the state, positioning federal responsibility as a "backstop" to encourage state authorities. However, the Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR) concluded that this application of federalism was too expansive and that the resulting decision to resolve the investigation through a Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA) constituted poor judgment. The OPR noted that Acosta resolved the federal investigation before significant investigative steps were completed and failed to consider the difficulties of relying on state officials over whom he had no authority.

Additionally, investigations and the subsequent production of materials may be limited by protective orders, grand jury secrecy laws, and Department practice. For example, the Department produced a Review Protocol to determine if materials were responsive to the Act and to identify information requiring withholding or redaction. Redactions were mandated for:

## Application to Practical Sciences
In the field of lubrication, verified theory is used for guidance to determine limiting conditions and for use outside the limits of experience. Determining the limits of lubrication requires the application of the theory of the flow of heat in specific circumstances, as well as knowledge of the coefficients of conduction of heat in the metal and lubricant, and the empirical law regarding how the lubricant's viscosity varies with temperature. This is necessary because the coefficient of viscosity diminishes as temperature increases, and lubrication would be impossible without the escape of heat by conduction through the metal and lubricant. Furthermore, the theory of lubrication has been applied to explain the slipperiness of ice.

In astronomy, a distinction is made between general astronomy (knowledge of the universe) and practical astronomy. Practical astronomy specifically comprises the principles and methods underlying the application of instruments used in astronomical observation.

## Preparations and Procedural Standards
Preparations for legal and administrative compliance often involve the establishment of protocols and the review of standards. For federal prosecutors, the United States Attorneys' Manual (USAM) provides general principles and desirable practices to ensure that prosecutorial decisions are made objectively and rationally on the merits of each case, promoting the fair administration of Federal criminal laws.

In other administrative contexts, preparations for transparency and oversight have included the passage of legislation, such as the Building Societies Act of 1894. This act was prepared following the Liberator disaster to avoid future calamities. It required incorporated societies to furnish returns in a prescribed form, including schedules of mortgages in arrear for more than twelve months, properties in possession for more than twelve months due to mortgagor default, and mortgages exceeding £5000.
