# Japan and the Possession of Port Arthur (1904)

The struggle for Port Arthur was a central element of the conflict between Japan and Russia in 1904, rooted in strategic competition over Manchuria and Korea. The possession of this naval base was viewed by Japan as essential to its security and regional interests, particularly regarding the independence of Korea.

## Strategic Importance and Background
Port Arthur is located on the Liaotung peninsula. In 1894, the port was taken from the Chinese, but the Japanese were later forced to quit the location at the instance of Germany. The Russians took possession of Port Arthur in 1899. For Japan, the Russian occupation of Manchuria—an area larger than the United Kingdom and France combined—presented a significant threat. Japan believed that if Russia possessed all of Manchuria, the subsequent absorption of Korea would be almost inevitable. 

Strategically, the Manchurian coast offered few convenient naval bases other than Talien and Port Arthur. Japan viewed the independence of Korea as an object of supreme solicitude; because Russia's East Asian extension would be strategically defective and economically incomplete without Korea, Japan watched Russian movements with profound anxiety.

## Naval Forces and Initial Operations
On the eve of hostilities in 1904, the Russian Pacific squadron, the bulk of which lay at Port Arthur, consisted of twenty-six ships. The total Russian naval force in eastern Asia included seven battle-ships, four armoured cruisers, seven protected cruisers, four gunboats, six sloops, twenty-five destroyers, two mining transports, and fourteen first-class torpedo-boats. The Japanese fleet possessed a numerical superiority, with six battle-ships, eight armoured cruisers, thirteen protected cruisers, fourteen small cruisers, nineteen destroyers, and eighty-five torpedo-boats. While the Russians had one more battle-ship, the Japanese ships had greater weight of broadside fire and better gun-protection.

In early February 1904, the Russian fleet performed a maneuver, steaming out to sea off the Shantung promontory before returning to Port Arthur on February 4. Despite Japan breaking off negotiations in St. Petersburg on February 6 and signaling its intention to protect its interests, the Russians took few precautions, leaving harbor lights and beacons in position and deploying only a patrol of three torpedo-boats. The Russian fleet was further weakened by its division into three squadrons: the largest at Port Arthur, and smaller ones at Vladivostok and Chemulpo.

## The Siege of Port Arthur
The Japanese campaign plan intended for the army sent against Port Arthur to capture the fortress quickly and then march up the Liaotung peninsula to join three other armies for a general advance northward toward the capital of Manchuria. General Nogi and his three divisions operated under the hypothesis that the capture would not be delayed.

However, the fortress proved difficult to take. Port Arthur was defended by a garrison of thirty thousand men, powerful artillery, and splendid fortifications. Despite the "unlimited bravery" and "heroic efforts" of the Japanese, the Russian soldiers offered stubborn resistance and indomitable resolution. Consequently, Port Arthur did not fall until the last day of 1904. This delay prevented General Nogi's army from participating in the great field-battles that characterized the advance of the other three Japanese armies. During the siege, the Japanese crippled the Russian fleet, blocked the harbor entrance, and conducted repeated bombardments.

## Aftermath and Transfer of Possession
Following the conflict, the lease of Port Arthur, Talien, and adjacent territory—including territorial waters and all connected rights and concessions—was transferred and assigned by the Imperial Russian Government to the Imperial Government of Japan. This transfer was conducted with the consent of the Government of China. 

Additionally, the Russian government transferred the railway between Port Arthur and Chang-chun (Kuan-cheng-tzu), including all its branches, properties, and associated coal mines, to Japan without compensation. Under the terms of the agreement, Japan undertook to respect the proprietary rights of Russian subjects within the territory.
