# Wind Instruments: Construction and Acoustics

The production of sound in wind instruments is designed to achieve four primary objectives: pitch, the range or scale of available notes, the quality of tone (timbre), and dynamic variation, such as diminuendo and crescendo.

## Acoustic Principles and Pitch
The pitch of wind instruments depends almost exclusively on the length of the air-column that is set in vibration, while the diameter of the bore has practically no influence. However, in conical tubes where there is a significant difference in diameter between the mouthpiece and the bell—as seen in the horn—the pitch is slightly higher than the theoretical length would suggest.

Comparing three tubes of identical length reveals that a conical diverging tube produces a fundamental tone somewhat higher than a cylindrical tube, while a conical converging tube (from mouthpiece to bell) produces one somewhat lower. In the case of the horn, the rate of vibration in the conical tube is slightly less than in ambient air. Consequently, the practical length of the horn is slightly less than its theoretical length; for a horn in B-flat normal pitch, this difference is approximately 5½ inches (13.9 cm).

Regarding pipe behavior, E. F. F. Chladni posits that the mouthpiece end of all wind instruments is considered open, except for reed instruments, even when lips appear to close the end in trumpets and horns. Victor Mahillon suggests the opposite for instruments played by reeds or lips acting as reeds. Despite these differing views, the result is the same: a closed pipe with a trunco-conical bore behaves as an open pipe and provides a consecutive scale of harmonics, provided the bell diameter is at least four times greater than the mouthpiece diameter.

## The Horn and Its Variants
Modern horns are categorized into three classes:
1. **Short horns with wide bore**, including the post-horn and bugles.
2. **Saxhorns**, a family of hybrid instruments designed by Adolphe Sax. These adapt cup-shaped mouthpieces and valves to instruments of the bugle's calibre. The German equivalent is the Flugelhorn family. These instruments possess a natural scale comprising only the second through eighth harmonics.
3. **The French horn** (*cor de chasse*, *Waldhorn*, *corno*), a complex orchestral instrument featuring a slender conical tube wound in coils.

The French horn consists of the mouthpiece, the slide, the crooks, and the body. The body is a trunco-conical main tube approximately 7 feet 4 inches long, with a gradual increase in bore diameter that becomes accentuated near the bell. The bell is parabolic and measures between 11 and 12 inches in diameter. In valve horns, the bore is only theoretically conical, as the tubing attached to the valves is practically cylindrical.

The horn's mouthpiece is distinct from the trumpet's; it lacks a cup and is shaped like a funnel or truncated cone. The choice of mouthpiece depends on the performer's lip shape and whether they intend to emphasize higher harmonics (requiring a smaller, shallower mouthpiece, typically for first horns) or lower harmonics (used by second horns). The horn's round, sonorous timbre results from the predominance of partial tones up to the 7th or 8th.

## Other Brass and Wood Wind Instruments
### The Cornet
The cornet family includes the obsolete wood wind cornet and the modern cornet a pistons. The old wood cornets were either straight or curved. Some straight cornets featured a detachable cup-shaped mouthpiece, while others had a funnel-shaped mouthpiece carved directly into the wood, producing a "sweet" or "mute" tone (*cornetto muto*).

The modern cornet a pistons is a transformation of the post-horn and shares the same pitch as the trumpet. While it should theoretically have a wide conical bore, in practice, only the sections from the mouthpiece to the first valve slide and from the third valve slide to the bell are conical. Its cup-shaped mouthpiece curves into the bore without the sharp angles found in trumpets, resulting in a loose, coarse tone quality.

### The Bassoon and Bass Clarinet
The bassoon is a wooden tube approximately 8 feet (93 inches) long with a conical bore that tapers from 1¾ inches at the bell to 3/16 inches at the reed. To reduce its height to about 4 feet, the tube is doubled back upon itself. It consists of five parts: the bell, the long joint, the wing, the butt, and a 12-inch curved metal crook.

The bass clarinet, despite having the same pitch as the bassoon, has a much shorter bore (42 to 43 inches). This is due to its cylindrical tube and reed mouthpiece, which cause it to act as a closed pipe, speaking an octave lower than an open pipe of the same length.

## Evolution of the Bugle
To expand the musical possibilities of the bugle, makers introduced keys and valves. The use of keys led to the Kent bugle and the ophicleide. For example, the "Royal Kent Bugle" patented by Joseph Halliday in 1810 featured five keys and a compass of twenty-five notes. A specific model exhibited in 1890 measured 17 inches with a total tubing length (including mouthpiece) of 50½ inches. This instrument featured a diameter of ½ inch at the mouthpiece and 5¾ inches at the bell, providing a chromatic compass of two octaves.
