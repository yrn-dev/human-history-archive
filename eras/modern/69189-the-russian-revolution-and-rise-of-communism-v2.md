# The Silent Engine: The Role of the Peasantry in the Russian Revolution

While most historical narratives of the 1917 Russian Revolution focus on the urban proletariat and the ideological battles between Bolsheviks and Mensheviks in Petrograd, the revolution was not merely a city-based phenomenon. To understand the rise of communism in Russia, one must look toward the countryside. The peasantry, comprising over 80% of the population, provided the volatile social foundation upon which the Soviet state was built.

For the rural population, the revolution was not initially about the abstract goals of Marxism or the establishment of a "dictatorship of the proletariat." Instead, it was a desperate struggle for *zemlya* (land). The failure of the Tsarist regime to resolve the agrarian crisis following the emancipation of the serfs in 1861 created a deep-seated resentment that the Bolsheviks were able to skillfully exploit.

## The Land Decree and Tactical Alliances
Vladimir Lenin recognized a critical flaw in orthodox Marxism: Russia lacked a large industrial working class. To seize power, he pivoted the Bolshevik strategy to include the peasantry. Shortly after the October Revolution, the Bolsheviks issued the *Decree on Land*, which effectively legalized the spontaneous seizure of noble estates by peasant committees.

This was a tactical masterstroke. By validating the peasants' desire for land redistribution, Lenin neutralized potential rural opposition during the early stages of the revolution. However, this alliance was one of convenience rather than ideological alignment. The peasants desired private ownership or communal village control, whereas the Bolsheviks envisioned collective state ownership.

## The Friction of War Communism
The tension between the urban communist center and the rural periphery exploded during the Russian Civil War (1917–1923). Under the policy of "War Communism," the state implemented *prodrazvyorstka*—the forced requisitioning of grain to feed the starving cities and the Red Army.

This policy turned the peasantry against the new regime, leading to widespread rural uprisings and the devastating famine of 1921–22. Historians continue to debate whether these requisitions were a necessary survival tactic for the fledgling Soviet state or a premature attempt to impose a command economy on a population that was not yet ready for socialization. This friction eventually forced Lenin to retreat toward the New Economic Policy (NEP), a temporary return to limited capitalism to stabilize the countryside.

## Key Facts
* **Demographics:** Approximately 80-85% of the Russian Empire's population were peasants in 1917.
* **The Land Decree:** Issued in October 1917, it abolished private ownership of land without compensation to the landlords.
* **War Communism:** A period of extreme economic centralization and forced grain seizures (1918–1921).
* **The NEP:** The New Economic Policy (1921) allowed peasants to sell surplus grain on the open market to prevent total economic collapse.
* **Collectivization:** The eventual forced merger of peasant farms under Stalin in the late 1920s, which ended the NEP era.

## Did You Know?
* **The Green Armies:** During the Civil War, "Green Armies" emerged—peasant militias who fought against both the "Red" Bolsheviks and the "White" monarchists to protect their local lands.
* **The Mir:** Much of the rural revolution was organized through the *mir*, a traditional village commune that had existed for centuries.
* **The Tambov Rebellion:** One of the largest peasant uprisings against the Bolsheviks occurred in Tambov (1920–1921), requiring massive Red Army intervention to suppress.
