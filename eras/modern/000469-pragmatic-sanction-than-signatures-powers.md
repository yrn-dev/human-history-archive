# Pragmatic Sanction

The term "Pragmatic Sanction" refers to several distinct legal instruments and decrees issued across different eras and nations to regulate succession, governance, and civil administration.

## The Habsburg Pragmatic Sanction
The most prominent Pragmatic Sanction was promulgated by Emperor Charles VI on April 19, 1713. This instrument was designed to secure the succession of the Habsburg monarchy, specifically to ensure the throne for his daughter, Maria Theresa, in the absence of a male heir. The decree established the principle of the indivisibility and indissolubility of the monarchy, settling the succession on the dynasty of Habsburg-Lorraine via primogeniture and lineal succession to male heirs, and to the female line should the male line become extinct.

To ensure the validity of this sanction, Charles VI sought the adhesion of various European powers. In 1731, Holland and Great Britain agreed to respect the instrument in exchange for the cession of Guastalla, Piacenza, and Parma to Don Carlos. Later, Spain and Sardinia also adhered to the sanction. However, the court of Vienna made further sacrifices to obtain European guarantees; in 1727, the charter of the Ostend Company was suspended to appease the English and Dutch.

Despite these diplomatic efforts, the sanction faced challenges. Charles VI neglected the advice of Prince Eugene to maintain an army of 200,000 men, which would have served as a more solid guarantee than the signatures of foreign powers. Following the death of Charles VI on October 20, 1740, several powers—including Saxony, Spain, France, Bavaria, and Sardinia—sought to profit from Austrian weakness despite their previous adherence to the sanction. This led to the invasion of Silesia by Frederick the Great in 1740 and the subsequent Prussian victory at Mollwitz on April 10, 1741. Ultimately, the present constitution of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy remains based on this 1713 sanction, alongside the 1860 diploma of Emperor Francis Joseph I and the 1867 "Compromise" (Ausgleich).

## The Austrian Netherlands Sanction
In 1725, Emperor Charles VI issued a similar Pragmatic Sanction regarding the "Austrian Netherlands." Following the principle of indivisibility previously established by Charles V through the Augsburg transaction, this act decreed that Belgium was to be kept as an indivisible whole. It stipulated that the eldest son would be the heir to the throne, while admitting the right of succession for female descendants if no male heir existed.

## Other Historical Pragmatic Sanctions
The term has been applied to various other legal decrees in European history:

*   **Justinian's Pragmatic Sanction:** Dated August 15, A.D. 554, this document consists of twenty-seven articles intended to regulate and restore the civil state of Italy. Addressed to Antiochus (Præfectus Praetorio Italiae) and Narses (V. J. Praepositus Sacri Cubiculi), the text includes provisions where Justinian restrains the *judices militares*. It has been preserved in the *Corpus Juris Civilis* and by Julian Antecessor.
*   **The Sanction of 1438:** A Pragmatic Sanction issued in 1438 was denounced by King Louis of France at the beginning of his reign, though he later navigated a complex relationship with the papacy and the Medici during the late 15th century.
*   **Denmark and Norway (1661):** On January 10, 1661, an act titled "Instrument, or pragmatic sanction" was issued regarding the king's hereditary right to the kingdoms of Norway and Denmark. This document declared that all regalia and prerogatives of majesty were granted to the king as an absolute sovereign lord, marking a stage in the transition from a limited to an absolute monarchy under Frederick III.
