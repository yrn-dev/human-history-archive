# Geography and Mapping of Asia

## Physical Form and Dimensions
Due to the vast extent of Asia, ordinary maps often fail to provide a correct conception of its actual outline because projections of large spherical areas onto flat surfaces result in misleading distortions. When viewed on a globe, Asia takes the form of a great isosceles spherical triangle. Its north-eastern apex is located at East Cape (Vostochnyi) in the Bering Strait. The two equal sides of this triangle measure approximately 6,500 miles (or a quadrant of the sphere), extending west to the southern point of Arabia and east to the extremity of the Malay peninsula.

The base of this triangle, situated between the southern point of Arabia and the Malay peninsula, occupies about 60 degrees of a great circle, or 4,500 miles. This base is deeply indented by the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea on either side of the Indian peninsula. 

The north-western boundary of Asia is indicated with fair accuracy by a great circle drawn from East Cape to the southern point of Arabia. This line passes over the Ural Mountains, along the Arctic Ocean coast-line, through the western Caspian, and nearly along the boundary between Asiatic Turkey and Persia. However, the north-western half of Arabia and Asia Minor lie outside this circle. Similarly, a great circle drawn from East Cape to the extremity of the Malay peninsula follows the eastern boundary, passing nearly over the coasts of Cochin-China, China, and Manchuria.

## Geodetic Surveys and Longitude
Exact surveys based on triangulation have been conducted in various regions of Asia. In Russia, these surveys extend east to Chinese Turkestan, at a longitude of approximately 75 degrees East of Greenwich. In India, geodetic triangulation serves as the basis for exact surveys extending as far east as the eastern boundaries of Burma (approximately 100 degrees East). By the close of the 19th century, the final links of the great geodetic triangulation of the Indian peninsula were forged, though a geodetic connection to European systems remained to be completed.

Regarding the determination of longitudes differentially with Greenwich, a more rigorous application of the telegraphic method since 1890 resulted in a correction of about 2 arc seconds to previous determinations made through Suez. This determination was carried out through four specific arcs: Greenwich to Potsdam, Potsdam to Teheran, Teheran to Bushire, and Bushire to Karachi. Additionally, independent values recorded between 1875 and 1895 via Omsk, Ekaterinburg, Saratov, Orenburg, Kiev, and Baku fell within a range of 0.25 seconds, suggesting the mean value error is unlikely to exceed 0.10 seconds.

## Historical Cartography and Exploration
Early attempts at geographical construction sought to represent the known world based on collected facts rather than pseudo-theological or pseudo-scientific theories. The Catalan Map of 1375 is noted as a remarkable work in this regard, utilizing the writings of Marco Polo as a basis for India and Central and Further Asia. In this map, Cathay is correctly positioned as a great Empire in the south-east of Asia. While the Eastern Peninsula of India is absent, the Peninsula of Hither India is represented with a fair approximation of its position and form for the first time in geography. Other locations, such as Sumatra (Jaua), Bangala, Mien, Vocian, and Carajan, are also placed with a happy conception of their relation to India and Cathay.

In the 19th century, Klaproth contributed to the study of Asia through his work *Asia polyglotta* (1823 and 1831), which served as a résumé of known information and introduced a new classification for Eastern languages, particularly those within the Russian Empire. His other works include historical, geographical, and ethnographic descriptions of the Caucasus and Georgia.

## Regional Observations
In Asia Minor, Austrian expeditions have provided insight into the architectural splendor of Graeco-Roman cities. Specifically, the Ephesian district has been mapped more satisfactorily than any other district of ancient interest in the region. The modern representative of Ephesus is the Turkish village of Ayassoluk, which features a castle, Turkish baths, mosque-tombs, and a ruined mosque built in 1375 by Isa Bey II. of Aidin.

Other regional observations include the plain of Yezd, described by Khanikoff as a narrow and arid plain of great extent from northwest to southeast. He noted that the soil had become desiccated, causing all traces of wood to disappear, and that inhabitants rely on subterraneous conduits to bring water from a great distance.
