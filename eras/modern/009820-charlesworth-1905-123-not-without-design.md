# Legal and Financial Developments (1901–1905)

The early 20th century saw specific developments in English contract law, international diplomacy regarding Korea, and the economic growth of British Honduras.

## English Bond Law and Legal Precedents
In English law, bonds created to secure actions that contradict legal policy are considered void. Examples of such void bonds include marriage brocage bonds—those intended to procure a marriage between parties—and bonds given to a woman for future cohabitation, though this is distinguished from bonds for past cohabitation. A specific legal reference for matrimonial agency is found in the 1905 case of *Hermann v. Charlesworth* (2 K.B. 123).

Other specific types of bonds include:
*   **Post Obit Bonds:** These are bonds provided by a legatee or expectant heir, which become payable on or after the death of the person from whom the obligor expects an inheritance. If the obligee has demanded unconscionable terms, such a bond may be set aside.
*   **Bottomry Bonds:** This is a contract of hypothecation where a ship's master (acting as an agent) or the owner borrows money for the ship's use to address emergencies, such as necessary repairs. The ship, or its keel/bottom (*partem pro toto*), serves as security. If the voyage is completed safely, the obligee is repaid with agreed interest; however, if the ship is totally lost, the obligee loses the money.

The distinction between English and foreign law is illustrated by the suit of Shylock against Antonio in Shakespeare's work; while the scene is set in a Venetian court, such a bond would have received "short shrift" in an English court.

## Commerce and Finance in British Honduras
Between 1901 and 1905, British Honduras experienced growth in trade and revenue. The tonnage of vessels accommodated at its ports increased from 300,000 to 496,465. During this same period, imports rose from £252,500 to £386,123, and exports grew from £285,500 to £377,623.

The region's exports included fruit, timber, other vegetable products, sponges, turtles, tortoiseshell, deerskins, and rum. Principal imports consisted of hardware, cotton goods, spirits, wine, beer, groceries, and specie. This sea-borne trade was primarily shared between the United States and Great Britain.

Financial infrastructure included:
*   **Currency:** The American gold dollar became the standard coin on October 14, 1894, replacing the Guatemalan dollar. Consequently, silver from Central, South, and North America ceased to be legal tender. The British half sovereign and sovereign remain legal tender. Government notes are issued in denominations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 50, and 100 dollars, with local currency consisting of bronze one-cent pieces and silver pieces of 5, 10, 25, and 50 cents.
*   **Banking:** The government savings bank was founded in Belize in 1846, later opening branches in principal towns. The British Bank of Honduras was established in Belize in 1903.
*   **Public Finance:** Revenue, largely from customs, rose from £60,150 in 1901 to £68,335 in 1905. Expenditure rose from £51,210 to £61,800, with education and police costs being important items. As of 1905, the public debt was £34,736, representing balances from three loans raised in 1885, 1887, and 1891 for public works in Belize; these are repayable between 1916 and 1923.

## Japanese Influence in Korea
Following the war of 1904-5, Japan's success against Russia allowed it to resume influence over Korea. On February 23, 1904, an agreement established Japan as an administrative adviser to Korea, with Japan guaranteeing the country's integrity and the position of the imperial house. This interest was recognized by Great Britain in the Anglo-Japanese agreement of August 12, 1905, and by Russia in the peace treaty of September 5, 1905.

The restoration of Japanese influence met with resistance. In August 1905, disturbances occurred when merchants sought treasury assistance due to embarrassment from Japanese financial reforms; these unrests spread to provinces and required a show of force by the Japanese. A treaty completed on November 17, 1905, gave Japan control over Korea's relations and foreign affairs. Opposition continued, leading a Korean delegacy headed by Prince Yong to protest the treatment of Korea at the Hague conference in 1907.
