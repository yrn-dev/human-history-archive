# John Ashburnham

John Ashburnham (c. 1603–1671) was an English Royalist and a trusted advisor to the monarchy. The son of Sir John Ashburnham of Ashburnham in Sussex, he entered the service of the king early in his life.

## Early Career and Political Service
In 1627, Ashburnham was dispatched to Paris by his relative, the duke of Buckingham, to make peace overtures. The following year, in 1628, he prepared to join an expedition to Rochelle, though this was interrupted by the assassination of the duke of Buckingham. Also in 1628, Ashburnham was appointed groom of the bedchamber and elected as a member of parliament for Hastings. He continued to represent the borough of Hastings in the Long Parliament of 1640, during which time he served the king by reporting on the proceedings of parliament.

During this period, Ashburnham acquired a considerable fortune and succeeded in recovering the Ashburnham estates that had been alienated by his father.

## Role in the English Civil War
Ashburnham became one of the chief advisers to the king and enjoyed his full confidence. At the start of the war with Scotland, he attended Charles at York. During the subsequent Civil War, he served as the treasurer of the royal army. In this role, he caused remonstrances and jealousy from Hyde, as Ashburnham infringed upon Hyde's province as chancellor of the exchequer.

In 1644, Ashburnham served as a commissioner at Uxbridge. In April 1646, he accompanied Charles in his flight from Oxford to the Scots. Ashburnham eventually escaped abroad, where he joined the queen in Paris. He later resided at Rouen and was sent to the Hague to seek aid from the prince of Orange.

## Later Conflict and Controversy
Following the army's seizure of Charles, Ashburnham joined the king at Hampton Court in 1647. While there, he participated in several conferences with other army officers and Cromwell. When Charles escaped from Hampton Court on November 11, Ashburnham provided advice that opposed that of Sir John Berkeley, who had urged the king to go abroad. Following Ashburnham's counsel, the king took refuge in the Isle of Wight, where Ashburnham placed him in the hands of the governor, Robert Hammond.

Upon realizing the situation, the king exclaimed, "Oh, Jack, thou hast undone me!" In response, Ashburnham fell into a "great passion of weeping" and offered to kill Hammond. This action led to Ashburnham being charged with disloyalty and treachery. While Clarendon censured his conduct, he absolved Ashburnham of any crime other than excessive self-confidence and folly. Both Charles I and Charles II acquitted him.

## Family and Legacy
John Ashburnham had eight children. His eldest son, William, had a son named John (1656–1710), who was created Baron Ashburnham in 1689. The second son of that John, also named John (1687–1737), became the 3rd Baron Ashburnham in 1710 and was later created earl of Ashburnham and Viscount St Asaph in 1730. A direct descendant of this line was the 5th earl (born 1840). Bertram, the 4th earl (1797–1878), was the collector of the famous Ashburnham library, which was dispersed in 1883 and 1884. This library contained a map from 970, the oldest of its kind.

Ashburnham's conduct regarding the king was defended in a 1648 publication titled *A Letter from Mr Ashburnham to a Friend*. A longer *Narrative* was published in 1830 by George, the 3rd earl of Ashburnham, though this championship of his ancestor is described as unconvincing and entirely uncritical. Additionally, Ashburnham wrote *A Letter to W. Lenthall* (1647), which repudiates the claim that the king had violated his parole.
