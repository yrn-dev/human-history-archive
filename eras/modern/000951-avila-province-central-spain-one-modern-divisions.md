# AVILA

Avila is a province located in central Spain and constitutes one of the modern divisions of the kingdom of Old Castile. In 1833, Old Castile was divided into several provinces, including Avila, Burgos, Logrono, Palencia, Santander, Segovia, Soria, and Valladolid. The province of Avila is bounded to the north by Valladolid, to the east by Segovia and Madrid, to the south by Toledo and Caceres, and to the west by Salamanca. As of 1900, the province had an area of 2,570 square miles and a population of 200,457.

## Geography and Climate
The province is naturally divided into two distinct sections that differ in social economy, productions, climate, and soil. The northern portion is generally level with soil of indifferent quality, though it is marly in some areas and rocky in the valleys of the Sierra de Avila. This region experiences a climate of extreme heat in the summer and severe cold in the winter.

The southern division consists of rugged granitic sierras interspersed with well-watered, sheltered valleys containing rich vegetation. While the climate here is not unhealthy, winters are long and severe, particularly in the waste lands of Avila and the elevated region of the Paramera.

The province's principal mountain chains include the Guadarrama, which separates Avila from Madrid; the Sierra de Avila and the Paramera to the west of the Guadarrama; and the Sierra de Gredos along the southern frontier, where peaks reach heights of 6,000 to 8,000 feet. The bare peaks of the Sierra de Gredos contrast with the ridges ramifying from the Paramera, which are covered in forests of firs, oaks, and beeches.

Water resources are provided by several rivers. The Tormes, Adaja, Trabancos, and Douro belong to one basin, while the Tietar and Alberche belong to the basin of the Tagus.

## Economy and Natural Resources
The economic activity of the province varies by region. The population in the north is primarily agricultural, while stock-breeding is a major industry in the southern region. The production of merino sheep wool once generated immense revenue. Other agricultural products include grapes, chestnuts, olives, and the keeping of silk-worms.

The mountains contain lead, iron, copper, silver, and coal. However, the mineral wealth of the area has been exaggerated, and mining had been practically abandoned by the start of the 20th century. Fine jasper and marble quarries are located in the district of Arenas.

Manufacturing is limited, consisting mainly of soap, lime, cloth, paper, combs, and copper utensils. Trade is described as minimal. During the 18th and 19th centuries, the province suffered a decline in population and wealth. This was attributed to strict laws of mortmain and entail, as well as oppressive feudal and manorial rights, which hindered progress.

## The City of Avila
The capital of the province, Avila (anciently *Abula* or *Avela*), is situated on the right bank of the river Adaja, 54 miles west-by-north of Madrid. The city is built on a rocky hill's flat summit, surrounded by a treeless, arid table-land of grey boulders and lofty mountains. In 1900, the city's population was 11,885.

The city is famous for its ancient brown granite walls, which feature nine gateways and eighty-six towers and remain in excellent repair, although some of the city now extends beyond them. Avila is the seat of a bishop and contains several significant ecclesiastical buildings:
* **The Gothic Cathedral:** Traditionally dated to 1107, though likely from the 13th or 14th century, it resembles a fortress with two solid towers and embattled walls. It houses a silver pyx from 1571 created by Juan de Arphe.
* **Romanesque Churches:** San Pedro, San Vicente, Santo Tomas, and San Segundo are primarily 15th-century Romanesque, though San Pedro and San Vicente may date back to the 12th century.
* **Santo Tomas:** This church contains a marble monument carved by the Florentine sculptor Domenico Fancelli for the tomb of Prince John (d. 1497), the only son of Isabella and Ferdinand.
* **Santa Teresa:** The church and convent of Santa Teresa mark the supposed birthplace of the saint (c. 1515-1582).

Other city landmarks include a foundling hospital, an infirmary, a military academy, training schools for teachers, and an old Moorish castle (*alcazar*) used as barracks. The city also served as the seat of a university from 1482 until 1807. Local trade includes pottery, leather, linen, cotton goods, hats, and agricultural products.

## Infrastructure and Society
Avila is connected by rail to Madrid, Valladolid, and Salamanca, though communications remain defective in many parts of the province. Elementary education is relatively good, with more than two-thirds of the population being literate, and the ratio of crime is proportionately low.
