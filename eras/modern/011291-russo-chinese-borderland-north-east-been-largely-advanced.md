# Russo-Chinese Borderland (North-East)

The geographical understanding of the Russo-Chinese borderland in the north-east was significantly expanded through the operations associated with the Russo-Japanese War, which concluded in 1905.

## Boundary Demarcation and Geography
The southern boundary of Siberia follows an irregular path toward the north-east. This line is defined in part by the Alatau and Tian-shan mountains. It reaches a northerly point at approximately 53 deg. N. latitude, marked by the Sayan range located to the west of Irkutsk. From this point, the boundary deflects south-east until it reaches the Kerulen, an affluent of the Amur river. According to unofficial maps, this point is situated at approximately 49 deg. 20' N. latitude and 117 deg. 30' E. longitude.

From the Kerulen, the boundary follows the affluent to its junction with the Amur river, and then continues along the Amur river to its junction with the Usuri. The boundary then follows the Usuri to its head, moving in a direction slightly west of south. Finally, the boundary reaches the Pacific coast at approximately 42 deg. 30' N. latitude at the mouth of the Tumen river. This point is located 100 miles south of the Amur bay, where the Russian port of Vladivostok is situated.

## Regional Populations
The uplands along the Russo-Chinese frontier, specifically between 70° and 85° E. longitude and 35° and 50° N. latitude, are occupied largely by the Kara-Kirghiz. These nomad highlanders have inhabited regions including the Tian-shan range, the Ala-tau range, the Issyk-kul basin, and the valleys of the Chu, Tekes, and Talass rivers. Their territory also encompasses the uplands draining into the Oxus and Jaxartes, as well as Shignan, Karateghin, and Khokand, with the Pamir table-land serving as a summer destination.

The Kara-Kirghiz are referred to as *Burut* by Mongolians and the Chinese. This designation is shared with the *Buryat*, the Siberian Mongolians of the Baikal district. The ancestors of the Kara-Kirghiz, known as the Khakas, originally lived in the south of the Tomsk and Yeniseisk governments, extending south of the Sayan range into the Tannuola hills of Chinese territory. In the 17th century, Russians encountered them and, with the assistance of the Kazaks, exterminated those located east of the Irtish, forcing the remaining population to move south-westwards and westwards to seek refuge with the Kara-Kirghiz.

## Related Regional Explorations
The advancement of knowledge in the north-east borderland occurred alongside broader geographical research in the Far East. Between 1874 and 1905, a surge of exploration focused on economic and commercial development in China. Notable contributors included:
* **Captain William Gill**: Traveled across China to Burma and eastern Tibet.
* **E.C. Baber**: Investigated the western provinces of the "Celestial empire" between 1877 and 1878.
* **Archibald Colquhoun**: Traveled from Peking to Talifu in 1881.
* **Mark Bell**: Illustrated ancient trade routes while crossing the continent in 1887.
* **C.C. Manifold**: Mapped the upper basin of the Yang-tsze river from 1900 to 1904.

In other regions, the demarcation of boundaries also facilitated survey extensions. The acquisition of Burma allowed for surveys of the mountains hiding the sources of the Mekong, Salween, and Irrawaddy rivers. Additionally, the Indian survey helped fix the geographical positions of Himalayan peaks to the south and west, while explorations in Chinese Turkestan connected surveys with India and Kashmir.
