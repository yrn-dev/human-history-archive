# The British Ship "Olive Blossom"

The "Olive Blossom" was a British vessel that played a role in the early history of the colonization of Barbados. In 1605, the ship touched the island, and its crew, discovering that the land was uninhabited, took possession of it in the name of King James I.

## Colonization of Barbados
The arrival of the "Olive Blossom" marked an early point in the process of peaceful colonization of Barbados. The island was acquired without conquest and remained under British possession from that point forward. While the "Olive Blossom" claimed the territory in 1605, the first actual settlement did not occur until 1625. This settlement was established under the patent of Lord Leigh (later the earl of Marlborough), to whom the king had granted the island, and was carried out at the direction of Sir William Courteen.

## Subsequent Governance and Administration
The administration of the island shifted in 1627 following a compromise with Lord Marlborough. A grant for the island was obtained by the earl of Carlisle, whose claim was based on a 1624 royal grant of all the Caribbean islands. In 1628, Charles Wolferstone, a native of Bermuda, was appointed as governor. That same year, the present capital was founded when sixty-four settlers arrived at Carlisle Bay.

During the English Civil War, Barbados became a refuge for many Royalists. Under the leadership of Lord Willoughby, who had leased the island from the earl of Carlisle, these refugees offered stout resistance against the forces of the Commonwealth. This period of resistance ended when Willoughby was defeated and exiled.

## Post-Restoration Changes
Following the Restoration, the crown took over the government of the island. This move was intended to appease the planters, who were uncertain about the legal titles of the estates they had developed into valuable properties. To satisfy the claims of the patentees, the proprietary or patent interest was abolished, and a duty of 4-1/2% was imposed on all exports. By 1684, during the governorship of Sir Richard Dutton, a census recorded that the population of the island had reached 20,000.

## Geography and Nomenclature
The name of the island is subject to differing opinions regarding its derivation. It may stem from a Spanish word referring to the hanging branches of a vine that strike root in the ground, or it may be derived from a species of bearded fig-tree. In maps from the 16th century, the name appeared in various forms, including Barnodo, Barnodos, Barbudoso, Bernados, and St Bernardo. The island is noted for having more numerous traces of the Carib Indians than any other of the Antilles, and it is thought that the Portuguese may have visited the island first.
