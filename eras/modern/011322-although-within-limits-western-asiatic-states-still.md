# Western Asiatic States and Regional Influence

## Characteristics of Asiatic Societies
The term "Asiatic" is often used to describe a homogeneous type, though significant differences exist between groups such as the Chinese, Hindus, and Turks. Generally, Asiatics are noted to stand on a higher level than the natives of America or Africa, yet they lack the specific material civilization found in western Europe. A common mental characteristic attributed to Asiatics is a lack of the sentiment of freedom and independence seen in Europeans; individuals are viewed as members of a religion, state, or family rather than as entities with their own rights and destiny.

This social structure leads to conservatism and fatalism in religion, as well as autocracy in politics. Consequently, Asiatic history is characterized by large, simple outlines and is less diversified or eventful than European annals, despite being chronologically longer. However, these same conditions allow those who achieve eminence—such as prophets and conquerors—to wield greater authority than their European counterparts. For example, Timur and Jenghiz Khan covered more ground than Napoleon, and Mahomet had a more significant effect on the world than any European.

## The Influence of Islam
Islam is described as perhaps the greatest transforming force in world history. It has subjugated and profoundly affected all of eastern and northern Africa, eastern Europe, Spain, and all of western Asia, including India. The influence of Islam is intensified by the fusion of religious and secular power, creating a Moslem state characterized by polygamy, slavery, and the autocracy of the ruler. While Moslems are theoretically equal, they maintain a political status superior to non-Moslems, often forming a ruling caste in countries of mixed belief, as seen in India under the Moguls and in Turkey.

Islam remains paramount in Afghanistan, Arabia, Persia, and Turkey. In India, it is strong in the central and northern regions but weaker in the south, with Moslems comprising one-fifth of the total population. While Islam spread to the southern Philippines and the Malay Archipelago—where it overwhelmed Hindu civilization—it made no progress in Japan or Indo-China. In China, the Chinese influence was strong enough to force Islam to function as a creed parallel to other religions, such as Buddhism, rather than a state power.

## European Influence and Interaction
European influence on Asia occurred primarily during two epochs: first following the conquests of Alexander the Great, and second from the 16th century onward. Alexander's conquests established Perso-Greek kingdoms that hellenized their region and influenced the religion and art of China and India. Following this, eastern Europe spent a long period combating Asiatic invasions, leaving little room to Europeanize the East. Later, the Crusades maintained communication with the Levant and established the power of the Roman Church, though this occurred to the detriment of oriental Christianity.

In the region of western Asia and eastern Europe, Asiatic influences have generally prevailed, with Islam remaining paramount and European culture at a low ebb. However, western Europe has asserted power in middle and eastern Asia due to improvements in communication.

## Regional Studies: Persia and Baluchistan
In Persia, although the region remained under Asiatic government and within the limits of western Asiatic states—beyond the active influence of European interests—material progress appeared stationary. Nevertheless, geographical knowledge expanded. Scientific inquiry and a new school of explorers have mapped the country, providing detailed information on the routes of the interior, the harbours of the Persian Gulf, and the possibilities for commercial development via trade roads linking India, the Persian Gulf, the Karun, and the Caspian via Seistan.

Baluchistan is no longer viewed as a distinct entity among Asiatic nations in the same way Afghanistan is. Its independence is qualified by British control. British Baluchistan officially consists of the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts, originally Afghan, were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, though they had nominally been handed to Kalat forty years prior. The Baluchistan Agency extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. While some districts within this agency are as independent as those in Afghanistan, the province's overall political status is similar to the native states of the Indian peninsula, with supreme control exercised by the agent to the governor-general of India.
