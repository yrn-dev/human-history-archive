# Bohemia in the Habsburg Empire

## Early Dynastic Transitions and Habsburg Influence
The historical trajectory of Bohemia was marked by shifts in sovereignty and internal power struggles. In 1197, Premysl Ottakar became the undisputed ruler and was crowned king in 1198, beginning an uninterrupted line of royal titles. Among the most significant of these rulers was Premysl Ottakar II, who expanded his dominions to include the archduchies of Austria, as well as Carinthia, Istria, and parts of northern Italy. To counter the power of the Bohemian nobility, Ottakar II encouraged the immigration of Germans into his lands.

Following the extinction of the Luxemburg dynasty in 1437, the Habsburg princes claimed a hereditary right to the Bohemian throne. While the Bohemians initially sought to maintain their right to elect their sovereign—choosing figures such as Henry, duke of Carinthia, and John of Luxemburg—the region eventually became integrated into the Habsburg realm.

## Religious Conflict and the Thirty Years' War
Religious tension defined much of the early Habsburg rule in Bohemia. Under Emperor Maximilian II (1564–1576), a liberal policy maintained peace, though various Christian beliefs continued to struggle for mastery in Bohemia, Silesia, and Moravia. This shifted with the accession of Rudolph II (1576–1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic who encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation. During Rudolph's reign, Bohemia existed in a state of semi-independence.

The subsequent ruler, Matthias, initially allied with Bohemian Protestants but later adopted the traditional Habsburg policy favoring the Church of Rome. In 1615, a general diet reaffirmed the use of the national language, decreeing that Bohemian be the official language of the country. However, the election of Ferdinand II in 1618, who was resolved to establish the rule of the Jesuits in Bohemia, led to the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War. Bohemia became a primary theatre of hostilities; it was occupied by Saxons in 1631 and frequently pillaged by Swedish troops, culminating in the Swedish capture of part of Prague in 1648. Despite Swedish efforts, imperial representatives remained inflexible regarding toleration for Bohemian Protestants.

## Administrative Centralization and Autonomy
In the 18th century, the estates of Bohemia recognized the Pragmatic Sanction on October 16, 1720, sanctioning female succession to the throne in favor of Archduchess Maria Theresa. Although Charles, elector of Bavaria, invaded and occupied Prague in 1741, Maria Theresa eventually recovered the territory.

Under Maria Theresa and her son Joseph II, the autonomy of Bohemia was systematically limited. A 1749 decree abolished separate Bohemian law-courts, and an Austro-Bohemian chancellor was later appointed to control the administration of Bohemia alongside the German domains of the house of Habsburg.

## The 19th Century: Reaction and Constitutional Struggle
By the end of 1849, constitutional government ceased in Bohemia and across the Habsburg empire. A severe reaction followed, characterized by the ruthless suppression of liberty and self-government. The state attempted to exterminate the national language, mandating the exclusive use of German in government offices and schools. Bohemian newspapers were suppressed, and the society of the Bohemian museum was placed under police supervision.

The Italian campaign of 1859 forced a move away from absolutism. A decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, modeled partly on the ancient diets of Bohemia. However, a subsequent decree on February 26, 1861, established a central parliament in Vienna with extensive powers and an electoral system heavily biased toward Germans. Bohemians left this parliament in 1863, arguing it encroached upon the constitutional power of the diet of Prague.

## Late 19th Century Constitutional Shifts
The Austro-Prussian war of 1866 fundamentally altered Bohemia's position. The treaty signed at Prague on August 23, 1866, excluded all Habsburg-ruled lands from Germany, leading to a decline in German influence within Bohemia. While Hungary achieved complete independence, the new constitution of 1867—applying to German and Slavic parts of the empire—maintained centralization. The Bohemians energetically opposed this constitution.

In 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the empire by recognizing the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia in an imperial message dated September 14, 1871. This effort failed when a government with a strong German tendency took office in 1872. Later, under Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe (1879), Bohemian representatives returned to the parliament in Vienna, though they maintained that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. In exchange, the Taaffe government removed some electoral anomalies favoring the German minority and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.
