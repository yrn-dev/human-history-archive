## Power Series and Convergence
A power series is defined as an infinite series where the general term is $a_nz^n$, with $a_n$ acting as a constant and $z$ as a variable for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Such a series is classified as a power series if there exists a real and positive number $M$ such that $|a_n z_0^n| < M$ for a given $z = z_0$ and every $n$. This condition is satisfied if the series converges for $z = z_0$.

Under these conditions, the series converges absolutely for every $z$ where $|z| < |z_0|$ and converges uniformly over every range $|z| < r'$ where $r' < |z_0|$. Consequently, every power series possesses a circle of convergence. Within this circle, the series converges absolutely and uniformly, and the function of $z$ it represents is continuous. If the series converges for a point $z_0$ on the circumference, the sum for an interior point $z$ is continuous with the sum for $z_0$ as $z$ approaches $z_0$.

Within a common circle of convergence, two power series, $\sum a_n z^n$ and $\sum b_n z^n$, can be multiplied using ordinary rules. Furthermore, if two converging power series agree in value at a point within every arbitrarily small neighbourhood of $z = 0$, the series are identical ($a_n = b_n$).

## Differentiable Functions and Polynomial Representation
A differentiable function $f(z)$ whose region of definition includes the area between two concentric circles of radii $r$ and $R$ (centered at the origin) can be expressed using integrals. For an interior point $z_0$, the function is represented as:
$$f(z_0) = \sum_{-\infty}^{\infty} A_n z_0^n$$
where $A_n = (1 / 2\pi i) \int [f(t) / t^{n+1}] dt$, taken around any circle with a radius between $r$ and $R$. If the region of definition includes the entire interior of the outer circle, $r$ can be taken as 0, causing coefficients $A_n$ for $n < 0$ to vanish. In this case, the function is expressed by a power series $\sum_{0}^{\infty} A_n z_0^n$. A significant result of this is that a differentiable function of $z$ is expressible by a power series in $z - c$ about every interior point $c$ of its region of definition.

Additionally, if an arbitrary finite region is constructed (excluding barriers joining singular points of $f(x)$ to $x = \infty$), it is possible to determine a polynomial $Q(x)$ of order $m$ for any arbitrary real positive number $\sigma$ such that $|f(x) - Q(x)| < \sigma$ for all interior points. Thus, $f(x)$ can be represented by a uniformly converging series of polynomials.

## Integral Functions and Zeros
For an integral function $f(z)$, if there are no finite positions of $z$ where the function vanishes, then $\lambda[f(z)]$ is an integral function $\phi(z)$, meaning $f(z) = \exp[\phi(z)]$. If there are a finite number of values where $f(z)$ vanishes ($z = a_1, \dots, a_m$), then $f(z) = \exp[\phi(z)](z - a_1)^{h_1} \dots (z - a_m)^{h_m}$, where $h_1, \dots, h_m$ are positive integers.

If $f(z)$ vanishes at $z = a_1, a_2, \dots$ where $|a_1| \le |a_2| \le \dots$ and the limit $|a_n| = \infty$, and assuming $z = 0$ is not a zero and all zeros are of the first order, the function is expressed as:
$$f(z) = \exp[\phi(z)] \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \{(1 - z/a_n) \exp[\phi_n(z)]\}$$
Here, $\phi(z)$ is an integral function and $\phi_n(z)$ is an integral polynomial of the form $\phi_n(z) = z/a_n + z^2/2a_n^2 + \dots + z^s/sa_n^s$. For the specific function $\sin[\pi x] / [\pi x]$, this is represented as $\prod_{-\infty}^{\infty} |(1 - x/n) \exp(x/n)|$.

## Geometric and Continuum Theory
In the context of $C_n$, a "homogeneous part" $H_n$ is defined as a perfect and connected aggregate. It possesses several properties:
1. There are points of $C_n$ that are not points of $H_n$, forming a complementary aggregate $H'_n$.
2. There are points "within" $H_n$ that have neighbourhoods consisting exclusively of points of $H_n$.
3. Points of $H_n$ not "within" $H_n$ are limiting points of $H'_n$; these form the "boundary" of $H_n$.
4. For any two points $a, b$ within $H_n$, there exists a number $\epsilon$ and a number $m$ such that a chain of points $x', x'', \dots, x^m$ can be chosen to connect $a$ and $b$ through neighbourhoods consisting exclusively of points within $H_n$.

Regarding algebraic equations, for an irreducible algebraic equation $f(x, y) = 0$ representing a plane curve of order $n$ with $1/2n(n-3)$ double points, taking $n-3$ arbitrary fixed points allows for the construction of a general curve of order $n-2$. This results in a rational irreducible equation $F(\xi, \eta) = 0$, representing a further plane curve where every point $(x, y)$ of $f$ corresponds to a definite point $(\xi, \eta)$ of $F$.
