# Russel (1683)

The work titled *An Antidote against Poison ... Remarks upon a Paper printed by Lady (Rachel) Russel*, published in 1683, is a text whose authorship is a subject of historical attribution. According to the *State Trials* (ix. 710), the authorship of the piece is ascribed to Sir Bartholomew Shower. However, Wood attributes the authorship of the work to another individual based on common rumour. Additionally, Sir Bartholomew Shower makes an allusion to the text on page 753 of the same record.

## Context of the Period and Associated Figures

The year 1683 was marked by significant political unrest and legal proceedings. During this time, Robert Baillie (also known as Baillie of Jerviswood), a Scottish conspirator and son of George Baillie, traveled to London. Baillie entered into communication with a party that included Russell and Monmouth to seek redress. Following the discovery of the Rye House Plot, Baillie was arrested. Although he denied knowledge of the conspiracy, he admitted he had been consulted regarding a potential insurrection in Scotland. He was eventually found guilty of treason and hanged in Edinburgh on December 24, 1684.

## Execution of William, Lord Russell

Lord William Russell was among the high-ranking offenders for whom beheading was the reserved mode of execution. He was executed on July 21, 1683. The execution was carried out by the public hangman, John Ketch (also known as "Jack Ketch"), who had been appointed to the office in 1663. 

The execution of Lord Russell is recorded as having been performed in a "clumsy way." John Ketch later issued a pamphlet titled "Apologie," in which he defended his performance. In this document, Ketch alleged that he was interrupted while taking aim and claimed that the prisoner, Lord Russell, did not "dispose himself as was most suitable" for the execution.

The manner in which Lord Russell was treated by Ketch later became a point of reference during the execution of the duke of Monmouth on July 15, 1685. While on the scaffold, Monmouth addressed Ketch regarding the treatment of Lord Russell. This interaction reportedly left Ketch "quite unmanned," resulting in the executioner requiring at least five strokes of the axe and the eventual use of a knife to sever Monmouth's head.
