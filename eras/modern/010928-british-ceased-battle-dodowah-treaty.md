# British Relations with the Ashanti and the Battle of Dodowah

## Diplomatic Efforts and Early Treaties
In the early 19th century, the British government sought to cultivate intercourse with the powerful African kingdom of the Ashanti. To facilitate this, they appointed Mr. Joseph Dupuis, formerly the consul at Mogador, as consul to Kumasi. Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast in January 1819 and reached Kumasi on February 28, 1820. Following several meetings with the king, a treaty was established. This agreement acknowledged the sovereignty of the Ashanti over the territory of the Fanti and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies.

This treaty was disowned by Mr. J. Hope Smith, the governor of Cape Coast, who viewed the agreement as a betrayal of the interests of the natives under British protection. The government in London supported Mr. Hope Smith, and in 1821, the British crown assumed direct control of the British settlements.

## Conflict and the Defeat at Essamako
Sir Charles M'Carthy was appointed as the first governor by the crown. He espoused the cause of the Fanti, which led to a military confrontation with the Ashanti. On January 21, 1824, at a location beyond the Prah called Essamako, Sir Charles was defeated in battle. The Ashanti forces numbered 10,000 men, while Sir Charles commanded only 500. 

The battle resulted in the death of Sir Charles and eight other Europeans. Following the defeat, the skull of the governor was used as a royal drinking-cup at Kumasi. It was asserted that the defeat occurred because the ordnance-keeper brought kegs of vermicelli instead of ammunition, though records indicate that this mistake merely hastened an inevitable catastrophe.

## The Battle of Dodowah and Its Aftermath
The day of the defeat at Essamako coincided with the death of Osai Tutu Quamina, who was succeeded by Osai Okoto. This transition led to a period of chronic warfare. This state of conflict continued until August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at the battle of Dodowah.

The outcome of the battle of Dodowah marked a turning point in the region, as the power of the Ashanti over the coast tribes began to wane. Following this defeat, the payment of ground rent for the forts held by the British ceased.

## The Settlement of 1831
By 1831, the Ashanti king was forced to seek peace from Mr. George Maclean, the administrator of the Gold Coast. Peace was purchased at the price of 600 oz. of gold. Additionally, as part of the terms, the king was required to send his son to Cape Coast as a hostage.
