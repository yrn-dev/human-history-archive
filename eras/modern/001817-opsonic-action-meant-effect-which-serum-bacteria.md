# Opsonic Action

Opsonic action refers to the effect a serum has on bacteria to make them more susceptible to phagocytosis by the white corpuscles (leucocytes) of the blood. This process is a key component of how the body destroys bacteria through direct cellular agency.

## Mechanism and Characteristics
Research by Wright and Douglas demonstrated that phagocytosis may occur when a small quantity of normal serum is present, but is absent if normal salt solution is used instead. This indicates that the serum contains substances that combine with organisms to produce an alteration in them, making them susceptible to phagocytosis. This substance, known as opsonin, does not act directly on the leucocytes.

Opsonins in normal serum are highly labile and are rapidly destroyed when heated to 55°C. Consequently, serum heated to this temperature has practically no greater effect in aiding phagocytosis than a normal salt solution. However, it has been observed that the serum of an animal immunized against a specific bacterium possesses a special action in bringing about the phagocytosis of that organism, and this specific property is retained even after the serum is heated to 55°C. It is generally accepted that opsonic action involves at least two distinct classes of substances: the labile opsonins of normal serum and thermostable immune opsonins developed as a result of active immunity.

## Laboratory Demonstration and the Phagocytic Index
Opsonic action can be demonstrated outside the body using a mixture consisting of a suspension of a specific bacterium, the serum being tested, and leucocytes from a normal person or animal. This mixture is placed in a thin capillary tube and incubated at 37°C for half an hour. A film preparation is then made on a glass slide, stained, and examined microscopically.

To quantify this effect, the average number of bacteria contained within a set number of leucocytes (for example, fifty) is counted. In cases of disease, a control using normal serum is conducted under identical conditions. The "phagocytic index" is then calculated by dividing the average number of bacteria contained within leucocytes in the tested case by the number found in the normal serum control.

## Role in Immunity and Treatment
In the context of natural immunity, variations in chemiotaxis—the process by which leucocytes are guided by chemical substances—likely depend on the opsonic content of the serum. While Metchnikoff noted that phagocytosis is present in immune animals and deficient in susceptible ones, it is understood that the increased ingestion of bacteria in active immunity depends on the presence of immune opsonins. These opsonins act as anti-substances that exert a preliminary effect on the bacteria, rather than "educating" the phagocytes or increasing their inherent digestive or ingestive activity.

Sir A. Wright and his co-workers utilized the opsonic action of serum to control the treatment of bacterial infections using vaccines (injections of dead cultures of the corresponding bacterium). The goal of this treatment is to raise the opsonic index as an indication of increased immunity. The process typically follows a specific pattern:
1. An initial small quantity of vaccine usually increases the opsonic index within a few days.
2. A subsequent injection may cause a fall in the opsonic index, known as the "negative phase."
3. This is followed by a further rise to a level higher than the original.

By carefully choosing the timing and amount of vaccine injections, the opsonic index can be raised to a high level through a series of steps. Monitoring the index is critical to avoid administering additional vaccines during the negative phase, as this could further diminish opsonic action. The underlying principle is to stimulate the general production of anti-substances throughout the body to aid tissue cells in destroying organisms at the sites of bacterial growth.

## Relation to Anti-Bacterial Sera
Opsonic action is one of the primary activities that can be demonstrated outside the body in the serum of an actively immunized animal. Such anti-bacterial sera are prepared by injecting bacteria (in living, dead, or maceration product form) into an animal, sometimes using a succession of dead, living virulent, and living supervirulent bacteria to achieve a high dosage. 

While these sera possess powerful protective properties, they are generally not antitoxic (having no action on the corresponding toxin). In addition to opsonic action, these sera may exhibit other activities, including agglutinative action (the aggregation of bacteria into clumps) and bacteriolytic or lysogenic action (where bacteria swell, become granular, and undergo dissolution).
