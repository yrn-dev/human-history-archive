# Nitrifying Bacteria and Nitrogen Circulation

Nitrifying bacteria, also referred to as nitro-bacteria, are a group of organisms that play a supreme role in agriculture and the natural circulation of nitrogen across the globe's soil, rivers, and surfaces. These organisms are part of a broader category of Schizomycetes, the vast majority of which are not pathogenic and are instead indispensable aids in natural operations.

## The Process of Nitrification
Nitrification is the process by which ammonium carbonate, which accumulates in the soil from sources such as urea, undergoes oxidation in two distinct stages. Research by Winogradsky established that this process is dependent on moisture, aeration, and temperature, and can be halted through sterilization. Winogradsky successfully isolated these organisms using gelatinous silica, as the bacteria refused to nitrify on nitrogenous media or gelatine.

The research revealed that two different kinds of bacteria are involved in this cycle:
*   **Nitroso-bacteria:** These bacteria are capable of oxidizing ammonia into nitrous acid.
*   **Secondary Stage:** Further bacterial action results in the oxidization of nitrous acid into nitric acid.

A significant discovery regarding Nitroso-bacteria is their ability to build up organic matter from purely mineral sources in the dark. By adding only pure mineral salts—such as chlorides, sulphates, and carbonates of magnesium, sodium, and ammonium—these bacteria assimilate carbon from carbon dioxide and obtain nitrogen from ammonia. The energy required to split the carbon dioxide molecule is provided by the energy liberated during the oxidation of nitrogen, a process analogous to how green plants use solar rays.

## De-nitrification and Agricultural Impact
The work of nitrifying bacteria is often reversed by other bacteria found in rivers and soil. Warington demonstrated that many species can reduce nitrates back to nitrites. Further research by Aberson, Giltay, Dupetit, and Gayon showed that some bacteria carry this reduction further, allowing ammonia or free nitrogen to escape.

This biological reversal explains a long-standing agricultural puzzle regarding the wastefulness of applying nitrates and manure to land simultaneously. Fresh manure is abundant in de-nitrifying bacteria. These organisms not only reduce nitrates to nitrites and set free ammonia and nitrogen, but they also undo the work performed by any nitrifying bacteria present.

## The Nitrogen Cycle in Nature
Nitrogen undergoes various vicissitudes in the environment. Combined nitrogen from dead organisms is broken down into ammonia by putrefactive bacteria. This ammonia, along with nitrogen from urea, the fixation of free nitrogen, and traces of nitrogen salts from meteoric activity, enters the cycle. From there, ammonia may be oxidized to nitrates, which are then absorbed by higher plants to be worked into proteids. These proteids are passed to animals and eventually broken down again into ammonia by bacterial action. Alternatively, nitrates may be degraded back into nitrites, ammonia, or free nitrogen, which then escapes.

## Classification and Nutrition
In the classification proposed by Fischer, nitrifying bacteria are categorized as "monotrophic" because they carry out only one particular series of decompositions. Because they can work up carbon or nitrogen from inorganic sources and require no organic food materials, Fischer terms them "Prototrophic." He regards these as primitive forms of life and the nearest existing representatives of the primary forms that first developed the ability to transform non-living materials into living materials.

This distinguishes them from "Metatrophic" bacteria (which include saprophytes and zymogenic forms that require organic materials) and "Paratrophic" bacteria (obligate parasites that live within the tissues, juices, or blood of living organisms).

## Related Biological Processes
The respiratory nature of nitrifying bacteria is compared to that of sulphur bacteria and acetic bacteria. For example, acetic bacteria (such as *Bacterium aceti* and *B. pasteurianum*) oxidize alcohol to acetic acid and potentially further to $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{OH}_2$. Similarly, sulphur bacteria oxidize hydrogen sulphide ($\text{SH}_2$) to obtain energy, much as higher plants use carbohydrates. Just as de-nitrifying bacteria reverse the work of nitro-bacteria, *Spirillum desulphuricans* attacks and reduces sulphates, undoing the work of sulphur bacteria.
