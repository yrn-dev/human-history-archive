# Book-Collectors by W.Y. Fletcher and Guigard's Nouvel Armorial

The study of book-collecting involves the assembly of books that possess permanent interest—whether through their content, form, or the history of the individual copy—and which are rare or prospectively rare. While the act of bringing together books has existed in limited forms since the Roman Empire and the end of the Middle Ages, book-collecting as it is understood in the modern era is a relatively recent growth.

## The Nature of Book-Collecting
Book-collecting is defined by the pursuit of items that are difficult to procure, though rarity is considered subordinate to interest. The hobby is compared in essence to the work of a museum curator or the duties of a librarian at an established library. As libraries age, books that were once tools for one generation become a "dead stock" from which critics and antiquaries salvage items whose interest cannot be fully reproduced by a reprint.

Collectors are often driven by different types of appeals:
*   **Imaginative Appeal:** This is most evident in books associated with famous events or persons, such as poets, soldiers, statesmen, or royalty (for example, Mary, Queen of Scots). Interest is further heightened if a book's pedigree can be traced through a long series of book-loving owners.
*   **Intellectual Appeal:** This includes works of historical value that have not been reprinted or where original editions are more authentic than modern versions. Examples of this include Lord Crawford's collections of papal bulls and English, Scottish, and Irish proclamations, as well as the Foxwell collection of early books on political economy.
*   **Physical and Technical Appeal:** Some collectors value first editions for their superiority in study or as specimens of printing. While the value of first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics has declined, the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces, such as the 1623 First Folio Shakespeare, has increased.

## Specialized Bibliographical Literature
The literature surrounding book-collecting has evolved to reflect changing collector ideals. Early "omnivorous" collectors relied on general manuals such as Brunet's *Manuel du libraire* (first published 1810) and W.T. Lowndes' *Bibliographer's Manual of English Literature* (first published 1834). These were supplemented by J.G.T. Graesse's *Tresor de livres rares et precieux* (1859-1867). In more recent times, these general manuals have been superseded by detailed handbooks and bibliographies focusing on special books.

Among the works produced to document the history and pedigrees of book ownership are *Great Book-Collectors* by Charles and Mary Elton (1893), *English Book-Collectors* by W.Y. Fletcher (1902), and Guigard's *Nouvel armorial du bibliophile* (1890).

## Book-Plates and Armorials
The taste for collecting book-plates—private tokens of ownership—is a modern development that hardly dates back further than 1875. A significant impetus for this study was the 1880 publication of the *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates* by Lord de Tabley. This work established a general classification of styles, including:
*   **Early armorial:** Styles previous to the Restoration.
*   **Jacobean:** A term including the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian, Queen Anne, and Restoration days.
*   **Chippendale:** A style described as rococo.
*   **Wreath and ribbon:** Associated with the period of the urn.

This interest led to the formation of collectors' societies in France, Germany, the United States, and England. These societies produce journals such as the *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* in Berlin, the *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* in Paris, and *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* in London.
