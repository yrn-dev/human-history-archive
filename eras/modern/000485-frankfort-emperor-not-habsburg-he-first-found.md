# Frankfort and the Habsburg Monarchy

The city of Frankfort has served as a significant site for the coronation and political assemblies of the Habsburg dynasty and the German states across several centuries, marking pivotal transitions in the nature of imperial authority and the relationship between Austria and Germany.

## The Coronation of Leopold and the French Revolution
On October 6, 1700, Leopold was crowned Roman emperor at Frankfort. It was in his capacity as emperor, rather than as a member of the house of Habsburg, that he first entered into direct antagonism with revolutionary France. This conflict was precipitated by the Constituent Assembly's decree of August 4, which abolished feudal rights and affected the possessions of German princes in Lorraine and Alsace. 

Leopold protested these actions as the sovereign of Germany, a protest that eventually expanded to be made in the name of Europe. While the declaration of Pillnitz (August 27) attempted to establish a common policy, it was hindered by the interests of Austria and Prussia regarding the Polish question. Despite a studiously moderate initial attitude, Leopold's refusal to disperse the corps of *emigres* on the French frontier and his insistence on the rights of dispossessed princes led to a crisis. By January 25, 1792, the French Assembly decreed that war would be declared if no satisfactory reply was received from the emperor by March 1.

## The Parliament of 1848 and the German Question
In May 1848, a parliament of united Germany met at Frankfort. The Austrian government, unable to refuse, sent delegates from its German provinces. This assembly faced a dilemma regarding the place of the Austrian monarchy within a united Germany: whether to include only the German provinces or the monarchy in its entirety. The Austrian government rejected any settlement that would split the monarchy or create double allegiance, while German patriots were unwilling to include a vast non-German population.

Although the Austrian archduke John was appointed regent while awaiting the election of an emperor, the political leaders remained tied to the tradition of Austrian hegemony. Eventually, the Austrian delegates withdrew, and the crown of Germany was offered to Frederick William of Prussia. However, the Habsburg monarchy utilized the prestige of imperial tradition and the victory of Radetzky at Novara to re-establish the system devised at the Congress of Vienna. The subsequent fall of revolutionary Vienna contributed to the collapse of the revolution in Pest and Frankfort. This period saw the military execution of Robert Blum, a deputy of the German parliament whose person should have been sacrosanct.

## Imperial Transitions and the 1863 Assembly
Following the revolution, the new ministry under Prince Felix Schwarzenberg remained inimical to the German ideals of Frankfort and devoted to Habsburg traditions. On December 2, 1848, Emperor Ferdinand abdicated, and his nephew, Francis Joseph, ascended the throne. 

In 1863, Emperor Francis Joseph summoned a meeting of princes to Frankfort. This assembly demonstrated Austria's ascendancy among the smaller states of the Confederation. However, it also highlighted that consolidation of the Confederation was impossible without the cooperation of Prussia. Otto von Bismarck, who stood outside the meeting, determined that the issue could only be settled through "Blood and Iron" and the total exclusion of Austria, a goal realized after the campaign of 1866 and the Austrian collapse at Koniggratz.

## Evolution of the Imperial Title
The title "emperor of Austria" was established in 1804 by Francis II (as Francis I) to prevent the house of Habsburg from being relegated to a position inferior to the Bonapartes following the collapse of the Holy Roman Empire. This title replaced "Imperator Romanorum semper Augustus" in 1806. 

Over time, the title became a source of contention. Magyars and Slavs viewed it as an ignore of their national rights, while Germans saw it as a poor substitute for the political unity of the Holy Empire. The 1866 struggle with Prussia and the 1871 proclamation of the German empire stripped the title of its broader significance, leaving it with a purely territorial meaning. Furthermore, the 1867 compact with Hungary meant the title no longer fully represented the emperor's relation to all his dominions.
