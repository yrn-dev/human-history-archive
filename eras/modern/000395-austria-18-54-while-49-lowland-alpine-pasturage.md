# Austria

The archive contains detailed records regarding the crown-lands of Upper and Lower Austria, as well as specific religious sites and military fortifications within the broader region.

## Upper Austria
Upper Austria, known in German as *Oberoesterreich* or *Oesterreich ob der Enns* ("Austria above the river Enns"), is an archduchy and crown-land. It is bounded to the north by Bohemia, to the west by Bavaria, to the south by Styria and Salzburg, and to the east by Lower Austria. The region covers an area of 4,631 sq. m.

The Danube river divides Upper Austria into two unequal sections. The smaller northern portion is a prolongation of the southern angle of the Bohemian forest, with the Sternstein (3,690 ft.) and Ploecklstein (4510 ft.) serving as culminating points. The southern portion is part of the Eastern Alps, featuring the Upper Austrian Alps and the Salzkammergut. Between these mountains and the Danube lies the Alpine foothill region, characterized by swelling undulations and terraces. A notable feature of this area is the Hausruckwald, a wooded mountain chain rich in brown coal that reaches its peak at the Goeblberg (2,950 ft.).

The region's hydrology is defined by the Danube watershed. The river flows from west to east, receiving the Salzach, Inn, Traun, Steyr, and Enns on its right bank, and the Little and Great Muehl rivers on its left. A direct navigable route between the Elbe and the Danube is provided by the Schwarzenberg canal, located between the Moldau and the Great Muehl.

As of 1900, the population of the duchy was 809,918, representing 174.8 inhabitants per sq. m., the highest density among the Alpine provinces. The population is almost exclusively Roman Catholic and of German stock. Administratively, the archduchy is divided into 12 districts and two autonomous municipalities: the capital, Linz (population 58,778), and Steyr (population 17,592). Other significant towns include Gmunden (7,126), Ischl (9,646), and Wels (12,187). Politically, the duchy sends 22 members to the Reichsrat in Vienna, and its local diet consists of 50 members, including the bishop of Linz as an *ex officio* member.

## Lower Austria
Lower Austria, or *Niederoesterreich* (*Oesterreich unter der Enns*, "Austria below the river Enns"), is an archduchy and crown-land with an area of 7,654 sq. m. It is bounded by Hungary to the east, Styria to the south, Bohemia and Upper Austria to the west, and Bohemia and Moravia to the north.

The Danube enters the province at its westernmost point and exits near Pressburg in the east, dividing the land into two parts. The northern region, the *Waldviertel*, consists of low hilly country continuing the Moravian and Bohemian plateau. Its highest point is the Peilstein in the Weinsberger Wald (3,478 ft.), with other elevations including the Manhartsgebirge (1,758 ft.) and Gfoehler Wald (2,368 ft.). The Bisamberg (1,180 ft.) is a south-easterly offshoot located near Vienna.

The southern division is primarily hilly and mountainous, comprising the Lower Austrian Alps. Major groups include the Schneeberg (6,806 ft.), the highest summit in the province, as well as the Raxalpe (6,589 ft.), Oetscher (6,205 ft.), Duerrenstein (6,156 ft.), and Voralpe (5,802 ft.). East of the Semmering ridge are the Leithagebirge (1,674 ft.) and the Wechsel (5,700 ft.). The Wiener Wald, which reaches 2,929 ft. at the Schoepfl, forms an offshoot of the Alpine group and ends at the Leopoldsberg (1,380 ft.) and Kahlenberg (1,404 ft.) northwest of Vienna.

Lower Austria's economy is heavily industrial, ranking alongside Moravia and Bohemia. The center of commerce and industry is Vienna, though many factories are located in the Wiener Wald up to the Semmering to utilize water for motive power. Key industries include chemicals, brewing, milling, metallurgics, textiles, sugar, tobacco, foodstuffs, leather, paper, silk, cloth, millinery, *objets de luxe*, and the production of musical and physical instruments.

The province's land use consists of 43.4% arable land, 34.2% woods, 13.1% meadows and gardens, 3.2% pastures, and 2% vineyards. While cattle-rearing is not well developed, fish and game are plentiful. Mining is of slight importance, though graphite is found near Muehldorf and small amounts of iron-ore and coal are extracted in the Alpine foothill region. The population in 1900 was 3,100,493.

## Geography and Infrastructure
The Danube enters Austria at Passau (height 800 ft.) after passing through Bavaria. In Lower Austria, the Danube is the primary waterway, with the March and Enns also being navigable. Other rivers in the province include the Pielach, Traisen, Wien, Schwechat, Fischa, Leitha, Ybbs, Erlauf, Isper, Krems, Kamp, and Goellersau. Notable small Alpine lakes include the Lunzer See and Erlaufsee. The region is also known for mineral springs, including the thermal springs of Voeslau, iron springs of Pyrawarth, iodine springs of Deutsch-Altenburg, and sulphur springs of Baden.

## Religious and Military Sites
In Styria, the village of Mariazell is situated in the Salza valley amid the north Styrian Alps. It is the most frequented sanctuary in Austria, attracting approximately 200,000 pilgrims annually to venerate a 18-inch miracle-working image of the Virgin carved from lime-tree wood. This image was presented in 1157 and is housed in a chapel adorned with silver. The larger church was built in 1644, expanding a smaller structure erected by Louis I, king of Hungary, following a victory over the Turks in 1363.

Regarding military defenses, the Alpine frontiers—particularly in Tirol—contain numerous fortifications centered around Franzensfeste and Trent. Defensive works are also located on military roads leading into Carinthia, such as at Predil Pass and Malborgeth. While the capitals of Budapest and Vienna are not fortified, other strategic locations in the broader Austria-Hungary region include the naval harbor of Pola and sites in Dalmatia such as Cattaro and Zara.
