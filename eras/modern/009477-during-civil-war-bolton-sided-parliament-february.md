# Bolton

## Civil War Conflict
During the English Civil War, the town of Bolton sided with the parliament. This alignment led to multiple assaults by royalist forces. In February 1643 and March 1644, royalist forces attacked the town, but they were repulsed on both occasions. However, on May 28, 1644, the town was attacked by Lord Derby and Prince Rupert, resulting in the town being stormed with great slaughter.

Later, on October 15, 1651, Lord Derby was brought to Bolton and executed on the same day. He had been taken prisoner following the battle of Worcester.

## Industrial Development
Bolton was famous for woollen manufactures from its early days. During the reign of Richard I, an aulneger was appointed to stamp and measure bundles of woollen goods, indicating the town's status as a center for the woollen cloth trade. The industry grew further in 1337 due to the settlement of Flemish clothiers. By 1566, an act of parliament appointed deputies to assist aulnegers in several places, including Bolton.

While Leland recorded in his 1558 *Itinerary* that Bolton produced "cottons," these were actually woollen goods. Actual cotton goods were not produced in Lancashire until 1641, at which time Bolton was recognized as the chief seat for the manufacture of dimities, vermilions, and fustians. The industry was further stimulated by the settlement of French refugees following the revocation of the edict of Nantes.

Technological advancements in the 18th century provided an astonishing impetus to the cotton trade, specifically the inventions of Samuel Crompton (1780) and Sir Richard Arkwright (1770), both of whom were born in the parish. The first spinning factory in Bolton is said to have been established in 1780. By 1851, the town had 66 cotton mills operating 860,000 throstle spindles. Other industrial developments included the bleaching industry, which began in the early 18th century, and large ironworks that emerged in the latter half of the 19th century. Specialized fabrics such as muslins and cotton quiltings were produced in 1763, and velvets were first made there around 1756 by Jeremiah Clarke.

## Trade and Infrastructure
The town's commercial history includes a weekly market and a yearly fair, for which William de Ferrers obtained a crown charter in 1251. Over time, the annual fair was replaced by four others, primarily for cattle and horses. The Whitsuntide Show and New Year fairs were later additions that arose during the 19th century. Regarding the market, Monday remained the market day until the beginning of the 19th century, when it was gradually superseded by the customary Saturday market.

Infrastructure improvements in the late 18th century included the construction of a canal from Manchester to Bolton in 1791. Additionally, Bolton Moor was enclosed by an act of parliament in 1792.

## Dukes of Bolton
The title of duke of Bolton was held by the Paulet (or Powlett) family between 1689 and 1794. 

Charles Powlett, the 1st duke (c. 1625–1699), served as a member of parliament for Hampshire and Winchester between 1660 and 1675. After supporting the claim of Mary and William to the English throne in 1688, he was created duke of Bolton in April 1689 and restored to the office of lord-lieutenant of Hampshire and the privy council. He was described as an eccentric man who was hostile to Marlborough and Halifax; it is said that in 1687 he traveled with 100 horsemen and four coaches, giving entertainments at night and sleeping during the day.

He was succeeded by his elder son, Charles, the 2nd duke (1661–1722), a supporter of William of Orange and a former member of parliament for Hampshire. His roles included lord-lieutenant of Dorset and Hampshire, governor of the Isle of Wight, and twice a lord justice of the kingdom. He also served as a commissioner for the union of Scotland and England and as lord chamberlain of the royal household. His third wife, Henrietta, was a natural daughter of James, duke of Monmouth. Swift referred to this duke as "a great booby."

The 3rd duke, Charles (1685–1754), was a member of parliament from 1705 to 1717 and was later made Baron Pawlet of Basing. He attained high rank in the British army and held many of the public offices his father had held. In 1733, he was deprived of several offices after displeasing Sir Robert Walpole, though some were later restored. In 1745, he raised a regiment to serve against the Jacobites. He was known as a famous gallant and married Lavinia Fenton, a singer and former mistress, as his second wife.

He was succeeded by his brother, Harry (c. 1690–1759), the 4th duke, who had been a member of parliament for forty years and served as lord-lieutenant of Hampshire.
