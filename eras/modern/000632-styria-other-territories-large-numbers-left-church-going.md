# Religious and Political Shifts in Styria and Austrian Territories

The region of Styria and surrounding Austrian territories have been characterized by complex intersections of nationality, political allegiance, and religious affiliation. These dynamics reached a point of significant tension in the late 19th century, resulting in a notable movement of people leaving the Roman Catholic Church.

## The "Los von Rom" Movement
In 1898, an agitation against the Roman Catholic Church emerged among the Germans of Styria and other territories. This movement, known as "Los von Rom," saw large numbers of individuals leave the Church to join either Old Catholicism or Protestantism. 

The movement was primarily driven by the hostility of the Church toward the German nationalist movement. Germans in Austria had been influenced by events within the German empire, leading to a tendency to exalt the principle of nationality over religion. This nationalist principle was granted an absolute authority that the Roman Catholic Church could not accept. Furthermore, the "Los von Rom" movement was sparked by the continued alliance between the Clerical party and the Slav parties. Because of these political underpinnings, the movement is characterized more as a political demonstration than a religious one.

## Religious and Ethnic Demographics
The influence of the Church varied across different ethnic groups. The Slav races provided more candidates for ordination than the Germans, which created a favorable influence for the Church among them. 

In the southern districts, religious affiliation often served as a dividing line between ethnic groups. While the Slovenes formed a large majority in Carniola and significant minorities in the south of Styria and Carinthia, the Serbs—though sharing the same race and language as the Croats—were separated by their adherence to the Orthodox Church. This religious connection attracted the Serbs toward Russia and fostered hopes for the creation of a great Servian kingdom, a goal that was difficult to reconcile with loyalty to Austria. In recent years, efforts have been made to attract Slovenes toward these Orthodox non-Austrian Slavs.

## Political Struggles and the Clerical Party
The political landscape of the region was marked by a struggle between Federalists and Centralists. The "kernel" of the empire consisted of purely German districts, which included all of Styria except for the southern districts, as well as Upper and Lower Austria, Salzburg, Vorarlberg, and a large part of Carinthia.

Within this environment, the Liberal party was dominant in Styria and Lower Austria and sought the development of central institutions. In opposition, a strong Clerical and Conservative party supported local institutions. The bishops and clergy viewed these local institutions as a protection against the bureaucracy and Liberal influence of a centralized parliament. Consequently, the clergy were willing to align themselves with the Federalists to gain support in this struggle.

## Religious Sites and Institutions in Styria
Styria remains home to significant religious landmarks. The village of Mariazell, located in the north Styrian Alps in the valley of the Salza, contains the most frequented sanctuary in Austria. It is visited annually by approximately 200,000 pilgrims who venerate a miracle-working image of the Virgin carved from lime-tree wood. This image, presented in 1157, is housed in a chapel within a large church erected in 1644. The church was an expansion of an earlier structure built by Louis I, king of Hungary, following a victory over the Turks in 1363.

Additionally, the town of Marburg in Styria serves as the seat of the bishop of Lavant. Marburg features a cathedral dating from the 16th century with a tower built in 1623. Nearby, the village of Mariarast is also noted for its church, which is a popular place of pilgrimage.
