# Religion in Bolivia

## State Recognition and Legal Status
The attitude of the Bolivian republic toward the Church is defined in Article 2 of the constitution. According to this text, the state recognizes and supports the Roman Apostolic Catholic religion. The public exercise of any other form of worship is prohibited, with the exception of the colonies, where such practice is tolerated. This toleration is also tacitly extended to foreigners residing in the country who belong to other religious sects.

## Demographics and Social Influence
According to a census conducted in 1900, the Roman Catholic population numbered 1,609,365, while those of other creeds totaled 24,245. This distribution equates to 985 Roman Catholics and 15 members of other creeds for every thousand people. 

Among the domesticated Indians, the Roman Catholic faith is professed, though it is tinged with the superstitions of their ancestors. These populations are deeply influenced by the ceremonies and forms of the church, which have remained largely unchanged since the period of the first Spanish settlements. Furthermore, the Indians hold the clergy in great reverence and fear.

## Ecclesiastical Organization and Administration
Bolivia is organized into three bishoprics and one archbishopric. The archbishopric of La Plata has its seat at Sucre and encompasses the departments of Potosi, Oruro, Tarija, Chuquisaca, and the Chilean province of Antofagasta. The three bishoprics have their sees located in Santa Cruz, Cochabamba, and La Paz.

The state provides a small subvention to the *Propaganda Fide*, the organization entrusted with mission work among the Indians. This organization maintains a large number of missions and five colleges, and it is estimated that these missions are responsible for as many as 20,000 Indians. The annual appropriation provided for the Church is approximately L17,150. Additionally, several convents are maintained by religious orders, which have never been suppressed within Bolivia.

## Government Oversight
The executive branch of the Bolivian government includes a cabinet of five ministers of state. One of these specific portfolios is designated as "foreign relations and worship," indicating the government's administrative link to religious affairs.
