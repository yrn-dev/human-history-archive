# ASHLAND (Pennsylvania, U.S.A.) AND ASTRAGAL

## Ashland, Pennsylvania
Ashland is a borough located in Schuylkill county, Pennsylvania, U.S.A. Geographically, it is situated approximately 100 miles northwest of Philadelphia and about 50 miles northeast of Harrisburg. The borough is built on the slope of Locust Mountain at an elevation of about 885 feet above sea level.

The town was laid out in 1847 and was named in honor of the home of Henry Clay, which is located at Lexington, Kentucky. Ashland was later incorporated in 1857.

### Demographics and Infrastructure
Population records for the borough indicate a count of 7,346 in 1890, 6,438 in 1900 (which included 969 foreign-born residents), and 6,855 in 1910. The municipality owns and operates its own waterworks.

Transportation to and from the borough is provided by the Philadelphia & Reading and Lehigh Valley railways. Additionally, the borough is served by electric lines operated by the Shamokin & Mount Carmel Transit Company and the Schuylkill Railway Company.

### Industry and Healthcare
The primary industry in Ashland is the mining of anthracite coal, with several collieries located in the vicinity. Related to this industry, a state hospital for injured persons of the Anthracite Coal Region of Pennsylvania was opened in 1883 at Fountain Springs, located one mile southeast of the borough.

## Astragal
The term astragal is derived from the Greek word *astragalos*, meaning the ankle-joint. It is used in two primary contexts: architecture and gaming.

### Architectural Definition
In architecture, an astragal is a convex moulding. This term is generally used to describe small mouldings, whereas the term "torus" is applied to larger mouldings of the same convex form. Vitruvius refers to a "Lesbian astragal," which was likely an astragal featuring a bead and reel enrichment.

### Gaming and the Use of Astragaloi
The term also relates to *astragaloi*, which were pastern-bones from calves, goats, or sheep used in a game of chance. In this game, stones were thrown from a cup or by hand onto a table, and the values were determined by the side upon which the bone fell.

The pastern-bone has two rounded ends that prevent it from standing, as well as two narrow sides and two broad sides. One of each pair is concave and the other is convex. The scoring system was as follows:
*   **The Dog (chios):** The convex narrow side, counting as 1.
*   **Convex broad side:** Counting as 3.
*   **Concave broad side:** Counting as 4.
*   **Concave narrow side:** Counting as 6.

Using four astragals, players could achieve 35 different scores in a single throw. Many of these scores had distinctive names; Greek names included Solon, Midas, Alexander, and Aphrodite, while Roman names included Venus and Vulture. The lowest possible throw in both Rome and Greece was the Dog. The highest throw in Greece was the Euripides, which counted as 40 and was likely a combination throw, as it is impossible to throw more than four sixes at once.
