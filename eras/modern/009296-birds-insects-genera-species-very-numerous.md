# Birds and Insects: Genera and Species

The study of birds and insects reveals a vast diversity of genera and species across the globe, with significant variations based on geographic region, evolutionary history, and ecological niche.

## Avifauna of the Neotropical and South American Regions
In South America, particularly Bolivia, the avian population is diverse. The high sierras are frequented by eagles and condors of the largest size, while the common vulture is found throughout the country. The tropical plains and valleys are inhabited by the American ostrich (*Rhea americanus*) and a large species of stork known as the *jaburu* or *bata* (*Mycteria americana*), which can reach a maximum height of 8 feet and a wing spread of 8 feet 6 inches. The forests of warm valleys are filled with song-birds and birds of beautiful plumage, and humming-birds are found even at high mountain altitudes. In the lowlands, toucans, araras, and parrots are common; these birds possess beautiful feathers but are characterized by harsh voices.

The Neotropical region is home to several remarkable families of birds. The humming-birds (*Trochilidae*) are particularly notable, with more than 400 species and nearly 150 genera, though only three of these genera occur in the Nearctic region. Other significant groups include:
*   **Tyrants (*Tyrannidae*):** Over 300 species and more than 70 genera.
*   **Tanagers (*Tanagndae*):** About 300 species and upwards of 40 genera.
*   **Piculules (*Dendrocolaptidae*):** Over 200 species and approximately 40 genera.
*   **Ant-thrushes (*Formicariidae*):** Nearly 200 species and more than 30 genera.

Other defined groups in this region include the screamers (*Palamedeidae*), trumpeters (*Psophiidae*), todies (*Todidae*), motmots (*Monotidae*), jacamars (*Galbulidae*), toucans (*Rhamphastidae*), and tapaculos (*Pteroptochidae*), as well as isolated forms such as the sun-bittern (*Eurypyga*) and the seriema (*Cariama*).

## Australian and Regional Ornithology
In the great Australian region, the family *Meliphagidae* (honey-eaters or honey-suckers) is highly characteristic. This family consists of more than 20 genera and over 200 species, most of which are small birds not exceeding the size of a missel thrush. They are distinguished by a long protrusible tongue with a brush-like tip that can form a suctorial tube for absorbing honey from flower nectaries. While they feed on honey and fruits, insects attracted by the honey provide the primary nourishment for many species. Most *Meliphagidae* have a confined range, often limited to a single island, though the white-throated honey-eater (*Glycyphila rufifrons*) is found across Tasmania and most of Australia. Only one species, *Ptilotis limbata*, is said to occur outside the limits of the Australian region.

Regarding global distribution, recent bird species are estimated at 10,000–11,000, though Dr. R.B. Sharpe suggests the number is closer to 15,000.

## Insect Diversity and Distribution
Insect distribution often follows broad geographic patterns. In southern Asia—including China, Siam, the Malayan Islands, and India south of the Himalaya—genera and species are often identical on opposite sides of the Bay of Bengal. There is a marked connection between India and Africa, evidenced by many common genera confined to those two regions. In east Siberia, nearly one-third of known Coleopterous insects are also found in western Europe.

Among parasitic insects, the lice are divided into the order Anoplura (true lice) and the suborder Mallophaga (bird-lice). True lice are found on mammals and cause intolerable irritation by inserting a rostrum into the skin to pump blood. In humans, these include the head-louse (*P. capitis*), the body-louse (*P. vestimenti*), and the crab-louse (*Phthirius pubis*). Bird-lice are more numerous in species but fewer in genera; with the exception of *Trichodectes* (found on mammals), they infest birds.

## Fossil Records
The fossil record provides insight into ancient genera. Dragon-flies are particularly well-preserved due to their strong dense reticulation and large wings. In the Carboniferous strata of France, C. Brongniart described the Protodonata, a group of gigantic insects allied to dragon-flies but with simpler wing-neuration. True *Odonata* are found in Mesozoic formations in England (such as the Stonesfield Slate and Purbeck beds) and the Rhine valley. The Solenhofen deposits in Bavaria contain numerous perfect remains of gigantic dragon-flies; because they lack subaquatic traces, it is believed they were drowned in the sea and washed ashore.

Fossil birds have also been recovered from various strata. The upper Eocene has yielded forerunners of recent genera, including birds of prey, rails, and Gallinaceous birds. Notable finds include the giant penguin *Palaecudyptes* from New Zealand and *Palaeospheniscus* from Patagonia. The Miocene has provided the greatest number of bird-bones, including eggs and feather imprints. Examples include *Palaelodus* (an ancestral flamingo with shorter legs) and *Limnatornis* (related to hoopoes).

## Aviculture and Diet
For those breeding birds in aviaries, understanding dietary needs is essential. There is a large group of fruit-eating and insectivorous birds suitable for aviculture. Some birds are exclusively insect-eaters, while others are partially insectivorous, switching to seeds when insects are scarce. Additionally, some species that eat grain as adults rely almost entirely on insects to feed their young during early development.
