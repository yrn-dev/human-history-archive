# Don Juan

The figure of Don Juan is a legendary character whose narratives have spread across various European countries. While the character is a universal type and the subject of local myths in many nations, he received his name in Spain.

## Literary Origins and the Spanish Legend
Don Juan was introduced into formal literature through the Spanish play *El Burlador de Sevilla y convidado de piedra*. First printed in Barcelona in 1630, this work is usually attributed to Tirso de Molina, though this ascription has been disputed. The play presents Don Juan as the incarnation of arrogant blasphemy and perverse sensuality.

The core plot—featuring a profligate who invites a dead man to supper only to find the invitation accepted—was current before 1630 and is not exclusive to Spain. While some superficial resemblances exist between *El Burlador de Sevilla* and Lope de Vega's earlier play *Dineros son calidad*, the specific characterization of Don Juan is largely considered a creation of Tirso de Molina.

Historical claims regarding the character's basis are unsubstantiated. Reports mention a Don Juan Tenorio who frequented the court of Peter the Cruel, and another dissolute gallant of the same name in Seville, but there is no satisfactory evidence of their existence. It is unlikely that the legend is based on historical facts.

## International Diffusion and Adaptations
The legend has appeared in various forms across Europe, including *Le Souper de fantome* in Picardy. Variants of the story have been found in locations as distant as the Azores and Iceland. The Spanish version of the legend is believed to have absorbed certain elements from the French story of Robert the Devil.

The Spanish drama was highly popular in Italy, where it was frequently performed in pantomime by Italian actors. In 1657, a company of these mimes brought the story to France. This led to dramatizations by De Villiers in 1661 and Dorimond in 1659. These versions inspired Moliere's *Le Festin de pierre* (1665), in which Moliere substituted prose for verse, reduced supernatural elements, and added comic effects.

## Influence on Music and Later Literature
The sustained popularity of the legend is attributed in large part to Mozart's setting of Da Ponte's libretto. Mozart's *Don Giovanni* eclipsed earlier operas by Gazzaniga, Gardi, Tritto, Righini, and Le Tellier. Other musical treatments include the oldest setting by Henry Purcell, which utilized lyrics by Shadwell, and ballet-music by Gluck.

The French romantic school encountered Don Juan through the "pale version" provided by Da Ponte. This influenced several authors, including:
*   **Alfred de Musset**: *Namouna* and *Une Matinee de Don Juan*.
*   **Balzac**: *Elexir d'une longue vie*.
*   **Merimee and Dumas**.

The character continued to fascinate later writers, including Barbey d'Aurevilly and Flaubert in France, Heyse and Landau in Germany, and Sacher-Masoch in Austria.

## Byron's Don Juan
Distinct from the legendary profligate, the poet Byron used the figure of Don Juan in his masterpiece of the same name to depict life as a whole. Byron's approach is described as humorous-realistic rather than mock-heroic. 

Byron's "plan was to have no plan" regarding a synopsis; instead, he used the hero to "unpack his heart," offer a philosophy of life, and provide a criticism of human nature and his own political or personal enemies. Despite occasional voluptuousness or grossness, the work is recognized as a "hymn to the earth" and is praised for its faithfulness, beauty, and humour.
