# The Silent Engine: The Role of the Peasantry in the Russian Revolution

While traditional narratives of the Russian Revolution often center on the urban proletariat and the intellectual vanguard of the Bolsheviks in Petrograd and Moscow, the revolution was not merely a city-born phenomenon. To understand the collapse of the Romanov dynasty and the subsequent rise of communism, one must examine the rural landscape. The Russian peasantry, comprising over 80% of the population, provided the volatile social energy that made the revolution possible, even if their goals often diverged from those of Lenin’s party.

The peasantry did not seek a Marxist utopia of industrial collectivism; rather, they sought *zemlya* (land). For decades, the remnants of serfdom and the inefficiency of land communes had left the rural population land-hungry and impoverished. Their participation in the revolution was less about ideological alignment with communism and more about a pragmatic, violent seizure of the soil.

## The Rural Explosion and the "Green" Movement
Following the February Revolution of 1917, the countryside descended into a state of spontaneous anarchy. Peasants began seizing estates from the nobility, burning manor houses, and redistributing land among themselves. The Bolsheviks, recognizing this momentum, strategically pivoted. Despite their preference for state-controlled agriculture, Lenin issued the *Decree on Land*, which effectively legalized the peasants' seizures. This tactical move secured the peasantry's neutrality—and occasional support—during the critical months of the October Revolution.

However, this alliance was fragile. Once the Bolsheviks consolidated power, the transition from "land to the people" to "state control" triggered a brutal backlash. The policy of *Prodrazvyorstka* (forced grain requisitioning) during the Russian Civil War turned the peasantry against the Red Army. This gave rise to the "Green Armies"—peasant militias who fought both the "White" monarchists and the "Red" communists to protect their local autonomy.

## Scholarly Debate: Class Consciousness vs. Pragmatism
Historians continue to debate whether the peasantry ever truly "supported" the communist project. Some scholars argue that the peasants were a revolutionary force in their own right, pushing the Bolsheviks toward more radical land policies. Others contend that the peasantry was merely a tool used by the urban intelligentsia, and that the subsequent horrors of Stalinist collectivization in the 1930s were the inevitable result of trying to impose an industrial ideology on a traditional agrarian society.

## Key Facts
* **Demographics:** Approximately 80-85% of the Russian Empire's population were peasants prior to 1917.
* **The Decree on Land:** Issued in October 1917, it abolished private land ownership and handed control to local peasant committees.
* **War Communism:** The early Bolshevik policy of forcibly seizing grain to feed cities and the army, leading to widespread rural famine.
* **The Green Armies:** Independent peasant forces that fought against both the Red and White armies during the Civil War (1917–1923).
* **Collectivization:** The later Stalinist policy that forcibly merged individual peasant farms into state-run *kolkhozy*.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Black Repartition":** This was the term used for the spontaneous, widespread seizure and redistribution of land by peasants across Russia in 1917.
* **The NEP Pivot:** Lenin was forced to introduce the New Economic Policy (NEP) in 1921 specifically to stop peasant uprisings by allowing them to sell surplus grain for profit again.
* **Village Soviets:** While the Bolsheviks claimed to rule through "Soviets" (councils), many rural Soviets remained fiercely independent and ignored orders from Moscow for years.
