# Regional Geography and Infrastructure of the Andean Highlands

## Geography of the Bolivian Highlands
The south-west corner of Bolivia is dominated by the Andean cordilleras, which project east toward the Brazilian highlands. These cordilleras are divided into two great parallel chains, forming the massif of the Andean system. This region consists of parallel ranges that enclose deep ravines and great elevated plateaus. To the north-east of Lake Titicaca, the Nudo de Apolobamba consists of a knot of intersecting lofty ridges containing some of the highest peaks in South America.

The western range, known as the Cordillera Occidental, serves as part of the boundary between Bolivia and the northern provinces of Chile. This range forms the western rampart of the Bolivian tableland, or *alta-planicie*. This tableland extends from the Vilcanota knot in Peru south to the Serrania de Lipez on the Argentine frontier, measuring approximately 500 miles in length and 80 miles in breadth, covering about 40,000 square miles. The plateau has an average elevation of roughly 12,650 feet near Lake Titicaca, though it descends about 1,000 feet toward its southern end.

The northern section of this plateau is referred to as the *puna*, while the southern section is known as the "desert of Lipez," which shares characteristics with the Puna de Atacama. The plateau is described as barren and arid toward the south and bleak and inhospitable in the north, featuring saline depressions that fill with water during the rainy season. The highest peak on the plateau is the Cerro de Tahua, reaching 17,454 feet. Other significant snow-clad peaks overlooking the plateau from the west include Sajama (21,047 ft.), Huallatiri (21,654 ft.), Parinacota (20,918 ft.), Pomarape (20,505 ft.), Lirima (19,128 ft.), and the volcanic peaks of Licancaur (19,685 ft.), San Pedro y Pablo (19,423 ft.), and Oyahua (19,226 ft.).

## Administrative Divisions and Urban Centers
Bolivia is organized into one territory and eight departments, which are further subdivided into 415 cantons, 54 provinces, 232 vice-cantons, 18 missions, and one colony. According to 1900 census data (which included a 5% addition for omissions and estimated wild Indians), the departments and their capitals are as follows:

*   **La Paz**: Capital is La Paz; population 445,616.
*   **Potosi**: Capital is Potosi; population 325,615.
*   **Cochabamba**: Capital is Cochabamba; population 328,163.
*   **Santa Cruz**: Capital is Santa Cruz de la Sierra; population 209,592.
*   **Chuquisaca**: Capital is Sucre; population 204,434.
*   **Tarija**: Capital is Tarija; population 102,887.
*   **Oruro**: Capital is Oruro; population 86,081.
*   **El Beni**: Capital is Trinidad; population 32,180.

The National Territory has a population of 31,883.

## Hydrography and the Region of Cuzco
The Beni is a Bolivian river and tributary of the Madeira. It rises in the Cordilleras near La Paz (where it is initially called the Rio de La Paz) and flows north-east to join the Mamore. The river is a raging torrent through the mountainous districts of central Bolivia. Below Reyes, it passes through open plains and forest-covered hills. The lower river is navigable for 217 miles from Reyes to the Esperanza rapids.

A principal affluent of the Beni is the Madre de Dios (or Mayu-tata), which rises in the eastern Cordilleras approximately 35 miles east of Cuzco. The Madre de Dios flows through northern Bolivia and is considered a more important river than the Beni in terms of size and its tributaries, which include the Paucartambo, Inambari, Tambopata, Marcapata, and Chandless. The Madre de Dios is navigable during the wet season to the foot of the Andes, 180 miles from Cuzco.

In Peru, the department of Ayacucho (renamed from Guamanga in 1825) is situated across the central Peruvian plateau. It is bordered by Cuzco to the east, Ica to the west, and the departments of Apurimac and Huancavelica. This mountainous region was part of the original home of the Incas.

## Transportation and Communications
Bolivia has sought to improve its isolation through international treaties. In 1903, a treaty with Brazil provided for a 180-mile railway around the falls of the Madeira to grant north-eastern Bolivia access to the Amazon; Brazil also provided L2,000,000 for internal Bolivian railway construction. A 1904 treaty with Chile (ratified 1905) involved the construction of a 295-mile railway from Arica to La Paz.

Other communication links include:
*   A railway extension from Jujuy to the Bolivian town of Tupiza, undertaken by Argentina.
*   A 54-mile line from Guaqui on Lake Titicaca to Alto de La Paz, connected to the city of La Paz by a 5-mile electric line. This route provides access to the Peruvian port of Mollendo and is expected to eventually provide railway communication with Cuzco.
*   Prior to 1903, the primary railways were the Antofagasta and Oruro line (574 miles total, with 350 miles in Bolivia), a 26-mile branch to the Pulacayo mines, and the Guaqui to Alto de La Paz line.

By 1907, several lines were projected, including routes from La Paz to Cochabamba and the navigable waters of the Beni, from Viacha to Oruro, from Uyuni to Sucre and Potosi, and from Uyuni to Tupiza.
