# No Sharp Line Between Pathogenic and Non-Pathogenic Schizomycetes

In the study of Schizomycetes, it has been determined that no sharp line can be drawn between organisms classified as pathogenic and those that are non-pathogenic. Modern knowledge of these organisms has progressed through the discovery that virulence or pathogenicity can be increased or diminished via definite treatment or through alterations in the environment during the natural course of epidemics.

## Classification and Evolutionary Perspectives
The difficulty in establishing a strict division extends to the categorization of Schizomycetes as either saprophytes or parasites. A number of species, known as facultative parasites, can function as one or the other depending on the circumstances. 

From an evolutionary standpoint, it is supposed that certain races within a group of bacteria gradually acquired the ability to produce disease and invade body tissues. While some original characters may have changed during the acquisition of pathogenic properties, these changes are often slight and, in many instances, not permanent. Because the characters that distinguish different species can be modified by relatively slight environmental changes, judging species depends on the stability of these characters. Consequently, the microscopical and cultural characteristics of a pathogenic organism may be closely similar to non-pathogenic members of the same group, making it extremely difficult to determine a definitive criterion for differentiation.

## Biological Properties and Toxins
Pathogenic bacteria are not viewed as organisms possessing special features, but rather as members of groups with closely allied characters. Furthermore, many bacteria that have never been observed as pathogenic forms or parasites are capable of producing poisons or toxins. These toxins are the result of their fermentations and decompositions of organic substances, a fact that has led to important results in the application of bacteriology to medicine.

Regarding the nature of these toxins, each has a minimum lethal dose proportionate to the weight of the animal. While some toxins resemble enzymes in their relative instability and conditions of precipitation, it has not been proved that any enzyme separated from a pathogenic bacterium can form toxic bodies from proteids outside the body. However, in the case of diphtheria, toxic albumoses were obtained in the spleen, which some consider the result of the digestive action of an enzyme formed by the bacillus in the membrane and absorbed into the circulation.

## Laboratory Culture and Identification
In bacteriological laboratories, two different temperature standards are typically used for incubation: 18° to 20° C for non-pathogenic organisms and 35° to 38° C for pathogenic forms. In temperate regions during summer or in hot countries, external temperatures often exceed the non-pathogenic standard, which can spoil cultures—particularly gelatine ones—unless cold water is run through the incubator.

To fully describe a bacterium, researchers must account for its biological properties, microscopical characters, growth characters in various culture media, and the effects of inoculation in animals. Various media are used to test specific actions:
* **Sugars (glucose, lactose, saccharose, etc.):** Used to test fermentative action.
* **Litmus:** Added to show changes in reaction.
* **Peptone solution:** Used to test if the bacterium forms indol.
* **Sterilized milk:** Used to determine if the growth curdles the milk.

For pathogenic bacteria, the general principle of preparing culture media is to mimic the natural habitat of the organism, which consists of the natural fluids of the body.

## Immunity and General Utility
The relationship between pathogenic and non-pathogenic organisms is also evident in the study of immunity. While high degrees of specific immunity require the use of a large dose of toxin or a bacterium in a very virulent state, a degree of non-specific immunity or increased tissue resistance can be produced locally (such as in the peritoneum) through injections of non-pathogenic organisms, nucleic acid, or peptone. This non-specific immunity cannot be transferred to another animal.

Despite the focus on pathology, the vast majority of Schizomycetes are not pathogenic. Most are harmless, and many are indispensable to natural operations and human life. They are involved in numerous fermentation processes, including the preparation of tobacco, indigo, ensilage, and hay, as well as the soaking of skins for tanning and rotting. Additionally, some species are capable of carbon-assimilation or fixing free nitrogen.
