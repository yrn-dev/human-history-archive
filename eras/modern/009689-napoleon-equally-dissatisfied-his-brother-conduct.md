# Napoleon and His Brothers

The relationship between Napoleon and his brothers was characterized by a mixture of early support, strategic appointments, and recurring personal and political friction. While Napoleon often utilized his siblings to secure power across Europe, he frequently expressed dissatisfaction with their conduct and personal choices.

## Joseph Bonaparte
Joseph, the eldest brother, was born in 1768. In 1006, Napoleon established him as king in Naples. Despite these elevations, Napoleon was dissatisfied with Joseph's conduct during his tenure as lieutenant-general of France, particularly while Napoleon was conducting the 1814 campaign in eastern France. On March 30 of that year, Joseph empowered Marmont to enter a truce with the assailants of Paris if their strength proved overpowering; upon the capital's surrender, Joseph retired.

During the Hundred Days of 1815, Joseph's role was insignificant. Although Napoleon urged him four days after Waterloo to inspire the Chamber of Deputies toward national resistance, Joseph primarily focused on assisting the emperor's plans to escape to America. Following Napoleon's surrender to the captain of H.M.S. "Bellerophon" at Rochefort, Joseph moved to the United States, settling in Bordentown, New Jersey, under the title of comte de Survilliers. From there, he attempted to organize the rescue of his brother from St Helena.

The two brothers also clashed over the succession of the French throne. Joseph held strict ideas regarding primogeniture and claimed he should be recognized as the heir since neither he nor Napoleon had a male heir. Napoleon, however, preferred the son of Louis Bonaparte. In May 1804, during the proclamation of the French empire, this friction intensified; Napoleon offered to make Joseph king of Lombardy if he waived his claim to the French throne, but Joseph refused.

## Louis Bonaparte
Louis was born in 1778 in Ajaccio. In his youth, Napoleon supervised Louis's education with great care, securing scholarships for him at royal military schools and sharing his own scanty fare with him while stationed at Auxonne. In 1795, Napoleon procured Louis's admission to the military school at Chalons, writing that Louis fulfilled his hopes through his intelligence, talent, kindness, and warmth.

Louis served as Napoleon's aide-de-camp in Egypt (1798-99) and participated in the Italian campaign of 1796-97. In 1802, the First Consul arranged a forced marriage between Louis and Hortense Beauharnais, which led to deplorable results. Louis's career progressed to the rank of general in 1804, and he served as governor of Paris. In 1806, Napoleon set him up as king in Holland.

## Lucien Bonaparte
The relationship between Napoleon and Lucien was marked by deep suspicion and eventual rupture. Lucien once wrote that he detected an ambition in Napoleon that surpassed his love for the general weal, suggesting Napoleon would "ride on the crest of events" during a counter-revolution. This suspicion grew during the consulate (1799-1804). Although Lucien served as minister of the interior, he was removed due to personal and political differences with the First Consul.

Napoleon attempted to soften this blow by appointing Lucien as ambassador to the court of Madrid in November 1800. However, Lucien again displeased his brother. In June 1801, Lucien signed the preliminaries of peace at Badajoz, which frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon. Following this, Lucien resigned and joined the opposition in the Tribunate.

The final breach occurred over Lucien's personal life. Despite an express prohibition from the First Consul—who wanted Lucien to marry the widow of the king of Etruria for reasons of state—Lucien secretly married Mme Jouberthou on October 23, 1803. Upon learning of this, Napoleon ordered Lucien to leave French territory. Lucien departed for Italy, further annoying Napoleon by publicly giving his wife the name of Bonaparte.
