# Turkish Government and Administration

The Turkish government's administrative structure and its relationship with subject populations varied across its territories, characterized by a combination of centralized control from Constantinople and specific local arrangements for religious and ethnic communities.

## Administration in the Balkans and Bulgaria
From 1396 to 1878, Turkish rule in Bulgaria was marked by a feudal system that replaced the previous rule of the boyars. During this period, the regions formerly ruled by Bulgarian tsars—including Thrace and Macedonia—were placed under the administration of a governor-general known as the beylerbey of Rum-ili, who resided in Sofia. Bulgaria proper was further subdivided into the sanjaks of Vidin, Nikopolis, Silistria, Kiustendil, and Sofia.

Under this system, Ottoman chiefs and renegade Bulgarian nobles were granted fiefs called *spahiliks*. The Christian population, referred to as *rayas*, was subject to the tithe on agricultural produce collected by feudal lords and a capitation-tax known as the *haratch*, which was paid to the imperial treasury. While the period was marked by the sacking of monasteries and towns, the Turkish government did not make systematic efforts to extinguish the language or religion of the Christians, nor did it exact military service from them. However, a cruel practice existed where boys between the ages of ten and twelve were requisitioned and sent to Constantinople to serve as recruits for the corps of janissaries.

## Local Administrative Councils and Religious Representation
In eastern Europe, the Turkish government maintained a close connection between political and religious administration. The government recognized the heads of various religious communities as the sole representatives of the Christian population. These religious leaders were granted seats in local administrative councils and were tasked with the supervision of Christian schools.

Within the Orthodox Church, the oecumenical patriarch at Constantinople served as the nominal head. However, the churches of the Greeks, Montenegrins, Servians, and Bulgarians were in reality autocephalous. The Bulgarian church held a unique position; its spiritual chief, the exarch based in Constantinople, controlled Bulgarian prelates both within the kingdom of Bulgaria and in European Turkey. Conversely, Greek prelates located in Bulgaria remained subject to the patriarch.

## Governance in Bagdad
In Bagdad, the Turkish government operated through a pasha who was assisted by a council. While the pasha and higher-ranking officials generally arrived from Constantinople, many other Turkish officials originated from the town of Kerkuk. This official class remained distinct from the native Arab population.

The administration of Bagdad included a court of superior officers to assist the pasha in general provincial administration. For municipal and commercial affairs, a mixed tribunal called a *mejlis* was utilized, which admitted merchants from both the Jewish and Christian communities. Additionally, certain religious heads, such as the Jewish high priest and the *nakib*, held extensive, though undefined, authority within their respective communities. The Jewish high priest was considered the successor to the *resh galutha* or *exilarch* of earlier periods.

## Relations and Reforms
The Turkish government and its officials were noted as being intensely unpopular among the Arabs in Bagdad, a sentiment exacerbated by religious differences, as the Turks were Sunnites and the Arabs were generally Shi'ites.

In later periods, the Turkish government (the Porte) dealt with external pressures for reform. Under the "Mürzsteg programme" of October 9, 1903, the Porte agreed in principle to reforms supervised by Austrian and Russian "civil agents" who worked alongside an inspector-general. This program included the reorganization of the gendarmerie under Italian General de Giorgis in April 1904. These reforms are described as failures due to the insufficient powers of European officials, racial feuds, financial instability, and the tacit opposition of Turkish authorities. Later, in 1905, the Porte resisted the establishment of a financial commission involving representatives from Germany, France, Italy, and Great Britain, only yielding when the fleets of those powers appeared near the Dardanelles.
