# Vinegar

Vinegar is a substance produced through the chemical oxidization of alcohol found in beer or wine. Its production, applications, and historical uses span chemical, industrial, medical, and culinary fields.

## Chemical Production and Process
The production of vinegar depends on the oxidization of alcohol into acetic acid. This chemical process generally occurs in two stages: first, the oxidation of alcohol leads to the formation of water and aldehyde, and subsequently, the aldehyde is further oxidized into acetic acid. If the process continues beyond this, the acetic acid can be oxidized into $\text{OH}_2$ and $\text{CO}_2$. The primary goal of the vinegar-maker is to prevent this final stage from occurring.

These oxidations are driven by the vital activity of several bacteria. Four specific types—*Bacterium aceti*, *B. pasteurianum*, *B. kutzingianum*, and *B. xylinum*—have been thoroughly studied by A. Brown and Hansen. These bacteria form the "mother of vinegar" zoogloea, although other organisms may also be present in this film. While it was once believed that this bacterial film oxidized alcohol by condensing atmospheric oxygen in its interstices, this theory has been abandoned. Current evidence suggests the alcohol is absorbed by the organism and used as a respirable substance, acting as an incomplete combustion depending on the organisms' peculiar respiration.

Further insight into these oxidation fermentations comes from the discovery of "oxydases"—peculiar enzymes found in fungi, bacteria, and the living cells of higher plants that can "carry" oxygen. These oxydases are responsible for the change in taste and color of certain wines when they are exposed to air after being poured from a bottle.

## Industrial and Regional Production
Vinegar is produced as a manufacture in various regions globally. In Germany, the town of Aschersleben in the Prussian province of Saxony has extensive vinegar production, an industry that grew alongside the discovery of coal in the area. In Austria, the town of Krems, located at the confluence of the Danube and the Krems, lists vinegar as one of its primary manufactures, alongside mustard and steel goods.

## Historical and Medicinal Applications
Throughout history, vinegar has been used in various medicinal and practical applications, though some were based on erroneous beliefs.

### Medical and Pharmacological Use
In the pharmacopoeia of the ancients, vinegar was used as a base for outward applications; specifically, kermes triturated with vinegar was applied to wounds of the nerves. 

Popular belief once erroneously suggested that drinking vinegar could remedy obesity and prevent corpulence. An account regarding the marquis of Cortona, a general of the duke of Alva, claims that he used vinegar to reduce his body from a state of enormous obesity to the point where he could fold his skin around himself like a garment.

### Practical and Chemical Use
Vinegar has been utilized for its chemical properties to alter materials. In the process of bronzing, vinegar—along with sal-ammoniac and dilute nitric acid—can produce a green bronze color on metals. To give new bronze articles an antique appearance, a solution of salt of sorrel in vinegar and sal-ammoniac may be brushed onto the metal.

Ancient accounts also attribute remarkable properties to vinegar regarding its ability to break stones and rocks. Pliny referred to it as *Succus rerum domitor*. Similarly, Dion reported that the walls of Eleutheræ were brought down by the force of vinegar during a siege, though some historians question the likelihood of Hannibal procuring enough vinegar in those mountains for such a purpose.

## Culinary Uses
Vinegar serves as a seasoning and preservative in various cultures and cuisines:
* **Japan:** Vinegar is used for seasoning and is a key component in *namasu*, a preparation of raw fish.
* **Great Britain:** The flower-buds of the marsh marigold (*Caltha palustris*), when preserved in salted vinegar, serve as a substitute for capers.
