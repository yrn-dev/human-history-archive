# Historical Records and Inscriptions of the 19th Century

The late 19th century saw a proliferation of scholarly works focused on the documentation of ancient inscriptions, regional surveys, and the formalization of international legal statements. These efforts spanned various geographies, from the ruins of Syria and Mongolia to the diplomatic halls of Cambridge.

## Inscriptions and Surveys of Syria and the Hauran
Research into the region of Syria and the Hauran during the 19th century involved extensive travel and the collection of epigraphic data. Notable contributors include Rev. W. Ewing, who compiled a large collection of inscriptions during his journey in the Hauran, and W. H. Waddington, whose work *Inscriptions of Syria* serves as a primary resource for the region. 

Other significant regional studies include G. Schumacher's works on the Jaulan (1888), *Across the Jordan* (1886), and *Abila, Fella and Northern Ajlun* (1890), published by the Palestine Exploration Fund. The archive also notes the *Palestine Exploration Fund Quarterly Statement* of 1895 as a relevant source. Further explorations were conducted by U. J. Seetzen in his four-volume *Reisen durch Syrien, Palastina, Phonicien, &c.* (1854), Rev. J. L. Porter in *Five Years in Damascus* (1855) and *The Giant Cities of Bashan* (1865), and J. G. Wetzstein in *Reisebericht ueber Hauran und die Trachonen* (1860). Later efforts include the 1901 *Voyage archeologique au Safa et dans le Djabel ed-Druz* by Rene Dussaud and Frederic Macler, as well as a 1900 survey of the Hauran and neighboring regions directed by Dr. Enno Littmann under American auspices, with results beginning publication in 1906.

## Mongol and Uighúr Epigraphy
The study of ancient Mongolian and Uighúr texts has relied heavily on the analysis of inscriptions and archival letters. In the Orkhon region, Professor Vilh. Thomsen of Copenhagen deciphered inscriptions in works published in 1894, while Professor W. Radloff of St. Petersburg produced the *Atlas der Alterthümer der Mongolei* (1892–6) and *Die alttürkischen Inschriften der Mongolei* (1894–7). H. Cordier provided a bibliography for this immense literature in *Etudes Chinoises* (1891–1894), published in Leiden in 1895.

Regarding the capital of the Mongols, Karákorum, Professor Axel Heikel and Professor Pozdneiev concluded that the Buddhist monastery of Erdeni Tso was built upon its ruins. This is supported by a Mongol chronicle stating the monastery was built on the town established by Okkodaï, son of Chinghiz Khan. 

Additionally, French archives preserve letters in the Mongol language using the Uighúr character—the parent of modern Mongol writing. These include a 1289 letter from Arghún Khan of Persia and a 1305 letter from his son Oljaitu to Philip the Fair. Facsimiles of these were published in the *Mém. de l’Acad. des Inscript.* (vols. vii and viii) and J. B. Chabot’s *Hist. de Mar Jabalaha III.* (1895).

## Classical and Old English Inscriptions
In the study of Greek history, inscriptions have been used to clarify financial and political records. For instance, inscriptions prove that the accounts of the Delphian temple were maintained by the Amphiktyons using the Æginæan scale of money. In the region of Olbia, an inscription (No. 2058 Boeckh) recognizes the presence of Μιξέλληνες.

In the field of Old English studies, the "Cædmon's Paraphrase" has been the subject of various editions, with R. Wülker's 1895 re-edition of Grein's *Bibliothek* superseding earlier versions by F. Junius, B. Thorpe, and K.W. Bouterwek. A point of scholarly contention exists regarding an inscription on a top-stone read by Stephens as *Cadmon mae fauaepo* ("Cadmon made me"), though this has been characterized as jargon not belonging to any known Old English dialect.

## International Legal Statements
Beyond archaeology, the year 1895 marked a significant development in international law. During its Cambridge session, the Institute of International Law produced a precise statement of theory regarding the immunity of public ministers. The rules established that public ministers are inviolable and enjoy "exterritoriality." This privilege of inviolability extends to all classes of public ministers representing their sovereign or country, as well as the official staff of a diplomatic mission. Non-official staff also enjoy this privilege, provided that those belonging to the country of residence do so only within the official residence.
