# Lucien Bonaparte

Lucien Bonaparte (1775–1840), the prince of Canino, was born on May 21, 1775, in Ajaccio. He attended the schools of Brienne and Autun alongside his elder brothers. Although he initially desired to join the French army, defective sight prevented this, leading him toward a career in the church. He entered the seminary at Aix in Provence in 1786, but his volatile and excitable nature clashed with the institution's discipline.

## Early Political Career and the French Revolution
Following the outbreak of the Revolution in 1789, Lucien embraced the anti-clerical and democratic movements. Upon returning to Ajaccio, he became a leading speaker for the Jacobin club. He urged his brothers to break ties with Paoli, the leader of the conservative party that sought an alliance with England against the regicide republic of France. Lucien led a Corsican deputation to France to solicit aid for the democrats and denounce Paoli. When the Paolists gained control, the Bonapartes left the island and joined Lucien at Toulon.

In southern France, Lucien continued his work for the Jacobinical cause, adopting the name "Brutus" in the Jacobin club of St Maximin, which had been renamed Marathon. On May 4, 1794, while still a minor and without family consent, he married Mlle Catherine Boyer, an act that resulted in near penury and disgrace. After the *coup d'etat* of Thermidor on July 28, 1794, Lucien fled St Maximin and took a small post at St Chamans. He was imprisoned for a time but was released through the influence of Napoleon, who subsequently secured him a position as a commissioner in the French army in Germany.

## Rise to Power and Conflict with Napoleon
Lucien eventually grew to dislike his military duties, as they offered no opportunity for political intrigue or oratory. He returned to Corsica and, in 1798, refused an appointment in the army of Egypt to pursue election as a deputy. He succeeded in entering the Council of Five Hundred in Paris in 1798.

Despite his early support, Lucien developed suspicions regarding Napoleon's character. In early letters to his brother Joseph, Lucien noted that Napoleon possessed an ambition that surpassed his love for the general weal and suggested that Napoleon might attempt to "ride on the crest of events" in the event of a counter-revolution. By helping Napoleon triumph over parliamentary institutions in France, Lucien inadvertently contributed to an event that was fatal to the cause of democracy, of which he had been an eager exponent.

## Diplomatic and Personal Struggles
During the consulate (1799–1804), relations between the brothers became strained. Lucien served as minister of the interior but was removed due to personal and political differences with the First Consul. To mitigate this, Napoleon appointed him ambassador to the court of Madrid in November 1800. 

While in Spain, Lucien became involved in a dispute regarding the partition of Portugal. On June 6, 1801, at Badajoz, Lucien and the Spanish minister Godoy signed preliminaries of peace with the court of Lisbon, allegedly induced by bribes. This frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon. Following a remonstrance from the First Consul, Lucien resigned and returned to Paris, where he joined the opposition in the Tribunate against Napoleon's schemes.

The final breach between the brothers occurred over Lucien's personal life. After the death of his wife in 1800, Lucien became enamoured with Mme Jouberthou in 1802. Despite an express prohibition from the First Consul—who wanted Lucien to marry the widow of the king of Etruria for reasons of state—Lucien secretly married Mme Jouberthou on October 23, 1803, at Plessis. Upon discovering this, Napoleon ordered Lucien to leave French territory. Lucien departed for Italy with his wife and infant son, publicly giving his wife the name of Bonaparte.

## Later Life and Death
Lucien lived in Italy for several years, primarily in Rome, where he maintained marked hostility toward the emperor. In December 1807, Napoleon offered Lucien a place as a French prince on the condition that he annul his marriage; Lucien refused. He later resided at his estate of Canino, taking the papal title of prince of Canino, before departing for America. During this journey, he was captured by a British ship and taken to Malta and then England, where he remained under surveillance until the peace of 1814.

During the Hundred Days in 1815, Lucien returned to Rome and offered his help to Napoleon. He stood by him at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris and was the final person to defend Napoleon's prerogatives during his second abdication. Lucien spent his remaining years in Italy and died in Rome on June 29, 1840. He is survived by six daughters and four sons. His literary works include the two-volume epic *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree* (1814), *La Verite sur les Cent Jours*, and unfinished *Memoirs*.
