# The Internet: From ARPANET to Global Connectivity

## Overview
The evolution of the internet is not the story of a single "eureka" moment, but rather a decades-long convergence of military strategy, academic collaboration, and commercial ambition. It began during the Cold War as a project of the U.S. Department of Defense’s Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA). The primary goal was to create a resilient communication network that could withstand partial destruction—such as a nuclear strike—by utilizing "packet switching," a method of breaking data into small chunks that could travel independently across various routes to reach their destination.

By the 1970s and 80s, the focus shifted from mere connectivity to standardization. The development of the Transmission Control Protocol and Internet Protocol (TCP/IP) by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn provided a universal "language" that allowed disparate networks to communicate, effectively creating a "network of networks." However, the internet remained the domain of scientists and military personnel until 1989, when Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web at CERN. By introducing HTML, HTTP, and the first web browser, Berners-Lee transformed a complex command-line system into a visual, navigable medium accessible to the general public.

Historians often debate the "inventor" of the internet. While the U.S. government provided the funding and infrastructure, some scholars argue that the conceptual groundwork laid by British and French researchers (such as Donald Davies and Louis Pouzin) regarding packet switching was equally fundamental.

## Key Facts
*   **Packet Switching:** Unlike traditional circuit-switching (used in telephony), packet switching allows multiple users to share the same data path, maximizing efficiency and reliability.
*   **The DNS Revolution:** Before the Domain Name System (DNS) was implemented in 1983, users had to remember numerical IP addresses or maintain a local "hosts" file of every computer on the network.
*   **Commercialization:** The internet remained largely non-commercial until the early 1990s, when the National Science Foundation (NSF) lifted restrictions on commercial traffic.

## Did You Know?
1. **The First Message:** The first message sent over ARPANET in 1969 was supposed to be "LOGIN," but the system crashed after the first two letters, making "LO" the first transmitted data.
2. **The Web $\neq$ The Internet:** The internet is the hardware and protocols (the tracks), while the World Wide Web is a service that runs on top of it (the train).
3. **CERN's Gift:** In 1993, CERN placed the World Wide Web software in the public domain, ensuring it remained free and open for everyone to develop.
