# Barcelona and the House of Austria (1701-1714)

## Historical Context of Barcelona
Barcelona, known in antiquity as Barcino, was traditionally founded in the 3rd century B.C. by the Carthaginian Hamilcar Barca. Following the Roman conquest, the city was named Julia Faventia by Augustus (27 B.C.–A.D. 14) and became a prominent Roman colony and a leading mart of the western Mediterranean. The city's early history included the establishment of a bishopric in 343 and periods as a temporary capital for the Visigoths in 415 and 531. After being captured by the Moors in 713, it passed to the Franks in 801. By 874, the counts of Barcelona ruled as independent monarchs.

The city reached its zenith of wealth and fame following the 1149 union between Catalonia and Aragon. During this era, Barcelona's merchant ships traded as far as the Baltic and North Seas to the west and Alexandria to the east. In 1258, James I of Aragon empowered the city to issue the *Consulado del Mar*, a maritime law code recognized by many European states. Barcelona was also among the first communities to implement marine insurance.

## Political Shifts and Conflict
The commercial supremacy of Barcelona declined after the 1479 union of Castile and Aragon, as the discovery of America in 1492 shifted trade to the ports of western Spain. This led to resentment toward the "Castilian" government and a desire among some citizens for annexation by France. This tension manifested in 1640 when Barcelona became the center of a Catalonian rebellion against Philip IV and sought French protection. Although the city returned to its allegiance in 1652, it was captured by the duke of Vendome in 1697.

Between 1701 and 1714, Barcelona adhered to the house of Austria. This period coincided with the War of Spanish Succession (1702–1713). During this conflict, Leopold died in 1705, leaving the war as an "evil inheritance" to his sons, Joseph I (who died in 1711) and Charles VI.

## The War of Spanish Succession and its Aftermath
The War of Spanish Succession resulted in the aggrandizement of the house of Austria, though not to the full extent hoped for. British and Austrian troops were unable to remove Philip V from the throne. Consequently, the Treaty of Utrecht determined that Spain would remain with the House of Bourbon. In exchange, the house of Austria received Naples, Sardinia, and the Spanish Netherlands.

The settlements of 1713–1714, along with the Treaty of Karlowitz, established a new starting point for Austria. The treaties of Baden, Rastadt, and Utrecht strengthened the Austrian monarchy's position in western Europe, ending the era of French invasions of Germany and establishing Austrian supremacy in Italy.

## Impact on Barcelona's Institutions
The political turmoil of this period had direct consequences for the city's educational infrastructure. The *Universidad Literaria* (University of Barcelona), which had been founded in 1430 by the city's magistracy and confirmed by Pope Nicholas V in 1450, was suppressed in 1714. The university, which had previously consisted of thirty-one chairs and four faculties endowed by the corporation, remained suppressed until its restoration in 1841.
