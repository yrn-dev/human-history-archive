# Society in Imitation of Bolingbroke's Style, but in Refutation of His

The intersection of the literary style and political philosophy of Bolingbroke and Edmund Burke is most prominently illustrated in the work *A Vindication of Natural Society*. Written in 1756, this piece served as Burke's first significant mark in the literary world, utilizing a sophisticated imitation of Bolingbroke's "magnificent" and "aristocratic" prose to deliver a sharp refutation of Bolingbroke's principles.

## The Nature of the Imitation
Bolingbroke was recognized in the 18th century for a style characterized by polish, fine manners, and a "flowing happiness of expression." His eloquence was considered unrivalled by men of all factions, and his written political works were often spirited, dignified, and splendidly eloquent, though frequently described as exceedingly rhetorical. 

In *A Vindication of Natural Society*, Burke adopted this fine style so effectively that the work purported to be a posthumous piece written by Bolingbroke himself. The imitation was so admirable that several acute critics, including Warburton and Chesterfield, initially believed the performance was a serious expression of opinion. Even Rousseau, who had written on the evils of civilization six years prior, likely would have read the text without detecting its irony.

## The Refutation of Principles
Despite the stylistic mimicry, the substance of *A Vindication of Natural Society* was a satire intended as a reduction to absurdity. The text presents a view of the miseries and evils that befall mankind as a result of every species of artificial society. While some have insisted the work was a serious expression of Burke's own views, this is considered incredible.

Burke's refutation was rooted in his belief that civil institutions cannot be safely or wisely measured by the tests of pure reason. He discerned that the rationalism employed by Bolingbroke and the deistic school—which they believed had overthrown revealed religion—was equally capable of undermining the structure of political government. Burke viewed this political rationalism with a "scornful antipathy" that persisted from the beginning of his life until its close. He recognized that the speculative course taken by the rising revolutionary school in France would have viewed his ironical demonstration as a theorem to be proved.

## Contrast in Literary and Personal Character
The archive reveals a stark contrast between the intellectual foundations of Bolingbroke and Burke. Bolingbroke's genius is described as superficial, with abilities exercised upon ephemeral objects rather than universal or lasting ideas. His philosophical essays, composed as amusements in retirement, are characterized as lacking originality and solidity, presenting a "feeble deism" and attacks on theology and metaphysics. Consequently, his writings are viewed as deficient in permanent interest for posterity.

In contrast, Burke's style is described as noble, earnest, and deep-flowing, driven by a lofty sentiment and the "ardent disciplined travail of judgment." While Bolingbroke's work is associated with the "cheerfulness" and "polish" of his social qualities, Burke's writing reflects an anxious conscience and a commitment to imperial themes, the sacredness of law, and the freedom of nations. Burke's work is noted for its ability to rise above immediate subjects to remind the reader of enduring truths of human society and lasting wisdom.

## Bolingbroke's Historical Context
Bolingbroke's influence was most felt by his contemporaries, such as George III and Bute, who derived political ideas from his work *The Patriot King*. His career was marked by high-level diplomacy and political strife, including the signing of the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713—a process for which he is held primarily responsible and which left a stain on national honor due to underhand conspiracies. 

His later life involved periods of exile in France, where he studied philosophy and criticized the chronology of the Bible, earning the admiration of Voltaire. Upon his return to England, he continued to engage in political opposition, notably through a series of letters to the *Craftsman* signed as an "Occasional Writer." Despite his brilliance in oratory and social charm, his lasting impression on posterity is considered far weaker than that of his contemporaries.
