# Africa and Colonial Administration

## British Administration in South Africa
British governance in South Africa involved various administrative shifts and challenges across different eras. In the early 19th century, Sir Benjamin D'Urban served as the governor of Cape Colony starting in January 1834. His four-year tenure was marked by significant events, including the establishment of municipal and legislative councils in the colony, the beginning of the Great Trek, and the abolition of slavery. D'Urban's administration was noted for its justice and firmness, which earned him the cordial support of both Dutch and British colonists.

During this period, D'Urban faced an unprovoked invasion of the colony's eastern borders by Xosa Kaffirs. To protect the eastern province's inhabitants, he erected military posts and extended the colony's boundary to the Kei river, keeping the Xosa under British supervision. However, this extension was opposed by Lord Glenelg, the secretary for the Colonies in the second Melbourne administration. Glenelg, believing the Kaffirs had been victims of systematic injustice, issued a despatch on December 26, 1835, instructing D'Urban to surrender the annexed territory. To implement the home government's policy, Sir Andries Stockenstrom was appointed lieutenant-governor for the eastern provinces.

In a later era, a new prime minister assumed power coinciding with the end of the South African War and the coronation of King Edward VII. This leader faced the task of clearing up post-war affairs both at home and in South Africa. He was supported by a large parliamentary majority and maintained cordial relations with Mr. Chamberlain. In the late autumn, Mr. Chamberlain traveled to South Africa in hopes that his personal influence would assist in the settlement there.

## Bantu History and Linguistic Research
Due to a nearly total absence of historical records, the history of the Bantu people is reconstructed through linguistic research and the study of existing dialects. Evidence suggests that the Bantu negroes left their primal home in the heart of Africa approximately 2,000 years ago. 

A key piece of evidence for this dating is the common root *kuku* (and its variations such as *nkuku*, *ngoko*, *nsusu*, *nguku*, and *nku*) used to describe the domestic fowl. The domestic fowl entered Africa through Egypt during the Persian occupation, no earlier than 500 to 400 B.C. It is estimated that it took several hundred years for the bird to reach the equatorial regions via the Nile valley. Because the *kuku* root is found throughout Bantu Africa—except in rare cases and not among the purest Bantu tribes—it is concluded that the Bantu had the domestic fowl established before leaving their original home. This root resembles several Persian words for "fowl" and is likely derived from the bird's cry. In contrast, Negro races that do not speak Bantu languages use a different name for the fowl, even when living in close proximity to Bantu speakers.

## Other Colonial and International Relations
British colonial administration extended to other regions with varying results. Sir Benjamin D'Urban, prior to his time in South Africa, served as governor of Antigua in 1820 and later in Demerara and Essequibo in 1824. In the latter, he dealt with a rising among slaves caused by the emancipation movement in Great Britain. By 1831, he amalgamated Berbice with other counties to form the colony of British Guiana.

In a broader political context, the spirit that created the Dominions of Australia, Canada, and South Africa is linked to an English tradition that rejects the idea of "subject peoples," a tradition that also informed the three Home Rule Bills for Ireland and the Declaration of Independence.
