# Language and National Claims in Bohemia

In the region of Bohemia, the struggle over linguistic recognition was a central component of the broader conflict between Czech and German populations. This contest involved disputes over the legal status of languages in government administration, education, and the overall political structure of the Austrian monarchy.

## The Concept of Customary Language
Under the constitution of 1867, Article 19 established that all races within the empire possessed an inviolable right to the preservation and use of their own nationality and language. The state recognized the equality of all "customary" (*landesueblich*) languages in public life, offices, and schools. In territories where multiple races resided, educational and public institutions were to be arranged so that each race could receive education in its own language without being compelled to learn a second *Landessprache*.

However, the application of this law granted the government significant power, as the state determined which languages were officially considered "customary." This led to direct conflict in Bohemia. The Czechs claimed that their language should be recognized as customary even in districts that were almost entirely German, such as Reichenberg. Conversely, Germans argued that the Czech language should only be recognized in towns and districts where there was a considerable Czech population.

## Administrative and Legal Language Disputes
The "external service"—communication between private individuals and government offices—historically allowed for the use of the language of the district. Over time, the Czechs sought to formalize and expand this. An 1880 ordinance mandated that business brought before any law court or government office in Bohemia and Moravia be dealt with in the language in which it was introduced, effectively giving Czech a position within the government service. This was further expanded in 1886, when business introduced in Czech was permitted in the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid frequent translations.

In 1897, Minister Badeni published ordinances that furthered these concessions. These ordinances determined that all documents and correspondence regarding any matter brought before government officials in the whole of Bohemia should be conducted in the language of introduction. Additionally, it was decreed that after 1903, no one could be appointed to a government post in Bohemia without passing an examination in Czech. These measures were viewed by Germans as a threat to their interests, as they believed such requirements excluded Germans from various posts.

## Educational Conflicts
Education was a primary point of contention. While the 1867 law required elementary education in Slav districts to be in Czech or Slovenian, the Slavs demanded their own schools even in towns where they were a small minority—a demand granted by Minister Prazak. As the Czech population increased and moved west into manufacturing districts and towns, they continued to claim special schools and the appointment of their own nationality to government offices.

The introduction of Czech into higher education was particularly sensitive. In 1882, Czech classes were started at the university of Prague, which Germans viewed as a desecration of the oldest German university. Germans maintained that German was the *Kultur-sprache* (culture-language) and argued that other languages could not substitute for German as a medium of higher education. In response to these trends, Germans founded the *Deutscher Schulverein* in 1880 to establish and assist German schools.

## Political Demands and the Unity of Bohemia
The linguistic struggle was tied to the political status of the Bohemian kingdom. A significant party, led by Rieger and Palacky, demanded the full restoration of the Bohemian monarchy, including Silesia and Moravia. They insisted that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia at Prague and that Bohemia receive a position in the monarchy similar to that of Hungary. This party was supported by many Czechs and some German-descent nobles, such as Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg and Count Leo Thun.

The Czechs maintained a policy of the "indissoluble unity of the Bohemian kingdom," insisting that German and Czech be recognized as equal and parallel languages throughout the entire territory. Because of this, they rejected proposals to divide the country into German-only and Czech-recognized zones. Even during the administration of Dr. von Koerber, who proposed dividing Bohemia into ten districts (five Czech, three German, and two mixed), the Czechs continued to demand that both languages be placed on an equal footing for all official purposes throughout Bohemia.
