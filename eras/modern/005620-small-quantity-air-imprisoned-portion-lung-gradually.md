# Lung Collapse and Respiratory Mechanics

The lung is the name given to each of a pair of respiratory organs found in humans and other air-breathing animals. Etymologically, the term is derived from a Teutonic root meaning "light," referring to the lightness of the organs. In the evolutionary history of vertebrated land animals, the lung developed as a bag-like growth from the throat, evolving from the swimming bladder of ancestral fish.

## Pulmonary Collapse in Bronchitis
A specific danger associated with bronchitis is the collapse of the lung. This occurs when a branch of a bronchial tube becomes obstructed, plugged up by secretion. When this happens, the specific area of the lung served by that bronchial branch can no longer be inflated during inspiration. 

In this state, the small quantity of air that was imprisoned in that portion of the lung gradually escapes. Because no fresh air is able to enter the obstructed area, the affected part of the lung collapses and takes on a solid consistence. This process results in increased difficulty in breathing. If a large bronchus is plugged, affecting a significant portion of the lung, the result can be rapidly fatal; this risk is particularly high in children. In some instances, the obstruction can be cleared through vigorous coughing, which provides relief.

## Traumatic Lung Collapse
Lung collapse can also occur due to severe chest injuries, such as when a wheel passes over a person. In such cases, ribs may be broken and driven into the lung tissue. This allows air to enter the pleural space, causing the lung to collapse. This condition can lead to death from asphyxia due to the extreme difficulty of breathing. Other symptoms of such injuries include a cough accompanied by the spitting of bright red blood or frothy, blood-stained mucus.

## Comparative Anatomy of Lungs
The structure of the lung varies across different species:

*   **Crossopterygian:** These possess two "lungs" that are thin-walled, smooth, and saccular. The right lung is small, while the left is very large, extending to the hind end of the splanchnocoele.
*   **Dipnoans:** In these animals, the lung is positioned dorsally, close to the vertebral column. In *Protopterus* and *Lepidosiren*, the walls are sacculated or almost spongy to increase the respiratory surface. In *Neoceratodus*, the lung shows an indication of division into two halves via two prominent longitudinal ridges (one ventral and one dorsal).
*   **Birds:** Birds possess highly specialized air-sacs formed by the bulged-out peritoneal or pleural covering of the lungs. These thin-walled membranes are poor in blood vessels and assist in the rapid ventilation of the lungs, which themselves have limited capacity for expansion and contraction.
*   **Amphibians:** Animals such as frogs and newts begin life breathing through gills. As they develop, the lung—developing as a baglike outgrowth from the throat—takes over the breathing process, and the gills eventually dwindle and disappear.

## Evolutionary Adaptation to Air Breathing
The transition from water-breathing to air-breathing required animals to find ways to keep respiratory surfaces moist, as a dry lung would lead to suffocation. This was achieved through the development of breathing organs located deep inside the body, moistened by watery secretions.

Certain mudfish, such as the African lung fish, demonstrate this transition. During rainy seasons, they breathe via gills. During dry seasons, when rivers become ditches of baked mud, they bury themselves and survive by swallowing air into their swimming-bladder. Similarly, the Australian lung fish gulps air at the surface when water becomes foul or deaerated. In reptiles, this adaptation is further advanced; they produce eggs protected by a tough case, and the young breathe using lungs from the moment they hatch, removing the necessity of a water-based life cycle stage.
