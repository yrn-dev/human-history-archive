# German Language and Philology

## The Concept of Kultur-sprache and Educational Conflict
In certain regions, particularly within the context of the Austro-Hungarian administration, the German language was viewed by Germans as the *Kultur-sprache*. This belief led to significant tensions regarding education and national identity. Under the law of 1867, elementary schools in Slav districts were required to provide education in Slovenian or Czech. However, Slav populations demanded that their own schools be provided even in towns where they existed only as a small minority—a demand granted by Prazak, the minister of education under Count Taaffe.

While Germans had hoped that education would lead people to abandon their native tongues, economic factors caused the more rapidly increasing Slav populations to move west into manufacturing districts and towns. Rather than becoming Germanized, these populations spread their own languages and demanded that government officials of their own nationality be appointed to handle their business. This trend occurred in Bohemia, Styria, and Vienna, where a Czech school was established following an increase in the Czech population.

The introduction of Slavonic languages into higher and middle schools was particularly contentious. To illustrate the perceived inadequacy of other languages for higher education, Count A. Auersperg (Anastasius Gruen) once entered the diet of Carniola carrying the entirety of Slovenian literature under his arm to argue that the language could not substitute for German.

## German Philological Research and Linguistics
German scholars have contributed extensively to the study of linguistics and the history of languages. Franz Bopp (1791–1867), a German philologist, focused on the grammatical origins of several languages. Influenced by Karl J. Windischmann and Fr. Schlegel's work on Indian wisdom and the "sacred language of the Hindus," Bopp studied Sanskrit in Paris. In 1816, he published *Uber das Conjugationssystem der Sanskritsprache in Vergleichung mit jenem der griechischen, lateinischen, persischen und germanischen Sprache*, seeking to trace the common origin of the grammatical systems of these languages.

Another significant contribution was made by the author of *Geschichte der deutschen Sprache*, which sought to uncover the national history of the Teutonic tribes hidden within the words of the German language. This work involved collecting allusions from classical writers to determine the relationship between German and the languages of the Scythians, Thracians, and Getae, though many of these remains were corrupted or doubtfully identified.

## Oriental and Byzantine Studies
German academia also produced prominent specialists in non-Germanic languages and cultures. Julius Furst (1805–1873), a German Orientalist born in Posen, studied philology and philosophy in Berlin and oriental literature in Halle, Breslau, and Posen. He served as a professor at the university of Leipzig and authored works such as *Hebraisches und chaldaisches Worterbuch* and *Kultur und Literaturgeschichte der Juden in Asien*. He also edited the *Bibliotheca Judaica* and edited the journal *Der Orient*, which focused on the history, literature, and language of the Jews.

In the field of Byzantine studies, Carl Krumbacher (1856–1909) held a professorship in middle age and modern Greek language and literature at the university of Munich. He authored *Geschichte der byzantinischen Litteratur* and founded the *Byzantinisches Archiv* and *Byzantinische Zeitschrift*. In his work *Das Problem der neugriechischen Schriftsprache*, Krumbacher opposed the efforts of purists who attempted to bring the classical style into modern Greek literature.

## Archaeological and Global Linguistic Discoveries
German excavators have played a role in uncovering early Indo-European linguistic evidence. At Boghaz Keui in Cappadocia, discoveries of treaties from the beginning of the 14th century B.C. between the king of Mitanni and the king of the Hittites appear to contain names from early Aryan mythology, specifically Indra, Varuna, and Mitra. Additionally, a German expedition to Turfan in Turkestan provided data on Tocharish, an Indo-European language that some reports suggest is more closely related to Western groups than to Aryan.

## Social Interactions and Language
The interaction of the German language with other cultures also occurred in unplanned social settings, such as in Siberia. Teuton prisoners from Germany and Austria settled in the great plains and mated with Russian peasant women. In these mixed-nationality households, conflicts occasionally arose between parents of different languages regarding which language should be taught to their children.
