# Scotland and Ireland

## Ancient Kingdoms and Early History
The name Dalriada refers to two ancient Gaelic kingdoms, one located in Ireland and the other in Scotland. The term signifies the home of the descendants of Riada. The Irish Dalriada consisted of the district that currently forms the northern portion of county Antrim. Around A.D. 500, emigrants from this district crossed over to Scotland and established the Scottish kingdom of Dalriada in Argyllshire. While the Scottish kingdom appeared to be dependent upon the Irish Dalriada for a period, King Aidan secured its independence around 575. Later, about 843, one of Aidan's successors named Kenneth became king of the Picts, and the name Dalriada eventually fell into disuse in both Scotland and Ireland.

## Banking and Finance
In Scotland and Ireland, Sir Robert Peel implemented a different policy than the one he established in England. Through acts passed in 1844-1845, banks in these two countries were permitted to exceed their authorized issues, provided they held specie to the amount of the excess. Furthermore, no restrictions were placed on bank amalgamations in these regions. Unlike in England, notes for amounts less than £5 continued to be allowed in Ireland and Scotland. Consequently, the power to issue notes was possessed by ten large banks in Scotland and six of the nine banks in Ireland, a capability that greatly assisted the large proportion of local branches in these countries.

## Agriculture and Fruit Culture
Data provided by the Irish Board of Agriculture regarding acreage under fruit culture up to the end of 1907 indicates specific trends in Ireland compared to Scotland and Wales. In the category of orchard fruit, Ireland had 5,829 statute acres of apples, which is nearly 5,000 acres more than Scotland. Ireland also grew more pears (224 acres) than Scotland. Regarding plums, Ireland grew 223 acres, an amount approximately the same as that grown in Scotland.

In the area of small fruit, Ireland lagged behind Scotland in the culture of raspberries and strawberries. However, Ireland remained very close to Scotland in the culture of gooseberries (675 acres) and currants (black currants at 234 acres; red and white currants at 159 acres). According to the latest available returns, there were over 62,000 holdings in Ireland between 1 and 5 acres, totaling 224,000 acres.

## Geology and Palaeontology
Several notable figures contributed to the geological and palaeontological study of these regions. Sir Richard John Griffith, an Irish geologist born in Dublin, examined mining districts in Scotland, Wales, and England. After studying under Robert Jameson and others in Edinburgh, he returned to Ireland in 1808. Griffith was appointed in 1809 to investigate the nature and improvement of Irish bogs and later produced the first geological map of Ireland in 1815.

William Hellier Baily, an English palaeontologist, was transferred to the Irish branch of the Geological Survey in 1857 as acting palaeontologist, a position he held until his death. He authored notes on fossils for the Geological Survey of Ireland's explanatory memoirs and published *Figures of Characteristic British Fossils, with Descriptive Remarks* between 1867 and 1875.

## Culture and Literature
The vernacular literatures of Ireland and Scotland are categorized separately from English literature (literature written in the English language). In terms of cultural traditions, the "coronach"—a Gaelic word derived from *comh* (with) and *ranach* (wailing)—is a dirge or lamentation for the dead. This practice accompanied funerals in Ireland and the Highlands of Scotland, though in Ireland the more common terms for this practice are "keen" or "keening."

## Genealogy and State Records
Sir John Bernard Burke served as the keeper of the state papers in Ireland starting in 1855 and was appointed Ulster king-at-arms in 1853. He edited and published several genealogical works concerning these regions, including editions of his father's *Dictionary of the Peerages of England, Scotland and Ireland, extinct, dormant and in abeyance*, as well as the *Encyclopaedia of Heraldry, or General Armoury of England, Scotland and Ireland*.
