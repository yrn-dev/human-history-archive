# Fractures

In medical terms, a fracture is the breaking of a bone. These injuries can occur through direct violence, where the bone is broken at the point of impact, or through indirect violence, resulting from a strain applied to the bone. Additionally, fractures can be caused by muscular action, such as a violent cough causing a rib to break. Generally, fractures from direct violence are transverse, while those from indirect violence are more or less oblique.

## Types of Fractures
Fractures are categorized based on the nature of the break and the condition of the surrounding tissue:

*   **Simple and Compound:** A simple fracture is analogous to a subcutaneous laceration in the soft parts. A compound fracture is analogous to an open wound in the soft parts. This open wound may be caused by the initial force (such as a cart-wheel passing over a limb) or by a sharp point of the fractured bone piercing the skin. Compound fractures are higher risk because they allow communication between the external air and the injured bone, which may lead to suppuration, septic inflammation, blood-poisoning, osteomyelitis, or necrosis (the death of large pieces of bone).
*   **Green Stick or Willow Fracture:** In young people, soft bones may simply bend rather than break cleanly across.
*   **Diastasis:** This is a special fracture in growing persons and children where the end of the bone becomes detached from the shaft. This can result in joint stiffness or the arrest of the bone's growth.
*   **Impacted Fracture:** A fracture in which the broken extremities are driven into one another.

## Diagnosis and Identification
In most cases of fracture, *crepitus* can be detected. Crepitus is the feeling elicited when two rough osseous surfaces are rubbed together. It is absent in cases where the bone is merely bent or in impacted fractures.

## Treatment and Recovery
The primary goal of treatment is to secure accurate apposition of the fragments to ensure firm bony union. The process of putting broken ends together is known as "setting the fracture."

### Immobilization and Exercise
Rest is typically obtained through the use of splints, and it is generally advisable to fix the joint above or below the fracture site. In modern practice, simple fractures are not kept in splints for as long; they are frequently "taken down" to allow for gentle movements and massage, which helps swelling and pain disappear more quickly and prevents stiff hands and feet.

In cases where bone splintering into a joint has occurred, particularly if tendons are injured, effusion may enter the joint and tendon sheaths. This can organize into fibrous tissue and cause permanent stiffness, especially in elderly patients. To prevent this, passive movement and gentle exercises are required during the cure. For example, a splint for a fracture near the wrist must be arranged to allow the movement of the thumb and fingers and must be removed daily for exercising the wrist and fingers.

### Surgical Intervention
Surgeons use X-rays to verify that fractured surfaces are in close apposition. If ordinary methods cannot achieve satisfactory adjustment, the surgeon may cut down to the broken ends and fix them with a strong wire suture that remains permanently in the tissues. This immediate wiring is also considered necessary for compound fractures where ends cannot be easily adjusted.

## Complications of Healing
*   **Delayed Union:** This occurs when consolidation takes place very slowly or not at all. If the bones are positioned correctly, treatment involves patience, general health measures, and massage.
*   **Ununited Fracture:** This is a condition where no attempt at consolidation is made by nature after many weeks or months. Causes include the ends not being close enough, constant disturbance of the fracture site, constitutional defects, or the interposition of a tendon or muscle between the ends. Treatment involves cutting down to the ends, sawing off a thin slice to freshen them, and fixing them with a screw or wire. This condition is particularly rebellious in the leg-bones of children.

## Non-Medical Contexts of Fracture
The term "fracture" is also applied in other fields:
*   **Metallurgy:** Cadmium exhibits a distinct fibrous fracture when broken. In industrial metalwork, violent operations like punching and shearing can leave minute cracks that may extend under stress, leading to fracture.
*   **Neurology:** In cases of Jacksonian epilepsy, seizures may be caused by a depressed fracture of the brain, which can be treated by elevation.
*   **History:** In ancient gymnastic combats such as boxing, combatants frequently suffered broken jaws or other considerable fractures.
