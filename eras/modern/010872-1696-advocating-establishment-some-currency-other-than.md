# Currency Advocacy and Monetary Systems

The history of currency is marked by various attempts to establish standards of value, ranging from traditional metallic coins to paper-money and specialized bank currencies. These systems have evolved through legislative mandates, economic crises, and the advocacy of writers and officials seeking to stabilize trade and finance.

## Early Modern Advocacy and Legal Perspectives
In 1696, the English writer John Asgill (1659–1738), who was trained in law, published a pamphlet advocating for the establishment of a currency other than the usual silver and gold. Asgill, who later gained notoriety for a pamphlet interpreting the relationship between God and man through the technical rules of law, also wrote on land title registries in 1698. His professional success in Ireland was partially attributed to the fame of his writings, though he was eventually expelled from both the English and Irish parliaments due to the "blasphemous" nature of his work.

## Metallic Standards and the Gold Standard
Throughout the 18th and 19th centuries, nations fluctuated between single and double standards of currency. England accepted the gold standard by law in 1816, a system that had already been in use during the 18th century. In contrast, the United States (1792) and France (1803) legally established the double standard in the early 19th century.

The mid-19th century saw a shift toward gold. Discoveries of gold in Australia (1851) and California (1848) led to the displacement of silver by gold in France. In the United States, the law of 1834 over-rated gold (1:16) compared to the French ratio (1:15½), which expelled most silver from circulation. By 1865, the Latin Union adopted the French system, and a 1867 international conference in Paris unanimously accepted gold as the standard for a proposed coordinated international system.

## Silver Depreciation and the Double Standard
Economic shifts in the late 19th century challenged the gold standard. The Franco-German War (1870–71) led Germany to establish a gold currency and demonetize its previous silver currencies. Simultaneously, the United States formally established the gold dollar as the standard coin in 1873.

As gold production diminished between 1850 and 1870 and productive silver mines were discovered in the American Pacific states, the gold price of silver fell rapidly. This triggered "Gresham's Law"—the economic principle that "bad money drives out good," where the worst form of currency regulates the value of the whole and drives other forms out of circulation. To combat the resulting import of silver, the Latin Union adopted the "Limping Standard" to limit silver coinage.

By 1876, the depreciation of silver led to the formation of an English parliamentary committee and an American silver commission, with many works advocating for a return to the double standard. Although an international monetary conference in Paris (1878) recognized the need for silver in world money, European states rejected the general use of the double standard. The United States, however, provided for the coinage of silver via the Bland-Allison Act in February 1878.

## Paper Currency and Bank Money
Alternative currencies have emerged in various forms to facilitate trade or address treasury shortages. In China, specifically in Fuchau, private and local promissory notes were used extensively, almost entirely displacing bullion. These notes, issued by hundreds of unregulated banking-houses, ranged from 100 cash to 1,000 dollars. The Imperial Government later attempted to resume a government paper-currency system, but it met with poor success. In Peking, the Regent Sushun issued a vast number of notes through his own banks; his failure to redeem them caused widespread bank failures and public resentment, leading to cheers from defrauded creditors when Sushun was executed on November 8, 1861.

In Europe, the Bank of Hamburg utilized "bank money"—a money of account that could be transferred between holders. This acted as an international coinage, allowing merchants to avoid the expense and delay of verifying various foreign coins that differed in weight, purity, and intrinsic value. The "Mark Banco" served as a representative of an equal value of silver.

## Regional Reforms and Global Visions
In Austria-Hungary, currency reform was a long-delayed task. The system had consisted of silver (gulden) and inconvertible paper notes issued by the Austro-Hungarian Bank. During the war of 1866, the government violated the bank's charter by issuing its own notes (*Staatsnoten*) totaling 312 million gulden. A reform operation begun in 1892 sought to redeem these notes, resume cash payments, and substitute a gold standard for the silver standard.

In Japan, historical currency forms included Chinese coins and the use of cloth as currency. Additionally, the use of copper for Buddha images eventually exhausted the copper currency.

Looking toward the future, some historians have suggested that the fusion of global economic affairs will inevitably lead to a common control of currency, requiring a world authority with significant powers of enforcement to ensure the free movement of goods and people.
