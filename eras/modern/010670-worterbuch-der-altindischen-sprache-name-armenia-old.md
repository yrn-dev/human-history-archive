# The Term Aryan and Related Philology

## Etymology and Usage of "Aryan"
The term "Aryan" is widely known to the general public through the works of Max Muller on comparative philology. Based on early Sanskrit writings, the word was utilized as a national name in India, as well as in Persia and Bactria. The term appears in various forms: *arya-* and *arya* in Sanskrit, *airya-* in Zend, and *ariya-* in Old Persian.

There is scholarly debate regarding the specific root of the word. Max Muller suggests a connection to *ira*, a Sanskrit word for earth, though this assertion is considered far from certain. Spiegel notes that while the word can be connected to a root *ar-*, there are several roots of that form with different meanings, and no certain criterion exists to determine the correct relationship.

## Linguistic Distinctions and Connections
Within the Sanskrit language, it is probable that two distinct words must be identified. The first is *arya*, which is the origin of the term "Aryan" and the source of the usual derivative *arya*. The second is *arya*, which appears frequently in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet used for deities. Additionally, the form *aryas* may be interpreted as the genitive of *ari*, which is explained as meaning "pious, devoted, active."

Regarding connections to other languages, the possibility that the word is related to *Erin* (Ireland)—a connection sought by Pictet—is regarded as impossible. However, the word may share an origin with the *Ario-* found in Celtic and Germanic names, such as *Ariovistus*, as noted in Uhlenbeck's *Kurzgefasstes etymologisches Worterbuch der altindischen Sprache*. The name of Armenia, appearing in Old Persian as *Armina-*, has often been connected to this group, but its origin remains uncertain.

## Indo-European Context and Discoveries
The process by which the Aryan languages separated from other Indo-European languages is not yet fully understood. Efforts to identify unknown languages in cuneiform inscriptions with this group have seen limited success until more recent discoveries. 

German excavators at Boghaz Keui (identified as Pteria in Cappadocia) discovered treaties from the beginning of the 14th century B.C. between the king of the Hittites and the king of Mitanni. These treaties appear almost certainly to contain the names Indra, Varuna, and Mitra, which are gods belonging to early Aryan mythology. Further information is expected from the analysis of collections from the German expedition to Turfan in Turkestan. This includes the study of Tocharish, an Indo-European language that may be more closely related to Western groups than to Aryan.

## Notable Scholars of Indian Philology
Several 19th-century scholars contributed significantly to the field of Indian and comparative philology. Otto von Bohtlingk (1815–1904), a German Sanskrit scholar, produced a *magnum opus* in the form of a seven-volume *Sanskrit-Worterbuch* (published between 1853 and 1889), completed over twenty-three years with the help of Albrecht Weber and Rudolf Roth. Bohtlingk also published *Vopadevas Grammatik* (1847), a treatise on Sanskrit accents (*Uber den Accent im Sanskrit*, 1843), and translations of the *Brihadaranyaka-upanishad* and *Chhandogya-upanishad* (1889). His earlier work included an edition of Panini's *Acht Bucher grammatischer Regeln* (1839–1840), which served as a criticism of the philological methods used by Franz Bopp.
