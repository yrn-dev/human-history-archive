# Juan

## Spanish Colonial and Military Figures
In the mid-16th century, a man named Aviles entered into an *asiento* (contract) with Philip II on March 20, 1565. Under this agreement, Aviles received 15,000 ducats to establish a colony in Florida. Following his release from a prior engagement, Aviles prepared to sail to the Bermudas to search for his son, Juan, who had been shipwrecked the previous year. Aviles eventually established a fort at the Bay of St Augustine and captured Fort Caroline from the French Huguenots. He remained in Florida until 1567, made one subsequent voyage to the region, and died on September 17, 1574.

Another figure of rank was Juan Duque de Estrada, a soldier and the father of Diego Duque de Estrada. Diego, born in Toledo on August 15, 1589, later became a memoir writer and soldier who served in the Thirty Years' War and the order of San Juan de Dios.

## Don Juan in History and Legend
The name Don Juan is associated with both historical figures and a widespread European legend. Historically, a Don Juan was appointed by Philip II as the Spanish governor in Belgium. The son of Charles V and a victor in the 1571 naval battle of Lepanto, this Don Juan lacked sympathy for the Belgians and struggled with the task of negotiating with rebels. His occupation of the fortress of Namur led the States-General, under the influence of William the Silent, to declare him a traitor.

In literature, Don Juan is a legendary character representing arrogant blasphemy and perverse sensuality. The character was introduced into formal literature in the Spanish play *El Burlador de Sevilla y convidado de piedra*, printed in Barcelona in 1630 and usually attributed to Tirso de Molina. The plot involves a profligate man who invites a dead man to supper, only to have the invitation accepted. This universal type appears in various forms across Europe, including the French *Le Souper de fantome* and versions found in the Azores and Iceland. The legend was later adapted by Moliere in *Le Festin de pierre* (1665) and famously set to music by Mozart in *Don Giovanni*.

## Literary Works
The poet Lord Byron authored a major work titled *Don Juan*, which he began in September 1817. Byron described the poem as being "meant to be a little quietly facetious about everything." Rather than following a strict plan, Byron utilized "materials" for the work, including books such as de Castelnau's *Histoire de la nouvelle Russie* and Dalzell's *Shipwrecks and Disasters by Sea*. 

The poem was published in stages: Cantos I and II appeared on July 15, 1819; Cantos III, IV, and V were published on August 8, 1821; and Cantos VI through XVI were released between July 15, 1823, and March 26, 1824. Canto XVII remained unfinished at the time of Byron's death, with a fragment of fourteen stanzas discovered in his room at Missolonghi and published in 1903. The work is regarded as Byron's masterpiece and a "hymn to the earth," utilizing a style described as humorous-realistic to analyze human nature and depict life as a whole.
