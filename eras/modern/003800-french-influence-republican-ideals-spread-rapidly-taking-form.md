# French Republicanism and Its Expansion

## The Rise of Republican Sentiment in France
The transition of the French mind toward republicanism was catalyzed by a loss of trust in the monarchy. While abstract republican sentiment existed previously, there was initially little expressed desire to abolish the monarchy. This changed following the flight to Varennes, an event that led the French people to realize the king was dangerous and could not be trusted. In the wake of this event, the Jacobins—the extreme republican party—grew rapidly in strength. Their leaders, including Marat, Danton, and Robespierre, who had previously been viewed as a group of "impossibles" on the extreme left, began to dominate the National Assembly.

## The Republican Flame and Military Expansion
A subsequent phase in French history was characterized by a "great flame of enthusiasm" for the Republic and France. This period was marked by a refusal to compromise; domestically, the state sought to stamp out royalists and all forms of disloyalty. Internationally, France positioned itself as the helper and protector of all revolutionaries, with the ambition that the entire world and all of Europe would become republican.

This enthusiasm manifested in the youth of France pouring into Republican armies. The period was further defined by the spread of the *Marseillaise*, a song described as warming the blood like wine. Supported by this chant and the use of enthusiastically served guns and leaping columns of bayonets, French armies pushed foreign forces back. By the end of 1792, these armies had surpassed the achievements of Louis XIV, occupying foreign soil in several locations, including:
* Brussels
* Savoy
* Mayence (via raids)
* The Scheldt (seized from Holland)

## Naval Limitations and Conflict with England
Despite the success of the infantry and artillery—which had been released from cramping conditions and aristocratic officers—the French government faced significant naval challenges. The revolution had destroyed the discipline of the navy, leaving the English supreme at sea.

In an unwise move, the French government declared war against England, provoked by the expulsion of a French representative from England following the execution of Louis. This action had two primary negative effects: it united all of England against France and destroyed a considerable liberal movement in Great Britain that had previously been sympathetic to the revolution, thereby robbing France of its only prospective ally.

## Republicanism and Democracy in Other Regions
While France pursued its republican vision, other nations developed their own democratic and republican institutions. In the United States, the Democratic-Republican party was organized by Thomas Jefferson in opposition to the Federalists, who were dominated by Alexander Hamilton. The party's origins lay in a repugnance to social and political aristocracy and the principles of local self-government. Jefferson's followers adopted the name "Republicans" specifically to distinguish themselves from monarchists, viewing the Federalists as a monarchist "court party." The Democratic-Republicans were responsible for attaching a democratic bill of rights to the constitution and held control of the government (excluding the judiciary) from 1801 until 1825.

In other parts of Europe, the influence of republicanism and democracy varied. In Greece, following an insurrection against the Turks beginning in 1821, joint action by Russia, France, and Britain eventually led to Greek freedom via the treaty of Adrianople in 1829. However, Greece was not permitted to resume its ancient republican traditions; instead, a German king, Prince Otto of Bavaria, was installed.
