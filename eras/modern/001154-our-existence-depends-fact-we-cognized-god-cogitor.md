# Cognition by God and Human Existence

The concept that human existence and consciousness are dependent upon being cognized by God is a central theme in certain philosophical and theosophical traditions. This perspective posits that the act of divine cognition is the primary cause of human being and self-awareness.

## The Philosophy of Baader
In the thought of Baader, the relationship between God and human existence is defined by the principle *cogitor ergo cogito et sum*—the idea that human existence depends on the fact that we are cognized by God. According to Baader, all human self-consciousness is simultaneously God-consciousness. He argues that human knowledge is never mere *scientia* (simple knowledge), but is invariably *con-scientia*, which is described as a participation in, or a consciousness of, God.

Baader's approach is characterized as a theosophy and a form of Scholasticism, as he believes that reason should be used to clarify truths provided by revelation, authority, and tradition, rather than attempting to reach its goals through reason alone. In this system, God is not viewed as an abstract Being (*substantia*) but as an everlasting activity or process (*actus*). This process of self-generation involves an immanent aspect where God's primitive will becomes spirit by becoming conscious of itself. This divine self-cognition involves a distinction between the knower and the known, a process that is mirrored through the eternal wisdom or idea of God.

## Cartesian and Malebranchian Perspectives
Other philosophical frameworks explore the dependency of the conscious being on a permanent cause. In the system of Descartes, the formal act of thinking constitutes human being, but the concrete contents of this thought lead back to a necessary pre-supposition: an infinite, perfect, and all-powerful being. Descartes identifies this being as God, the sole author from whom all things depend. In this view, God is not only the creator of the material universe but the father of all truth in the intellectual world; the laws of thought and truths of number are regarded as the decrees of God.

Malebranche, expanding on these ideas, suggests that all things are known through the idea of God and exist through His existence. This line of reasoning leads to the theoretical possibility that human consciousness of God is merely a part of God's own consciousness of Himself, and that human consciousness of self and other things is actually God's consciousness of them. Under this interpretation, there would be no existence for humans or other things except within this divine consciousness.

However, Malebranche diverges from this total dependency regarding self-knowledge. He asserts that humans are conscious of their own existence through an "inner sense" (*sentiment interieur*), which allows them to know *that* they are, even if they do not know *what* they are. While he maintains that external things are known only through their ideas in God, he treats material things as having an existence apart from divine thought, resulting from the free decrees of God's arbitrary will.

## Concepts of Immanence and Divine Relation
The relationship between the Infinite and the finite is often discussed through the concept of immanence. Immanence refers to the theological conception of God existing in and throughout the created world, as opposed to Deism, which views God as separate and above the universe. 

Religious thinkers have utilized the doctrine of immanence to formulate a close relationship between God and man. Some views, such as the "New Theology" of Rev. R. J. Campbell, postulate a divine element within human beings to bridge the gap between the finite and the infinite. This contrasts with views that see the world as a non-necessary emanation of divinity, where God remains perfectly self-sufficient and the existence of creatures depends entirely on His free will.
