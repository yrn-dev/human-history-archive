# Continental Divides and Mountain Systems of Asia

The geography of Asia is characterized by a complex system of elevations and depressions that dictate the continent's hydrography and regional divisions. A primary feature is the great central water-parting, or divide, which stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and Elburz, continuing to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, and extending finally to the Bering Strait in the extreme north-east.

## The Great Continental Divide
This extensive divide is not consistently marked by steep, well-defined ranges. In various areas, the axis of the main ranges runs transverse to the water-parting, resulting in intermediate highlands that create lacustrine regions with indefinite watersheds. While certain ranges—including the Altai, Khangai, Tian-shan, and Hindu Kush—reach great heights, a considerable portion of this great divide remains below 5000 ft. in altitude.

## The Central Depression and Southern Barriers
South of the continental divide, the land drops into a central depression. This vast, almost waterless interior space begins east of the Gobi desert and extends westwards through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. The level of this depression, which was once a vast inland sea, generally does not exceed 2000 ft. above sea level; research suggests that in central portions of the Gobi, such as near Lop Nor, it may actually be below sea level. To the west of Kashgar, this depression is limited by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol, of which the Sarikol serves as the eastern face.

Further south of this enclosed depression lies a second hydrographic barrier. This barrier is described as one of the world's mightiest upheavals, separating the central depression from the low plains of India, Siam, China, and the Amur.

## Mountain Belts of Central Asia
A mountainous belt extends from the 115th meridian at the western end of the Yablonoi range to the extremity of the Pamir. This belt is broken into several branches that enclose isolated drainage areas and numerous lakes. Key ranges within this system include:

*   **Tian-shan (Celestial Mountains):** Located between 40° and 43° N and 75° and 95° E, these mountains form the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. Some summits are said to exceed 20,000 ft. The region's few towns and principal cultivated districts are situated along the foot of this range.
*   **Ala-tau:** A prolongation of the Tian-shan that flanks the Syr to the north, rising to altitudes of 14,000 or 15,000 ft. It acts as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes, the latter of which has an elevation of approximately 5000 ft.
*   **Altai:** Situated near the 50th parallel and rising to 10,000 or 12,000 ft., this range separates the waters of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria from the great rivers of western Siberia.

## Low-Altitude Divides and Regional Connections
West of the Altai, a line of elevation continues toward the Ural Mountains. This specific section does not rise to considerable altitudes, but it serves a critical hydrographic function by dividing the drainage of south-west Siberia from the great plains located north east of the Aral Sea.

When viewing the continent in three broad sections—a northern section (average altitude under 5000 ft.), a central depression (average level not exceeding 2000 ft.), and a southern section (comprising lowlands and high table-lands of 15,000 to 16,000 ft.)—there is only one direct connection between the northern and southern sections that lacks formidable barriers or mountain passes. This connection is found in the narrow valley of the Hari Rud to the west of Herat. Between Karachi and the Caspian, the only significant orographic obstacle is the divide separating the Helmund hamun basin from the valley of the Hari Rud, which stands at approximately 4000 ft. above sea level.
