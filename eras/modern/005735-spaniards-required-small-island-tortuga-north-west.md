# The Rise of the Buccaneers and the Struggle for Tortuga

## Origins in Santo Domingo
The emergence of the buccaneers was rooted in the colonial conditions of the West Indies during the early 16th century. The island of Santo Domingo had been largely depopulated due to the oppressive policies of Spain. Along the coast, isolated establishments were managed by Spaniards who, restricted by monopolies imposed by their mother country, lacked a convenient market for their produce. This led them to welcome and purchase cargoes from French, Dutch, and English vessels.

The depopulated landscape of the island became home to immense herds of wild cattle. Smugglers began to use Santo Domingo for provisioning, utilizing a method of preserving meat called "boucanning," which they learned from the remaining natives at small establishments known as *boucans*. This gave rise to an extensive and illicit butcher trade.

## The Buccaneer Identity
The combination of Spanish monopolies and the allure of a roving life created a class of men known as buccaneers. These individuals functioned alternately as hunters, sailors, and occasionally planters. They were characterized as bold, unscrupulous, and often savage, possessing an intense detestation of Spain. This hatred was fueled by the Spanish refusal to recognize the right of other races to trade or establish settlements in the West Indies.

Because of this Spanish stance, the governments of England, France, and Holland did not control their subjects who invaded the islands, leaving them to establish settlements at their own risk. Early efforts included simultaneous plantings of English and French colonists on the island of St Kitts (or St Christopher). However, tensions between the English and French persisted, and in 1629, a Spanish fleet of thirty-nine sail surprised and partly dispersed the remaining colonists. Following the fleet's departure, the scattered bands returned and found further encouragement among their countrymen in Santo Domingo.

## Conflict over Tortuga
The island of Tortuga became a focal point of conflict between the buccaneers and the Spanish. For a period, Spain was unable to take immediate action against the settlers there. However, eight years later, the Spaniards launched an attack on Tortuga while many buccaneers were away on the larger island, massacring every settler they could seize.

Despite this, the settlers returned, and the buccaneers entered a state of open hostility toward Spanish arms. They began recruiting from every European trading nation, and for seventy-five years, they served as the scourge of Spanish dominions and trade in the Americas.

## International Intervention and the Role of France
France actively sought to expand its influence in the region. The French government appointed a "Governor-General for the French West India Islands" based in St Kitts. In 1641, this governor took possession of Tortuga and expelled the English from the island; he attempted a similar expulsion in Santo Domingo, though with less success. During this period, England was preoccupied with its own Civil War, forcing the buccaneers to maintain themselves primarily at sea.

The control of Tortuga shifted several times. In 1654, the Spaniards regained the island from the French, but it fell back into French hands six years later. These transitions were consistently supported by the roving community of buccaneers.

## The Conquest of Jamaica and Mercenary Activity
The instability of Tortuga led the buccaneers to seek a more secure refuge, which they found in 1655 when the navy of the English Commonwealth conquered Jamaica. The buccaneers played a significant role in this conquest, just as they had assisted the French in the taking and re-taking of Tortuga.

The buccaneers essentially functioned as a mercenary navy. They were available for employment by any nation willing to act against the power of Spain, provided they were allowed to share in the plunder. They were highly regarded for their extraordinary skill in seamanship, as well as their daring and cruelty.
