# Ashanti

## Annexation and Administration
By an order in council dated September 26, 1901, Ashanti was formally annexed to the British dominions. Under this order, which served as the first constitution granted to the region by the British, Ashanti was given a separate administration under the control of the governor of the Gold Coast. In the governor's absence, a chief commissioner represents him, supported by a staff of four commissioners and four assistant commissioners. Military security is provided by a battalion of the Gold Coast regiment, which is headquartered at Kumasi.

The 1901 order in council stipulates that when the governor issues ordinances regarding the raising of revenue, the administration of justice, or other matters, he must respect native laws regulating the civil relations of populations, tribes, or chiefs, provided these laws are not clearly injurious to the welfare of the natives or incompatible with British sovereignty.

Following the annexation, relations between the governed and the governing power improved. By 1906, Mr. F.C. Fuller, who became chief commissioner in early 1905 succeeding Sir Donald Stewart, reported that suspicion regarding the ulterior motives of the "white man" was quickly diminishing. This confidence was bolstered by the maintenance of the tribal system and support for lawful chiefs, as well as a preference among the natives to use British criminal and civil courts due to their impartial treatment.

## Origins and Social Structure
Tradition suggests the Ashanti originated from bands of fugitives driven south in the 16th or 17th century by Moslem tribes migrating from the Senegal and Niger countries. After settling in a region of impenetrable forest, they developed a national character of valour, becoming a powerful, conquering nation. They are of the pure negro type and are believed to be of the same race and language as the Fanti. One theory suggests the two groups separated during a famine; the Fanti moved south and lived on herbs (*fan*), while the Ashanti subsisted on Indian corn (*san*), leading to their respective names.

The Ashanti are composed of numerous tribes, with twelve being distinguishable: the Adansi, Bekwai, Juabin, Kokofu, Kumasi, Mampon, Nsuta, Nkwanta, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Ofinsu, and Adjisu. While each tribe has its own king, the king of Kumasi has been recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti since the beginning of the 18th century. The symbol of this authority is the "golden stool." Following the deposition of Prempeh in 1896, no king of Kumasi was chosen, and Prempeh was never "enstooled."

Historically, the government was a blend of military aristocracy and monarchy. The state's social fabric was based on six great divisions or clans organized for war. The King's Council consisted of the chiefs of these clans and certain sub-chiefs with hereditary rights; this council often exercised more power than the king. Land is held commonly by tribes, with unallotted "stool lands" attached to the king or head chief. Polygamy is practiced by those who can afford it.

## History and Conflict
The Ashanti rose to power under Osai Tutu, who extended the empire through conquests to the east and west, subdued Banna and Gaman (Mahommedan countries), and either built or expanded Kumasi. His successor, Osai Apoko, made further acquisitions toward the coast. In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina became king. In 1807, after the Fanti refused to surrender two Assin chiefs, the Ashanti invaded Fantiland, reaching the coast near Anamabo. While they failed to storm the English fort, they destroyed the town and killed 8,000 inhabitants. Governor Colonel G. Torrane eventually concluded an agreement acknowledging the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland and paying rent for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

Later conflicts occurred in the 19th century. In 1873, an Ashanti force invaded the British protectorate after the British took over Elmina fort from the Dutch in 1872—an act the Ashanti resented as they considered Elmina their subjects. This led to an expedition by Sir Garnet Wolseley, whose forces blew up the red sandstone king's palace in Kumasi on February 6, 1874.

More recently, a revolt in 1900 was suppressed by Colonel Willcocks. His force included 134 British officers, 35 British non-commissioned officers, and 3,368 natives (including Sikhs from Central Africa and Yaos), supplemented by Ashanti levies. The rebels were beaten at Obassa on September 30, and the final two remaining rebel chiefs were captured on December 28. Peace was restored at the start of 1901, with total campaign casualties (including disease) numbering 1,007.

## Economy and Infrastructure
Kumasi serves as the distributing centre for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti. Trade between Kumasi and Cape Coast was improved following the 1900 revolt. A railway from Sekondi to Kumasi, completed in 1903, passes through gold-bearing regions.

The economy is based on several key resources:
*   **Agriculture:** The rubber tree (*Funtumia elastica*) is the most important commercial vegetable product. Kola nuts are in high demand, and cocoa plantations were established in eastern districts starting in 1905. Tobacco is grown in the north.
*   **Mining:** Gold is found in western districts. Between 1895 and 1901, several companies were formed to work these mines, with the Obuassi mines providing most of the gold exported from the Gold Coast after 1902. In 1905, gold output was 68,259 oz, valued at L254,790.
*   **Other Exports:** Gum copal is exported.

Ashanti maintains trade with the Niger countries to the north and with German and French neighbours. Revenue for the administration is derived from liquor licences, caravan taxes, contributions from gold-mining companies, and rents from government land.
