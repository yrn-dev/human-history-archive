# The Barrier Treaties

The Barrier Treaties were a series of diplomatic agreements between Great Britain and the states-general of the United Netherlands designed to secure Holland against aggression from France. These treaties focused on the establishment of a "barrier" of fortified towns and strong places on the side of the Netherlands.

## The First Barrier Treaty (1709)
Signed on October 29, 1709, the first Barrier Treaty established a reciprocal arrangement between Great Britain and the United Netherlands. Under the terms of this agreement, the states-general of the United Netherlands engaged to guarantee the Protestant succession in England in favor of the house of Hanover. In exchange, Great Britain undertook to procure an adequate barrier for the Dutch. 

This barrier consisted of the following towns:
* Furnes, Nieuport, Ypres, Menin, Lille, Tournai, Conde, Valenciennes, Maubeuge, Charleroi, Namur, Halle, Damme, Dendermond, and the citadel of Ghent.

The principle of this treaty—securing Holland through the garrisoning of frontier fortresses by Dutch troops—was based on the same logic that had inspired the treaty of Ryswick in 1698.

## Subsequent Treaties and Modifications
A second Barrier Treaty was signed on January 29, 1713. This agreement reduced the number of strong places designated for the barrier. The revised list included:
* Furnes, the fort of Knocke, Ypres, Menin, Tournai, Mons, Charleroi, the citadel of Ghent, and certain fortresses located in the neighborhood of Bruges and the citadel of Ghent.

Under this second treaty, Great Britain undertook to obtain the right for the Dutch to garrison these locations from the future sovereign of the Spanish Netherlands. These terms were later included in the treaty of Rastatt, signed on March 7, 1714, between France and the emperor. A third Barrier Treaty followed in November 1715.

## Decline of the Barrier System
The obligation to maintain these foreign garrisons in Belgium was viewed as humiliating and drastic. Empress Maria Theresa, who became the sovereign of the Austrian Netherlands via the "Pragmatic Sanction," attempted to avoid the obligations of the Barrier by withholding payments due to the foreign garrisons.

The system was eventually ended by her son, Emperor Joseph II. He ordered the demolition of the fortresses on Belgian soil that were still occupied by the Dutch. Joseph II also attempted to secure the complete opening of the Scheldt, which the Dutch had kept closed. Despite diplomatic negotiations beginning in 1784, the United Provinces—supported by France—opposed the move. Joseph II attempted a practical stratagem by ordering vessels to sail from Antwerp to the sea and from Ostend to Antwerp, hoping the Dutch would not fire. However, the Dutch did fire upon the vessels, forcing them to withdraw. To avoid war, Joseph II did not pursue the matter further, and the Scheldt remained closed.
