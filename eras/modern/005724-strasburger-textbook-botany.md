# Strasburger's Textbook of Botany

The materials attributed to Strasburger's *Textbook of Botany* (and its German counterpart, *Lehrbuch der Botanik*) provide detailed anatomical and morphological observations across various plant groups, ranging from primitive liverworts to the complex fruit structures of higher plants.

## Liverworts and Jungermanniales
The archive contains significant data on the *Jungermanniales*, a large series of liverworts characterized by a wide variety in the organization of their sexual generation. This group is divided into two primary categories based on the growth of the branch in relation to the formation of archegonia:

*   **Anacrogynous Jungermanniaceae**: Considered the more primitive group, these plants do not utilize the apex for archegonia formation. Consequently, the branch continues to grow after the archegonia are formed, leaving the archegonia and subsequent sporogonia on the leafy plant or the dorsal surface of the thallus.
*   **Acrogynous Jungermanniaceae**: These plants are entirely foliose. In this group, the archegonia are located at the ends of the branches or the main shoot.

General characteristics of the *Jungermanniales* include globular, long-stalked antheridia and capsules that open by splitting into four halves. The Acrogynous Jungermanniaceae specifically occur in diverse environments, including on stones, rocks, tree trunks, the ground, and on leaves within damp tropics. While they typically form green tufts, some forms exhibit a reddish tint.

Specific examples of related flora include *Marchantia polymorpha*, which features air-chambers containing filaments of assimilating cells and stoma, and *Blasia pusilla*, where the thallus margin bears leaf-like lobes and rhizoids. Additionally, *Anthoceros laevis* is noted for its sporogonium and columella.

## Fruit Morphology and Classification
A substantial portion of the records focuses on the formation and classification of fruits. Fruits are categorized based on the number of flowers involved in their production:

*   **Simple Fruits**: These are formed from the ovary of a single flower. They may consist of one or several carpels, which can be either combined or separate.
*   **Multiple, Confluent, or Anthocarpous Fruits**: These result from the ovaries of several flowers uniting. In these cases, floral envelopes and bracts are often joined into a single mass. An example is the pine-apple (*Ananassa sativa*), which develops from a spike of numerous flowers with bracts; its crown consists of empty bracts that extend beyond the fruit.

Within simple fruits, further distinctions are made based on the carpels:
*   **Apocarpous**: Composed of one mature carpel or several separate, free carpels.
*   **Syncarpous**: Composed of several carpels that are more or less completely united.

## Specialized Fruit Types and Examples
The archive details various specific fruit structures and their mechanisms of opening or composition:

*   **Capsules and Pods**: The siliqua of the Wallflower (*Cheiranthus Cheiri*) opens from the base upwards via two valves, leaving seeds attached to a dissepiment supported by the replum. The silicula of the shepherd's purse (*Capsella*) is described as angustiseptal due to its narrow partition, and it opens from the top downwards via two folded valves. Other examples include the capsule of the orchid *Xylobium* and the seed-vessel of *Anagallisarvensis*, which opens through circumscissile dehiscence.
*   **Winged and Dry Fruits**: The samara of the Ash (*Fraxinus*) is a winged fruit consisting of two cells, one of which is enlarged and contains a seed while the other is abortive. The fruit of the Common Sycamore (*Acer Pseudoplatanus*) divides into two mericarps. The lomentum of *Hedysarum* separates transversely into single-seeded mericarps when ripe.
*   **Fleshy and Composite Fruits**: The strawberry (*Fragaria vesca*) consists of an enlarged succulent receptacle with small dry achenes on its surface. The cherry (*Prunus Cerasus*) is illustrated by its epicarp, mesocarp, and endocarp. The rose fruit features a fleshy hollowed receptacle and persistent sepals. The fig (*Ficus Carica*) possesses a peduncle ending in a hollow receptacle that encloses numerous female and male flowers.
