# Belize and British Honduras

Belize, also known as Balize, is the capital and principal seaport of the British crown colony of British Honduras in Central America. The colony is bounded by the Mexican province of Yucatan to the north and northwest, the Bay of Honduras (an inlet of the Caribbean Sea) to the northeast and east, and Guatemala to the south and west. The territory covers an area of 7,562 square miles and had a population of 40,372 in 1905.

## Etymology and Early Settlement
The name "Belize" likely originates from the French word *balise*, meaning "a beacon," referring to a signal or light used to guide buccaneers. Local tradition also associates the name with a Scottish buccaneer named Wallis (or Wallace), who settled on St George's Cay in 1638 with a group of logwood cutters. During the 18th century, the names Wallis and Belize were used interchangeably to refer to the country, the river, and the town.

The settlement, formerly officially styled "His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras," began with these logwood cutters and was later joined by agents of the Chartered Company, who exploited pearl fisheries on the Mosquito coast.

## Conflict with Spain and the 1779 Attack
The early British settlers frequently raided Spanish logwood establishments. Spain attempted to expel the settlers, most notably in April 1754, when an expedition of 1,500 men organized at the town of Peten was routed by 250 British forces. Following this, the treaty of 1763 between Great Britain and Spain included a clause stating that logwood cutters and their workmen were not to be molested or disturbed.

Despite this, British settlers continued to encroach on Spanish territory. Accusing the settlers of illicit practices and smuggling, the Spaniards organized a large force that attacked and destroyed the establishment at Belize on September 15, 1779. During this assault, the Spaniards took the inhabitants prisoners, transporting them first to Mérida in Yucatan and subsequently to Havana. Most of the prisoners died in Havana; the survivors were liberated in 1782 and permitted to go to Jamaica. By 1783, these survivors returned to the region with new adventurers to resume cutting woods.

## Political Status and Sovereignty
A new treaty signed on September 3, 1783, granted British subjects the right to cut, load, and carry away logwood in the district. While some argue that the defeat of a Spanish attempt in 1798 established permanent British sovereignty through conquest, others note that the British government did not claim such rights for many years. Between 1817 and 1819, acts of parliament still described Belize as a "settlement, for certain purposes, under the protection of His Majesty."

Following the independence of Central America (1819–1822), Great Britain secured its position through treaties with Mexico (1826), New Granada (1825), and the United States of Central America (1831). The British claimed territories between the Belize and Sarstoon rivers in 1836. Legal sovereignty over the entire colony was eventually confirmed through the settlement of boundary disputes with Guatemala in 1859 and the Clayton-Bulwer Treaty of 1850.

## Demographics and Society
The population of British Honduras is smaller than that of Chester, England, and the colony is slightly larger than Wales. As of 1904, there were 1,500 inhabitants of European descent, 253 Europeans, and 118 white Americans. The majority of the population is a hybrid race descended from white settlers, aboriginal Indians, and negro slaves. Six distinct racial groups are identified:
1. Native Indians, primarily in northern and western forest villages.
2. Descendants of English buccaneers mixed with German and Scottish traders.
3. "Belize Creoles," woodcutters of African descent imported as laborers or slaves from the West Indies.
4. Caribs of the southern districts, descendants of a population deported from St Vincent in 1796.
5. A Spanish-Indian mixed population from Honduras and Guatemala in the south.
6. A Spanish-Indian group from Yucatan that arrived in 1848 in the north.

## Geography and Economy
The capital, Belize, is located at 17 deg. 29' N and 88 deg. 11' W. The town consists of wooden houses with wide verandahs and high roofs, surrounded by mangrove swamps. Key buildings include the court house, government house, Fort George, the British bank of Honduras, the Wesleyan church, and a Roman Catholic convent.

The economy is driven by the export of cabinet-woods and dye-woods—including mahogany, cedar, rosewood, and logwood—as well as bananas, fruit, sugar, cocoanuts, turtles, deerskins, and tortoiseshell. Imports consist primarily of hardware, cotton fabrics, and breadstuffs. The American gold dollar became the standard coin on October 14, 1894, replacing the Guatemalan dollar.
