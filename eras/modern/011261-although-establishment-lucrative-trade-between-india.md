# Trade and Exploration Related to India

## Maritime Trade and European Competition
The maritime trade of India was initially controlled by the Red Sea Arabs until the Portuguese seized it through a series of sea fights, utilizing larger ships with heavier armament. Following Vasco da Gama's voyage around the Cape to Calicut, Portugal established a littoral empire across the coasts of China, India, and East Africa, maintaining a significant influence in Goa. For a period, Lisbon surpassed Venice as the primary mart for oriental spices.

In the 17th century, the Dutch challenged this monopoly. The Dutch East India Company, formed in 1602, established its headquarters at Batavia in Java and expanded its operations to include factories in Bengal and on the coasts of Coromandel and Malabar. At the peak of its prosperity in 1669, the company possessed 40 ships of war, 150 trading ships, and 10,000 soldiers. However, the Dutch policy of rigid monopoly, directed by the first governor-general Pieter Both, led to violent hostilities with other European nations. Between 1613 and 1632, the Dutch nearly expelled the English from the Malay Archipelago and the Spice Islands, reducing the English to a precarious position at Bantam in Java. A notable conflict during this era was the judicial murder and torture of English factors at Amboyna in 1623.

## English and French Expansion
Stimulated by rivalry with the Dutch, London merchants organized the East India Company. In 1613, the ship *Clove*, commanded by Saris, reached Hirado. Despite advice from Will Adams to establish trade at Uraga due to its superior harbor and proximity to the shogun's capital, Saris chose Hirado. There, he received a liberal charter from Ieyasu allowing the English to carry on all kinds of trade without hindrance.

By the 18th century, the English and French were in vigorous competition with the Dutch for privileges and trade throughout India. The English established headquarters at Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, while the French established chief settlements at Chandernagore and Pondicherry. Because the Mogul empire was disorganized, these commercial companies used military force to protect their interests, eventually assuming financial control of their trading districts and being recognized as political powers. England's ultimate victory in the struggle for supremacy (roughly 1740–1783) is attributed to European political considerations and a superior command of the seas.

## Land Routes and Central Asian Exploration
While maritime routes dominated, the establishment of lucrative trade between central Asia and India remained a goal for successive Indian viceroys. Significant geographical knowledge of the highlands of the Pamirs and the central depression was lacking until the 1870 mission of Sir Douglas Forsyth to Yarkand. Earlier pioneers included George Hayward and Robert Barkley Shaw, who arrived at Yarkand in 1868. In 1870, Henry Trotter produced the first maps of Chinese Turkestan.

Further geographical progress occurred between 1884 and 1886 via the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission, where Sir Adelbert Talbot, Colonel St George Gore, and Colonel Sir T.H. Holdich mapped the Oxus regions and Afghan Turkestan. During this time, Ney Elias identified "Dragon Lake" (Rangkul) while crossing from China through Badakshan and the Pamirs. Other expeditions included Captain William Lockhart's crossing of the Hindu Kush into Wakhan and Colonel Woodthorpe's verification of W.W. M'Nair's explorations. In 1885, Andrew Dalgleish and Arthur Douglas Carey added to the map of Asia, and in 1886, Captain Francis Younghusband crossed the Muztagh mountain barrier between Kashmir and China.

## Regional Trade Hubs and Connections
Bagdad historically served as a vital center of communication between India, Persia, and the west via land and water. It sat at the junction of caravan routes from Khorasan, the Tigris to the Black Sea and Armenia, and the Euphrates to Syria and the Mediterranean. However, Bagdad's importance declined as transportation methods changed; India began communicating with the west directly through the Suez Canal, and Persian trade shifted to the ports of the Persian Gulf or Trebizond. Despite this, Bagdad remains a distribution center and a destination for wealthy Indians seeking sanctity.

In the Persian Gulf, the island of Kish (or Kais) served for a long time as a chief port of trade with India and the East. Similarly, Síráf was once a great port of Indian trade on the northern shore of the Gulf.

In Africa, Mombasa maintains a regular service to and from India. In the coast region and near Victoria Nyanza, cultivation is largely managed by Indian immigrants or natives. Furthermore, in Uganda, India and Great Britain together supply over 50% of total imports, which consist mainly of hardware, textiles, and manufactured goods, while the retail trade is largely managed by Indians.
