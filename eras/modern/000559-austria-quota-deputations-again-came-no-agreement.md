# Austria: Financial and Commercial Relations with Hungary

The relationship between Austria and Hungary was governed by a system of dualism established in 1867. This system was structured around a fundamental political settlement, a periodical financial settlement regarding common expenses, and a voluntary, terminable agreement concerning the Customs Union and currency.

## The Common Budget and the Quota
The Dual Monarchy maintained a common budget to fund "common affairs," which specifically included the ministry of finance, the army, and the conduct of foreign affairs. The revenues for this joint budget were derived from the net proceeds of customs, the revenues of the joint ministries, and the "quota"—the proportional contributions provided by the two states.

The quota was fixed for several years and typically aligned with the duration of the commercial and customs treaties. From the establishment of the system until 1897, Austria contributed 70% of the joint expenditure remaining after common revenues were deducted, while Hungary contributed 30%. Between 1897 and July 1907, this was adjusted to 66-46/49 for Austria and 33-2/49 for Hungary. In 1907, Hungary's contribution was increased to 36.4%. Additionally, 2% of total charges were debited to Hungary due to the incorporation of the former military frontier.

For example, in 1905, the total revenue for the common administration was £20,762,410, with proportional contributions accounting for £15,650,448 and customs providing £4,780,000.

## The Ausgleich and Negotiating Process
The general settlement between Austria and Hungary, occurring every ten years, was known as the *Ausgleich* (compact or compromise). This process involved a complex bargain where concessions in one area, such as commercial policy, were offset by gains in financial agreements. For instance, in 1877, Austria granted Hungary a share in the management of the bank, while Austria benefited from the arrangement for paying bonuses on exported sugar. Conversely, disputes arose when duties on petroleum and coffee fell more heavily on Austria, leading the Austrians to seek a larger quota contribution from Hungary.

Negotiations were frequently fraught with difficulty due to national jealousy and race hatred within the two parliaments. The first settlement took two years (1876 to June 1878) to complete. The process required the two ministries to agree on bill drafts, followed by review by elected committees from each parliament who communicated via notes and verbal discussions before the bills were debated in the respective parliaments.

## Administrative Institutions: Delegations and Deputations
To manage common institutions while maintaining parity, the monarchy utilized "Delegations." Each parliament elected a Delegation of sixty members. These Delegations were responsible for the common ministers of war, finance, and foreign affairs. To prevent the Delegations from overshadowing the parliaments, they met separately in Vienna and Pest and communicated in writing with translations in German or Magyar. A common session was only held if three exchanges of notes failed to produce an agreement; in such cases, they voted in silence by simple majority.

While the Delegations submitted estimates for joint services, they lacked legislative power. They could not vote taxes or raise money; once a budget was passed, the funds had to be provided by the separate parliaments. The determination of the proportional contribution (the quota) was not left to the Delegations but was fixed every ten years by separate deputations.

## Customs Unions and Commercial Treaties
The Customs Union was a voluntary arrangement. In one settlement, the Bank charter was renewed until 1910, granting Hungarians a larger share in direction. The Customs Union, while lapsing as a binding treaty, was continued in practice until December 31, 1907.

Tensions peaked around 1905-1906 when the Hungarian Coalition and the crown entered a pact. This pact required the suspension of military questions until universal suffrage was introduced and the ratification of commercial treaties. Although the Coalition government was obliged to a dualist policy, many of its members belonged to the Independence or Kossuth party, which sought economic and political separation from Austria. This led to a deadlock in 1905 and the attempt to create an autonomous Hungarian tariff. This move caused the resignation of Austrian premier Prince Konrad Hohenlohe.

## The 1907 Agreement
A severe economic crisis in Hungary and the realization that separation would increase the cost of living in Cisleithania and ruin Hungarian commerce led to a new "customs and commercial treaty" in October 1907. Key features included:
*   The establishment of separate but identical customs tariffs for each state.
*   Mutual facilitation of railway communications: Hungary for the line between Vienna and Dalmatia (by 1911), and Austria for communication between Hungary and Prussia.
*   The abolition of practical impediments to free trade in sugar.
*   The creation of a court of arbitration with four judges from each state and a presidency decided by lot.
*   A provision allowing Hungary to redeem its share of the old Austrian debt at a rate of 4.325% over ten years.
*   The recognition of both governments' securities as investments for insurance companies and savings banks.
