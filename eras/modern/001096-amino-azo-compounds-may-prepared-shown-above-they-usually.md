# Amino-azo Compounds

Amino-azo compounds are organic substances characterized by the presence of amino groups within an azo structure. They are typically identified by their coloration, appearing as yellowish brown or red. The specific shade is influenced by the chemical composition: the presence of additional amino groups results in browner shades, while the introduction of alkylated amino groups produces redder shades. These compounds generally crystallize well and are readily reduced.

## Chemical Properties and Reactions
Amino-azo compounds can be converted into indulines when heated with aniline and aniline hydrochloride. They are often prepared through the coupling of diazonium salts with amines, provided there is a free para position relative to the amino group. 

Research by H. Goldschmidt and A. Merz indicates that the velocity of formation for these compounds depends on the nature of the reagents rather than their concentration. In instances where the hydrochloride of a tertiary amine is coupled with diazobenzene sulphonic acid, the reaction occurs between the acid and the base released by the hydrolytic dissociation of its salt. Furthermore, the formation of amino-azo compounds occurs most rapidly when carried out in the presence of the weakest acid.

## Specific Amino-azo-benzene Derivatives
Several distinct derivatives of amino-azo-benzene are documented in the archive:

*   **Amino-azo-benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{NH}_2$):** This compound crystallizes as yellow needles or plates and has a melting point of 126°C. Its constitution is evidenced by the fact that it can be prepared by reducing nitro-azo-benzene using ammonium sulphide. When reduced with stannous chloride, it yields meta-phenylene diamine and aniline.
*   **Diamino-azo-benzene (Chrysoidine, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2\text{C}_6\text{H}_3(\text{NH}_2)_2$):** First prepared by O. Witt in 1877, this compound is obtained by coupling meta-phenylene diamine with phenyl diazonium chloride. It forms red octahedra crystals and is used to dye wool and silk yellow.
*   **Triamino-azo-benzene ($\text{NH}_2\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{N}_2\text{C}_6\text{H}_3(\text{NH}_2)_2$):** Also known as vesuvine, phenylene brown, Manchester brown, or Bismarck brown, it is prepared by the action of nitrous acid on meta-phenylene diamine. It forms brown crystals that are readily soluble in hot water and can dye mordanted cotton a dark brown.

## Related Azo and Diazo Compounds
The broader category of azo compounds ($\text{R.N:N.R}'$) includes substances where R is an aryl radical and R' is a substituted aryl or alkyl radical. These are intensely colored but require salt-forming basic or acid groups to function as dyestuffs.

### Diazo-Amines
Diazo-amines ($\text{R.N:N.NHR}_1$) are crystalline solids, usually yellow, obtained through the action of primary amines on diazonium salts, the action of nitrosamines on primary amines, or the action of nitrous acid on a free primary amine (via an iso-diazohydroxide intermediate). They are readily converted into amino-azo compounds. Diazoamino benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N:N.NHC}_6\text{H}_5$), first obtained by P. Griess in 1862, crystallizes in yellow laminae that melt at 96°C and explode at slightly higher temperatures.

### Mixed Azo Compounds
Mixed azo compounds feature an azo group united with an aromatic radical on one side and an aliphatic radical on the other. These can be formed by uniting a diazonium salt with the sodium or potassium salt of a nitroparaffin. Examples include:
*   **Benzene-azo-methane ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2\text{CH}_3$):** A yellow oil boiling at 150°C.
*   **Benzene-azo-ethane ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5$):** A yellow oil boiling at approximately 180°C.

## Other Related Derivatives
The archive also notes several other nitrogen-based organic compounds:
*   **Diazoimino benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_3$):** A yellow oil with a stupefying odour that boils at 59°C (12 mm) and explodes when heated.
*   **Azoxy Compounds ($\text{R[=N.O.N].R}'$):** Usually red or yellow crystalline solids resulting from the oxidation of azo compounds or the reduction of nitroso or nitro compounds using alcoholic potash. Azoxybenzene ($\text{(C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N)}_2\text{O}$) melts at 36°C and yields azobenzene and aniline upon distillation.
*   **Diazo Cyanides and Carboxylic Acids:** Diazobenzenecyanide ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2\text{CN}$) is an unstable oil, while phenyl-azo-carboxylic acid ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2\text{COOH}$) forms orange-red needles that decompose in water.
