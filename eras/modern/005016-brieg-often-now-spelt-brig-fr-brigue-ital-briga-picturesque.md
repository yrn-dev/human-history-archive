# BRIEG

The name Brieg (also spelled Brig, and referred to in French as *Brigue* and Italian as *Briga*) refers to two distinct locations: a small town in the Swiss canton of the Valais and a town in the Prussian province of Silesia in Germany.

## Brieg, Switzerland
Brieg is a picturesque small town located in the Swiss canton of the Valais. It is situated on the right bank of the Saltine stream, slightly above where the stream joins the Rhone, at the foot of the northern slope of the Simplon Pass. In medieval times, the town was known as *Briga dives*.

The town's development is closely linked to the Simplon Pass. Brieg gradually supplanted the older village of Naters, which lies opposite, and became a separate parish in 1517, though its church is located a few minutes away at Glis. The town's importance and size grew significantly following the 1807 opening of the carriage road across the Simplon and the 1906 opening of the tunnel beneath the pass. Additionally, the area above Brieg consists of the less fertile and steeper portion of the Upper Valais, which is frequently visited by tourists. Further prosperity is expected from the opening of the railway tunnel beneath the Lötschen Pass, which provides direct communication with the Bernese Oberland and Bern.

Architecturally, the older houses of Brieg have a very Italian appearance. The town's most prominent structures—including the castle, the Ursuline convent, and the former Jesuits' college—date from the 17th century and were funded by a single member of the local Stockalper family. The new section of the town is located below the old town, closer to the right bank of the Rhone.

As of 1900, the population of Brieg was 2,182. The inhabitants were almost all Romanists. Linguistically, the population consisted of 1,316 German-speakers, 719 Italian-speakers (who were workmen for the Simplon tunnel), 142 French-speakers, and one person who spoke Romonsch.

## Brieg, Germany
Brieg is a town in the Prussian province of Silesia, located on the left bank of the Oder and on the Beuthen and Breslau railway, 27 miles southeast of the former city of Breslau. Its name is derived from the Polish word *Brzeg*, meaning "shore," and it is referred to in early documents as *Civitas Altae Ripae*.

The town obtained municipal rights in 1250 from Duke Henry III of Breslau and was fortified in 1297. It has faced several periods of destruction: it was burned by the Hussites in 1428 and suffered greatly during the Thirty Years' War. It was heavily bombarded by Prussian forces during the war of the Austrian succession and was captured by the Bavarians and French in 1807, during which time the French destroyed its fortifications. These fortifications have since been replaced by promenades. The town was fortified again in 1595 by Joachim Frederick, duke of Brieg.

Brieg's infrastructure includes a theatre, several hospitals, several churches, a lunatic asylum, and a gymnasium with a good library. It also features a castle, which served as the residence of the old counts of Brieg and was built in 1341.

Economically, Brieg is a center for considerable trade and holds important cattle-markets. Its primary manufactures include leather, machinery, cigars, pasteboard, hats, ribbons, and cotton, woollen, linen, and embroidery goods. In 1900, the population was 24,090.

## Political History of Brieg, Germany
From 1311 to 1675, Brieg served as the capital for an independent line of dukes who were a cadet branch of the Polish dukes of Lower Silesia. In 1537, Frederick II, duke of Brieg, Wohlau, and Liegnitz, entered into a treaty with Joachim II, elector of Brandenburg, stating that the duchy would pass to the house of Brandenburg if Frederick's line became extinct.

However, upon the death of the last duke, George William, in 1675, Austria annexed the duchies and refused to recognize the treaty's validity. This dispute led Frederick II of Prussia to assert his claim in 1740, sparking a war that resulted in the cession of Silesia to Prussia two years later.

During the Silesian Campaign of 1741, Brieg was a point of contention. While the Austrians in Brieg and Neisse initially held out, the Prussian forces eventually secured Brieg following the battle of Mollwitz on April 10, 1741. This battle, the first pitched battle fought by Frederick and his army, saw the Prussian infantry prevail despite the routing of the Prussian right wing of cavalry. Following the victory, Count Neipperg's Austrian forces fell back to Neisse.
