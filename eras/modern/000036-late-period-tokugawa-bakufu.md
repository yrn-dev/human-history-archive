# THE LATE PERIOD OF THE TOKUGAWA BAKUFU

The late period of the Tokugawa Bakufu is characterized by a gradual decline in administrative strength, severe financial instability, and the eventual movement toward the restoration of power to the sovereign. This era began with the succession of the eleventh shogun, Ienari, and continued through a period of increasing internal and external pressures.

## The Succession of Ienari and the Administration of Sadanobu
In 1786, the tenth shogun, Ieharu, died and was succeeded by Ienari, a great-grandson of Yoshimune and son of Hitotsubashi Harunari. Because Ienari was only fifteen years old at the time of his accession, he was unable to personally address the administrative, economic, and financial problems of what is described as the darkest period of Tokugawa sway.

To manage these crises, the Bakufu turned to Matsudaira Sadanobu, the grandson of Yoshimune and son of Tayasu Munetake. Appointed prime minister in 1788 at the age of thirty, Sadanobu was a capable administrator known for his insight, energy, and courage. He sought to remedy a systemic failure in the Bakufu's leadership; during the middle period, ministers from the Hotta, Sakai, and Ii families had lacked marked ability, allowing the shogun's personal attendants to usurp their functions. Sadanobu corrected this by appointing the heads of the three Tokugawa families—Owari, Kii, and Mito—to the council of State.

Sadanobu's primary immediate objective was to combat the national obsession with famine. He implemented specific rules for the storing of grain, which, combined with several years of favorable harvests, helped restore public confidence.

## Financial Decline and Social Unrest
Despite early administrative efforts, the strength of the Bakufu began to wane steadily. A primary cause of this decline was a depleted treasury. During the first half of the nineteenth century, the Bakufu faced a financial situation where expenditures consistently exceeded income. To manage this, the administration repeatedly resorted to the debasing of the currency.

These displays of impecuniosity undermined public respect for the Bakufu. Furthermore, the appreciation of commodities made the cost of living more severe, which served to alienate the general population. This instability provided an opportunity for the *tozama daimyo*—the feudatories in the west whose loyalty to the Yedo administration was weak—to assert themselves against the Bakufu.

## Foreign Relations and the Path to Downfall
The decline of the Tokugawa was driven not only by internal financial failure but also by the complications arising from foreign intercourse. As the Bakufu's power diminished, discussions regarding the restoration of administrative power to the sovereign began to emerge, moving from whispered conversations to more open discourse.

The long-term strategy of the Tokugawa had included efforts to avoid being stigmatized as "rebels." This was evidenced by the establishment of the Kwanei-ji temple on Ueno hill in Yedo, intended to mirror the Enryaku-ji in Kyoto and associate an Imperial prince with the Tokugawa family. This strategic intention reached a practical application only at the time of the Tokugawa's downfall, when the remnants of the Bakufu army set up the priest-prince of Ueno, Prince Kitashirakawa, as their leader.
