# Ludwig van Beethoven and the Influence of J.S. Bach

## Study and Influence of Bach
Ludwig van Beethoven profoundly studied all the accessible works of Johann Sebastian Bach. This engagement was documented in Beethoven's sketch-books, where he frequently quoted Bach's works, often in a manner that had a direct bearing on his own compositions. 

The nature of Beethoven's interpretation of Bach is a subject of historical record; specifically, his rendering of the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier* is said to be preserved in the tempo and expression marks found in Czerny's edition. Some suggest that if this record is accurate, Beethoven may have been "completely in the dark" regarding Bach's intended meaning in several important respects. Despite this, the relationship serves as an illustration of how great minds influence one another across barriers of time and language.

## Comparative Works and Kinship
The kinship between the two composers is evident in specific structural and emotional parallels. Beethoven's *Thirty-three Variations on a Waltz by Diabelli* were described by the publisher as being related to Bach's *Goldberg Variations*. This connection is most revealed when comparing Beethoven's 31st variation with Bach's 25th, as both composers use these moments for their most intimate expressions of feeling.

Furthermore, the *Goldberg Variations* and Beethoven's 33 variations on Diabelli's waltz are noted as the only works that surpass Beethoven's Sonata, op. 106 (B-flat) in length, the latter being the largest and most symphonic pianoforte work extant.

## Development of Style and Technique
Beethoven's artistic evolution is often divided into three styles, though these periods are not rigidly separated chronologically. In his later years, Beethoven demonstrated a deep interest in strict forms. His *op. 102* violoncello sonatas include his first modern instrumental strict fugue, and his Overture, *Die Weihe des Hauses, op. 124* (published 1825), is described as a magnificent essay in orchestral free fugue.

Additionally, Beethoven's *op. 104* arrangement for string quintet of the C mi. trio, *op. 1, No. 3*, is regarded as a wonderful study in translation. This specific work is compared to the arrangements of Bach and is noted as being very different from Beethoven's earlier attempts at such translations.

## Legacy and Broader Musical Context
Beethoven is characterized as perhaps the greatest artist and musician to have ever lived, possessing an absolute conscientiousness of execution and a perfect sense of beauty. His influence on subsequent music is described as being as vast and difficult to measure as that of Shakespeare. While some later "revolutionists" cited the works of Beethoven's third period to justify their own experiments, his true influence is seen in the works of composers such as Schumann, Wagner, and Brahms.

In the broader history of music, Bach's influence was often subtle and unnoticed by the general public, being recognized primarily by great composers. For example, Mozart developed a new depth of harmony and polyphonic richness after reading manuscript copies of Bach's motets and the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier*. Later, Brahms developed the artistic devices of both Bach and Beethoven *pari passu*, resulting in a unification of form and polyphony.

## Early Training and Documentation
Beethoven's path to mastery involved significant struggle. He studied with Haydn starting around 1792, but the relationship was strained; Haydn found Beethoven's manners lacking and his free compositions audacious. Beethoven also studied with Albrechtsberger to gain a necessary grounding in strict counterpoint, despite Albrechtsberger having a poor opinion of him.

Beethoven is unique among artists for the authoritative documentary evidence he left regarding his development. Since boyhood, he maintained a habit of noting all his musical ideas exactly as they occurred to him in his sketch-books. The significance of these sketch-books is surpassed only by the evidence provided by Bach's own constant re-arranging of his compositions.
