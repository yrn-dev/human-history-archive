# Chinese and Half Castes

## Population in Australasia and Eastern Colonies
In the region of Australasia, the population includes Chinese and half castes, who are described as yellow and numbered at 50,000 in Australia. In New Zealand, the population consists of Maoris (brown) and 53,000 Chinese and half castes. Other populations in the region include 400,000 Polynesian people (black and brown) in Papua and 121,000 Polynesian people (black and brown) in Fiji.

Within the Eastern dependencies, Chinese populations are present in the Straits Settlements and Hong-Kong. The Straits Settlements are noted as having a population of 570,000 consisting of brown, mixed, and Chinese individuals, while Hong-Kong has 306,130 Chinese and brown individuals. In other areas of the Eastern colonies, such as Ceylon, the population is described as high type, brown, and mixed.

## Classification and Global Distribution
The native populations of the empire are categorized into principal black, yellow, and brown races. This classification includes high-type races of the East, which are described as almost white. Within the Indo-Chinese group, the population is further divided into the Mon-Khmer, Tibeto-Burman, and Siamese-Chinese.

Beyond the Eastern colonies, "yellow" populations are also noted in the West Indies, specifically in Jamaica, where the population of 790,000 is described as chiefly black, with some brown and yellow individuals.

## Chinese Social and Political Structure
The Chinese civilization is characterized as being organized for peace, with the warrior playing a small role in the social scheme. The leading class is an intellectual one consisting of mandarins. Unlike the Brahmin caste in India, mandarins are not a caste by birth; they are literate men drawn from all classes of the community through education and examination. Consequently, the son of a mandarin possesses no prescriptive right to succeed his father.

The traditional social system of the "yellow civilization" recognized four main classes situated below the priest-emperor. This routine of life remained largely unchanged from the period before Alexander the Great until the year 1900 A.D., despite the arrival and departure of various dynasties and invaders.

## History and Governance in China
Under the rule of Kublai Khan, the Chinese state saw the opening of the Imperial Academy, the revision of the calendar, the exploration of the Yellow River's source, and the introduction of bank-notes. While Kublai Khan was an ardent Buddhist, he maintained honors for Confucius and ordered the destruction of "baser" Taoist literature. However, his reign was marked by extortionate taxation, which led to disorder. By 1368, the mandate of the Mongols—who were viewed as an alien race—was exhausted, and they were removed by the Chinese.

Chinese military history is noted for a tendency where soldiers may run away, though they have also fought to the death. The effectiveness of the soldiers is often tied to the regularity and sufficiency of their pay.

## Intellectual and Literary Achievements
China achieved a massive literary feat through the employment of over two thousand scholars who spent five years compiling a vast encyclopaedia. This work covered four departments: general literature (including arts, handicrafts, medicine, divination, geography, astronomy, cosmogony, Buddhism, and Taoism), philosophy, history, and the Confucian Canon. The resulting work consisted of 22,877 sections and an index of 60 sections, bound in 11,000 volumes. In total, the work contained 917,480 pages and 366,000,000 words. Due to the condensed nature of the Chinese literary style, 100 Chinese words are considered equivalent to 130 English words.

## Regional Interactions
In other territories, such as the state of Keng Tung, the Chinese Shans are estimated at 5,000 people. Historically, the Chinese made several attempts to subdue this state, successfully taking the capital in 1765-66 before being driven out by Burmese and Shan troops. Additionally, some Koreans have emigrated into Chinese territory.
