# The History and Administration of Kumasi and Ashanti

Kumasi serves as the capital of the Ashanti kingdom and is the primary distributing center for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti. Historically, the city was a center of high culture with broad, regular streets and a royal palace built of red sandstone, modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina. However, the city suffered significant destruction over several decades, including the blowing up of the royal palace by Sir Garnet Wolseley's forces on February 6, 1874, and the burning of ancient houses in the Bantama suburb in 1896.

## Early British Relations and Conflict
British interaction with the Ashanti began with attempts to cultivate intercourse with the kingdom. In 1819, Mr. Joseph Dupuis was sent as consul, arriving at Kumasi on February 28, 1820. He drew up a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory, though this was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith. 

Hostilities escalated in 1824 when the Ashanti, with 10,000 men, defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy and 500 troops at Essamako on January 21. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was subsequently used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. After a period of chronic warfare, the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826. By 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from George Maclean for 600 oz. of gold and provide his son as a hostage.

## The 19th Century Wars and Political Shifts
The war of 1873-74 was triggered primarily by the British acquisition of Elmina fort from the Dutch on April 2, 1872, and the captivity of four Europeans, including Mr. and Mrs. Ramseyer. Following this war, several dependent states declared independence. The Ashanti leadership saw various shifts: Kofi Karikari was deposed in favor of his brother Mensa, whose rule was characterized as tyrannous and marked by human sacrifices. After a civil war, Prince Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888.

Tensions returned in 1893 when Prempeh closed roads to traders. After the king failed to respond to British demands to accept a protectorate and a resident at Kumasi by October 31, 1895, he was eventually deported.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Following Prempeh's deportation, no successor was appointed to the throne. Captain Donald W. Stewart was installed as a British resident, and Kumasi's affairs were administered by chiefs under British guidance. A fort was built in the city and garrisoned with Gold Coast constabulary.

In 1900, a serious rebellion broke out involving the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes, who commanded a force of 40,000. The conflict was sparked by Governor Sir F. Hodgson's demand on March 28 that the chiefs pay 4,000 oz. of gold annually and surrender the "golden stool"—the symbol of sovereignty—which had been hidden since 1896. On March 31, Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa searching for the stool.

As the rebels threatened the Kumasi fort, Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, along with Basel missionaries, took refuge inside. Reinforcements arrived in stages: 100 Gold Coast constabulary arrived on April 18, and 250 Lagos constabulary reached the city on April 29, the same day the Kumasi attacked in force but were repulsed. By May 15, Major A. Morris arrived with another 250 men, bringing the total garrison to 700.

## Economy, Trade, and Infrastructure
Kumasi is the meeting point for ancient caravan routes and is connected to coast towns and Northern Territories via telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service. A 141-mile wide road connects Kumasi to Cape Coast, though much of the region is navigated via narrow forest paths or palanquins. A railway from Sekondi was completed in 1903, passing through the Obuassi gold-fields.

The region's economy is based on several key products:
*   **Agriculture:** The rubber tree (_Funtumia elastica_) is commercially important, and cocoa plantations were established in eastern districts after 1905. Kola nuts are in great demand, and tobacco is grown in the north.
*   **Mining:** Gold is found in western districts. The Obuassi mines provided most of the gold exported from the Gold Coast after 1902. In 1905, Ashanti's gold output was 68,259 oz., valued at L254,790.
*   **Crafts:** The Ashanti are noted for weaving cotton, pottery, and elaborate goldsmith work, including beaten gold masks.

Revenue for the administration is derived from liquor licenses, caravan taxes, government land rents, and contributions from gold-mining companies.

## Society and Culture
The majority of the population are spirit-worshippers, with a general repugnance toward the doctrines of Islam, though some Mahommedan traders reside there. Basel and Wesleyan missionaries have secured some converts to Christianity. Aside from Kumasi, other notable towns include Obuassi (the gold-mining center), Wam, Nkoranza, Atabubu, and Kintampo. Kintampo, located 130 miles north-east of Kumasi, serves as a depot for government stores and a center for the kola-nut commerce.
