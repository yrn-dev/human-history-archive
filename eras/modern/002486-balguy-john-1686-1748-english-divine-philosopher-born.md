# BALGUY, JOHN (1686-1748)

## Early Life and Education
John Balguy was an English philosopher and divine born in Sheffield on August 12, 1686. His early education took place at the Sheffield grammar school, after which he attended St John's College, Cambridge. He graduated with a B.A. in 1706 and was ordained in 1710. In 1711, Balguy obtained a small living in Tanfield and Lamesley, located in Durham. He married in 1715.

## Early Career and the Bangorian Controversy
Balguy began his career as an author in 1715 during the "Bangorian controversy." This controversy was sparked by a famous sermon delivered by Bishop Hoadley titled "The Kingdom of Christ." Writing under the pseudonym Silvius, Balguy entered the debate to support Hoadley against several of his opponents from the High Church.

## Philosophical Works and Moral Theory
In 1726, Balguy published *A letter to a Deist concerning the Beauty and Excellency of Moral Virtue, and the Support and Improvement which it receives from the Christian Religion*. The primary objective of this work was to demonstrate that while the highest principle of morality is a love of virtue for its own sake, religious punishments and rewards serve as indispensable and valuable sanctions for conduct in certain cases.

Between 1727 and 1728, he published a two-part tractate titled *The Foundation of Moral Goodness*. The first part appeared in 1727, followed by a second part in 1728 titled *Illustrating and enforcing the Principles contained in the former*. This work had two main goals: to refute the theory of Hutcheson regarding the basis of rectitude and to establish the theory held by Clarke and Cudworth. Balguy argued that virtue consists of conformity to reason, which involves acting according to fitnesses that arise from the eternal and immutable relations of objects to agents.

## Later Appointments and Theological Inquiry
Through the friendship of Bishop Hoadley, Balguy was appointed as a prebendary of Salisbury in 1727. In 1729, he became the vicar of Northallerton in the county of York.

Balguy also authored an essay titled *Divine Rectitude: or, a Brief Inquiry concerning the Moral Perfections of the Deity, particularly in respect of Creation and Providence*. In this text, he attempted to show that the works and ways of God are underpinned by the same moral principle that should direct human life. He posited that goodness in the Deity is not merely a disposition toward benevolence, but rather a regard for harmony, beauty, and order.
