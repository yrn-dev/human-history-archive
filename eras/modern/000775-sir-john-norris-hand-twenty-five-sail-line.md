# Sir John Norris and the French Invasion Scheme of 1744

## The French Invasion Plan
In 1744, a French invasion scheme was organized in coordination with Jacobite leaders. The plan involved the transportation of soldiers from Dunkirk to Great Britain. Although the British government demonstrated a complete lack of foresight regarding this threat, the French plan ultimately failed to materialize.

## Naval Confrontation in the English Channel
In February 1744, a French fleet consisting of twenty sail of the line entered the English Channel. This force was commanded by Jacques Aymar, comte de Roquefeuil. At the time of the fleet's entry, the British forces under Admiral John Norris were not yet ready to oppose them.

Despite the initial British lack of readiness, the French operation was hampered by several factors. The French force was ill equipped, and the weather conditions were poor. Furthermore, the French admiral, M. de Roquefeuil, was described as nervous, with a mind preoccupied by all the potential misfortunes that might occur.

## The Retreat of the French Fleet
M. de Roquefeuil advanced his fleet almost as far as the Downs. However, upon learning that Sir John Norris was nearby with twenty-five sail of the line, Roquefeuil precipitately retreated. Because the French fleet retreated, the military expedition that had been prepared at Dunkirk to cross under the cover of Roquefeuil's fleet did not start.

## French Naval Weakness and Subsequent Conflicts
The failure of the 1744 scheme highlighted the utter weakness of the French at sea, which resulted from a bankrupt treasury and a long period of neglect regarding the fleet. This weakness remained evident during the Jacobite rising of 1745, during which France made no attempt to take advantage of the distress facing the British government.

During this period, the naval power opposed to France was increased when the Dutch joined Great Britain. However, the role of Holland at sea remained subordinate because they were required to maintain an army in Flanders. 

The British government was slow to utilize its latest naval strength, as it was not stimulated by a formidable attack and had immediate interests in Germany and at home. Consequently, Spain was almost neglected, as it was incapable of offensive action. British naval operations during 1745 were generally sporadic and subordinated to unimportant ends or the supply of convoys, though a British naval force did cover the New England expedition that took Louisburg between April 30 and June 16.

In the East Indies, the British naval forces offered no effectual resistance to Mahe de la Bourdonnais, who used a small squadron to capture Madras between July 24 and September 9, 1746. Madras was later exchanged for Louisburg at the conclusion of the war.
