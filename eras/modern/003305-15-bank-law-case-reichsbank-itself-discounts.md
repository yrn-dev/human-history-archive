# The Reichsbank and German Banking Regulations

The Reichsbank is the most powerful banking institution in Germany, exercising a pervasive influence across the various kingdoms of the empire through its network of branches. This uniformity was made possible by the laws of 1871-1873, which introduced a uniform coinage. The bank is managed by a directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire, who maintains complete powers of control. Shareholders participate in management via a committee, with membership requiring the holding of at least three shares.

## Discount Rates and Private Note-Issuing Banks
The Reichsbank maintains a specific arrangement with four private note-issuing banks to ensure they work in harmony with the central bank, particularly when the Reichsbank must raise its bank-rate to protect gold reserves. Under the amending act of June 7, 1899 (*Gesetz, betreffend die Abaenderung des Bankgesetzes vom 14. Maerz 1875*), and specifically *Artikel 7, S. 2* (effective after January 1, 1901), these private banks are subject to strict discount rate regulations:

*   **Binding Rates:** The official published rate of discount of the Reichsbank is binding on private note-issuing banks once it reaches or exceeds 4%.
*   **Rate Limitations:** At other times, private banks may not discount at more than 1/4% below the official Reichsbank rate.
*   **Lower Rate Exceptions:** In instances where the Reichsbank itself discounts at a rate lower than the official rate, private banks may not discount at more than 1/8% below that lower rate. The Reichsbank is required to announce any such discounting below the official rate in the *Gazette*.

## Note Circulation and Legal Limits
Note circulation is governed by the law of 1875 and the amending law of 1899. The system includes an uncovered limit for notes not represented by specie, which began at £12,500,000 and was extended to £23,641,450 by the act of June 5, 1902. 

To cover notes not represented by specie, the bank must hold treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*), notes from issuing banks reckoned as specie, or discounted bills. These bills must generally have three good indorsements (never fewer than two) and mature no later than three months after being taken. Additionally, at least one-third of notes in circulation must be covered by gold in bullion, foreign coin (reckoned at £69, 12s. per pound fine), money, current German notes, or imperial treasury notes.

The Reichsbank is legally bound to redeem its notes in current German money, which may include silver thalers, gold coin, or bar-gold at a rate of 1392 marks per pound fine of gold.

## Statutory Powers and Financial Status
Unlike the Bank of England, the Reichsbank possesses an automatic statutory power to exceed the legal limits of note circulation if business conditions require it, provided it pays 5% on the surplus. This mechanism is intended to provide necessary banking accommodation and prevent panic. While the rate was generally 5%, it reached 7% for a short period in 1899. Between 1881 and the end of 1906, the duty on extra issues totaled £839,052.

As of the 1906 balance sheet, the Reichsbank's financial position was as follows:
*   **Bills held:** £67,000,000.
*   **Loans and advances:** £14,200,000.
*   **Average notes issued for the year:** £69,000,000.
*   **Gold held (as of December 30, 1906):** £24,069,000.
*   **Capital:** Raised to £9,000,000 by the 1899 bill.
*   **Reserve fund:** Raised to £3,240,000 from surplus profits.

## Operational Services and Industrial Trends
The Reichsbank assists business primarily through its note circulation and its clearing system (*Giro-Verkehr*). The clearing system, which the Reichsbank developed after absorbing the Bank of Prussia in 1876 and utilizing the former Hamburg Clearing House building, allows for the free remittance of money across 443 offices. This system functions as a risk-free, chargeless money-order system; in 1906, these transfers exceeded £1,860,000,000.

In terms of currency, a 1906 law expanded the Imperial Bank's note issues (*Reichsbanknoten*) to include 20 mark (£1) and 50 mark (£2, 10s.) notes, leading to the withdrawal of treasury notes of those same values.

Recent trends indicate that German banks are increasingly employing resources in industrial concerns, sometimes beyond the empire's borders, to obtain higher interest rates than those available from domestic loans or discounting bills. Dr. R. Koch, president of the Reichsbank, has criticized this practice as being opposed to banking tradition and has emphasized the need for powerful banks that remain unconnected to such speculative business.
