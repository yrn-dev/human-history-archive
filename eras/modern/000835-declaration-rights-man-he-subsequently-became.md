# Declaration of the Rights of Man

The Declaration of the Rights of Man was a foundational document formulated by the Assembly during the French Revolution. The Assembly spent six weeks on its formulation, utilizing a process characterized by extensive rhetoric. The document was developed along the lines of the English Bills of Rights, which served as preliminaries to organized change.

## Context of the French Revolution
The creation of the Declaration occurred during a period of significant constructive activity by the Assembly. On the Fourth of August, the Assembly achieved a major success by passing a series of resolutions that abolished tithes, feudal courts, tax exemptions, privileges, and serfdom. This movement also extended to titles; before France became a republic, it was considered an offence for a nobleman to sign his name using his title.

During this era, the political atmosphere was strained by suspicions between the people and the court, with the people believing the court was plotting for reaction. This tension was intensified by a shortage of provisions, for which the government of the king was held responsible. Additionally, Philip of Orleans, the king's cousin, utilized his gardens at the Palais Royal as a center for advanced discussion and employed agents to increase popular suspicion toward the king in hopes of replacing Louis on the French throne.

## Proposed Pendant: Declaration of the Duties of Man
During the states-general of 1789, Claude Antoine de Besiade, the marquis d'Avaray, served as the deputy for the bailliage of Orleans. In this capacity, he proposed a *Declaration of the Duties of Man* to serve as a pendant to the *Declaration of the Rights of Man*. 

Claude Antoine de Besiade's subsequent career included several high-ranking appointments:
* In 1814, he became a lieutenant-general.
* In 1815, he was named a peer of France.
* In 1818, he became the duc d'Avaray.
