# British Influence and Public Opinion in Uganda

## Diplomatic Spheres and the Hinterland Doctrine
In July 1887, Great Britain and Germany engaged in diplomatic correspondence regarding the "hinterland doctrine." Under this accepted doctrine, the territory of Uganda was designated to fall within the "sphere of influence" of Great Britain. While this was the formal diplomatic understanding, the perception of the matter differed between the two nations. German maps assigned the territory to Germany, and German public opinion did not regard the matter as being settled in favor of the British. Conversely, public opinion in England strongly expected British influence to be paramount in the region.

## Conflict and Occupation
Tensions escalated in 1889 when Karl Peters, a German official, led a raiding expedition into Uganda after running a blockade of the ports. During the same year, a company expedition led by F.J. Jackson had been sent to Victoria Nyanza; although Jackson was initially instructed to avoid Uganda, he decided to enter the country following representations from Uganda and news of Peters's activities. 

Upon Jackson's approach, Peters retired, though he claimed to have established "effective occupation" through treaties, one of which was dated March 1, 1890. Jackson concluded a separate treaty in April. These events occurred while negotiations continued in Europe, eventually resulting in the Anglo-German agreement of July 1, 1890, which officially assigned Uganda to the British sphere.

## Consolidation of Authority and Imperial Aid
To solidify the British position and counter the influence of French missionaries—who were hostile to Great Britain—the company dispatched Captain F.D. Lugard. Lugard arrived at the capital, Mengo, in December 1890, where he established the company's authority despite intrigues by the French.

The company's interference in Uganda was viewed as a material step toward the suppression of the slave trade, a goal agreed upon by representatives of the powers at Brussels in July 1890. Additionally, there were efforts to open the country by constructing a railway from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza. Because the company lacked the necessary resources for such a project, they sought imperial aid. While the prime minister, Lord Salisbury, praised the company's efforts and a preliminary grant for the survey was nearly agreed upon, the scheme was wrecked in parliament. The railway was eventually constructed at a later date, funded entirely by the government.
