# Fire-Resisting Construction

The recognition of the importance of fire-resisting construction has increased across all countries. This development is driven by evidence provided by fire brigade experts and the occurrence of serious fires, leading to the replacement of traditional materials with those capable of resisting fire.

## Flooring and Structural Materials
Historically, floors for ordinary residential purposes were primarily constructed of timber. Until approximately 1895, nearly every modern building utilized wood joists. However, fire-resisting floors have since been introduced, consisting of steel girders and joists filled with concrete or various patented brick materials. These are implemented according to by-laws, such as those passed by other authorities and the London County Council. Currently, the majority of floors in large residential flats, schools, factories, and public buildings are constructed from these fire-resisting materials.

In the context of furnace construction, materials are divided into ordinary and refractory (fire-resisting) classes. Ordinary materials—including granite, most building stones, clay-slate, and red or yellow bricks—are used for pillars, walls, and casing. Refractory materials are reserved for areas in direct contact with flame and fuel, such as the roof, flues, arches, and the lining of the fire-place. These include firebricks and fireclay, certain sandstones, alumina, ferric oxide, magnesia, lime, chromium oxide, and silica in the form of ganister, as well as Dinas bricks and stone and carbon (graphite and coke).

## Regulations in London and New York
In London, fire-resisting construction is governed by the London County Council Theatre and Factory Acts, as well as the London Building Act and its second schedule. Specific regulations include:
* **Residential and Commercial Buildings:** Dwelling-houses in London must follow building acts that forbid the use of timber-framed buildings. For premises used as both a dwelling-house and a shop, if the area exceeds 10 squares or 1,000 square feet, a large portion of the building and the stairs must be built of fire-resisting materials.
* **Flats:** Under certain conditions, corridors and stairs in London flats must be constructed of fire-resisting materials.
* **Height and Roofs:** A 1907 section of the London Building Act requires all existing buildings over 50 feet in height to have means of escape to and from the roof during a fire. Additionally, all existing projecting one-storey shops are required to have fire-resisting roofs.

In New York, the building code (parts 19, 20, and 21) addresses fire-proof floors, buildings, doors, shutters, and escapes. The code requires all tenement houses to be equipped with an iron ladder for escape. Unlike London, timber buildings are permitted in parts of New York.

## Safety and Exit Requirements in France
French regulations for workshops, shops, and factories place a heavy emphasis on "exits" and safety:
* **Doors:** All doors must open outwardly if they lead to interior passages, staircases, vestibules, or courts. If doors open to the open air, outward opening is only obligatory if judged necessary for safety. Doors opening onto staircases or passages must be fixed so they do not project into those areas when open.
* **Windows and Stairs:** Windows are required to open outwardly. In buildings serving as places for work or offices, staircases must be walled in fully in plaster or constructed from incombustible materials. The number of staircases must be proportional to the number of employees.
* **Signage and Hazards:** Exits must be numerous, with conspicuous signs indicating the quickest way out. The use of any liquid that emits inflammable vapours under 35° C for heating or lighting is prohibited unless the apparatus is solidly closed during work to avoid oozing.

## General Planning and Oversight
The planning of buildings is subject to the rules of various controlling authorities. In England, the Board of Education governs public schools, while the Incorporated Church Building Society exercises strong control over churches. Factories in both France and England must meet separate acts. 

Fire insurance companies also influence construction by laying down requirements based on the building's size and the specific trade for which it is intended, adjusting their premium rates accordingly. In cities such as New York, Paris, and London, public buildings and theatres must conform to regulations regarding not only construction but also the stage (including the flies and scenery dock), seating accommodation, and exits.
