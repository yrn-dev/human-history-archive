# Laura Dewey Bridgman

## Early Life and Health
Laura Dewey Bridgman (1829–1889) was an American blind deaf-mute born on December 21, 1829, in Hanover, New Hampshire. She was the third daughter of Harmony and Daniel Bridgman; her father was a substantial Baptist farmer who died in 1868. Her mother, Harmony, was the daughter of Cushman Downer and the granddaughter of Joseph Downer, who was one of the first five settlers of Thetford, Vermont, in 1761.

As an infant, Laura was puny, rickety, and delicate, suffering from fits until she was twenty months old. While her senses initially appeared normal, she suffered a severe attack of scarlet fever at the age of two. This illness destroyed her hearing and sight, blunted her sense of smell, and left her system a wreck. Although her general health eventually recovered, she remained a blind deaf-mute.

## Early Socialization and Communication
During her early years, Laura was treated kindly and found a playmate in Mr. Asa Tenney, an eccentric bachelor friend of the Bridgmans. Once she was able to walk, Mr. Tenney would take her on rambles in the fields.

Before receiving formal education, Laura became familiar with her surroundings and was imitative of the actions of others. However, her communication was limited to the use of touch. She developed a narrow system of signals: rubbing her hand indicated disapproval, patting her head signified approval, pushing in one direction meant she wanted to go, and drawing in another meant she wanted to come. By this time, her mother, who was preoccupied with housework, had lost the ability to control her, and her father's authority was based on the fear of superior force rather than reason.

## Education at the Perkins Institution
In 1837, Mr. James Barrett of Dartmouth College observed Laura and brought her case to the attention of Dr. Mussey, the head of the medical department. Dr. Mussey wrote an account of her condition that attracted Dr. S.G. Howe, the head of the Perkins Institution for the Blind in Boston. Dr. Howe determined to attempt to educate the child and, with the assent of her parents, Laura entered the school in October 1837.

Despite some deformity caused by the loss of her eyeballs, she was described as a comely child with an affectionate and sensitive nature. Upon her arrival, Dr. Howe immediately began teaching her the alphabet through the sense of touch. His instructional method involved teaching whole words before focusing on individual letters.
