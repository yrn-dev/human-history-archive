# The Napoleonic Empire in Southern Italy

The Napoleonic Empire in Southern Italy is the subject of a two-volume historical work authored by R.M. Johnston and published in London in 1904. This work is noted for containing an excellent bibliography and is cited as one of the sources dealing with the life and reign of Joseph Bonaparte.

## Historical Context and Administration
The administration of Italian territories during the Napoleonic era involved various members of the Bonaparte family and their associates. Joseph Bonaparte, a primary focus of Johnston's work, was a central figure in the Napoleonic family's political reach. Other family members also held significant influence over Italian affairs. For instance, Marianne Elisa Bonaparte, who married the Corsican Felix Bacciochi on May 5, 1797, was granted the principality of Piombino and Lucca by Napoleon in 1805. In 1808, she acquired the grand duchy of Tuscany. Due to her ability and pride, she exerted considerable influence over the general administration of Italian affairs, though her relationship with Napoleon was often strained.

## Scientific and Political Developments in Italy
Beyond the direct imperial administration, the Bonaparte family maintained a presence in Italy that intersected with science and regional politics. Charles Lucien Jules Laurent, the son of Lucien Bonaparte and Prince of Canino, resided in Italy and focused heavily on zoology. He published several scientific catalogs in Italian cities, including the *Catalogo metodico dei pesci europei* in Naples (1845), the *Catalogo metodico dei mammiferi europei* in Milan (1845), and the *Catalogo metodico degli uccelli europei* in Bologna (1842).

In the later years of his life, Charles Lucien Jules Laurent engaged in Italian political agitation. Around 1847, he presided over scientific congresses, including one in Venice where he advocated for the expulsion of the Austrians and the independence of Italy. He was elected as a deputy by Viterbo to the national assembly and entered the Junto of Rome in 1848, though he was forced to leave Italy in July 1849 following the failure of the revolution.

## Other Related Figures and Works
The broader Napoleonic influence in the region is further documented through the lives of other associates. Daru, an administrator highly praised by Napoleon for his intellect and firmness, wrote the *Histoire de Venise*, published in Paris in seven volumes in 1819. Additionally, Lucien Bonaparte lived in Italy for several years, primarily in Rome, and held an estate at Canino.

The historical study of this period is supported by various other texts. Biographies of Napoleon by Holland Rose and a compact biography by R.M. Johnston titled *Napoleon* provide further context on the era. The family's history is also detailed in works such as F. Masson's *Napoleon et sa famille* and various memoirs, including those of Count Miot de Melito.
