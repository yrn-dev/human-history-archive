# The Sale of the Marlborough Library and the Boccaccio Edition

## The Roxburghe Auction and the Boccaccio Competition
In 1812, the books of the duke of Roxburghe were sold at auction. This event generated significant excitement, particularly due to the intense competition between Lord Spencer and the duke of Marlborough (who was the marquess of Blandford at the time). The two men competed for a specific edition of Boccaccio, which had been printed in Venice in 1471 by Valdarfer. Following the auction, a commemorative dinner was held, which led to the formation of the Roxburghe Club.

## The Sale of the Marlborough Books
The books belonging to the duke of Marlborough were subsequently sold in 1819. During this sale, the aforementioned edition of Boccaccio—for which the duke had previously paid £2,260—was sold to Earl Spencer (who died in 1834) for the sum of £750.

## Provenance of the Boccaccio Volume
The Boccaccio volume remained with Earl Spencer until 1892, when it passed, along with the rest of his rare books, to Mrs. Rylands. In 1899, Mrs. Rylands gifted the collection to the John Rylands library at Manchester.

## Context of Book Collecting in the Era of George III
The competition for the Boccaccio volume occurred within a broader culture of book-collecting during the reign of George III. While some collectors followed a public-spirited tradition—such as Garrick, who bequeathed English plays to the British Museum, or Sir Joseph Banks, who left natural history books to the same institution—others were celebrated as "noble book-buyers." These collectors often focused on first editions and rarities, though their aims were noted to be less patriotic than those of their predecessors.

The value placed on such works was often fanciful. For example, high prices were paid during the era of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics, despite these often having little value as texts and no direct association with the authors' personalities. Collectors were instead drawn to the pedigree of a work, valuing copies that could be traced through a series of book-loving owners.
