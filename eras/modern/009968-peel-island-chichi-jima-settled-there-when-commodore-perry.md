# Peel Island (Chichi-jima)

Peel Island, known in Japanese as Chichi-jima (Father Island), is the second largest of the Bonin Islands. This chain of small islands, also called Ogasawara-jima by the Japanese, consists of twenty islands stretching nearly due north and south, located approximately 500 miles from the mainland of Japan. The islands are volcanic in nature.

## Geography and Nomenclature
The Bonin Islands have a total superficies of 28.82 square miles and a coastline of 174.65 miles. While twenty islands exist, only ten are of appreciable size. These include Muko-shima (Bridegroom Island), Nakadachi-shima (Go-between Island), Yome-shima (Bride Island), Ototo-jima (Younger-brother Island), Ani-shima (Elder-brother Island), Chichi-jima (Father Island), Haha-jima (Mother Island), Mei-jima (Niece Island), Ani-jima (Elder-sister Island), and Imoto-jima (Younger-sister Island).

European geographers historically divided these into three groups: the Parry Islands in the north, the Beechey Islands in the center, and the Coffin or Bailey Islands in the south. In 1827, Captain Beechey used the name Peel Island for Chichi-jima. Beechey also named Ototo-jima as Stapleton Island and Ani-jima as Buckland Island. Additionally, Beechey named Walker Bay on the south-west of Buckland Island and Fitton Bay on the south of Peel Island.

## Early History and Japanese Possession
According to Japanese annals, the islands were discovered toward the end of the 16th century and were added to the fief of a Daimyo named Ogasawa Sadayori, which provided the name Ogasawara-jima. The name *Bunin-jima* (corrupted by foreigners to "Bonin") was used because the islands were without (*bu*) inhabitants (*nin*).

Effective occupation of the islands was not maintained. In 1635, communications ceased entirely due to the Japanese government's veto against the construction of sea-going vessels. Fitful communication was briefly restored in 1728 by a representative of the Ogasawara family, but this was interrupted again until 1861. In that year, an unsuccessful attempt was made to establish a Japanese colony at Port Lloyd.

## Foreign Settlement and Administration
In 1830, a small party consisting of a Dane, two American citizens, and two British subjects sailed from the Sandwich Islands to Port Lloyd, accompanied by some Hawaiian natives. This group settled on Peel Island and hoisted the British flag.

By 1853, when Commodore Perry visited the islands, the population of Peel Island consisted of thirty-one inhabitants. This group included four Americans, four English, one Portuguese, and the remainder were natives of the Ladrones and the Sandwich Islands. By 1875, when Mr. Russell Robertson visited, the colony had grown to sixty-nine people, though only five were "pure whites." Robertson observed that the settlers lived comfortably on cultivated land but lacked laws, religion, education, and a system of government. The settlers spoke English and viewed themselves as a British colony.

Despite this settlement, the British government renounced all claims to the islands in 1861, recognizing Japan's right of possession. Following this, the population grew to approximately 4,500, and the islands were duly administered with regular steam communication established.

## Port Lloyd and Commodore Perry
Port Lloyd serves as the chief anchorage on Peel Island. During his visit in 1853, Commodore Perry strongly urged the establishment of a United States coaling station at this location. Perry believed that Port Lloyd had formerly been the crater of a volcano, suggesting that the surrounding hills were thrown up from it and that the harbor entrance was a fissure through which lava once poured into the sea.
