# Jacques Pierre Brissot

Jacques Pierre Brissot (1754–1793), who adopted the name de Warville, was a prominent French Girondist. Born in January 1754 in Chartres, he was the son of an inn-keeper. Brissot received a quality education and pursued a legal career, entering the office of a lawyer in Paris.

## Early Writings and Philosophy
Brissot's initial literary contributions focused on the philosophy of law. In 1781, he published *Théorie des lois criminelles*, followed by *Bibliothèque philosophique du législateur* in 1782. These works demonstrated that he was thoroughly influenced by the ethical precepts of Rousseau. His first work was dedicated to Voltaire, who received it with significant favor.

## Journalistic Career and Political Activism
Recognized as an able and facile writer, Brissot contributed to several publications, including the *Courrier de l'Europe* and the *Mercure*. Driven by a devotion to the service of humanity, he attempted to establish a general concourse of all European savants. To facilitate this, he started a paper in London called the *Journal du Lycée de Londres* to serve as an organ for their views.

This project proved unsuccessful. Upon returning to Paris, Brissot was imprisoned in the Bastille for four months on charges of publishing a work against the government. Following his release, he returned to pamphleteering but was eventually forced to retire to London for a period of time.

## Abolitionism and American Visit
During his second visit to London, Brissot became acquainted with several leading Abolitionists. This connection led him to found the Société des Amis des Noirs in Paris, where he served as president during 1790 and 1791. In his capacity as an agent for this society, Brissot visited the United States in 1788. He later documented this experience in his three-volume work, *Nouveau Voyage dans les États-Unis de l'Amerique Septentrionale*, published in 1791.
