# Geography of British India and Adjacent Plateaus

## Territorial Extent of British India
British India encompasses an area situated approximately between the 70th and 95th meridians. Its northern boundary is defined by the Tibetan table-land, while its southern boundary is the Indian Ocean. 

The region is characterized by diverse topographical features. From 25 degrees North southward, the Indian peninsula consists of a table-land. The elevation of this peninsula is greatest in the west, where the highest points exceed 8,000 ft., although the higher hills typically do not exceed 4,000 ft. The general level of this table-land ranges from a maximum of 3,000 ft. down to 1,000 ft.

## The Great Plain of Northern India
Between the foot of the Himalayan slope of the Tibetan plateau and the peninsula's table-land lies the great plain of northern India. This plain extends from the Indus delta in the west to the deltas of the Brahmaputra and Ganges in the east. Including its prolongation up the valley of Assam, the plain covers an area of approximately 500,000 sq. m. and contains the most civilized, populous, and richest districts of India.

The plain reaches a maximum height of about 1,000 ft. and maintains an almost unbroken surface from the eastern to the western extremity of British India. The deposits composing the plain are so finely comminuted that a traveler could theoretically journey over 2,000 m.—from the Bay of Bengal up the Ganges, through the Punjab, and down the Indus to the sea—without encountering a single pebble.

## The Western Asian Plateau
To the west of British India lies another extensive plateau, excluding Syria and Arabia, which is similar in size to Tibet but situated at a lower altitude. This region is nearly conterminous with Armenia, Asia Minor, Iran (or Persia), Baluchistan, and Afghanistan.

The borders of this plateau are defined as follows:
*   **North:** The high land follows nearly 36 degrees N. to the Black Sea, the Sea of Marmora, and the southern shore of the Caspian.
*   **South and Southwest:** The border follows the coast of the Persian Gulf from the mouth of the Indus. Further west, it is formed by a mountain scarp that flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris, rising in many places to 10,000 ft. and extending through Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian before turning along the Taurus range and the northeast angle of the Mediterranean.
*   **South:** The southern border runs along the Tigris, the Persian Gulf, and the Arabian Sea.
*   **East:** The eastern flank consists of a line of hills located a short distance from the Indus, between the Himalaya and the river's mouth, approximately on the 72nd meridian. While these hills generally do not exceed 5,000 or 4,000 ft., some summits reach 10,000 ft. or more.

## Mountain Ranges and Water Dividers
The northeastern portion of the Afghan table-land meets Tibet and the Himalaya, forming a continuous mountain mass between the 71st and 72nd meridians and between 34 and 36 degrees N. 

From the intersection of the 36th parallel and the 71st meridian, an unbroken mountain range extends in two directions:
1.  **Northeast:** Up to the crest of the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau.
2.  **West:** Nearly due west as far as the Caspian.

The northeastern section of this range is of high altitude and serves as a divide between different water systems. It separates the headwaters of the Oxus, which flow toward the Aral Sea, from the headwaters of the Indus and its tributary, the Kabul. The Indus and Kabul unite below Peshawar and discharge southward into the Arabian Sea. The western portion of this range, known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons, decreases in height west of the 65th meridian.
