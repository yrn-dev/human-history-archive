# Literacy and Education in Global Contexts

The ability to read and write varies significantly across different regions and historical periods, influenced by the complexity of writing systems, the availability of educational infrastructure, and technological advancements in the production of texts.

## Literacy and Education in Bolivia
In Bolivia, despite the existence of a compulsory and free school system, progress in education has been limited. Only a small percentage of the population is literate. A primary barrier to education is the linguistic divide; while Spanish is the language used by the dominant minority, the majority of the population are natives who speak Guarani, Aymara, and Quichua. Many of these native people do not understand Spanish and resist efforts to impose the language upon them. This linguistic gap extends to the *cholos* (mestizos), who are often more familiar with native idioms than with Spanish.

Official estimates from 1901 indicate that the republic had 733 primary schools, 938 teachers, and 41,587 pupils. The cost of maintaining these schools was estimated at 585,365 bolivianos, which averages to approximately 14.07 bolivianos per pupil. School enrollment was low, with only one in 43.7 of the population enrolled, a figure significantly lower than the one in 10 ratio found in Argentina. While nearly half of the schools are maintained by the national government, private means, or the Church, the municipalities hold large-scale control. However, the Church practically supervises all educational matters.

Higher education in Bolivia includes 14 institutions of superior instruction, including universities in Oruro, Santa Cruz, Potosi, Tarija, Cochabamba, La Paz, and Sucre. All of these provide instruction in law, while the first four provide instruction in theology and the first three provide instruction in medicine. Only the universities at La Paz and Sucre—the latter dating from colonial times—are considered sufficiently well-equipped to merit the title of university. Secondary instruction is managed by the universities, and general public instruction is directed by a cabinet minister. Specialized institutions also exist, including a military academy and an agricultural school in La Paz, a civil engineering and mining school in Oruro, and commercial schools in Trinidad and Sucre.

## Writing Systems and Literacy in Asia
The complexity of a writing system directly impacts the ease with which a population can achieve literacy. Most of the world's great writing systems are based on Mediterranean alphabets, which are more easily mastered than the Chinese system. In China, learners must master a vast number of complex word groups and signs, as well as the established groupings used to represent meanings, often requiring the study of exemplary classical works. Consequently, while many people in China may recognize frequent characters, only a few possess the knowledge required to understand a newspaper paragraph, and even fewer can grasp fine shades of meaning or subtleties of intention.

Japanese writing, derived from Chinese, utilizes a system of forms that are written more rapidly. It employs ideograms taken from Chinese as well as a syllabary to express syllables. While less clumsy than Chinese, it is still considered a clumsy system, similar to cuneiform, leading to movements within Japan to adopt a Western alphabet. Korea developed a true alphabet from Chinese origins.

## The Impact of Printing in Europe
The advent of printing had a transformative effect on European literacy. The achievement of printing led to a swift spread of reading knowledge due to the cheapening of school-books and an abundance of Bibles. Books became plainer and easier to understand, allowing readers to think unimpeded rather than struggling with "crabbed" texts. This shift expanded the reading public and changed the nature of authorship; books were no longer treated as scholar's mysteries or decorated toys, but were written for ordinary people using ordinary language instead of Latin. This transition marked the beginning of the real history of European literature in the fourteenth century.
