# Agglutination

Agglutination is a property that may be possessed by an anti-bacterial serum. It is defined as the aggregation of bacteria, which are uniformly distributed in an indifferent fluid, into clumps. If the bacteria involved are motile, their movement is arrested during this process.

## Observation and Physical Effects
The process of agglutination is observed via a microscope. However, the resulting clumps eventually settle within the fluid to form a sediment, which leaves the upper portion of the fluid clear. This specific change, which is visible to the naked eye, is referred to as sedimentation.

The phenomenon was first demonstrated by G. E. H. Roger and B. J. A. Charrin using *B. pyocyaneus*. They observed that adding a small quantity of homologous serum—defined as the serum of an animal immunized against the bacterium—to a fluid culture of the bacillus resulted in the growth forming a sediment rather than a uniform turbidity. Further research by Durham and Gruber showed that sedimentation occurred when homologous serum was added to a bacterial emulsion in a small test-tube, noting that this occurred in all cases where Pfeiffer's lysogenic action could be demonstrated.

## Mechanisms of Action
The phenomenon of agglutination depends essentially on the union of agglutinins with the corresponding agglutinogens, which are molecules in the bacteria. Additionally, the presence of a certain amount of salts in the fluid is essential; it has been shown that if agglutinated masses of bacteria are washed salt-free, the clumps resolve.

There are differing views regarding the mode of action of agglutinins:
* **Envelope Change:** Gruber and Durham proposed that agglutinins cause a change in the envelopes of the bacteria, leading them to swell and become adhesive.
* **Coagulative Ferments:** E. Duclaux considers agglutinins to be coagulative ferments. This view is supported by findings from C. Nicolle and F. Kruse, who discovered that an agglutinating serum still produces changes and gathers particles into clumps even when a bacterial culture is filtered germ-free.

During the process of agglutination, agglutinins are used up, apparently combining with an element of the bacterial structure.

## Medical Application and Immunity
Agglutination has served as the foundation for serum diagnosis. Widal and Gruenbaum demonstrated that the serum of patients suffering from typhoid fever agglutinated the typhoid bacillus, even during the early stages of the disease. Similar phenomena have been demonstrated in various other infections, including plague, cholera, Malta fever, infection with *B. coli*, and "meat-poisoning" caused by Gaertner's bacillus.

Some observers previously suggested that because agglutinins appear early in a disease, they are a reaction of infection and unrelated to immunity. However, this conclusion is considered unjustified. Instead, it is supposed that the process of immunization begins at an early period of the disease, gradually increases, and ultimately results in a cure. Agglutinins and immune bodies are viewed as the result of corresponding reactive processes and are likely related.

The formation of antitoxins and all antagonistic substances that give antimicrobic sera their special character can be expressed as the formation of bodies with a specific combining affinity for an introduced organic substance, such as a red corpuscle, bacterium, or toxin. Because a bacterium is a complex organic substance, it may give rise to more than one combining or antagonistic substance.
