# Prefectural Administration

In several national administrative systems, the role of the prefect serves as a primary instrument of territorial governance, acting as the chief executive officer of a specific regional division. This position is characterized by its appointment from a central authority and its role in extending national executive power into local districts.

## Administration in Bolivia
In Bolivia, the supreme administration of each department is vested in a prefect. These officials are appointed by the president and are solely responsible to him. Because the prefect holds the power to appoint subordinate department officials—including the *alcaldes*—the authority of the national executive is extended to every hamlet within the republic, a structure that may easily become autocratic. 

The administrative hierarchy beneath the prefect consists of sub-prefects in the provinces, *corregidores* in the cantons, and *alcaldes* in the rural districts, all of whom are appointed officials. In Bolivia, there are no legislative assemblies within the departments; instead, government is managed by the congress and the national executive. While department capitals have municipal councils with jurisdiction over certain local affairs and the maintenance of some highways, the overarching administrative power remains centralized.

## Administration in France
France utilizes a system of departments that were established following the French Revolution to replace the provinces of the old régime, which were viewed as being tied to administrative mismanagement. At the suggestion of Mirabeau, the thirty-four provinces were divided into eighty-three departments, intended to be roughly equal in population and size and named primarily after geographical features such as mountains and rivers. 

The number of departments fluctuated over time: increasing to one hundred and thirty under Napoleon, reducing to eighty-six in 1815, and increasing by three in 1860 with the annexation of Nice and Savoy. Following the German war in 1871, three departments (Moselle, Bas-Rhin, and Haut-Rhin) were lost. The remaining fragments of Moselle were incorporated into Meurthe, which became Meurthe-et-Moselle, and a territory called Belfort was formed from the remains of the Haut-Rhin, resulting in eighty-seven departments. Each French department is presided over by a prefect appointed by the government and assisted by a prefectorial council (*conseil de préfecture*). These departments are further subdivided into arrondissements, each managed by a sub-prefect, which are then divided into cantons and communes.

## Administration in Japan
In Japan, each prefecture is led by a prefect, also referred to as a governor. Both the prefect and the headman of the county assembly are appointed by the Central Administration. While the prefect holds executive authority, the system includes a mechanism for oversight: a prefectural assembly, which holds one annual session of thirty days, has the competence to appeal the decisions of the prefect to the minister of Home Affairs.

## Administration in Costa Rica
For the purposes of local administration, Costa Rica is divided into two maritime districts (*comarcas*)—Puntarenas and Limon—and five provinces: San Jose, Heredia, Guanacaste, Cartago, and Alajuela. With the exception of Guanacaste, these divisions share names with their chief towns. Each of these administrative divisions is controlled by a prefect or governor who is appointed by the president.
