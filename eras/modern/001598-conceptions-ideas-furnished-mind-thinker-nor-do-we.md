# Conceptions and Ideas in Human Thought

The study of ideas and conceptions encompasses a wide range of philosophical, psychological, and historical interpretations. In general usage, an "idea" is understood as a "mental picture," such as when a person lacks an idea of how an event occurred. In philosophical terminology, however, the term has been applied with numerous varying shades of meaning across different eras and schools of thought.

## Philosophical Interpretations of the "Idea"

Different philosophers have defined the nature and origin of ideas in contrasting ways. Plato utilized the term in metaphysics to describe an eternally existing reality or archetype. According to this view, physical objects—such as chairs of various sizes and colors—are imperfect copies of a single permanent idea or type "laid up in the mind of God." For Plato, these ideas constitute the sole reality. This stands in opposition to empirical thinkers who locate reality in particular physical objects.

In contrast, John Locke defined an idea as "whatever is the object of understanding when a man thinks," applying the term to the object of the mental process rather than the process itself. David Hume differed from Locke by limiting "idea" to vague mental reconstructions of perceptions, while describing the actual perceptual process as an "impression." 

Other psychological and philosophical perspectives further refine these definitions. Wundt expanded the term to include the conscious representation of an object or process from the external world, incorporating both imagination and memory. G. F. Stout and J. M. Baldwin defined an idea as the reproduction of an object not present to the senses using a more or less adequate image.

## Methodological and Systematic Applications

The application of general ideas to practical or scientific problems varies by thinker. Edmund Burke is noted for his success in using the general ideas of the thinker to judge the particular problems of the statesman, maintaining that practical politics can only be understood through the aid of broad conceptions of political philosophy.

In the realm of scientific induction, the Baconian method presents a different approach. The *notiones* discussed by Francis Bacon do not correspond to "conceptions or ideas furnished by the mind of the thinker," nor did Bacon view such elements as necessary for the inductive process. Instead, he focused on establishing axioms through induction to avoid grasping at "abstract forms" and instead focus on things "realized in matter."

Similarly, the Associationist School, featuring thinkers such as Condillac, sought to explain all knowledge through the single principle of the association (*liaison*) of ideas. This was opposed by Immanuel Kant, who argued that there are elements in knowledge that no mere association of experiences could explain.

## Religious and Mythological Conceptions

Ideas often evolve through cultural contact or the observation of nature. In the Book of Daniel, a complete system of angelology is presented, where a hierarchy of commanding angels—including Gabriel and Michael—manages human affairs. This conception was foreign to the primitive Israelitish view of the indivisibility of Yahweh's power and is considered a borrowed idea from Persian sources. Similarly, the doctrine of the resurrection of the dead, first appearing in the Old Testament in Daniel, is attributed to Persian influence and the Zoroastrian religion.

In early human mythology, Neolithic man developed a disposition to associate the sun and the serpent in worship. With the advent of agriculture, new ideas arose, such as the association of sowing with burial. This led to the conception of sacrificial persons killed at seedtime, a purified class of priests to perform these killings, and the conception of a sacrament involving a ceremonial feast to share in sacrificial benefits.

## Metaphysical and Psychological Frameworks

The concept of immanence in philosophy and theology refers to the condition of being entirely within something. This is often applied to the conception of God as existing throughout the created world, as opposed to Deism, which views God as separate from the universe. This immanence doctrine can arise from metaphysical speculation regarding the unity of mind and matter, leading to pantheism (where the universe is a mode of deity) or naturalism (where the deity is the sum of nature's forces).

Regarding the development of the human mind, the thinker Vico proposed that poetical and religious susceptibilities develop first. He identified a "personifying instinct" (*istinto d’ animazione*) as a spontaneous philosophy where man supposes a quasi-human agency as the determining cause of the universe. In this early stage, the language of poetry and the conceptions of reality coincided.

Finally, the thinker Lucretius is noted for his grasp of speculative ideas and their application to nature and human life. He viewed all phenomena as part of one great organic whole termed "Natura daedala rerum," a conception that united ideas of law, order, and ever-changing life.
