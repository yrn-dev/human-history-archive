# Commercial Treaties and the Austro-Hungarian Customs Union

The economic relationship between Austria and Hungary was governed by a series of customs and commercial agreements, most notably a Customs Union established in 1867. This arrangement was designed to be renewable every ten years and covered several key areas of shared administration, including commercial affairs, customs legislation, and the determination of coinage and legal tender. It also governed legislation on duties related to industrial production—specifically mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer—as well as the principles regulating the Austro-Hungarian Bank and certain railway ordinances.

## Evolution of Trade Policy
In the early stages of the dual state, the customs tariff was based on various commercial treaties with nations such as Italy, France, Great Britain, and Germany. These treaties included "most favoured nation" clauses and specified maximum duties for certain articles, resulting in a system that nearly lacked customs duties. Because these treaties were in effect, the tariff could not be revised through legislation.

Following 1873, Austrian manufacturers, facing competition from Belgian, English, and German goods, pushed for protective duties. This aligned with a broader shift in economic thought against free trade. Conversely, Hungary favored free trade to ensure a secure market for its agricultural produce, as it lacked significant manufacturing industries. After the commercial treaties expired after 1875, Hungary gave notice to terminate the commercial union. Negotiations during 1877 led to a new treaty where raw materials remained duty-free, but low duties were placed on corn and textile goods, and excises on brandy and sugar were increased. Additionally, all duties were required to be paid in gold. During this period, treaties with France and Great Britain were not renewed, and attempts to reach an agreement with Germany failed due to the policy changes of Bismarck.

## The 1899 Agreement and the 1903 Deadline
A customs and commercial compact renewed in 1899 established the monarchy as a single commercial and customs territory, which included the principality of Liechtenstein as well as Bosnia and Herzegovina. 

The agreement contained specific provisions regarding its renewal and the expiration of treaties:
*   **Bank Charter:** The charter for the Bank was renewed until 1910, with Hungarians granted a larger share in its direction.
*   **Customs Union:** The reciprocal and binding treaty lapsed, though both parties continued it in practice with a promise to do so until December 31, 1907.
*   **Renewal Timeline:** Negotiations for a renewal of the alliance were to begin no later than 1901. The goal was to renew the alliance starting in 1903, the year in which the commercial treaties were set to expire.

If renewal occurred by 1903, the tariff would be revised before new commercial treaties were established. If not, no new treaties would be made extending beyond 1907. This meant that if the Union was not renewed by 1907, each party would be free to determine its own policy without the constraints of previous treaties.

## Political Conflict and the 1907 Settlement
The implementation of these arrangements faced political hurdles. While the Hungarian parliament sanctioned the arrangements, the Austrian government could not procure similar sanction; consequently, the measures—including the Bank charter and new duties on sugar, brandy, and beer—were proclaimed by imperial warrant. In November, it was agreed that Hungary would pay 33-3/4%, a small increase, which was also proclaimed in Austria.

By 1905 and 1906, political tensions rose as the Hungarian Coalition and the crown negotiated conditions including the ratification of commercial treaties concluded by Tisza. During this period, the Hungarian government sought more autonomy, including the authorization to compile an autonomous Hungarian tariff. This led to the resignation of the Austrian premier, Prince Konrad Hohenlohe. His successor, Baron Beck, asserted that the only remaining economic ties were the management of excise, the joint state bank, freedom of trade, and commercial treaties with foreign countries. He emphasized that a new agreement must be reached before the end of 1907, when the 1899 reciprocity arrangement would lapse.

Ultimately, an agreement was reached in October 1907, driven by a severe economic crisis in Hungary that highlighted the risk of commercial ruin if separated from Austria. Austria similarly recognized that separation would increase the cost of living in Cisleithania and remove the best market for Austrian manufacturers. The new treaty established that each state would have a separate but identical customs tariff and created a court of arbitration to settle differences.
