# Germany

## Political Evolution and Unification
The development of the modern German state was shaped by two contrasting factors: a scientific, organizing, and educating spirit, and the influence of the Hohenzollern monarchy. The former was a progression of the liberal Germany of 1848 and grew from efforts to recover after the Napoleonic conquest, with schoolmasters playing a primary role in this advancement. The latter, the Prussian monarchy, preserved the traditions of the eighteenth-century Grand Monarchy and was guided by the influence of Frederick the Great.

In 1848, a German parliament attempted to unite all of Germany, including German Austria, under a democratic framework. This body insisted that the German provinces of Schleswig-Holstein belong to Germany and ordered the Prussian army to occupy them. However, the King of Prussia refused to take orders from the parliament, leading to the body's downfall. Later, Von Bismarck, a minister of the seventeenth-century type, utilized a conflict with King Christian IX of Denmark over these same duchies to champion German nationality. After persuading Austria to join Prussia in a military intervention to defeat Denmark, Bismarck initiated a fratricidal war against Austria to ensure the ascendancy of the Hohenzollern dynasty and the glory of Prussia.

## International Relations and Diplomacy
Germany's diplomatic standing was significantly impacted by the events at Algeciras. During this period, Great Britain's attitude turned the scale against Germany, as Russia, Spain, and other signatory powers aligned with England and France. While a compromise suggested by Austria allowed Germany to withdraw without a total loss of dignity, the event left Germany isolated. This isolation led Germany to seek a closer bond with Austria and caused France to implement a thorough reform of its army and strengthen its *Entente* with England.

In later conflicts, Germany's military strategy shifted. By the summer of 1916, following the battle of Verdun, Germany had remained largely on the defensive, with one exception south of Cambrai. Because victory cannot be achieved through defensive action alone, Germany faced an absolute necessity to resume offensive action. Furthermore, Germany's relations with Russia were strained by continued aggression. Despite a peace agreement, Germany's tendency toward materialism and force led it to march toward Petrograd and Moscow once the Russian border was undefended, regardless of morality or treaties.

## Post-War Reparations and Economic Obligations
Following the war, Germany was subject to strict requirements managed by the Reparations Commission. To effect payment through deliveries in kind, Germany was required to provide specific amounts of chemical drugs, dyestuffs, coal-tar products, and coal for a limited number of years. Regarding dyestuffs and chemical drugs, including quinine, the commission held an option for up to 50 percent of Germany's total stock at the time the treaty took effect, and 25 percent of the output every six months until the end of 1924. Coal deliveries were primarily intended to compensate allied countries for production losses caused by the war.

Germany was also obligated to use its economic resources for the physical restoration of invaded areas. This included manufacturing reconstruction materials and replacing destroyed articles with machinery and animals existing within Germany, provided these requirements did not unduly interfere with essential domestic needs.

## Financial Oversight and Legal Constraints
The Reparations Commission was authorized to examine the German system of taxation to ensure that reparation payments took priority over the discharge of any domestic loans. The commission also sought to verify that Germany's taxation scheme was proportionately as heavy as those of the other powers on the commission. In the event of a voluntary default by Germany, the allied and associated powers reserved the right to implement financial and economic reprisals and prohibitions, which Germany agreed not to characterize as acts of war.

Regarding property and debts, Germany was required to pay for or restore all seized or damaged private enemy property, with damages determined by a mixed arbitral tribunal. Allied and associated States were permitted to liquidate German private property within their own territories as compensation for any property not restored. Additionally, pre-war contracts between German nationals and nationals of allied and associated states (excluding Brazil, Japan, and the United States) were cancelled, except for accounts already performed.

## Treaties and Transit Rights
Germany's treaty obligations were heavily modified. All treaties entered into by Germany since August 1, 1914, with enemy states, as well as those with Russia, Rumania, and governments representing parts of Russia, were abrogated. Concessions granted to German subjects under pressure from Russia were annulled. However, allied and associated States were granted most favored nation treatment under treaties Germany had signed with neutral states during the war and with enemy states before August 1, 1914.

Until 1923—unless Germany was admitted to the League of Nations or the relevant aerial navigation convention—aircraft from allied and associated powers were granted full liberty of passage and landing in German territory. These aircraft were to receive treatment equal to German planes regarding the use of airdromes and most favored nation planes regarding internal commercial traffic.
