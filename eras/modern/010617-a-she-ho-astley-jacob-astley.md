# JACOB ASTLEY

## Early Life and Continental Service
Jacob Astley, Baron (1570–1652), was a royalist commander during the English Civil War who originated from a family in Norfolk. His military career began in 1598 when he joined Counts Henry and Maurice of Orange in the Netherlands. During his time there, he served with distinction. He later fought in the Thirty Years' War under Gustavus Adolphus and Frederick V, the elector palatine. Astley was held in high regard by the states-general; this was evidenced by the fact that his company in the Dutch army was kept open for him while he was away serving under the king of Denmark.

## Military Career under Charles I
Upon returning to England with a well-deserved reputation, Astley entered the service of Charles I in various military roles. In 1639, acting as general of the infantry (or "sergeant-major"), he traveled north to organize defenses against a predicted invasion by the Scots. In this capacity, his responsibilities were both military and diplomatic, occurring as the discontent that would eventually lead to the Civil War reached a head. Astley provided good service to the king's cause during the "Bishops' War" and was involved in the "Army Plot."

## The English Civil War
At the start of the Great Rebellion in 1642, Astley joined Charles I and was appointed major-general of the foot. He became known for a characteristic battle-prayer delivered at Edgehill: "O Lord, Thou knowest how busy I must be this day. If I forget Thee, do not forget me. March on, boys!"

Throughout the conflict, Astley held several key commands:
* **Gloucester:** He commanded a division.
* **First Battle of Newbury:** He led the royal army's infantry.
* **Arundel and Cheriton:** He served alongside Hopton in 1644.
* **Second Battle of Newbury:** He conducted a memorable and gallant defense of Shaw House.
* **Naseby:** He commanded the main body of the foot.

During the war, the king granted him the title of baron.

## Final Campaigns and Later Life
Astley later served in the west. In March 1646, he fought the final battle for the king at Stow-on-the-Wold with 1,500 men, though the effort was vain. Following his capture, he made a remark that became as famous as his prayer at Edgehill, telling his captors, "You have now done your work and may go play, unless you will fall out amongst yourselves."

Because he had given his parole at Stow-on-the-Wold, Astley's sense of scrupulous honour prevented him from participating in the Second Civil War. However, he still experienced the discomforts typical of vanquished royalists. He died in February 1652.
