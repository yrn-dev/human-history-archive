# Philadelphia

Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, has served as a significant center for American education, science, religion, and the arts. The city has hosted numerous influential academic institutions, learned societies, and religious organizations that have shaped national and international intellectual life.

## Academic and Scientific Institutions
The University of Pennsylvania (formerly the College of Philadelphia) has been a cornerstone of the city's academic landscape. Early figures associated with the college include Thomas Hopkinson (1709–1751), who was one of its first trustees, and his son, Francis Hopkinson (1737–1791), who was the first student to enter the institution, receiving his bachelor's degree in 1757 and master's degree in 1760. Benjamin Smith Barton (1766–1815), a naturalist, was appointed professor of botany and natural history at the college in 1789, later becoming professor of *materia medica* in 1795 and holding the chair of practical medicine starting in 1813.

The city is also home to the Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia, where Daniel Garrison Brinton (1837–1899) served as professor of ethnology and archaeology beginning in 1884. Additionally, the American Philosophical Society has had notable leadership within the city; its first president was Thomas Hopkinson, and Benjamin Smith Barton was chosen as its president in 1802.

## Religious Organizations and Missions
Philadelphia played a pivotal role in the establishment of national religious societies. The earliest Bible society in America was founded in Philadelphia in 1808. This organization eventually amalgamated with other regional societies to form the American Bible Society in 1816, a process completed in 1839 when the Philadelphia Society joined the national body and changed its name to the Pennsylvania Bible Society.

The city was also central to Baptist missionary efforts. In May 1814, thirty-three delegates from eleven states met in Philadelphia to organize the "General Missionary Convention of the Baptist Denomination in the United States of America for Foreign Missions," which became known as the "Triennial Convention" because its meetings occurred every three years. The convention's Board of Commissioners established its headquarters in Philadelphia, though these were transferred to Boston in 1826. Furthermore, Henry Christopher McCook (b. 1837) served for a long period as the pastor of the Tabernacle Presbyterian Church in Philadelphia.

## Intellectual and Cultural Figures
Philadelphia has been the residence and place of work for various prominent scholars and artists. Daniel Garrison Brinton, an archaeologist and ethnologist, lived and worked in the city, serving as a professor at the University of Pennsylvania from 1886 until his death in 1899. Brinton was a leader in several learned societies, serving as president of the Numismatic and Antiquarian Society of Philadelphia, the American Folk-Lore Society, and the American Association for the Advancement of Science.

In the realm of literature and journalism, Rufus Wilmot Griswold (1815–1857) edited *Graham's Magazine* in Philadelphia from 1841 to 1843, where he recruited many leading American writers. The city also saw the work of Francis Hopkinson, a versatile author and satirist. During the War of Independence, Hopkinson wrote the popular ballad *The Battle of the Kegs* (1778), which referenced an American effort to alarm British forces in Philadelphia by floating gunpowder-charged kegs down the Delaware River.

In the arts, the Philadelphia Exposition served as a venue for exhibiting works by artists such as Albert F. Bellows (1829–1883), who displayed his water-colour painting "Sunday in Devonshire" (1876) there.
