# Unionist Free-Traders in the Cabinet: Mr Ritchie and Lord George

The presence of "out-and-out Unionist free-traders" within the cabinet during the premiership of Mr Balfour was a central point of tension regarding the fiscal policy of England. Among the most prominent of these figures were Mr Ritchie and Lord George Hamilton. Their tenure and eventual resignations were dictated by the conflict between traditional free-trade orthodoxy and the "Tariff Reform" movement led by Mr Chamberlain.

## Cabinet Appointments and Roles
Following the resignation of Lord Salisbury on July 11, 1902, Mr Balfour assumed the role of prime minister. In the subsequent reconstitution of the cabinet, several key positions were filled by individuals who held varying views on fiscal policy. Mr Ritchie was appointed as the chancellor of the exchequer, taking the place of Sir Michael Hicks-Beach. Simultaneously, Lord George Hamilton was appointed as the secretary for India. Other members of this administration included the duke of Devonshire as leader of the House of Lords and Mr J. Chamberlain as colonial secretary.

## Conflict Over Tariff Reform
The stability of the cabinet was challenged starting in mid-May when Mr Chamberlain began pressing for a new fiscal programme. Mr Chamberlain advocated for a general tariff that included new taxes on food, designed to provide a preference for colonial products. This proposal represented a radical alteration of the established fiscal system.

Mr Ritchie and Lord George Hamilton, as strait free-traders within the cabinet, were entirely opposed to this scheme. They were joined in their opposition by others outside the cabinet, such as Mr Arthur Elliot (the secretary to the treasury) and Lord Hugh Cecil. While Mr Balfour admitted that the English free-trade system was one-sided and supported retaliation against "dumping" and unfair competition by foreign countries, he remained uncertain about the details of Mr Chamberlain's plan and the feelings of the country. Consequently, Mr Balfour professed to have "no settled convictions" on the subject, attempting to avoid a rupture within his party.

## Resignations and the "Insular Free Trade" Pamphlet
The tension over the fiscal question continued through the end of the session in August. On September 16, Mr Balfour published a pamphlet titled "Insular Free Trade." In this manifesto, the prime minister reviewed economic history since the time of Cobden, argued that England remained the only free-importing country, and claimed he was in harmony with the "true spirit of free-trade" by seeking the freedom to negotiate to increase the freedom of exchange.

The publication of this pamphlet served as a catalyst for cabinet departures. On September 18, it was announced that Lord George Hamilton and Mr Ritchie had resigned. They were followed a day or two later by Mr Arthur Elliot and Lord Balfour of Burleigh. At the same time, Mr Chamberlain also resigned. 

The resignations were marked by confusion and personal distrust. Lord George Hamilton stated that Mr Balfour had circulated a second, different document among colleagues that was in fuller agreement with Mr Chamberlain's views. Despite these departures, the duke of Devonshire, whose orthodoxy was viewed as typical, initially remained in the cabinet.

## Aftermath and Party Division
The departure of the free-trade ministers was part of a broader split within the Conservative and Unionist forces. Protection, or "Tariff Reform," divided the party, leading to the resignation of six ministers and the withdrawal of support from fifty Conservative or Unionist members. This loss of personnel was described as the most formidable the party had sustained since the expulsion of the Peelites.

The free-trade Unionists, led by figures such as the duke of Devonshire, Lord James, Lord Goschen, and Lord Hugh Cecil, formed a "Free Food league" to oppose Mr Chamberlain's "Tariff Reform league." By November 24, at a meeting at Queen's Hall in London, they explicitly rejected food taxes and criticized Mr Balfour's assumption that retaliation would be desirable.

The fiscal dissensions eventually contributed to the wrecking of Mr Balfour's administration. While Mr Balfour attempted to maintain a middle ground—refusing to go "the whole hog" with Mr Chamberlain while not discouraging his campaign—the internal struggle continued throughout 1903. The administration eventually collapsed, partly due to these fiscal divisions and partly due to other issues, including the Education Act and the introduction of Chinese labour into South Africa in 1904.
