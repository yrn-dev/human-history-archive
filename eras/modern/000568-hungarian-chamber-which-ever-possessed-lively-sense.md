# Hungarian Chamber

The Hungarian Chamber (also referred to as the Hungarian Chamber of Deputies) served as a central political body in the governance of Hungary, particularly within the dual system established with Austria. The Chamber was characterized by a strong sense of its own prerogatives and was frequently the site of conflict between the Hungarian government, the crown, and various political factions regarding national identity, military control, and economic autonomy.

## Political Composition and the Independence Party
The political landscape of the Chamber was often dominated by the struggle between the crown and the Opposition. A primary force within the Opposition was the independence party, led by Francis Kossuth, the eldest son of Louis Kossuth. This party advocated for the political and economic separation of Hungary from Austria, with the exception of the person of the ruler. 

By May 1906, a general election resulted in a majority of members belonging to the Kossuth or Independence party. This group was pledged to the separation of the two nations and sought to emphasize Hungarian independence through various legislative measures. Another political entity mentioned in the archive is the Deák party, to which Georg Klapka—a former soldier and minister of war—belonged after being elected to the Chamber of Deputies in 1867.

## Military Disputes and National Identity
The Hungarian Chamber frequently clashed with military authorities over the recruitment and character of the army. In the autumn of 1902, bills were presented to increase annual recruit contingents by 21,000 men, with Hungary contributing approximately 6,500 men. A conflict arose when military authorities detained several thousand supernumerary recruits in barracks before the bills received parliamentary sanction to avoid the delay of summoning them again.

The Hungarian Chamber resented this action as a violation of its prerogatives. The Opposition, led by Francis Kossuth, used this grievance to obstruct ministerial measures and refuse the sanctioning of estimates until the supernumeraries were discharged. Even after the recruits were released at the end of 1902, the Opposition demanded that the Army bills be withdrawn or counterbalanced by concessions to Magyar nationalist feeling. These demands included promoting the use of the Magyar language within the Hungarian part of the army and increasing the Magyar character of Hungarian regiments, many of which were not purely Magyar.

## Constitutional Deadlocks and Suffrage
Between January 1905 and April 1906, a significant deadlock occurred between the crown and Hungary. The Coalition, which held the majority in the Chamber, refused to take office unless the crown granted demands regarding customs separation from Austria and the use of Magyar words of command. 

During this period, a non-parliamentary administration under General Baron Fejervary took power in June 1905. Fejervary proposed a scheme, drafted by Interior Minister Kristoffy, to resolve the dispute through the introduction of universal suffrage. This proposal alarmed the Coalition, as they feared universal suffrage would destroy the hegemony of the Magyar gentry and nobility and undermine the supremacy of the Magyar race by granting political equality to non-Magyars. The deadlock only ended after the Chamber was dissolved by military force on February 19, 1906, leading the Coalition to form an administration to avoid the menace of universal suffrage or to ensure its introduction in a form that safeguarded Magyar supremacy.

## Economic Autonomy and the 1906 Pact
A pact concluded on April 8, 1906, between the crown and the Coalition established several conditions to end the political crisis. The Hungarian Chamber was tasked with voting for the normal contingent of recruits and estimates for 1905 and 1906, as well as voting for extraordinary military credits that had been sanctioned by delegations in 1904. Other conditions included the ratification of commercial treaties concluded by Tisza and the introduction of a suffrage reform based on the Kristoffy scheme.

The Chamber also sought economic independence. On June 2, 1905, Kossuth obtained a motion to compile an autonomous Hungarian tariff. By May 28, 1906, the Coalition cabinet was authorized to present the Szell-Koerber tariff to the Chamber as a Hungarian autonomous tariff. Although this tariff was identical to the Austrian one, the concession of form caused the resignation of the Austrian premier, Prince Konrad Hohenlohe.
