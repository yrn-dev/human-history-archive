# Provincial Assemblies and Governance

The establishment and function of provincial assemblies and governments vary significantly across different historical and geographical contexts, ranging from the early autonomy of the Basque Provinces to the planned constitutional reforms in China and the structured provincial systems of Canada.

## The Basque Provinces
The three Basque Provinces maintained a distinct status as a "land apart" (*tierra apartada*) despite their union with the crown of Spain. Their historical trajectory involved varying dependencies; Guipuzcoa was definitively united to Castile in 1200, Alava joined the crown of Castile in 1332 via the confederation of Arriaga, and Biscay's definitive union occurred in 1370 after a period where the counts of Haro served as lords from 1093 to 1350.

These provinces preserved their own customs and laws, known as *fueros*, which the Spanish kings were required to swear to maintain. The provincial juntas held significant power, as royal orders, Spanish legislation, and decrees of Cortes lacked force unless they were countersigned by these juntas. The juntas occasionally acted in common, using a seal engraved with *Iruracbat* ("The Three One") as a symbol of unity. This autonomy allowed the provinces to conduct their own foreign affairs; for example, in 1481, Guipuzcoa proposed a ten-year treaty of peace, friendship, and free trade with England, signed in Westminster on March 9, 1482. The Basques continued to make independent treaties with France and England and were listed separately from Spain in the treaty of Utrecht (1713). This system of autonomy lasted until 1833.

## Provincial Governance in Canada
In Canada, provincial governments operated alongside the national parliament. Following the passage of federation in 1867, specific figures were tasked with organizing these systems. John Sandfield Macdonald was entrusted by the Conservatives to organize the provincial government of Ontario, which he ruled with efficiency and economy until his defeat by the Liberals in December 1871. 

Other statesmen also navigated both levels of government. Alexander Mackenzie served in the Ontario provincial assembly from 1871 to 1872, during which time he held the position of provincial treasurer, in addition to his role in the first Dominion house of commons. In Quebec, Sir Georges Etienne Cartier played a pivotal role in carrying his native province into federation between 1864 and 1867. Cartier also served as provincial secretary and, from 1857, as attorney-general for Lower Canada, where he worked on the codification of civil law and the abolition of seigneurial tenure.

## Planned Reforms in China
In the early 20th century, China initiated a transition toward representative government through Imperial decrees. A decree issued in late 1907 established the principle of representative government, followed by a 1908 decree ordering the convening of provincial assemblies in all provinces and certain other empire portions after one year. These assemblies were designed to deliberate on matters of local government as an initial step toward a broader constitution.

Membership in these provincial assemblies was determined by election, though it required a small property qualification. The number of representatives was proportional to the number of electors, calculated at approximately one representative for every one thousand electors. This provincial phase was part of a larger timeline that included the drawing up of provincial budgets and the promulgation of a new criminal code. The ultimate goal was the creation of a National Assembly or Parliament by 1917, which was to feature a prime minister and both an Upper and Lower House.

## Other Provincial and Local Jurisdictions
In ancient Babylon, the legal system included courts in provincial towns. While judges at Babylon formed a superior court, defendants maintained the right to elect to answer charges before their local provincial court rather than pleading at Babylon.
