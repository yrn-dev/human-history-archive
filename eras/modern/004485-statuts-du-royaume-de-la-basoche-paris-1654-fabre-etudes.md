# Basoche

The Basoche (also referred to as Bazoche, Basoque, Basogue, or Bazouges) was a French gild of clerks who served as the recruitment pool for legal representatives known as *procureurs*. The term is derived from the Latin *basilica*, referring to law courts.

## Origins and Evolution
The gild is described as very ancient, predating the gild of the *procureurs*. It originated during a period when the profession of *procureur* (advocate or legal representative) was "free," meaning those providing these services were not yet public or ministerial officers. During this era, groups of men skilled in law established themselves near important juridical centers to act as advocates or representatives.

The Basoche of the parlement of Paris formed into a gild similar to other medieval trades and professions. Over time, this organization disintegrated into more specialized bodies. One branch became the advocates, and another became the legal representatives, whose profession was regularized in 1344 and subsequently became a saleable charge. The remaining clerks formed the new Basoche, consisting of those working for *procureurs*. Some of the wealthier members of the Basoche aspired to become *procureurs* themselves.

## Structure and Traditions
The Basoche maintained several picturesque traditions and names. It was referred to as the "kingdom of the Basoche," and its chief, who was elected annually by a general assembly, held the title of "king." However, by order, this title had to be relinquished toward the end of the 16th century.

Despite these changes, the clerks retained certain professional privileges. An 18th-century author noted that they were permitted to plead before the *juge auditeur* and M. le lieutenant civil *sur les referes*. Consequently, the *procureurs* of that era were essentially former clerks of the Basoche who were granted the preference and exclusive right to officiate in important cases.

## Prerogatives and Legal Powers
The Basoche held two primary prerogatives:

1.  **Professional Qualification**: To be recognized as a qualified *procureur*, an individual was required to complete a "stage" in the Basoche and be entered by name on its register for ten years. Simply serving as a clerk to a *procureur* and being registered at that officer's office was insufficient. This requirement led to frequent conflicts during the 17th and 18th centuries between *procureurs* and the Basoche. While some decisions favored the *procureurs*, the parlement generally maintained the rights of the Basoche. This support was driven by a dread of *certificats de complaisance* issued by *procureurs*, though these certificates remained valid in regions where no Basoche existed.
2.  **Judiciary Powers**: The law recognized the judiciary powers of the Basoche. It exercised disciplinary jurisdiction over its own members and adjudicated civil law personal actions brought by one clerk against another, or by an outsider against a clerk. Judgments delivered by a *maitre des requetes* were authoritative and could only be contested via a civil petition before the ancient council of the Basoche.

## Regional and Specialized Basoches
Various specialized versions of the Basoche existed across Paris and other regions:
*   **Paris**: The Chatelet of Paris had its own Basoche, which claimed to be older than that of the Palais de Justice, leading to disputes over certain rights. Additionally, the clerks of the *cour des comptes* of Paris had an ancient Basoche known as the "empire de Galilee."
*   **Provincial**: The Basoche of the Palais de Justice once possessed the right to create provostships within the jurisdiction of the parlement of Paris, resulting in several local basoches. Other independent organizations existed, such as the Basoche of the parlement of Toulouse and the "regency" of Rouen.
