# Federated Groups of Colonies and Dependencies

In the administration of a colonial empire comprising over fifty distinct governments, certain colonies and dependencies are associated into federated groups for administrative purposes. These groups are formed when matters of common interest to several separate governments are, by mutual consent, placed under the authority of a common government. In the case of self-governing colonies, this common government is created voluntarily for that specific purpose.

## Structure and Governance of Federations
Within a federal arrangement, the associated states constitute a single federal body under the federal government. However, the individual parts maintain control over local matters and continue to exercise all their original governmental rights regarding those local concerns.

The British empire contains two primary self-governing groups of federated colonies: the Commonwealth of Australia and the Dominion of Canada. Other examples of federated groups include the federated Malay States and the Leeward Island group of the West Indies, which are classified as federated crown colonies and lesser dependencies. India serves as an example of the practical federation of dependencies, where associated provinces are managed by a central government.

Different approaches to unification have been employed in other regions. In South Africa, unification was chosen over federation, resulting in the 1910 creation of a single state known as the Union of South Africa.

## Classification of Colonies
Colonies are divided into three distinct classes, alongside dependencies:

*   **Crown Colonies:** The crown maintains entire control over legislation, and the home government controls public officers. Laws are generally made by the governor with the concurrence of a crown-nominated council. In some instances, such as Gibraltar and St Helena, the governor may make laws alone. Councils in these colonies are either based entirely on the crown (as in Ceylon) or created under imperial or local laws (as in Jamaica).
*   **Representative Colonies (without responsible government):** These colonies possess representative institutions, but the home government retains control of public officers. The crown holds a veto on legislation but generally cannot legislate by order in council. Laws are passed by the governor with the concurrence of legislative bodies. For example, the Bahamas, Barbados, and Bermuda utilize two legislative bodies (one elected and one nominated), while Malta and the Leeward Islands use a single body that is partly elected and partly nominated.
*   **Responsible Colonies:** These colonies possess both representative institutions and responsible government. Legislation is conducted via parliamentary means with a cabinet responsible to parliament. The home government has no control over public officers except for the governor. The crown retains a right of veto, exercised at the governor's discretion for certain bills.

## Colonial Administration and Officials
The crown receives advice on colonial administration from the secretaries of state for India and for the colonies. Colonial governors represent the crown, and their specific titles—including governors-general, governors, lieut.-governors, administrators, high commissioners, and commissioners—depend on the status of the colony, dependency, or group they preside over.

In certain dependencies, British sovereignty is not absolute. For instance, Cyprus was handed over to Great Britain for administration in 1878 while nominally remaining part of the Turkish empire, for which Great Britain makes an annual payment of £5000 to the Porte. In Zanzibar and other equatorial African regions, native rulers keep considerable powers, and some areas in the Far East are held via leases from China.

## Historical Evolution of Imperial Federation
The development of colonial self-government reached its peak between 1845 and 1875 under the influence of the "Manchester school," which viewed self-governing institutions as a precursor to inevitable separation. However, a subsequent reaction occurred as foreign enterprise increased, leading to discussions on the value of federation for common interests.

Imperial federation was discussed in New Zealand as early as 1852 and officially suggested by the general association of Australian colonies in London in 1857. The Royal Colonial Institution was founded in 1868 with the motto "United Empire." In 1872, Lord Beaconsfield rejected the idea that self-government led to separation, describing these constitutions as part of a "policy of imperial consolidation." W. E. Forster later advocated for imperial federation to replace dependence with association, leading to the 1884 foundation of the Imperial Federation League. This evolution continued with the Colonial Conferences starting in 1887 (renamed the Imperial Conference in 1907), where colonial opinions were sought regarding imperial organization and defence.

## Other Historical Federations
Beyond the British system, other ancient colonies exhibited federal characteristics. Carthage, a Tyrian colony, acquired a federal preëminence over other Phoenician colonies on the African coast. At its maximum dominion, Carthage managed a political aggregate that included the towns of Utica, Hippo, Adrumêtum, and Leptis—which maintained a qualified autonomy—as well as three hundred smaller towns and foreign dependencies in Spain, Sardinia, the Balearic isles, and Sicily.
