# The Silent Engine: The Role of the Peasantry in the Russian Revolution

While most historical narratives of the 1917 Russian Revolution focus on the urban proletariat and the intellectual vanguard of the Bolsheviks in Petrograd, the revolution's success was inextricably linked to the countryside. To understand the rise of communism in Russia, one must look beyond the factory gates and into the agrarian struggle of the peasantry, who comprised over 80% of the population.

For the peasants, the revolution was not initially about the theoretical tenets of Marxism—which focused on the industrial working class—but about the tangible ownership of land. The collapse of the Tsarist regime in February 1917 unleashed a wave of spontaneous land seizures that fundamentally altered the political landscape, forcing the Bolsheviks to adapt their ideology to survive.

## The Land Question and Bolshevik Pragmatism
Vladimir Lenin recognized a critical flaw in orthodox Marxism: Russia lacked a massive industrial proletariat. To secure power, he pivoted toward a strategic alliance with the peasantry. Through the "Decree on Land," issued shortly after the October Revolution, the Bolsheviks effectively legalized the seizure of noble estates by peasant committees.

This was not an act of socialist altruism, but a pragmatic necessity. By endorsing the peasants' desire for land, Lenin neutralized a potential counter-revolutionary force and gained a temporary, albeit unstable, truce with the rural masses. This "Smychka" (union) between the city and the village was the fragile foundation upon which the early Soviet state was built.

## From Autonomy to Collectivization
The relationship between the communist state and the peasantry soured rapidly during the Russian Civil War (1917–1922). The policy of *Prodrazvyorstka* (forced grain requisitioning) to feed the starving cities led to widespread peasant uprisings and famine. 

Historians continue to debate whether the subsequent shift toward "Collectivization" under Joseph Stalin in the late 1920s was an inevitable extension of Marxist-Leninist ideology or a calculated move to fund rapid industrialization. By forcing peasants into collective farms (*kolkhozy*), the state sought to break the independence of the peasantry and extract the surplus grain needed to build a modern industrial military complex.

## Key Facts
* **The Decree on Land (1917):** Abolished private ownership of land and redistributed it among those who worked it.
* **War Communism:** The early Soviet policy of forced requisitioning that alienated the rural population.
* **The NEP (New Economic Policy):** A temporary retreat by Lenin in 1921 that allowed peasants to sell surplus grain on the open market to stabilize the economy.
* **Dekulakization:** The campaign to eliminate "Kulaks" (wealthier peasants), which resulted in mass deportations and executions.

## Did You Know?
* **The Green Armies:** During the Russian Civil War, "Green Armies" emerged—peasant militias who fought against both the "Red" Bolsheviks and the "White" monarchists to protect their local autonomy.
* **The Bread Riots:** The February Revolution was sparked in part by bread shortages in Petrograd, highlighting the city's total dependence on the rural harvest.
* **Agrarianism:** Many peasants were more attracted to the Socialist Revolutionary (SR) party than the Bolsheviks, as the SRs focused specifically on peasant land rights.
