# Decisive Battles and Strategic Impossibilities

In the study of human history, certain military engagements are categorized as decisive because they irrevocably alter the trajectory of a conflict, shift the balance of power, or render previously possible strategic goals impossible. These events range from the ancient struggles of Greece and Rome to the global conflicts of the twentieth century.

## Ancient and Classical Decisive Engagements

In the Roman Republic, the general Marius is credited with saving his country from northern barbarians. After two Roman armies were destroyed near the Lake of Geneva, the capture of Rome itself seemed possible. Marius organized a well-disciplined army from demoralized soldiery and inflicted two decisive defeats on the Cimbri and Teutones: the first in 102 at Aquae Sextiae and the second in 101 on the Raudian plain near Vercellae. Following these victories, Rome remained unmolested by northern barbarians for some centuries.

In Greece, the rise of Philip of Macedon was marked by a decisive battle against Onomarchus on the Laconian and Arcadian frontier. This victory materially increased Philip's military reputation and power, demonstrating the efficiency of the Macedonian phalanx. The result was ruinous for the vanquished and left Athens in a position where, according to the orator Demosthenes, it was impossible to provide a land-force competent to contend against Philip in the field.

Conversely, the Athenian expedition against Syracuse resulted in a complete and ruinous defeat. While the Athenians initially saw success, the narrow space of the Great Harbor rendered their naval tactics impossible. Despite the arrival of Demosthenês and his decisive resolutions, the Athenian forces suffered a final, complete defeat.

## Medieval and Early Modern Conflicts

During the First Crusade, Bohemund I, the son of Robert Guiscard, emerged as a leader. Bohemund had previously served in an attack on the East Roman empire (1080-1085), where he was repulsed by Alexius Comnenus in Thessaly. Driven by a Norse instinct of *Drang nach Osten*, Bohemund sought to carve out an eastern principality for himself. He led the crusading host across Asia Minor and negotiated with Alexius in Constantinople in April 1097.

In Japan, the Taira clan faced a period where famine and pestilence in the west and home provinces made it impossible to maintain armies in the field. When the Taira attempted to crush the growing power of the Minamoto in the summer of 1182, they suffered a signal defeat. This allowed Yoshinaka to carry the white flag into Kaga, Noto, Etchu, and Echigo.

## The Roman Empire and the East

The Roman Empire's interactions with eastern powers often hinged on the outcome of great contests. Under Emperor Diocletian, the Roman power displayed its resources in Syria. Following a victory by Galerius, the Persian King Narses found his spirit broken by his last defeat. Narses viewed an immediate peace as the only means to stop the progress of Roman arms and sent the ambassador Apharban to negotiate. Apharban described the Roman and Persian monarchies as the "two eyes of the world," suggesting that the world would be mutilated if either were put out.

In later Roman history, the rapid succession of revolutionary events made it impossible for Theodosius to march to the relief of his benefactor before receiving news of that benefactor's defeat and death.

## Modern European Warfare

The Napoleonic era saw decisive clashes such as the Battle of Dennewitz on September 6, 1813. In this engagement, 50,000 Prussians under Generals Tauentzien and Bülow defeated 58,000 French, Poles, and Saxons under Marshal Ney.

In the First World War, the Battle of the Marne in 1914 prevented Germany from capturing Paris and winning the war in that year, though it did not decisively defeat the German army. The German army suffered greater losses at Verdun, where nearly half a million men were lost. However, the Battle of Picardy (which included Flanders, Artois, and Picardy), beginning March 21, 1918, was viewed as the truly decisive battle of the war, as it would irrevocably determine whether Germany received the "laurel of victory or the thorny crown of defeat."

Strategically, German planners argued that "German Africa" was essential for a balance of power in the East and Africa. They believed that without a great German Central or East African Empire, "Nearer Asia" could not exist because its flank would be uncovered. They asserted that German Africa would make the employment of black troops against European frontiers impossible and would remove Egyptian pressure on Asia Minor.
