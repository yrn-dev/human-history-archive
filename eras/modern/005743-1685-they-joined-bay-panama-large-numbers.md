# The Buccaneers in the Pacific and the Bay of Panama

The history of the buccaneers is divided into three distinct epochs. The first spanned from their initial rise until the capture of Panama by Morgan in 1671, a period characterized by a lack of government restriction or aid. The second epoch, lasting from 1671 to 1685, represented the height of their power, daring, and prosperity. During this second phase, their operations expanded beyond the Caribbean to encompass the Pacific range from Chile to California. The third and final epoch began after 1685, marking a period of disintegration and disunion where previous notions of honor devolved into brutality and vice.

## Expeditions to the South Sea
The capture of Panama in 1671 had a lasting influence on the buccaneers. Despite various successes on land and in the Caribbean, including a second plunder of Porto Bello, they viewed the South Sea as a more lucrative and wider field for their united power.

In 1680, a well-provisioned and armed group of over 300 marauders landed at Darien. They crossed the country with the eager assistance of native Indians, who acted as guides during a difficult nine-day journey, provided canoes, and supplied food. This native support was a reaction to the cruelty and mismanagement the Spaniards had shown toward the Indians. After taking the fort of Santa Maria, the buccaneers entered the Bay of Panama under the command of John Coxon. Despite a small Spanish fleet prepared to meet them, the buccaneers captured four Spanish ships within a week.

While Coxon returned with seventy men, others under the leadership of Watling, Sharp, and Sawkins continued to ravage the coast of Peru. Though they possessed gold and silver, they often lacked basic necessities. To avoid the risk of recrossing the isthmus, some eventually cleared Cape Horn to reach the Indies. In 1683, further numbers under John Cook departed for the South Sea via Cape Horn. Following Cook's death, Edward Davis—described as the most prudent and greatest commander of the buccaneer sea forces—joined Captain Swan of England in a cruise that proved disastrous for Spanish trade in the Pacific.

## The Events of 1685
In 1685, large numbers of buccaneers who had crossed the isthmus under Townley and others joined forces in the Bay of Panama. This increase in manpower led to a greater scale of adventure. In response, the viceroy of Peru sought to end the harassment of property, the burning and raiding of towns, and the cutting off of colonial trade and supplies through vigorous means.

During this year, a Spanish fleet of fourteen sail encountered ten buccaneer vessels in the Bay of Panama, though the two forces did not engage. While the buccaneers' power was at its peak at this time, the combination of men was too extensive for its own stability. Discord grew due to the different nationalities of the members.

## Internal Conflict and Decline
The buccaneers' ideal of equality was never sustained. While the immense spoil from the capture of wealthy cities was divided equally, the subsequent debauchery and gambling often left half of the conquerors in pressing want by the following day. This created a divide: those who had increased their wealth wished to return home, while those who had lost their shares demanded renewed attacks.

The unity presented to the Spanish fleet in 1685 eventually collapsed. The separation of the French and English buccaneers marked the beginning of the third epoch of their history, characterized by the disintegration of their collective power.

## Earlier Activities and the Capture of Panama
Prior to the peak of their power, the buccaneers had been supported by figures such as Thomas Modyford, the governor of Jamaica (1620–1679), who maintained a close alliance with them under the official title of "privateers." Modyford granted commissions to Morgan and others for a major attack on the Isthmus of Panama, the primary route for transporting bullion from South American mines to Porto Bello for shipment to Spain.

In 1671, approximately 2,000 buccaneers seized Chagres and marched to Panama. After a difficult journey involving canoes and travel on foot, they engaged the town's defenders on the tenth day. The town was routed and burnt, and the sack was marked by great barbarities, although the Spaniards had managed to remove the treasure before the city fell. Following the expedition, Morgan was accused of defrauding his followers; the resulting small shares left many buccaneers to die of starvation while attempting to return to Jamaica. Consequently, Modyford was recalled, and Morgan was imprisoned in the Tower in 1672. Though Morgan later returned as lieutenant-governor in 1674, he remained unpopular and spent his later years suppressing the buccaneers.
