# Musical Education and Performance in the 19th Century

The development of musical proficiency during the 19th century often involved a combination of institutional study at conservatories and academies, private tutelage under established masters, and extensive international travel to refine artistic skills.

## Institutional Training and Scholarships
The Royal Academy of Music served as a significant center for training accomplished musicians. John Francis Barnett, the son of Joseph Alfred Barnett, obtained a queen's scholarship at the institution. Similarly, Sir Alexander Campbell Mackenzie won the King's Scholarship at the Royal Academy of Music, where he remained for three years. The institution also employed distinguished practitioners; Walter Bache served as a professor of the pianoforte for several years and was primarily responsible for the foundation of the Liszt scholarship at the academy.

Other institutions played pivotal roles in the development of musicians. The Leipzig Conservatorium was a frequent destination for international students. Walter Bache attended the institution to become an excellent pianist, and Edvard Grieg entered the conservatorium in 1858 upon the recommendation of Ole Bull. While at Leipzig, Grieg studied under the influence of the Schumann and Mendelssohn school of romantics, though he eventually found the academic curriculum too narrow.

## The Role of Private Tutelage and Mentorship
Beyond formal academies, many musicians sought the guidance of specific masters. Hans Guido von Bülow began his studies at age nine under Friedrich Wieck and later became a pupil of Moritz Hauptmann. His career was further shaped by Franz Liszt, under whom he studied and later married Liszt's daughter, Cosima. Von Bülow also received instruction in the elements of conducting from Richard Wagner in Zürich.

In Norway, Edvard Grieg's initial musical talent was fostered by his mother, who was an educated pianist and began teaching him at age six. Later, Grieg's musical direction was shifted by Richard Nordraak, who introduced him to northern folk tunes and helped him move away from the "effeminate Gade-Mendelssohn mixture of Scandinavism."

Other notable mentorships included Francis Edward Bache, who studied violin with Alfred Mellon and composition with Sterndale Bennett before pursuing further studies in Leipzig with Plaidy and Hauptmann. Johann Ludwig Dussek received his early instruction from his father, Johann Joseph Dussek, and later studied under Philip Emmanuel Bach in Hamburg.

## International Study and Performance Tours
Visiting foreign musical centers was a common practice for those seeking to reach a professional standard. John Francis Barnett visited Germany in 1857 for study and performed at a Gewandhaus concert in Leipzig in 1860. Hans Guido von Bülow conducted his first concert tour in 1853, traveling from Vienna to Berlin. Later in his career, he toured most European countries and the United States of America.

Johann Ludwig Dussek's career was marked by significant travel; after studying in Germany, he attained distinction as a pianist in Holland, where he served as a musical instructor to the stadtholder's family. He also spent time in Lithuania in the service of Prince Radziwill and lived in Paris under the patronage of Marie Antoinette before moving to London in 1789.

## Professional Appointments and Contributions
Many who achieved mastery in their instruments transitioned into teaching and leadership roles. Hans Guido von Bülow served as the principal professor of the piano at the Stern Academy, the chief of the Conservatorium in Munich, and later held classes for the higher development of piano-playing in Frankfort. John Francis Barnett contributed as a professor to both the Royal College of Music and the Guildhall School of Music.

Some musicians attempted to establish new institutions. The Norwegian violinist Ole Bull made an attempt to found an academy of music in Christiania, although this effort did not produce a permanent result.
