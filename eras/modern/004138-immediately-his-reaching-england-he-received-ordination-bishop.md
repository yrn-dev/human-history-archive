# Ordination and Ecclesiastical Appointments in England and Beyond

The process of ordination and the subsequent appointment of clergy to various academic and religious offices have been central to the administration of church and state across different eras. These processes often intersect with political loyalty, academic achievement, and theological disputes.

## Academic and Religious Career of Dr. Barrow
Upon his arrival in England, an individual referred to as Barrow received ordination from Bishop Brownrig. Following this, in 1660, he was appointed to the Greek professorship at Cambridge. While he initially intended to lecture on the tragedies of Sophocles, he instead chose to lecture on Aristotle's rhetoric; however, these lectures were lost after being lent to a friend.

Barrow's career expanded into mathematics and science. In July 1662, on the recommendation of Dr. John Wilkins (the master of Trinity College and later bishop of Chester), Barrow was elected professor of geometry at Gresham College. In May 1663, he became a fellow of the Royal Society during the first election held by the council after they obtained their charter. That same year, he was named the first professor of a mathematical chair at Cambridge founded by the will of Henry Lucas. To avoid inconsistency between his roles, Barrow resigned from Gresham College on May 20, 1664.

In 1669, Barrow resigned his mathematical chair to his pupil, Isaac Newton, as he decided to shift his studies from mathematics to divinity. At that time, he was a fellow of Trinity College. He received a small sinecure in Wales from his uncle and was granted a prebend in the church of Salisbury by Bishop Seth Ward. By 1670, he was created a doctor in divinity by mandate. On February 13, 1672, by the king's patent, he succeeded Dr. Pearson as master of Trinity College following Pearson's promotion to the see of Chester. Barrow later served as the university vice-chancellor in 1675. He died on May 4, 1677, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

## Episcopal Administration and Conflict: Antony Bek
Antony Bek, a member of a Lincolnshire family, was elected bishop of Durham in 1283 after attracting the attention of Edward I. Bek's tenure was marked by significant involvement in military and diplomatic affairs. In 1294, he was sent on a diplomatic mission to the German king, Adolph of Nassau. He also participated in Edward I's Scottish campaigns, leading a division of the English army at the battle of Falkirk in 1298 and receiving the surrender of John de Baliol at Brechin in 1296.

Upon returning to England, Bek entered a conflict with Richard de Hoton, the prior of Durham. Although Bek deposed and excommunicated the prior, the prior gained the support of the king. Bek's refusal to yield led to lasting enmity with Edward I. In 1302, Bek visited Rome following a command from Pope Boniface VIII; during this period, the king seized and administered Bek's lands. While he eventually recovered these lands after submitting to Edward, he continued his hostility toward Richard de Hoton. Bek was later restored to royal favor under Edward II, who named him lord of the Isle of Man. He died at Eltham on March 3, 1311.

## Theological Disputes and the Validity of Ordination
The validity of ordination has historically been a point of severe contention, notably during the schism involving the Donatists in Africa. The Donatists refused to obey those they considered usurpers, denying their spiritual powers and claiming that the Apostolical succession had been interrupted. They asserted that all bishops in Asia and Europe were infected by schism and guilt, specifically citing the "pretended ordination" of Cæcilian from the Traditors.

The Donatists practiced a rigid system where they repeated the rites of ordination and baptism for any proselyte, regardless of whether the individual had already received these rites, as they rejected any ordination performed by schismatics or heretics. This dispute led to an imperial commission under Melchiades, bishop of Rome, and a trial in 313. Later, the synod of Arles in 314 passed a canon stating that ordination remained valid even if performed by a traditor, provided it was otherwise regular. This decision was eventually finalized in 316 at Milan, leading the state to suppress the Donatists.

## Other Notable Ecclesiastical Figures
In France, Charles-Michel, Abbe de l'Epee (1712-1789), faced obstacles to his ordination. He was initially denied ordination by the bishop of his diocese because he refused to sign a religious formula that opposed Jansenist doctrines. He briefly studied law before the bishop of Troyes eventually granted him ordination and a canonry. However, he was later deprived of his ecclesiastical functions by the archbishop of Beaumont due to his associations with the Jansenist Soanen. Epee subsequently devoted his life to the instruction of deaf mutes, founding a school in 1755.

In the United States, John McCloskey (1810-1885) was ordained a priest in 1834 after studying theology at Mt St Mary's College. His career progressed from the rectorship of St Joseph's in New York City to being consecrated bishop of Axieren *in partibus* in 1844. He served as coadjutor to Bishop Hughes of New York, became bishop of Albany in 1847, and succeeded to the archdiocese of New York in 1864. In April 1875, he became the first American citizen invested as a cardinal.
