# Social Democrats

The Social Democrats represent a political movement and party structure that emerged primarily in the context of industrialization and the growth of organized labour, most notably within the German state.

## Development in Germany
The Social Democrats grew as a political party in Germany, professing the doctrines of Marx. The party's rise occurred during a period of rapid industrialization and the spread of education, which led organized labour to develop its own ideas and a steady antagonism toward the military and patriotic nature of the ruler. 

The party faced significant opposition from official and clerical organizations, as well as violently repressive laws targeting its combinations and propaganda. Despite the Kaiser denouncing the party and the imprisonment or exile of its leaders, the movement expanded significantly. While the party polled fewer than half a million votes when the Kaiser first ascended the throne, by 1907 it had polled over three million.

## Political Stance and Objectives
The Social Democrats positioned themselves as a party of the "common sense of the common man." They frequently attacked the colonial adventures of new German capitalists and denounced the naval ambitions of the state. While the Kaiser attempted to provide old age and sickness insurance as "condescending gifts," the Social Democrats claimed these benefits as a right for the workers.

Despite their detestation of their own domestic autocrat, the Social Democrats provided moderate support to the army. This support was rooted in their hatred and dread of the "barbaric and retrogressive autocracy" of Russia located on Germany's eastern frontier.

## Parliamentary Presence and Shifts
In various parliamentary records, Social Democrats are listed as a distinct political group. In one recorded instance of parliamentary distribution, the Social Democrats held 82 seats, consisting of members of all races. In other recorded parliamentary tables, the party's representation fluctuated, holding 14 seats in 1897 and 10 seats in 1901.

The identity of the Social Democrat shifted during times of conflict. In an account from the trenches of the West, a pastor's inquiry into how many soldiers were social democrats was met with laughter and derision. A soldier responded on behalf of the group, stating that there were no longer any social democrats, but only "German soldiers."

## Distinction from Early Socialism
The Social Democrats and their associated doctrines differ from early forms of socialism, such as that of Owen. Early socialism was not initially a "democratic" or worker's movement; rather, it was a "master's movement" with a patriarchal form. In that early stage, the initiative was benevolent, and it was expected that workers would be educated into the system by leaders and employers who were liberally disposed.
