# London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon (1903)

*London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903), A.C. 240, is a legal case that provided significant interpretations regarding the liability and protection of bankers under English statutory law, specifically concerning the Stamp Act 1853 and the Bills of Exchange Act 1882.

## Application of the Stamp Act 1853
The case addressed the scope of section 19 of the Stamp Act 1853. Under this section, a banker who pays a draft or order payable to order on demand, which is drawn upon him, is relieved from liability if the endorsement of the instrument was unauthorized or forged. This enactment remained in effect and was not repealed by the Bills of Exchange Act 1882. In *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon*, it was held that the protections offered by section 19 of the Stamp Act 1853 extend to cases involving drafts that are drawn by a branch of a bank on its own head office.

## Interpretation of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882
The ruling in *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* also touched upon the limitations of section 60 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882. While section 60 provides similar protections to bankers in the case of cheques, the legal definition of a cheque—as a "bill drawn on a banker payable on demand"—excludes the types of drafts mentioned under the Stamp Act. 

Furthermore, the case established that documents issued by corporations or other entities that direct bankers to make payments upon the presentation of a duly signed, specific attached receipt are precluded from the protection of section 60. This is due to the requirement for the instrument to be unconditional in character.

## Qualified Immunity and Section 82
The House of Lords used *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* to provide a final exposition on section 82 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, which grants qualified immunity to bankers collecting crossed cheques for a customer. To qualify for this protection, a banker must meet several specific conditions:
*   The banker must receive the cheque and the money from a customer, defined as a person with whom the banker has existing and definite business relations.
*   The cheque must already be crossed, either specially or generally, to the banker. A crossing made by the banker themselves under section 77 is considered inefficacious for this purpose.
*   The banker must receive the money and the cheque without negligence and in good faith.

In this context, negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care in the interest of the true owner (who may not necessarily be the customer). To avoid a finding of negligence, the banker must ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct, verify authority for endorsements made per procuration, and refuse cheques marked "account payee" if they are intended for a different account. Additionally, the banker must not take a cheque for a private account if the face of the document indicates the holder possesses it in an official capacity, as an agent, or for partnership purposes.

## The "Conduit Pipe" Principle
A critical determination in the *Gordon* case was that a banker only maintains protection under section 82 as long as they act strictly as an agent for the customer or as a "conduit pipe." If the banker assumes the position of the owner of the cheque, they no longer fulfill the requirement of receiving the money solely for the customer.

The court found that the practice of crediting cheques as cash in the bank's books before the money had actually been received was equivalent to taking the cheques as an owner or transferee. Consequently, this practice debars the bank from claiming the protections afforded by section 82.
