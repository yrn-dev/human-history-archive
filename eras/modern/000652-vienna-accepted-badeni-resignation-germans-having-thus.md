# The Ministry of Count Badeni

The administration of Count Badeni in Austria during the late 19th century was characterized by attempts to maintain a non-partisan government while navigating intense nationalistic conflicts, specifically regarding the linguistic rights of the Bohemian population.

## Appointment and Early Governance
Count Badeni, who had previously earned a reputation as the governor of Galicia, was appointed minister-president by the emperor. His administration was designed to belong to no specific party and possess no set programme. Badeni's primary mission was the successful completion of the *Ausgleich* (composition) with Hungary, a goal to which all other government activities were to be subordinated. 

During 1896, Badeni successfully passed a franchise reform bill. This legislation maintained existing categories of members but introduced a fifth *curia*, allowing nearly anyone who had resided in one place for six months and was not in domestic service to vote. This reform added seventy-two members to the parliament.

## The 1897 Elections and Political Instability
Following the franchise reform, parliament was dissolved. The 1897 elections resulted in a House that made strong government nearly impossible. The United German Left largely disappeared, replaced by the German Radicals, the German Popular party, and the German Nationalists, all of whom prioritized nationality over constitutional standpoints. Additionally, fourteen Social Democrats won seats under the new franchise. 

The Right was similarly fragmented, consisting of forty-one Clericals and twenty-eight Christian Socialists led by Dr. Lueger. Lueger, a powerful orator who had gained predominant influence in Vienna, held a two-thirds majority in the town council. Although the emperor initially refused to confirm Lueger's election as burgomaster and personally asked him to withdraw, Badeni later advised that the election be confirmed due to the large number of followers Lueger had gained in the Reichsrath.

## The Language Ordinances of 1897
Following the elections, Badeni sent in his resignation, but the emperor refused to accept it. Seeking support to govern, Badeni turned to other nationalities, specifically sixty Young Czechs and fifty-nine Poles. While the Poles were consistently ready to support the government, the moderate Young Czechs were willing to negotiate in exchange for concessions regarding language in Bohemia.

In May 1897, Badeni published celebrated ordinances to remedy grievances in Bohemia. These decrees established two primary rules:
1. All documents and correspondence brought before government officials were to be conducted in the language in which they were first introduced. This effectively introduced the Czech language into government offices throughout the kingdom of Bohemia.
2. After 1903, any individual appointed to a government post in Bohemia was required to pass an examination in Czech.

## German Opposition and the Fall of the Cabinet
These ordinances were met with violent opposition from German Nationalists and Radicals, who feared the loss of their influence. The German parties declared that no business would be conducted until the ordinances were repealed and Badeni was dismissed. They employed tactics of obstruction and brought repeated motions to impeach ministers. Consequently, parliament had to be prorogued in June, as no business had been transacted.

The unrest spread across the country, with serious disturbances occurring at great meetings in Aussig and Eger, attended by Germans from across the frontier. During the recess, Badeni attempted to open negotiations, but the Germans refused to discuss any matters until the ordinances were withdrawn. This violent opposition eventually led to the fall of Count Badeni's cabinet at the end of 1897.

## Aftermath and Subsequent Administrations
Badeni was succeeded briefly by Count Thun and then by Count Clary. The government of Count Clary repealed the decrees that had granted equal rights to the Bohemian language, which triggered troubles in Prague that were severely repressed by Austrian authorities. 

Under the subsequent ministries of Gautsch and Korber, Bohemians continued to assert their national rights and oppose the central government in Vienna. By the close of the 19th century, parliamentary life in Austria remained paralyzed by the feud between Germans and Czechs. Government was increasingly carried on by cabinets of officials utilizing paragraph 14 (the emergency clause) of the constitution, meaning ministers were responsible to the emperor rather than to parliament.
