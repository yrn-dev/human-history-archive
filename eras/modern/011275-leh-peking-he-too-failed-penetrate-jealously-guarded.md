# Leh to Peking

The journey from Leh to Peking represents a significant route of exploration and geographical study in the late 19th century, particularly concerning the penetration of the Tibetan plateau and the mapping of the regions between India and China.

## Explorations of Tibet and Lhasa
During the late 1800s, several explorers attempted to navigate the Tibetan plateau and enter the city of Lhasa. In 1886-1887, Pierre G. Bonvalot and Prince Henri d'Orleans crossed the plateau from north to south, though they failed to enter Lhasa. This was followed by the American traveler W.W. Rockhill between 1889 and 1891, who also attempted to reach Lhasa without success.

In 1891, Hamilton Bower undertook a famous journey from Leh to Peking. While Bower also failed to penetrate the jealously-guarded portals of Lhasa, he succeeded in securing a splendid addition to existing Tibetan mapping with the help of a native surveyor. Other notable figures in the region during this era included the French explorer Dutreuil de Rhins (1891-1893), who died during his work, and St George Littledale. Littledale's first great Tibetan journey ended at Peking, and while his second journey in 1894-1895 brought him almost within sight of the sacred walls of Lhasa, he was unable to pass inside.

## Geographical Findings and Mapping
Research into the region has redefined the understanding of the boundaries between Tibet, Turkestan, and China. The Kuen-lun mountains, extending from the frontiers of Kashmir north of Leh east to the Chinese province of Kansu, are now recognized as the northern limit of the Chang Tang (the elevated highland steppes of Tibet) rather than the southern limit of the Turkestan depression or Gobi.

A minor system branches north-east from approximately 83 deg. E. longitude, culminating in the Altyn Tagh and extending to the Nan Shan mountains north of the Koko Nor basin. This creates a system of elevated valleys known as Tsaidam, situated 8,000 to 9,000 feet above sea level, which serves as an intermediate steppe between the highest regions and the low-lying sands of the Gobi east of Lop Nor. These geographical features explain why the primary trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed far to the north.

Further geographical contributions were made between 1896 and 1898 by British cavalry officers Captain M.S. Wellby and Captain H. Deasy. Captain Deasy continued the Pamir triangulation into the sources of the Zarafshan and the plains of Kashgar, building upon work done by Colonels R.A. Wahab and Sir T.H. Holdich during the Pamir Boundary Commission of 1895.

## Peking and the Middle Kingdom
Peking served as a central point for diplomatic and military tension at the turn of the century. In 1900, the Boxers became a threat to Europeans in China. After the German ambassador was shot by a soldier of the imperial guard, foreign ambassadors fortified their embassies and endured a two-month siege. They were eventually relieved by a combined allied force of 20,000 under a German general, causing the old Empress to flee northwestward.

Diplomatically, Peking took "serious umbrage" at Japan's conduct regarding Formosa. The Chinese government contended that Japan had invaded Chinese territory and disputed the status of the Ryukyu Islands. This was resolved when the Tokyo Government sent an ambassador to Peking, resulting in the recognition of Formosa as part of the Middle Kingdom and a payment of 500,000 Mexican dollars as an indemnity.

## Administration and Economy in Peking
Historical records of Peking describe various systems of security and finance. The Great Kaan maintained a guard of 12,000 horsemen known as Keshican ("Knights devoted to their Lord"). These men were divided into four captains of 3,000 each, with one body guarding the palace for three days and nights before being relieved by the next.

Economically, Peking experienced volatility regarding currency. While private promissory notes were common in cities like Fuchau, the Imperial Government attempted to resume a paper-currency system. In Peking, the value of the dollar was recorded by Dr. Rennie in 1861 as being valued at 15,000 and later 25,000 paper cash. Financial instability occurred when the Regent Sushun issued a vast number of notes through his own banks in Peking but failed to redeem them. This caused the failure of the banks and significant commotion in the city. Following the death of Emperor Hien Fung, who had been led into debauched habits and paralysis by Sushun, the Empress had Sushun arrested and executed on November 8, 1861.
