# The Bonapartes of Baltimore

The Bonapartes of Baltimore constitute a branch of the Bonaparte family settled in the United States of America. This lineage is descended from Jerome Bonaparte (VII.), the King of Westphalia, through his marriage to Elizabeth Patterson.

## The Marriage of Jerome Bonaparte and Elizabeth Patterson
Jerome Bonaparte married Elizabeth Patterson in Baltimore on December 24, 1803. Elizabeth, born in 1785, was the daughter of William Patterson, a merchant in Baltimore who was likely descended from the Robert Paterson who served as the inspiration for Sir Walter Scott's "Old Mortality."

The union was a subject of significant contention. Napoleon greatly disliked the marriage and refused to recognize its legality. However, the marriage was considered valid under American law, and Pope Pius VII. refused to declare the union void. Despite this, Jerome was forced by his brother to separate from his wife. After a period of residence in England, Elizabeth—who was commonly referred to as Madame Patterson or Madame Bonaparte—returned to Baltimore, where she lived until her death in 1879.

## Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte (1805–1870)
The only child resulting from the marriage of Jerome Bonaparte and Elizabeth Patterson was Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte. Born in England in 1805, he spent the majority of his life residing in Baltimore. It has been noted that he bore a marked resemblance to his uncle, the emperor.

Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte maintained a positive relationship with his father; the two met occasionally, and for a period of time, Jerome provided his son with a large allowance. He died in 1870.

## Subsequent Generations and American Political Influence
The lineage continued through the sons of the first Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte. His elder son, also named Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte (1832–1893), served in the French army, seeing service in Italy and the Crimea.

The younger son, Charles Joseph Bonaparte, achieved a distinguished position in the political sphere of the United States. Born in Baltimore on June 9, 1851, and educated at Harvard University, Charles Joseph became a lawyer in 1874. His public career included serving as the president of the National Municipal League. He held high-ranking government offices in the early 20th century, serving as the secretary of the navy in the cabinet of President Roosevelt from July 1905 to December 1906, and subsequently serving as the attorney-general of the United States until March 1909.
