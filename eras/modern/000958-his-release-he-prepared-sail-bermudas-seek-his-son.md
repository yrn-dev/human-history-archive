# Search for Juan and the Colonization of Florida

## The Mission of Aviles
Following his release, a man named Aviles prepared to sail to the Bermudas. His objective was to seek his son, Juan, who had been shipwrecked during the previous year. During this period, French Huguenots were attempting to establish a colony in Florida. Because the Spaniards had already explored the region, they claimed ownership of the territory. The Spanish viewed the possession of Florida by any other power as highly dangerous due to its location on the trade route returning from Mexico.

## The Asiento and Establishment of St Augustine
To prevent this peril, Philip II entered into a contract known as an "asiento" with Aviles. Under the terms of this agreement, signed on March 20, 1565, Philip II advanced 15,000 ducats to Aviles. In return, Aviles was named the proprietor of any colony he could establish in Florida, provided that the advanced funds were repaid.

Aviles departed on July 28, 1565, commanding a fleet that consisted of ten sloops and one vessel of 600 tons, carrying 1,500 men. On August 28, he arrived at a bay which he named the Bay of St Augustine, where he began the construction of a fort.

## Conflict with the French
On September 20, 1565, Aviles captured Fort Caroline, a French post. In October of that year, he exterminated a group of Frenchmen who had arrived on the Florida coast to provide relief to their colony; these men were under the command of the Huguenot Jean Ribault. After killing nearly all the prisoners, the Spanish commander hung their bodies from trees. These bodies were marked with the inscription, "Not as Frenchmen but as Lutherans."

Aviles remained in Florida managing his colony until 1567, at which point he returned to Spain. He later made one additional voyage to Florida and died on September 17, 1574.

## Personal Life and Perspectives
Aviles married Maria de Solis at a young age and fathered three daughters. According to his letters, he was a high-minded and pious officer. He did not believe that any honest man could suggest he had gone too far in the massacre of the Frenchmen, as he viewed those individuals as heretics and pirates.
