# RATIONALIZATION

In the context of historical and mathematical analysis, rationalization refers to the process of reducing complex functions or narratives to simpler, rational forms or logical explanations. This process manifests differently across the fields of integral calculus, algebraic geometry, and historical commentary.

## Rationalization in Integral Calculus

In the study of integral calculus, rationalization involves evaluating indefinite integrals of rational functions. It is proved that if $R(z)$ denotes any rational function, an indefinite integral $\int R(z)dz$ can be evaluated using logarithmic and rational functions, which include inverse trigonometrical functions.

This concept extends to more complex relations. For instance, if $s^2 = az^2 + bz + c$ and $R(s, z)$ is a rational function of $s$ and $z$, the integral $\int R(s, z)dz$ can be evaluated in terms of logarithms of rational functions and rational functions of $s$ and $z$. The simplest case of this is $\int s^{-1}dz$ or $\int(az^2 + bz + c)^{-1/2}dz$. 

More generally, if a relation $f(s, z) = 0$ connects $s$ and $z$ such that both are rationally expressible in terms of an appropriate rational function $\theta$, then the integral $\int R(s, z)dz$ is reducible to the form $\int H(\theta)d\theta$. Because $H(\theta)$ is rational in $\theta$, it can be evaluated by logarithms of rational functions and rational functions of $s$ and $z$. However, this theorem does not hold if $s^2$ is a cubic polynomial in $z$. In such cases, every integral $\int R(s, z)dz$ can be expressed using rational and logarithms of rational functions of $s$ and $z$ only when combined with integrals of three specific types. A similar theorem applies when $s^2$ is a quartic polynomial in $z$; specifically, when $s^2 = A(z - a)(z - b)(z - c)(z - d)$, the relation can be transformed into a cubic polynomial.

## Rational Functions and Approximation

Rational functions are also utilized to approximate monogenic functions within specific regions. Given a region $R_0$ of the plane with boundary points $C_0$, and another region $R$ with boundary $C$ that contains $R_0$, a rational function can be constructed to differ arbitrarily little from a given function $f(z)$ for all points $z$ of $R_0$.

This is achieved by using a sum of terms involving poles at positions external to $R_0$. For example, if $C_0$ and $C$ are simple closed polygons, a rational function can be found whose poles are at a single finite point $c$ external to the path $\Gamma$ (which $C$ approximates). By applying a transformation $t - c = r^{-1}$, this point $c$ can be moved to infinity, turning the rational function into a polynomial. An infinite series of such polynomials $P_n(z)$ can then be constructed to represent $f(z)$ within $C_0$.

## Rationality in Algebraic Geometry

In the theory of algebraic curves and surfaces, rationality relates to whether the coordinates of points can be expressed as rational functions of parameters. For a plane curve of deficiency zero, the coordinates are rational functions of a single parameter.

For surfaces, the conditions for rationality are more complex. While it is necessary that $p_g - p_a = 0$, this is not sufficient. A sufficient condition for a surface to be rational is the combination of $p_a = 0$ and $P_2 = 0$. Surfaces are often classified by numbers that remain invariant under birational transformation to determine the simplest birationally equivalent form. For example, a hyperelliptic surface is characterized by $p_g = 1$ and $p_a = -1$. Additionally, any surface with a linear system of curves where the order exceeds twice the deficiency of the individual curves diminished by two is either a rational surface or reducible by birational transformation to a ruled surface.

## Rationalization in Historical Analysis

Outside of mathematics, rationalization refers to the attempt to provide logical or naturalistic explanations for historical accounts that may contain supernatural or legendary elements. In the study of old Japan, learned commentators have attempted to rationalize the events described in the Chronicles and the Records. 

These processes of analysis have been compared by one author to estimating the "historical residuum" of the Cinderella legend by subtracting the godmother and the pumpkin coach. Despite these academic efforts to rationalize the annals, a great bulk of the Japanese population continues to believe in the literal accuracy of these works, similar to the belief held by a great bulk of Anglo-Saxon people regarding the miracles and cosmogony of the Bible.
