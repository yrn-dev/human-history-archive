# Bolivia

Bolivia is a South American nation that declared its independence in August 1825. At that time, fifty-four deputies assembled at the capital, Chuquisaca, to decide whether Upper Peru would remain separate from the Argentine provinces. The assembly declared it the national will that Upper Peru constitute a distinct and independent nation, subsequently naming the country Bolivia and issuing a formal declaration of independence. General Sucre was invested with supreme command in Upper Peru until a regular constitutional government could be established.

## Territorial Disputes and Relations with Chile

Bolivia has a long history of boundary disputes and conflict with Chile, primarily concerning its access to the sea. Following a war from 1878 to 1881, Bolivia ceded its entire sea-coast, including the port of Cobija, to Chile. This resulted in the loss of the Litoral department's railways, which were 282 miles long, to Chilean control.

In 1895, a treaty was signed at Santiago intended to strengthen friendship and provide Bolivia with the free sea access required for its commercial prosperity. Under this agreement, Chile declared that if it acquired permanent sovereignty over the territories of Arica and Tacna (via a plebiscite under the treaty of Ancon with Peru or direct arrangement), it would transfer them to Bolivia in the same form and extent. In exchange, Bolivia would pay a silver indemnity of $5,000,000. If this cession occurred, Chile would move its frontier north of Camerones to Vitor. Should Chile fail to obtain Tacna and Arica, it bound itself to cede the roadstead (_caleta_) of Vitor, or another analogous port that satisfied Bolivia's commercial requirements, along with $5,000,000 silver.

However, Chile changed its conciliatory attitude after discovering secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina. Because Bolivia was unable to compel concessions or venture into hostilities, and because the boundary dispute between Argentina and Chile was settled, Presidents Pando and Ismail Montes (who took office in 1904) sought the best possible terms for a settlement. A treaty ratified in 1905 required Bolivia to cede the maritime provinces occupied by Chile since 1881, leaving the republic without a seaport. In return, Chile agreed to:
* Construct a railway at its own cost from Arica to the Bolivian capital, La Paz.
* Provide Bolivians with free transit through Chilean territory to specific coastal towns.
* Pay a cash indemnity and provide pecuniary assistance for the construction of other interior railways.

## Infrastructure and Communications

Due to its isolated geography, Bolivia has relied on various international agreements to develop its railway network. In 1903, Brazil agreed to construct a 180-mile railway around the falls of the Madeira to provide north-eastern Bolivia access to the Amazon; Brazil also provided £2,000,000 in cash for railway construction within Bolivian territory. 

Under a 1904 treaty (ratified 1905), Chile agreed to build a 295-mile railway from Arica to La Paz, with the Bolivian section becoming Bolivian property fifteen years after completion. Chile also agreed to pay interest (not exceeding 5%) on capital invested in certain interior railways constructed within thirty years, capped at £100,000 annually and £1,600,000 in total. Additionally, Argentina undertook to extend its northern railway from Jujuy to the Bolivian town of Tupiza.

The Peruvian Corporation constructed a 54-mile line from Guaqui on Lake Titicaca to Alto de La Paz, which connects to the city of La Paz via a 5-mile electric line. This provides access to the Peruvian port of Mollendo, located 496 miles away, and offers potential future communication with Cuzco. Up to 1903, Bolivia's only railways were the Antofagasta and Oruro line (574 miles total, with 350 miles in Bolivia), a 26-mile private branch to the Pulacayo mines, and the Guaqui to Alto de La Paz line.

## Economy and Natural Resources

Bolivian foreign trade is limited by the poverty of its population and the high cost of transportation. In 1904, imports were valued at £1,734,551 and exports at £1,851,758. Common imports include machinery, hardware, provisions, livestock, cottons, woollens, clothing, wines, and spirits. Principal exports in 1903 included tin and its ores (£1,039,298), silver and its ores (£636,743), rubber (£260,559), copper ores (£157,609), coca, bismuth, and cinchona. These statistics are often incomplete because trade passing through Arica, Mollendo, and Antofagasta is frequently credited to Peru and Chile.

The most profitable immediate industries are forest products. Rubber-collecting, which began between 1880 and 1890, reached an annual output of nearly 3,500 metric tons before the best forests were transferred to Brazil in 1903. Rubber-producing _Hevea_ is still found on the Madre de Dios and Beni. Cinchona bark collection is one of the oldest industries; while the forests are diminishing due to destructive methods, the product is highly valued for its high percentage of quinine sulphate.

## Geography and Demographics

Bolivia's geology is characterized by the Eastern ranges of the Andes (Palaeozoic rocks with granitic intrusions) and the Western Cordillera (Jurassic and Cretaceous beds with volcanic lavas and ashes). The intervening plateau contains freshwater and terrestrial deposits. The volcanoes are located almost entirely in the Western Cordillera.

The chief ethnical element in Bolivia is the Aymara (formerly Colla), a tribe of South American Indians who originally inhabited the valleys of the Andes and the region around Lake Titicaca. The Aymaras possessed a significant culture before being conquered by the Incas in the 13th and 14th centuries, as evidenced by the megalithic ruins of Tiahuanaco. Though they were in a decadent state by the time the Spaniards arrived, they retained privileges such as their own language. Physically, the pure Aymara is described as short and thick-set with a broad face and reddish complexion. Including half-breeds, they number approximately half a million in Bolivia.
