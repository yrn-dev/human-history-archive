# Delegations and the Preservation of Parliamentary Authority

In the administrative structure of the Habsburg monarchy, the principle of dualism established a parity between Hungary and the other territories of the king. A central component of this arrangement was the creation of the Delegations, bodies designed to manage common affairs without overshadowing the parliaments from which they were appointed.

## Composition and Selection
The Delegations consisted of sixty members each, chosen for a term of one year. The selection process was divided between the upper and lower houses of the respective parliaments: one-third of the members were elected by the Hungarian Table of Magnates and the Austrian Herrenhaus (the Upper House), while the remaining two-thirds were elected by the Hungarian and Austrian Houses of Representatives.

## Operational Procedures
To ensure that the Delegations did not eclipse the authority of the parliaments, strict procedural limitations were implemented. The Delegations did not sit together; instead, each met separately in public sittings. These meetings were summoned annually by the monarch, alternating between Vienna and Budapest (or Pest).

Communication between the two bodies was conducted exclusively in writing, with every document accompanied by a translation in either German or Magyar. A common plenary session was only convened if the two Delegations failed to reach an agreement after exchanging notes three times. In such a common session, no discussion was permitted; the members voted in silence, and a simple majority was sufficient to reach a decision. For any common decision to be valid, it required the sanction of the monarch.

## Scope of Authority and Limitations
The Delegations were tasked with the political control and voting of money applicable to common affairs, specifically the empire's foreign policy and the army. Three common ministers—responsible for finance, war, and foreign affairs—were accountable to the Delegations. While the Delegations could formulate resolutions independently and arraign these ministers, they were granted no actual legislative power.

The limitations on their power were evidenced in the following areas:
*   **Military Recruitment:** Although the minister of war controlled the common army, the laws determining recruitment methods had to be voted on separately within each individual parliament.
*   **Financial Control:** The minister of finance submitted the common budget to the Delegations, but the Delegations lacked the power to vote taxes or raise money. Once a budget was passed, the necessary funds had to be provided by the separate parliaments.
*   **Contribution Proportions:** The determination of the proportion each half of the monarchy contributed to common expenses was not left to the Delegations, but was fixed every ten years.

## Political Function and Immunity
In practice, the annual meetings of the Delegations often served as a forum for the foreign minister to justify his policies, sometimes utilizing "red books" containing important despatches. Because of their limited scope, debates within the Delegations generally caused less embarrassment to the government than those found in typical parliamentary assemblies, and army budgets were usually passed with only unimportant alterations. Members of both Delegations enjoyed constitutional immunity and were considered irresponsible in the exercise of their office.
