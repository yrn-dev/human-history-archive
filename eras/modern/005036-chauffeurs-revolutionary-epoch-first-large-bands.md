# Revolutionary Bands and Armed Groups

The history of revolutionary movements across various epochs and regions is frequently characterized by the formation of armed bands. These groups, ranging from secret societies to organized paramilitary forces, have been employed to challenge established governments, seek regional autonomy, or pursue national unity.

## Revolutionary Organizations in Macedonia and Bulgaria
In the late 19th century, revolutionary activity intensified in Macedonia and Bulgaria. In 1893, secret revolutionary societies known as *druzhestva* were established in Macedonia, followed by the organization of similar bodies as legal corporations in Bulgaria in 1894. 

The "Supreme Macedo-Adrianopolitan Committee" (*Vrkhoven Makedoni-Odrinski Komitet*), formed in Sofia in 1895, dispatched armed bands into northern Macedonia. One such enterprise, led by Boris Sarafoff, briefly occupied the town of Melnik before ending in failure. Following this, the "Vrkhovists" (as the Bulgarian revolutionaries were called) experienced a five-year period of paralysis due to dissensions between rival parties led by General Tzoncheff and Sarafoff.

Simultaneously, the "Internal Organization" was founded in 1893 by Gotzé Delcheff and Damian Grueff. This local Macedonian power operated as an *imperium in imperio*, maintaining its own tribunals, police, and a system of collecting contributions. The organization divided the country into districts (rayons), which were further subdivided into communes and departments. Their primary objective was the attainment of Macedonian autonomy under the program "Macedonia for the Macedonians," though they were unsuccessful in gaining the support of the Servians and Greeks.

## Italian Patriotic Raids
In the mid-19th century, Italian patriots sought freedom and unity through secret societies such as *Giovane Italia* (Young Italy). Attilio and Emilio Bandiera, brothers and members of the Austrian navy, became involved in these revolutionary ideas and corresponded with Giuseppe Mazzini.

After attempting to propagate these ideas among Italian sailors in the Austrian navy and planning the seizure of a warship to bombard Messina, the Bandieras fled to Corfu in 1844. Encouraged by Mazzini and reports of unrest in the Neapolitan kingdom, they assembled a band of approximately twenty men and landed near Cotrone on June 16, 1844. Their goal was to reach Cosenza to issue proclamations and liberate political prisoners. However, they were betrayed by peasants and a member of their own party, Boccheciampe. Following a short fight with gendarmes and volunteers, the band was captured. On July 23, the two Bandiera brothers and nine companions were executed.

## Samurai Bands in Japan
In Japan, the transition toward a conscript army and foreign tactics led to the formation of traditionalist armed groups. Saigo Takamori retired to Satsuma, where he organized and equipped a strong body of samurai. While it is debated whether Saigo had revolutionary intentions—with some suggesting he sought control rather than revolt—Satsuma became a center for conservative influences. This augmenting band of samurai was drilled in European manners and armed with rifles, though they retained the traditional samurai sword.

## Other Armed Groups and Brigandage
The archive distinguishes between revolutionary bands and brigandage, noting that the latter often develops due to bad administration and the availability of hiding places in mountains or forests. Examples include the "gentlemen reavers" of Scotland, the "banditos" of Italy, the "bandoleros" and "salteadores" of Spain, and the "bushranger" of Australia. In England, outlaws were associated with the forests of Epping and Dartmoor.

In ancient Greece, specifically in Thebes, the government under Leontiades and his partisans feared conspiracies from a body of Theban exiles. These exiles were led by Androkleidas, the chief of their band, who was eventually assassinated by hired killers acting on behalf of the Theban rulers.

## Military Logistics and Chauffeurs
In later military contexts, the use of cars and chauffeurs provided high-speed transport for officials. During operations in the West, chauffeurs such as Deffner operated vehicles capable of speeds exceeding fifty English miles per hour. These cars were often equipped with flags, streamers, and rifles lashed to the front. The speed of these vehicles was noted as a potential means of making the target more difficult for *franctireurs* to hit.
