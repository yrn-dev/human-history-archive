# Academy of Armoury

The *Academy of Armoury* is a work written by Randle Holme some time before 1688. The manuscript, identified as Harleian MS. 2034 and held at the British Museum, serves as a detailed record of various instruments, costumes, and cultural observations of the period.

## Musical Instruments
The *Academy of Armoury* contains numerous descriptions and sketches of musical instruments, providing insight into the instrumentation of the 17th century.

### Wind Instruments
Holme provides a drawing in pen and ink of a curved cornet, which he describes as a "delicate pleasant wind musick, if well played and humoured." Additionally, the manuscript includes a reproduction of a drawing of a "double curtaile." Holme describes this instrument as being "double the bigness of the single" (referencing a single curtail mentioned in chapter xvi, n. 6) and notes that it is played eight notes deeper. He further explains that the double curtaile consists of two pipes fixed into one thick bass pipe, with one pipe being significantly longer than the other; a crooked brass pipe containing a reed is fixed to the top of the lower pipe.

### Percussion
The manuscript includes a pencil sketch and a "quaint description" of castinatts (castanets). The sketch in the *Academy of Armoury* aligns with the description of the instrument as consisting of two hard wood bowls, pear or mussel-shaped, hinged by a silk cord.

## Observations on Costume and Dress
Randle Holme acted as an observant chronicler of national and personal dress during the 17th century.

### National Dress
Holme noted distinct differences in how various nationalities dressed. He observed that Frenchmen tended to "muff themselves" to keep warm during cold weather, but wore "fantastical dresses" in the summer that left them "almost naked." 

His description of the Spaniard depicts a man wearing a high-crowned hat with a narrow brim, a ruff around the neck, and a doublet featuring broad wings at the shoulders and short, narrow skirts. The Spanish attire also included ruff-cuffs at the hands, hose that were gartered, narrow breeches close to the thighs, rounded-toe shoes, a short cloak, and a long sword. Holme used these details to contrast the Spanish style with that of the Englishman.

### General Clothing
In his writings, Holme provides a definition of "drawers" in its later sense. He also commented on the changing fashions of the era, noting that the "comely cloak," which had been widely used at the beginning of his time, had become scarce and was primarily worn only by "old and grave persons."

## Local History and Traditions
Beyond technical and sartorial descriptions, Holme recorded the customs of his home city, Chester. He documented the evolution of horse-racing prizes during the reign of Henry VIII. He noted that in 1540, a ceremony on Shrove Tuesday involving the company of saddlers and drapers changed from the presentation of a wooden ball embellished with flowers to a silver bell valued at three shillings and sixpence (or more). This bell was awarded to the person who could run the best and furthest on horseback. According to Randle Holme the younger, these "challenge bells" continued to be used as prizes at Chester until approximately 1623 or 1624.
