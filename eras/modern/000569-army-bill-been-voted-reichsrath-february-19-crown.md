# Army Bills and Parliamentary Conflict in Austria-Hungary

## The Army Bills of 1902
In the autumn of 1902, the Austrian and Hungarian governments proposed bills to increase the annual contingents of recruits by 21,000 men. These proposals were made at the instance of the crown and in agreement with the Austrian and Hungarian ministers for national defence, as well as the joint minister for war. Of the requested increase, 16,700 men were intended for the joint army, while the remaining recruits were designated for the Hungarian honved and the Austrian Landwehr (national defence troops). The projected contributions were approximately 14,500 men from Austria and some 6,500 men from Hungary.

## The Supernumerary Recruit Controversy
A political crisis emerged when military authorities detained several thousand supernumerary recruits in barracks. These recruits were liable for military service but exceeded the legal annual enrollment limit of 103,000. The authorities took this step to avoid the delay and expense of re-summoning these men once the parliaments adopted the Army bills.

This action was resented by the Hungarian Chamber, which viewed it as an infringement on its prerogatives. The Opposition, led by Francis Kossuth (the eldest son of Louis Kossuth) and consisting primarily of the independence party, used this grievance to obstruct ministerial measures. Consequently, the estimates could not be sanctioned. Although the Szell cabinet was granted a vote on account for the first four months of 1903, the government remained at the mercy of the Opposition.

## Demands for Magyarization
Although the supernumeraries were discharged at the end of 1902, the move failed to appease the Opposition. The opposition parties demanded either the total withdrawal of the Army bills or the provision of concessions to Magyar nationalist feeling. These requested concessions were intended to promote the use of the Magyar language within the Hungarian portion of the army and to increase the Magyar character of Hungarian regiments, many of which were not purely Magyar. Despite advice from Szell to the military authorities and the crown to make timely concessions, these demands were rejected.

## The Reichsrath and Constitutional Framework
The Reichsrath served as the parliament for the empire, established under the February Constitution of 1861. It consisted of a Chamber of Deputies and a House of Lords (Herrenhaus). The House of Lords included members of the imperial family, archbishops, prince bishops, and other members appointed for life or holding hereditary seats. 

Members of the Chamber of Deputies were not chosen by direct election but were elected by the diets of the various territories. These diets were elected every six years through a system of four electoral bodies, or curiae:
1. The great proprietors (owners of estates with pre-1848 feudal privileges).
2. The chambers of commerce.
3. The towns.
4. The rural districts.

In the rural and town categories, suffrage was granted to those paying at least ten gulden in direct taxes.

## Political Instability and Reform
The history of the Reichsrath was marked by significant instability and ethnic tension. In 1867, during the process of the *Ausgleich* (compact with Hungary), Minister-President Beust replaced Belcredi to ensure the constitution was viewed as valid, as the Federalists and Slavs were often opposed to the dualist system. Later, in 1870, the ministry collapsed due to divisions over how to handle Slav races, leading to a period where the House consisted almost entirely of Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia.

Later efforts at reform were led by Baron von Beck. Supported by the emperor, who informed recalcitrant party leaders that reform "must be accomplished," Beck succeeded in October 1906 in reaching a final agreement. This resulted in the adoption of reform on December 1, 1906, which included raising the number of constituencies to 516, divided according to provinces.
