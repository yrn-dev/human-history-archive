# Smuggling and Coastal Security in Colonial South America

## The Role of Buenos Aires and the La Plata Region
The development of Buenos Aires was marked by several failed attempts at colonization. The first occurred in 1535, led by Don Pedro de Mendoza, whose expedition failed due to mismanagement and hostility from the Indians. A second attempt in 1542 under Cabeza de Vaca was similarly unsuccessful. The city was finally established in 1580 by Don Juan de Garay, the governor of Paraguay. Garay, who had previously established a post at Santa Fé in 1573, sought to create a port where ships could be provisioned and supplies from Spain received to support the Spanish colonists at Asunción.

Garay laid out a town in the prescribed Spanish style and named it "Ciudad de la Santissima Trinidad," though the descriptive name "Buenos Aires" was retained because of the invigorating and agreeable atmosphere of the location. Buenos Aires functioned as a dependency of Asunción until 1620, at which point the La Plata region was divided into three provinces: Tucuman, Paraguay, and Buenos Aires. The city of Buenos Aires became the capital of its province and the seat of a new bishopric.

## Smuggling and Spanish Neglect
Despite the growth in trade and population within the La Plata settlements, the Spanish crown, its councillors, and merchants showed little interest in the town. At the time, the stores of gold and silver in Peru dominated their imagination. Consequently, the Spanish authorities viewed Buenos Aires as a small trading station, initially believing that a single small shipment of supplies per year would be sufficient.

This lack of attention and the unprotected state of the coast created an environment where smuggling became easy. This was further facilitated by the proximity of Portuguese settlements in Brazil, and the colonists soon learned to take advantage of these conditions.

## Portuguese Brazil and the State of Maranhão
The Portuguese presence in South America extended across a vast empire that eventually separated from Portugal. In the north, the coast of Maranhão was discovered by Pinzon in 1500 and included in the Portuguese grant of captaincies in 1534. However, the first European settlement there was actually a French trading expedition in 1594 led by Jacques Riffault. Later, in 1612, Daniel de la Rivardière founded a colony on the island of Maranhão under commission from the French crown. The Portuguese expelled the French in 1615, and the Dutch subsequently held the island from 1641 to 1644.

In 1621, Pará, Maranhão, and Ceará were united into the "Estado do Maranhão," which operated independently of the southern captaincies. While Ceará was later detached, the state of Maranhão remained independent until 1774, when it returned to the colonial administration of Brazil. Maranhão did not join the declaration of independence in 1822, but the Portuguese were driven out in 1823 by Admiral Lord Cochrane, and the province became part of the new empire of Brazil.

## Geography and Trade of the Brazilian Coast
The coast of Maranhão is described as broken and dangerous, featuring many shoals and small indentations often masked by islands. Key features include the Bay of Tury-assú and the contiguous bays of São José and São Marcos. The region is a heavily forested plain traversed by numerous rivers, such as the Parnahyba, the Tocantins, and the Gurupy, most of which have navigable channels flowing north to the Atlantic.

Other significant maritime centers included Bahia (or Sao Salvador), founded in 1549 by Thome de Souza, the first Portuguese governor-general of Brazil. Situated on the Bay of All Saints, Bahia possesses one of the most accessible and best harbors on the east coast of South America, supporting extensive foreign and coastwise trade.

## Political Evolution of Brazil
The relationship between Portugal and Brazil shifted dramatically in 1807 when the Portuguese monarchy fled to Brazil following the occupation of the mother country by Napoleon's French armies. During this period, Portugal became more of a dependency of Brazil than vice versa. In 1822, Brazil declared itself a separate Empire under Pedro I, a son of the Portuguese King. This monarchy lasted until 1889, when the Emperor was shipped back to Europe and the United States of Brazil became a republic.
