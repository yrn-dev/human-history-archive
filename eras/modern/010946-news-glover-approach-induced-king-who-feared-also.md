# The Anglo-Ashanti War of 1873-1874

The conflict between the British and the Ashanti was precipitated by two primary events. The most significant cause was the transfer of Elmina fort from Dutch to British control on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti regarded the people of Elmina as their subjects and the king of Ashanti held the "custom-note" for the fort. This note entitled the king to an annual payment from the Dutch, which the Dutch viewed as a present for trade purposes, but which the Ashanti considered a ground rent for territory they believed to be their own. Consequently, the Ashanti deeply resented the British occupation of this land.

A secondary, though minor, cause of the war involved the captivity of four Europeans held by the Ashanti. In June 1869, an Ashanti force invaded Krepi, a territory located beyond the Volta, where they captured Mr. J. Kuhne and members of the Basel mission, Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife, and their infant son. The child died shortly after the capture due to privation. Additionally, a French trader named Monsieur M.J. Bonnat was captured at a different location. These prisoners were taken to Kumasi. Although negotiations for their release had commenced, the Europeans remained prisoners at the time the sale of Elmina occurred.

## Outbreak of Hostilities

The Ashanti delayed the commencement of war until their preparations were complete. During this period, officials on the Gold Coast appeared to believe that the risk of hostilities was remote. However, on January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force crossed the Prah and invaded the British protectorate. The Ashanti forces defeated the Fanti and created disputes at Elmina. They eventually encamped at Mampon, near Cape Coast, causing significant alarm among the local inhabitants.

In response, the British took measures to defend the territory and punish the assailants. This led to the appointment of Sir Garnet Wolseley (later Viscount Wolseley) as the British administrator. To fund the expedition, the British parliament voted for expenses totaling £800,000.

## The British Expedition and Military Strategy

Sir Garnet Wolseley landed at Cape Coast on October 2. Upon his arrival, he discovered that the Ashanti forces, having been decimated by fever and smallpox, were preparing to return to their homes. Despite this, Wolseley determined to march toward Kumasi. 

To support this advance, Captain John Glover (later Sir John Glover), the administrator of Lagos, was tasked with cooperating from the east. Glover led a force of native levies with the objective of taking the Ashanti in the rear. As the British moved forward, the enemy broke up their camp, though they continued to be harassed by the native levies.
