# Moral Support and Legitimate Authority

The concept of legitimate authority and the moral support afforded to it varies across different historical, political, and legal contexts. In the records of the archive, legitimacy is often tied to the existence of an established system of government, the maintenance of imperial stability, or the adherence to specific legal and social codes.

## Legitimacy in Ancient Greece
In the political legends of ancient Greece, legitimacy was fundamentally linked to a determining rule or system, similar to a modern constitution. For a government to be considered legitimate and capable of inspiring a feeling of moral obligation to obey in the mind of a Greek citizen, it required an established system comprising three elements: temporary functionaries, specialized functions, and ultimate responsibility to the mass of qualified citizens (such as an Ecclesia or Senate).

While citizens might feel dissatisfaction with specific functionaries based on their competence or popularity, these personal feelings were typically secondary to the individual's attachment or aversion to the general system. Conversely, any individual who used craft or audacity to dismantle the constitution and establish himself as a permanent ruler according to his own will was viewed as illegitimate from the start. Such a ruler was branded as a *tyrannos* (despot), an object of dislike and fear. Because his authority was illegitimate, the act of taking his life was not viewed as a violation of the moral feelings that condemned blood-shedding in other cases, but was instead considered meritorious.

## Imperial Stability and the Habsburg Monarchy
In the context of 19th-century Europe, the pursuit of "stability" under Metternich was designed to protect the Habsburg monarchy, a structure described as artificial, unsound, and "rotten." This monarchy survived primarily due to the apathy of the populations it oppressed. To prevent the collapse of this system, Metternich worked to suppress national aspirations and Liberal principles in Germany and elsewhere in Europe.

This policy of stability extended to foreign affairs and the support of other monarchies. Metternich persuaded Frederick William III that granting a popular constitution would be fatal to the Prussian monarchy. Furthermore, the Troppau Protocol and the Vienna Final Act were utilized to ensure Germany remained quiet to avoid disturbing the "sleep of Austria." In the Eastern Question, the moral support provided to the "legitimate" authority of the sultan over the "rebel" Greeks was not based on a general principle of legitimacy, but was dictated solely by Austria's interest in maintaining the integrity of Turkey.

## Authority in Early English Statehood
In early English history, the king's council (known by terms such as *witan*, *þeod*, *plebs*, or *concilium*) served as a central authority. This body included senior officials and young warriors, some of whom were members of the royal family, though others were nobles attracted to the service of popular kings like Oswine.

The council functioned in a deliberative capacity. It was customary for the king to seek the moral support and advice of the council on important questions. However, there is no evidence that the king was required to abide by the majority opinion. While some have claimed the council had the right to depose or elect kings, the available information suggests this may be an overstatement of the feeling for constitutionalism at the time; such events may instead have been instances of the transference of personal allegiance.

## Legal and Philosophical Definitions of Legitimacy
Outside of political governance, the archive provides definitions of legitimacy in legal and philosophical terms:

*   **Legal Legitimacy:** In a legal sense, legitimacy refers to birth in accordance with law (lawful wedlock). Illegitimacy is the state of being born out of wedlock. The prevalence of illegitimacy varies by locality due to social, racial, and climatic differences, as well as legislation—such as laws requiring parental consent or the completion of military service before marriage.
*   **Philosophical and Academic Legitimacy:** In philosophy, certain topics are identified as "legitimate problems for philosophical investigation," such as the origin of the universe and the destiny of the human race. In academic and historical criticism, "legitimate" refers to evidence or traditions that cannot be avoided or evaded, such as the domestic tradition regarding the authorship of the *Imitation* by Thomas.
