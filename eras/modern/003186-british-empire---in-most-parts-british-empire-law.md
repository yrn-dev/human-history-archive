# British Empire

The British Empire was a unique and varied political combination characterized by a wide array of legal systems, administrative structures, and economic dependencies. Its expansion was driven primarily by trade, which served as both the most active cause of growth and the most potent bond of union.

## Political Structure and Governance
At the center of the imperial system was the "crowned republic" of the United British Kingdoms, which included Ireland, though this was against the will of a considerable portion of the Irish people. The effective supreme government was the ministry, which held powers of peace and war over the rest of the empire. This ministry's policy and headship were determined by the British Parliament, composed of the three united parliaments of England (and Wales), Scotland, and Ireland, and was largely influenced by British domestic politics.

The empire's constituents were organized into several tiers of political importance:
*   **Self-Governing States:** Australia, Canada, New Zealand, South Africa, and Newfoundland (the oldest possession, dating to 1583) were "crowned republics." These were practically independent states in alliance with Great Britain, each featuring a representative of the Crown appointed by the government in office.
*   **The Indian Empire:** An extension of the empire of the Great Mogul, this region reached from Baluchistan to Burmah and included Aden. Here, the British Crown and the India Office, under Parliamentary control, assumed the role of the original Turkoman dynasty.
*   **Ambiguous and Joint Possessions:** Egypt remained nominally part of the Turkish Empire under its own monarch, the Khedive, but was subject to almost despotic British official rule. The "Anglo-Egyptian" Sudan province was administered jointly by the British and the British-controlled Egyptian government.
*   **Partially Self-Governing Communities:** Entities such as Bermuda, the Bahamas, Jamaica, and Malta possessed elected legislatures and appointed executives.
*   **Crown Colonies:** In places like Fiji, Trinidad, and Ceylon, rule via the Colonial Office verged on autocracy. In Gibraltar and St. Helena, governance was conducted through a governor.
*   **Protectorates:** These were primarily tropical raw-product areas with politically weak native communities. They were administered either through a chartered company (as in Rhodesia) or a High Commissioner overseeing native chiefs (as in Basutoland).

## Legal Systems and Domicile
Because the British Empire included a great variety of laws—including those of Scotland, England, the Cape Colony, and the province of Quebec—it did not adopt political nationality as a criterion for law in the way many continental states did. Instead, within the British dominions, domicile remained the only available criterion for the legal character of a British subject. British courts applied this principle of domicile to foreigners and subjects outside the dominions; for instance, a British court would refer the age of majority for a Frenchman domiciled in Germany to German law.

In the realm of commercial law, the English system of bankruptcy served as a model for most of the empire, particularly in New Zealand and Australia. While Victoria, Western Australia, and South Australia followed existing English acts, New South Wales, Tasmania, and Queensland utilized the English act of 1869, which favored creditors' management over officialism. Notably, New Zealand, Victoria, and New South Wales improved upon the English system by requiring bankrupts to apply for discharge within a fixed period to avoid contempt of court, whereas in the United Kingdom, there was no such obligation, resulting in 70,000 undischarged bankrupts.

## Economic Development and Industry
The empire's growth was heavily facilitated by trading companies, which founded most of the West Indian and American colonies, colonized the north-western districts of Canada, and won India. The command of the sea, established through 18th-century commercial wars, enabled the settlement of Australasia and the acquisition of South Africa. In the 19th century, chartered companies extended the British flag into the interior of Africa and developed Fiji and Borneo.

The revolution in transport—specifically steam, electricity, and railways—allowed the empire to expand beyond river valleys and sea-shores into the interiors of continents. This opened vast inland spaces to individual enterprise and increased the production of raw materials. By the early 20th century, the empire's tropical and temperate possessions produced every main staple of human food and every material for the textile industry. Globally, the British Empire produced one-sixth of the wheat supply, one-third of the coal supply, and nearly two-thirds of the gold supply, while employing more than one-third of the world's mining and quarrying workforce.

## Strategic Importance of the Navy
The imperial navy served as a critical material link, protecting colonial commerce and connecting the crown to outlying possessions. The empire's might, dominion, and the livelihood of its population were seen as floating upon its fleet. It was believed that if the fleet were lost, the central power of union would break, and the empire would dissolve into isolated communities and drifting provinces, potentially leaving the United States as the sole maintainer of law and freedom.
