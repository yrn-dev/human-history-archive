# Statute of Limitations on Money Left in a Banker's Hands

## The Legal Relation Between Banker and Customer
The relationship between a banker and a customer is defined in law as that of debtor and creditor. This was established by the House of Lords in the 1848 case of *Foley v. Hill*, which clarified that the relation is not one of trustee and *cestui que trust*, nor is it that of depositee and depositor. Consequently, when a person pays money into a bank account, the legal effect is simply that the person is lending the money to the banker. This arrangement does not create a fiduciary relation, and the banker is not held responsible to the customer for how the deposited money is used.

## Application of the Statute of Limitations
Because the money held by a banker is classified as a mere debt, the customer's right to recover these funds is subject to the Statute of Limitations. Under this statute, the right to recover money paid in is barred upon the expiration of six years. This bar applies if there has been no payment made in the meantime regarding the interest or principal, and if there has been no acknowledgment sufficient to bar the statute, as seen in *Pott v. Clegg*.

In practice, this state of affairs is unlikely to occur due to the standard system of appropriation in current accounts. Unless there is a specific appropriation, earlier drawings are attributed to earlier payments, meaning items on the debit and credit sides typically cancel each other out.

## Exceptions and Special Circumstances
An apparent exception to the standard system of appropriation occurs when an individual wrongfully pays money into their own account that they are holding in a fiduciary capacity. In such instances, it is presumed that the individual has drawn out their own funds rather than the money affected by the trust. As long as the account remains in credit, any balance is attributed to the trust money. If there are competing claims to the money based on different breaches of trust, the ordinary rules of appropriation are applied.

## Withdrawal and the Role of Cheques
It has been suggested that the presentation of a cheque is a condition precedent to a banker's liability to repay money, or that it is the only method of withdrawing funds. However, this view is inconsistent with the effect of the Statute of Limitations on money left in a banker's hands. It is also inconsistent with numerous cases where a bank balance has been attached as an unconditional and simple debt via a garnishee order, such as in *Rogers v. Whiteley* (1892).

While the banker and customer are debtor and creditor, the banker has a superadded obligation to honor a customer's cheques, provided there is a sufficient and available balance. If a banker dishonors a cheque despite having sufficient funds, they are liable for substantial damages to the customer without the need for proof of actual injury (*Rolin v. Steward*).

## Other Banking Obligations and Rights
The banker's role involves several specific legal parameters regarding the handling of funds:

*   **Available Balance:** Money paid in so shortly before a cheque is presented that it has not had time to pass through the bank's books is not treated as available for drawing.
*   **Branch Restrictions:** A person with an account at one branch is not entitled to draw cheques on another branch where they are overdrawn or have no account.
*   **Banker's Lien:** Bankers possess an exceptional right known as the banker's lien, part of the law merchant. This allows them to retain and apply any of the customer's securities coming into their possession as a banker to discharge the customer's liability. This includes cheques and bills paid in for collection (*Currie v. Misa*). However, money paid in to meet particular bills or goods deposited for safe custody are exempt from this lien.
*   **Deposit Accounts:** Whether a deposit account is on fixed notice or at call, it constitutes a debt due from the banker to the depositor and does not establish a fiduciary relation.
