# Geographical and Political Developments in the Farther East

## Geographical Exploration and Trade
Since 1874, geographical research in the Farther East has progressed rapidly, beginning with investigations into the route connecting Burma and China. This period of exploration was marked by the death of Augustus Margary and followed by a "flood of inquiring experts" seeking to unlock doors to trade for economic and commercial development in China. 

Several key figures contributed to the mapping of the region. Captain William Gill of the Indian survey traveled across China to Burma and eastern Tibet, documenting his experiences in the *River of Golden Sand*. In 1877-1878, E.C. Baber explored the western provinces of the Celestial empire. Later, in 1881, Archibald Colquhoun traveled from Peking to Talifu, and in 1887, Mark Bell crossed the continent, illustrating ancient trade routes.

The acquisition of Burma and the demarcation of its boundaries further extended geographical surveys. Prince Henri d'Orleans was among the first to travel to the mountains containing the sources of the Mekong, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy. While James M'Carthy handled map-making in Siam, Warrington Smyth and Curzon provided information on its coast districts. Between 1900 and 1904, C.C. Manifold mapped the upper basin of the Yang-tsze river. Additionally, the Russo-Japanese war, which ended in 1905, advanced knowledge of the geography of the north-east Russo-Chinese borderland.

## Geography of Korea
Korea is characterized as a distinctly mountainous region lacking significant plains. The north contains mountain groups with definite centers, most notably Pei-shan (or Paik-tu San), which reaches 8,700 feet and holds the sources of the Tumen and Yalu rivers. A lofty range runs southwards, dividing the empire into two unequal parts. 

The eastern side of this range consists of a fertile strip of land that is difficult to access. The western side is a chaos of steep-sided valleys and corrugated, precipitous hills, each featuring a rapid perennial stream. To the south, the axial range—which includes the Diamond Mountain group—descends toward the sea in small, often infertile levels and treeless spurs. While the Diamond Mountain and northern groups are heavily timbered, other hills are primarily covered in oak and chestnut scrub and coarse, sour grass.

Most Korean rivers are rocky and shallow, remaining navigable for only a few miles from the sea. Notable exceptions include the Naktong, Tumen, Mok-po, Tai-dong, Yalu (Amnok), and Han. The Han river rises in Kang-wön-do, 30 miles from the east coast, and cuts the country nearly in half before reaching the west coast near Chemulpo; it serves as a valuable commercial highway for over 150 miles despite serious rapids.

## Regional Political History and Conquests
Asia has often been viewed as the birthplace of civilization due to the antiquity of its records, though it is noted that the Aryans brought civilization to India from the west. Historically, small aristocratic or feudal states in Asia consolidated into monarchies. Unlike Europe, where royalty was sometimes replaced by popular government, revolution in Asia generally resulted only in a change of dynasty, with Arabia representing a persistence of very ancient non-monarchical conditions.

In East Asia, China flourished under the native Ming dynasty after throwing off the Mongol yoke in 1360. This lasted until 1644, when the Manchus, another Mongol people, reconquered the region and remained in power until 1912. 

Simultaneously, Russia expanded eastward. This growth was largely driven by the Cossacks, a Christian steppe people who acted as a barrier between the Tartar to the east and the feudal agriculture of Hungary and Poland to the west. The Cossacks were composed of various fugitives, including criminals, rebellious serfs, religious secretaries, and those persecuted or fleeing the Tartars. Eventually incorporated into the Russian imperial service, the Cossacks were used as a weapon against Mongolian nomads, expanding through Siberia as far as the Amur and into Turkestan.

## Religious Progress in Burma
In Burma, the progress of Christianity has varied by ethnic group. While the faith has progressed very slowly among the Burmese—with not quite two in every thousand professing the religion—it has seen rapid progress among the Karens, particularly the Sgaw Karens, where spirit-worshippers are fewer. 

The number of Burmese Christians has been increased by the descendants of Portuguese settlers from Syriam who were deported to the village of Tabayin in the Shwebo Ye-u subdivision. The most successful forms of Christianity in the region are the Roman Catholic and Baptist faiths; specifically, American Baptist missionaries have made many conversions among the Muhsö or Lahu hill tribesmen. Mahommedans exist in Burma in numbers roughly equal to those of the Christians.
