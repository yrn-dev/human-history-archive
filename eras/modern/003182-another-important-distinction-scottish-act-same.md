# Scottish Bankruptcy and Tenure Laws

## Bankruptcy Law and Sequestration
In Scotland, bankruptcy law was developed as a remedy to address the frauds of insolvent debtors. An act of the Scottish parliament in 1621 (c. 18) established that no debtor should fraudulently diminish the funds belonging to creditors following insolvency. Under this act, fraudulent dealing was presumed if a deed of assignment was executed gratuitously in favor of a confidential friend or near relation after debts were contracted.

The definition of a "notour" or notorious bankrupt was settled by the act 1696, c. 5. A notour bankrupt was defined as any debtor who, while under diligence by caption or horning at the instance of creditors, either fled, absconded for personal security, defended their person by force, retired to the abbey or another privileged place, or was imprisoned, and was subsequently found insolvent by a sentence of the lords of session. While originally intended to remedy fraudulent preference rather than serve as a general distribution process, later statutes made this definition a necessary requisite for such processes. The act of 1696 included exceptions for persons with special privileges or those absent from Scotland (and thus not liable to imprisonment), though later legislation removed these exceptions. Additionally, the statute rendered null and void all assignations, voluntary dispositions, and other deeds made within sixty days before bankruptcy or after it occurred.

A significant distinction between the systems is that Scottish law does not recognize the English distinction between non-traders and traders.

## The Process of Sequestration
Sequestration in Scotland may be awarded by the sheriff or the court of session. Creditors may recall a sequestration if a majority in number and four-fifths in value agree to wind up the estate through a private arrangement. If the sequestration continues, creditors hold a meeting to elect a trustee to administer the estate by a majority in value. They also elect three commissioners—who must be mandatories or creditors—to control the administration, assist, and declare dividends.

The bankrupt is required to provide all available information regarding their estate under pain of imprisonment and must undergo a public examination on oath before the sheriff. "Confident and conjunct persons" may also be examined.

## Discharge of the Bankrupt
A bankrupt may be discharged either with or without composition. In cases without composition, discharge can occur through the following means:
* By petition with the concurrence of all creditors.
* After six months, with the concurrence of a majority and four-fifths in value of the creditors.
* After eighteen months, with the concurrence of a bare majority in number and value.
* After two years, without any concurrence.

In the instance of discharge without concurrence, a judge may refuse the application if the bankrupt wilfully failed to comply with the law or fraudulently concealed effects.

## Legislative Amendments
Several acts have modified these laws over time:
* **Bankruptcy and Real Securities Act 1857:** Addressed the cost of competition for trusteeships.
* **Bankruptcy Amendment (Scotland) Act 1860:** Allowed the court to recall sequestration if winding up the estate in Ireland or England was more convenient.
* **Bankruptcy Amendment Act (Scotland) 1875:** Established preferential claims for the wages of servants, shopmen, and clerks for an amount not exceeding L50 and a period not exceeding four months. Workmen's claims were placed on a similar footing for a period not exceeding two months.
* **Debtors (Scotland) Act 1880:** This act abolished imprisonment for debt, though it maintained provisions for the punishment of fraudulent bankrupts.

## Burgage Tenure
Burgage (from the Latin *burgus*, meaning a borough) is a form of tenure applicable to property connected with old municipal corporations and their privileges in both Scotland and England. In Scotland, burgage-holding held a significant place in conveyancing practice, as real property was generally divided into burgage-holding and feudal-holding.

The Scottish system preserved this distinct form because burgage-holding was an exception to the system of subinfeudation, which remained prevalent in Scotland even after it was suppressed in England. While other vassals held through a graduated hierarchy of overlords up to the crown, a burgess always held directly from the sovereign. 

Furthermore, while English burgage-tenure was considered a species of socage to distinguish it from military holdings, in Scotland it was strictly a military holding, requiring the service of warding and watching for the defense of the burgh. Following the Conveyancing (Scotland) Act 1874, the distinction between free holding and burgage tenure has largely disappeared.
