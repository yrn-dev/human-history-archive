# Diplomatic Missions and Secret Services

Diplomatic missions serve as the primary mechanism for communication and negotiation between sovereigns and independent states. The evolution of these missions has transitioned from occasional ambassadors to the establishment of resident representatives with permanent residences. This development was driven by the increasing volume of intercourse between states and their nationals.

## The Doctrine of Exterritoriality
The function of diplomatic missions is supported by the doctrine of exterritoriality, which ensures the inviolability of legations and embassies. This immunity grew from the necessity for sovereigns to respect one another's persons to facilitate the discussion of joint interests without the danger of interference or arrest. Consequently, the inviolability originally granted to a sovereign is vested in the sovereign's delegate, extending to the delegate's residence and the accompanying personnel, including various classes of diplomatic secretaries.

## French Diplomatic and Secret Services
France has utilized both formal diplomatic posts and secret services to advance its state interests across various eras.

### The Secret du Roi
Under Louis XV, the *Secret du roi* functioned as a secret service. Charles Francois Dumouriez, a French general, served as a member of this organization, utilizing his diplomatic resources. In 1770, Dumouriez was sent on a mission to Poland, where he performed political business and organized a Polish militia.

### Diplomatic Careers under the Monarchy and Republic
Marc Marie, Marquis de Bombelles (1744-1822), entered the diplomatic service in 1765. In 1786, he served as the French ambassador to Portugal with the objective of winning the country over to the Family Compact, though the death of the king and the madness of the queen hindered his success. Later transferred to Vienna in 1789, his career was interrupted by the Revolution, and he lost his post in September 1790. Following this, he remained attached to Louis XVI and was tasked with secret missions to other sovereigns to secure aid for the king.

During the French Republic, Hugues-Bernard, Duc de Bassano Maret (1763-1839), served in the ministry of foreign affairs. In December 1792, Maret was sent on a mission to London, where he interviewed Pitt, though an accommodation was not reached. After the execution of Louis XVI in January 1793 and the subsequent declaration of war in February, a second mission to London in January became impossible. Maret later served as the ambassador of the French Republic at Naples, though he was captured by the Austrians and imprisoned for approximately thirty months until late 1795. He later participated in peace negotiations with Great Britain at Lille in the summer of 1797.

## Missions in Other Sovereignties
Diplomatic and exploratory missions have also been central to the administration of other empires. Messer Marco Polo spent seventeen years in the employment of the Great Kaan. He was entrusted with weighty and distant missions, which he executed with success and discretion. Polo utilized these missions to gather knowledge of various countries and the interests of the sovereign, relating his findings in regular order upon his return to Court.

## Military and Political Employment
Beyond formal diplomacy, individuals have been employed in strategic military or political capacities to gain advantages. In ancient history, Philip employed specific tactics against the Illyrians, strengthening his right wing to defeat their left before attacking their center in flank. Additionally, mercenaries were employed in Phokis, some of whom later served Timoleon in Sicily. In Athens, the intrigues of Elpinikê were employed by opposing political parties to either calumniate Kimon or discredit Periklês.
