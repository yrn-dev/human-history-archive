# Military Personnel and Ranks

## Republic Military Forces (1903-1905)
In 1905, the military forces of the republic consisted of 2890 regulars and an enrolled force totaling 80,000 men. This enrolled force was divided into a second reserve of 40,000, a first reserve of 30,000, and 10,000 territorial guards, though these forces were unarmed and unorganized. 

The strength of the army was determined by each year's budget. The budget for 1903 provided for 2933 officers and men. This total was comprised of 275 commissioned officers, 558 non-commissioned officers, 181 musicians, and 1906 rank and file. To support these forces, the republic maintained an arsenal at La Paz and a military school with 60 cadets. A conscription law passed in 1894 mandated compulsory military service for individuals between the ages of fifty and twenty-one. Those between twenty-one and twenty-five were required to perform two years of actual service in the regulars, although the law was practically a dead letter.

## Non-Commissioned Officers: Ranks and Definitions
Non-commissioned officers (NCOs) occupy various roles across different military branches. The corporal is a non-commissioned officer found in the artillery, cavalry, and infantry, ranking below a sergeant. This rank is nearly universal in armies. In the 16th and 17th centuries, the cavalry employed corporals but no sergeants; this tradition continues in the three British household cavalry regiments, where "corporal-major" replaces sergeant-major and "corporal of horse" replaces sergeant. During the 16th and early 17th centuries, a superior officer serving as a staff-officer to the sergeant-major-general was often called a "corporal of the field." In naval contexts, the "ship's corporal," once a semi-military instructor, is now a petty officer who assists the master-at-arms with police duties.

In the British army's artillery, the rank of bombardier is a non-commissioned officer who ranks below a corporal. Historically, the term bombardier referred to an artilleryman in charge of a bombard, a type of medieval cannon used primarily in sieges to throw heavy stone balls.

## Accommodations and Facilities
The housing and social arrangements for non-commissioned officers vary by nation and marital status. In the British army, senior NCOs are provided with a sergeants' mess that includes a reading-room, dining-room, billiard-room, liquor store, and kitchen premises; the liquor store also features a jug department for the families of sergeants. While single NCOs eat all meals in this mess, married members use it as a club.

Married NCOs and warrant officers are housed at some distance from the barracks for men. Recent designs favor self-contained cottages on open sites over older tenement buildings with common verandahs or staircases. Warrant officers are provided with a bathroom, three bedrooms, a kitchen, a scullery, and a living-room. Married soldiers receive a living-room, a scullery, and one to three bedrooms depending on family size. A laundry with an ironing-room, drying-closet, wringer, and washing-troughs is located adjacent to the married quarters.

Other national systems differ slightly. German and French barracks often use a large block of three or four storeys to house a complete regiment or battalion, including men, rank and file, and married and single NCOs. While the German army provides a public-funded officers' mess-house for every regiment, married quarters are provided for some NCOs but not for privates. American barracks typically utilize a separate block system centered around a soldiers' club or post-exchange, which combines a gymnasium, sergeants' mess, and recreation establishment with an attached bath-house.

## Field Operations and Personnel
Military forces in the field often include a mix of officers and NCOs. For example, during a campaign to crush a rebellion following the relief of Kumasi, Colonel Willcocks commanded a force of 3368 natives (including Sikhs from Central Africa and Yaos), supported by 35 British non-commissioned officers and 134 British officers. This force, along with Ashanti levies, defeated the Kumasi at Obassa on September 30. The campaign resulted in 1007 total casualties, including those who died of disease; among the British, six died of disease, forty-three were wounded, and nine officers were killed in action.
