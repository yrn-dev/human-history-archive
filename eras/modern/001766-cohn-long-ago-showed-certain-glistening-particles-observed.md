# Sulphur Bacteria and the Contributions of Cohn

## Overview of Sulphur Bacteria
Sulphur bacteria consist of a series of genera, including *Thiothrix*, *Chromatium*, *Spirillum*, and *Monas*. These organisms play significant roles in the natural circulation of sulphur within environments such as estuaries, sulphur springs, and marshes. In these habitats, cellulose bacteria release marsh gas, and the resulting nascent gas reduces sulphates (such as gypsum), liberating hydrogen sulphide ($\text{SH}_2$). Sulphur bacteria thrive under these conditions by oxidizing the $\text{SH}_2$ and storing the resulting sulphur within their own protoplasm.

If the supply of $\text{SH}_2$ becomes insufficient, these bacteria oxidize the stored sulphur further into sulphuric acid. This acid then combines with any available calcium carbonate to form sulphate once again. In similar environments, nascent methane may reduce iron salts, leading to the formation of $\text{FeS}$, which gives the mud in these areas its black color.

## Physiological Activity and Respiration
Research by Jegunow and Beyerinck has demonstrated that certain partially anaerobic sulphur bacteria exist only at specific depths beneath quiet waters. In these strata, $\text{SH}_2$ is released from below via the bacterial decomposition of vegetable mud and rises to meet atmospheric oxygen descending from above. This zone of physiological activity fluctuates based on the partial pressure of gases, which is influenced by the rate of $\text{SH}_2$ evolution.

Within this zone, bacteria absorb $\text{SH}_2$ in the deeper sections. As they rise, they oxidize it and store the sulphur; upon reaching more highly oxygenated planes, they oxidize the sulphur to $\text{SO}_3$. Consequently, these bacteria utilize $\text{SH}_2$ as their respiratory substance. While higher plants utilize carbohydrates to liberate energy, sulphur bacteria achieve this through the oxidation of hydrogen sulphide rather than the respiratory combustion of sugars. Furthermore, Beyerinck identified *Spirillum desulphuricans* as a definite anaerobic form that reduces sulphates, effectively reversing the work performed by sulphur bacteria.

## Classification and Morphology
The study of these organisms is part of the broader study of Schizomycetes. Cohn's classification of bacteria, published in 1872 and extended in 1875, dominated the field for a significant period. Cohn based his system on the assumption that bacterial forms are practically constant, treating the constancy of forms as the basis for defining species and genera. For example, he observed that rod-like bacilli regularly produced similar bacilli.

Within the Order Trichobacterinae, which consists of branched or unbranched cell-filaments that separate as swarm-cells (*Gonidia*), different families and genera are distinguished by their morphology:
*   **Family Trichobacteriaceae**:
    *   *Crenothrix* (Cohn): Rigid, non-motile, sheathed filaments that are unbranched and lack sulphur particles.
    *   *Thiothrix* (Winogr.): Rigid, non-motile, sheathed filaments that contain sulphur particles.
    *   *Cladothrix* (Cohn): Rigid, non-motile, sheathed filaments that are branched in a pseudo-dichotomous manner.
    *   *Beggiatoa* (Trev.): Filaments that show creeping and slow pendulous movements, lack a distinct sheath, and contain sulphur particles. Cohn specifically showed that the glistening particles observed in the cells of *Beggiatoa* consist of sulphur.

## Evolution of Bacteriological Theory
The doctrine of constant forms faced challenges in 1873 when Lankester noted that *Bacterium rubescens* (later named *Beggiatoa roseo-persicina* by Zopf) passes through various conditions. These phases—which include forms known as *Bacillus*, *Micrococcus*, *Bacterium*, and *Leptothrix*—might have been mistaken for separate species or genera by those following the current doctrine. This led to discussions regarding pleomorphism (the mutability of shape). While some forms show different cell types during their life-history, it is generally agreed that the majority are uniform.

Cohn also contributed to the discovery of spores in Schizomycetes. Although he expressed this dubiously in 1872, he had discovered the fact in 1857 and observed spores germinating in 1876, a finding later confirmed by others including Koch and Brefeld.

## Relation to Disease and Fermentation
By 1872, Cohn was justified in grouping various "pathogenous" Schizomycetes together, contributing to the foundations of the "germ theory of disease." This theory posits that contagious and infectious diseases—such as tuberculosis, septicaemia, fowl-cholera, and glanders—are caused by the action of bacteria, and in some instances, protozoa or fungi. This followed earlier work by Pasteur, who showed in 1857 that lactic fermentation depends on an organism, and later proved in 1862 that the ammoniacal fermentation of urea is caused by a minute Schizomycete, a finding Cohn confirmed in 1874.
