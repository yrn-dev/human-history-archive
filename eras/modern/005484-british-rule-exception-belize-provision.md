# British Rule in Belize and British Honduras

British Honduras, formerly known as Balize or Belize, is a British crown colony located in Central America. It is bordered by the Mexican province of Yucatan to the north and northwest, the Bay of Honduras (an inlet of the Caribbean Sea) to the northeast and east, and Guatemala to the south and west. The colony covers an area of 7,562 square miles and had a population of 40,372 in 1905.

## Origins and Early Settlement
The territory, once officially styled as "His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras," originated in 1638. It was established by log-wood cutters who had previously been buccaneers. These settlers were later joined by agents from a Chartered Company that exploited pearl fisheries on the Mosquito coast. The early settlers frequently raided Spanish logwood establishments, and while the Spanish attempted to expel them, these efforts were generally resisted. A notable encounter occurred in April 1754, when a Spanish expedition of 1,500 men was routed by 250 British forces near the coast.

The 1763 treaty with Spain required Great Britain to demolish fortifications in the Bay of Honduras, but it included a clause ensuring that logwood cutters and their workmen would not be molested in their places of cutting and loading. Despite this, tensions continued; on September 15, 1779, Spanish forces destroyed the establishment at Belize and took the inhabitants prisoner to Mérida and Havana. Survivors were liberated in 1782 and allowed to go to Jamaica, though many returned in 1783 to resume woodcutting.

## Establishment of Sovereignty
The legal transition to British sovereignty involved several treaties and military events. While some cite the defeat of a Spanish attempt in 1798 as an act of conquest, others note that a new treaty with Spain in 1814 revived earlier provisions. Between 1817 and 1819, acts of parliament still described Belize as "a settlement, for certain purposes, under the protection of His Majesty."

Following the independence of Central America between 1819 and 1822, Great Britain secured its position through a 1826 treaty with Mexico, which incorporated the provisions of the 1786 treaty. Similar provisions were included in draft treaties with the United States of Central America (1831) and New Granada (1825). In 1836, the British claimed the territories between the Sarstoon and Belize rivers.

A critical turning point occurred with the Clayton-Bulwer Treaty of 1850. While this treaty forbade the United States and Great Britain from colonizing or fortifying any point on the Central American mainland, Belize was granted an exception from this provision. This exception, combined with the settlement of boundary disputes with Guatemala in 1859, finally confirmed Great Britain's legal sovereignty over the entire colony.

## Administration and Geography
The colony's frontier is defined by conventions from 1859 and 1893. It begins at the mouth of the river Sarstoon, follows that river to the rapids of Gracias à Dios, runs in a straight line to Garbutt's Rapids on the Belize river, and proceeds north to the Mexican frontier, following the river Hondo to Chetumal Bay.

The capital and principal seaport is Belize, located at the mouth of the river Belize. As of 1904, the town had a population of 9,969. The town's architecture typically features wooden houses with wide verandahs and high roofs. Key buildings include the court house, government house, Fort George, the British bank of Honduras, a hospital, a Roman Catholic convent, and a Wesleyan church. The surrounding mangrove swamps have historically led to frequent epidemics of yellow fever and cholera, though sea breezes and high tides mitigate the climate's unhealthiness.

## Economy and Infrastructure
The economy of British Honduras is heavily based on the export of timber—including mahogany, rosewood, cedar, logwood, and other dye-woods and cabinet-woods—as well as bananas, sugar, cocoanuts, sarsaparilla, turtles, deerskins, and tortoiseshell. Principal imports include hardware, cotton fabrics, and breadstuffs.

Between 1901 and 1905, the colony saw growth in trade: vessel tonnage rose from 300,000 to 496,465, and exports increased from £285,500 to £377,623. The American gold dollar was adopted as the standard coin on October 14, 1894, replacing the Guatemalan dollar. The British sovereign and half sovereign remain legal tender.

Infrastructure is limited; there are no railways and road communication is defective. The colony relies on telegraph and telephone lines connecting the capital to towns like Corosal and Punta Gorda, and on steamers plying the coast from ports such as Liverpool, New Orleans, Colon, and Puerto Cortes.

## Society and Defense
The population is diverse, consisting of six distinct racial groups: native Indians; descendants of English, Scottish, and German buccaneers and traders; "Belize Creoles" (descendants of African slaves or laborers); Caribs of mixed African and Carib origin; and two Spanish-Indian groups from Guatemala, Honduras, and Yucatan.

Religion is represented by Anglican, Roman Catholic, Baptist, Presbyterian, and Wesleyan churches, though none receive public funds. Education is primarily denominational, with most schools under a board established in 1894.

Defense is managed by the police (numbering about 120) and the Belize volunteer light infantry corps, which was raised in 1897. A mounted section was added in 1904, and a volunteer fire brigade consists of 335 members.
