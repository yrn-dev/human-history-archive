# The Austro-Hungarian Delegations

The Delegations were common institutions of the Dual Monarchy, established to exercise political control and the right of voting money applicable to the common affairs of Austria and Hungary. Their creation was part of a system of dualism intended to establish complete parity between Hungary and the other territories of the king.

## Composition and Election
Each Delegation consisted of sixty members who were chosen for a one-year term. The selection process was divided between the upper and lower houses of the respective parliaments: one-third of the members were chosen by the Austrian Herrenhaus and the Hungarian Table of Magnates, while the remaining two-thirds were elected by the Austrian and Hungarian Houses of Representatives. Members of both delegations enjoyed constitutional immunity and were considered irresponsible in the exercise of their office.

## Meetings and Procedures
The monarch summoned the delegations annually, with meetings held alternately in Vienna and Budapest (referred to in some records as Pest). To ensure that the Delegations did not overshadow the parliaments that appointed them, the two bodies did not sit together; instead, each held separate, public sittings.

Communication between the two delegations was conducted in writing, with every document accompanied by a translation in either German or Magyar. If the delegations failed to reach an agreement after three such written interchanges, a common plenary sitting was convened. During this joint session, an equal number of members from both delegations voted on the question in silence, without discussion. A simple majority was sufficient for a decision, though all common decisions required the sanction of the monarch to be valid.

## Powers and Responsibilities
The Delegations served as the body to which the common ministers were responsible. There were three ministers designated for common purposes:
1. Foreign Affairs
2. War
3. Finance

The Delegations had the authority to formulate independent resolutions and to arraign or call the common ministers to account. They were also the body to which estimates for joint services were submitted. 

Despite these powers, the Delegations were not granted legislative power. For example, while the minister of war controlled the common army, the laws regarding army recruitment had to be voted on separately in each parliament. Similarly, while the minister of finance presented the common budget to the Delegations, they could not vote taxes or raise money; the required funds had to be provided by the separate parliaments. The proportion of the contribution from each half of the monarchy was fixed every ten years.

## Role in Foreign Policy
The Delegations played a critical role in the monarchy's foreign policy. Because the numbers of members were fewer and "passions cooler" than in the parliaments, discussions on foreign policy were held within the Delegations. This environment helped maintain a peaceful and unambitious foreign policy and supported the close connection with Germany, preventing internal national enmities from reacting too violently upon the monarchy's external relations.

## Historical Context and Crisis
The framework of the Dual Monarchy was established in 1867 following the defeat of Austria by Prussia at Sadowa in 1866. This system remained the primary polity until the Austro-Hungarian crisis of 1903–1907, which raised questions about whether the dual system represented a permanent form of government. 

During this period, the Hungarian Delegation's election and the voting of extraordinary military credits (sanctioned by the delegations in 1904) became points of contention in pacts between the crown and the Coalition government. These events occurred alongside movements in Budapest to Magyarize institutions and efforts by the Independence party to seek economic and political separation from Austria, except regarding the person of the ruler.
