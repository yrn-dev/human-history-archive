# Albania and Macedonia

The regions of Albania and Macedonia, located on the Balkan Peninsula, have been characterized by complex ethnographic shifts, diverse religious affiliations, and various political administrations, including significant periods of Turkish rule.

## Historical Development
The ethnographic composition of the peninsula was fundamentally altered by a great Slavonic immigration beginning in the 3rd century A.D. and continuing for four centuries. During this period, the Byzantine empire held nominal or actual possession of regions south of the Danube. While the native Thraco-Illyrian populations of the interior were largely romanized and spoke Latin, the aboriginal Illyrian element in Albania maintained its ancient language in the mountains and eventually forced back the immigrant race. In the 7th century, Serbo-Croats invaded north-western regions, including northern Albania and Servia, expelling or assimilating the Illyrian population.

The region saw the rise and fall of several empires. The first Bulgarian empire extended its reach into central Macedonia under Tsar Krum (802-815) and from the Adriatic to the Black Sea under Simeon (893-927). Following the overthrow of the first empire in 971, power was revived under the Shishman dynasty at Ochrida. In 1186, a "Bulgaro-Vlach" second empire was established after an insurrection by Vlachs and Bulgars. Under Ivan Asên II (1218-1241), this empire established sway over Macedonia, Albania, Epirus, and Thrace. This period of prosperity ended with the extinction of the Asên dynasty in 1257, leading to a period of decadence and eventual subjugation by Servia under Stephen Dushan (1331-1355). Later, the Christian races of the peninsula became prey to Moslem invaders.

## The Vilayet of Kossovo
Kossovo was a vilayet of European Turkey with an area of 12,700 square miles and a population of approximately 1,100,000 in 1905. It comprised the sanjak of Uskub in Macedonia and the sanjaks of Novibazar and Prizren in northern Albania. The capital was Usküb, where a railway from Salonica bifurcated, with branches leading to Nish in Servia and Mitrovitza in Albania.

The economy of Kossovo was noted for its agricultural trade, which was unsurpassed by any other Turkish province. Exports worth about £950,000 included grain, fruit, livestock, tobacco, skins, hemp, opium, and vegetables. Sericulture was a growing industry encouraged by the Administration of the Ottoman Debt. Imports, valued at approximately £1,200,000, consisted of metals, machinery, chemical products, groceries, and petroleum. Petroleum was used in factories and flour-mills because coal prices were prohibitive. Mineral wealth existed, though in 1907 only two chrome mines—Verbeshtitza and Orasha—were operational. Major towns included Usküb (32,000), Prizren (30,000), Koprülü (22,000), Ishtib (21,000), Novibazar (12,000), and Prishtina (11,000).

## Ethnography and Population
The inhabitants of the region include Albanians, Slavs, Greeks, Turks, Vlachs, and gipsies. Albanians, known as *Arnauts* by Turks and Slavs and *Shkyipetar* by themselves, have historically been a dominant force in western Macedonia. They are divided into the Gheg stock in the north—who advanced through Usküb, Kalkandelen, and Dibra—and the Tosk race in the south, extending to the shores of Lakes Ochrida and Prespa and the upper Bistritza valley. The total Albanian population in Macedonia was estimated at 120,000, with about 10,000 being Christians.

The Greek and Vlach populations often overlap due to the hellenization of many Vlachs. Greeks historically occupied a privileged position in Macedonia due to the influence of the Phanariot families and the patriarchate of Constantinople. Greek was the language of religion, commerce, and the upper classes. This ascendancy declined following the War of Independence and the establishment of the Hellenic kingdom. 

Other groups include the Circassians, numbering fewer than 3,000 near Serres, and Jews. While early colonies existed in the time of St Paul, current Jewish populations descend from 15th-century Spanish refugees who write a Spanish dialect using Hebrew characters.

## Religion
Following the Turkish conquest, many inhabitants converted to Islam. More than half of the Albanian nation adopted the faith. The total Moslem population of the peninsula was approximately 3,300,000. 

The majority of the Christian population is Orthodox, nominally headed by the oecumenical patriarch at Constantinople, though the Greek, Servian, Montenegrin, and Bulgarian churches are effectively autocephalous. The Bulgarian exarch, based in Constantinople, controls Bulgarian prelates in both the kingdom of Bulgaria and European Turkey. Roman Catholicism is found among some Gheg tribes in Albania, Serbo-Croats in Croatia-Slavonia and Dalmatia, and small numbers of Bulgarians and Greeks. In Macedonia, some Bulgars at Kukush form a "uniate" church that accepts Roman dogma while preserving other traditions.

## Geography and Administration
The territory of Upper Albania is described as so impracticable and the means of its inhabitants so narrow that many resident tribes were considered free and paid no tribute to the Turkish government. In contrast, Middle Albania did not present such difficulties, and no such exemptions were granted.

In Macedonia, major urban centers included Salonica (130,000) and Monastir (60,000), both capitals of vilayets. Other significant towns included Serres, Vodena, Ochrida (which features the fortress of Tsar Samuel), and Dibra on the Albanian border.
