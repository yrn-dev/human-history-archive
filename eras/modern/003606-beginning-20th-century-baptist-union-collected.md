# The Baptist Union and the Baptist Movement

## The Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland
The Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland was formed in 1813 by the Particular Baptists. In 1891, largely due to the influence of Dr. John Clifford, a leading General Baptist, the General and Particular denominations united into this single body. The Union is purely voluntary and does not exert authoritative action over separate churches; consequently, some prosperous and powerful Baptist churches remain aloof from the organization.

One of the primary functions of the Union is the collection of information. As of 1909, the United Kingdom contained 3,046 Baptist churches, 4,124 chapels, 424,008 members, and 2,078 pastors in charge. Additionally, there were 5,615 local preachers, 58,687 Sunday school teachers, and 578,344 Sunday scholars, with a total of 1,450,352 sittings.

At the start of the 20th century, the Baptist Union collected a "Twentieth Century Fund" totaling £250,000. This fund was used largely to assist in the formation of new churches. The strength of the denomination was further evidenced by a 1904 Religious Census in London, which showed that Baptists ranked second only to Anglicans in three categories: the number of places of worship (443), the aggregate of attendances (54,597), and the percentage of attendances at public worship (10.81 in London County and 10.70 in Greater London).

## Church Government and Denominational Structure
Baptists maintain that each separate church is complete in itself, possessing the power to make its own regulations and choose its own ministers to advance the kingdom of Christ. While most churches are voluntarily united in associations based on geographical limits, these associations and independent churches are linked through the Baptist Union.

A small section of the denomination, known as Scotch Baptists—found primarily in the north of England and Scotland—believes a "plurality of elders" or pastors is necessary for a church's complete organization. The first Baptist church in Scotland appears to have been formed around 1750 in Keiss, Caithness, followed by a larger church in Edinburgh in 1765 which grew to approximately 500 members.

Outside the Baptist Union, "Strict" Baptist churches form a separate denomination. These are often organized into geographical associations, such as the Suffolk and Norfolk Association of Particular Baptist Churches or the Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches. The Strict Baptists are further divided into the "Vessel" and "Standard" parties, named after their respective organs, the *Earthen Vessel* and the *Gospel Standard*.

## Theological Origins and Evolution
The Baptist movement had a double origin, split between those holding Arminian views ("General Redemption") and those following Calvinistic theory ("Particular Redemption"), known as General Baptists and Particular Baptists.

Over time, many General Baptists adopted Socinian or Arian theories. In 1770, the orthodox General Baptists, influenced by Dan Taylor, formed the General Baptist New Connection. By the early 19th century, this group had 3,400 members and 40 churches, while the "Old Connection" merged into the Unitarian denomination. Meanwhile, the Particular Baptists saw a shift toward more moderate Calvinism toward the end of the 18th century, largely due to the writings of Andrew Fuller.

Historically, most Baptists required baptism for membership or communion. However, churches in Hertfordshire and Bedfordshire, influenced by Bunyan, argued that differences regarding water baptism should not bar communion. This practice of mixed communion spread, and some modern Baptist churches admit those professing faith in Christ to full membership regardless of their views on the ordinance of baptism.

## Global Expansion
### Europe and the British Colonies
The modern Baptist movement appeared across nearly every European country during the 19th century. In Roman Catholic nations, churches were established by English or American missionaries, starting in France (1832), Italy (1866 and 1884), and Spain (1888). In Protestant countries and Russia, the movement grew without such intervention. J. G. Oncken formed the first church in Hamburg in 1834, leading to the establishment of churches in Denmark (1839), Holland and Sweden (1848), Switzerland (1849), Norway (1860), Austria and Rumania (1869), Hungary (1871), and Bulgaria (1884). Churches also formed in Finland and Russia during the 1850s and 1860s.

In the British colonies, unions of Baptist churches exist in New Zealand, Tasmania, Canada, South Africa, and several Australian states (New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia, and South Australia). In Canada, Baptists arrived at the close of the 18th century from the United States, supplemented by Scottish, Welsh, and English migrants. By 1909, Canadian Baptists numbered 120,000.

### The United States
Baptist presence in the American colonies grew early on, with churches founded in Pennsylvania at Cold Spring (1684) and Pennepek (1688). The Philadelphia Association, formed in 1707, became highly influential in maintaining doctrine and promoting educational and missionary work. By 1812, Baptist membership in the U.S. had reached 173,972.

American Baptist missionary work was significantly impacted by the conversion of Luther Rice and Adoniram Judson in 1812. This led to the 1813 formation of "The Baptist Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in India and other Foreign Parts" in Boston. In 1814, the "Triennial Convention" was organized in Philadelphia to coordinate foreign missions. To address the need for educated ministers, Columbian College (now George Washington University) was established in 1821. Home mission work expanded from 1817, leading to the creation of the American Baptist Home Mission Society in 1832.
