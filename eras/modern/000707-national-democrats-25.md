# National Democrats

The term "National Democrats" appears in the archive's records in two distinct contexts: as a political designation for a specific group of Ruthenes and in relation to the organizational structure of the Democratic Party in the United States.

## Ruthene National Democrats
Within the records of political representation, the National Democrats are identified as a group among the Ruthenes. In a documented distribution of representatives, the National Democrats numbered 25. They are listed alongside other Ruthene groups, such as the Old or Russophil Ruthenes, who numbered 5.

## The Democratic National Committee and Conventions
In the United States, the term "National" is associated with the organizational apparatus of the Democratic Party. This includes the Democratic National Convention and the National Democratic Committee. 

August Belmont (1816–1890), an American banker and financier, played a significant role in these national structures. In 1860, Belmont served as a delegate to the Democratic National Convention held in Charleston, South Carolina, where he supported Stephen A. Douglas for the presidential nomination. Following a withdrawal to a subsequent convention in Baltimore, Maryland, Belmont was selected as the chairman of the National Democratic Committee. He maintained his leadership of the Democratic organization until 1872. Later, his son, August Belmont (born 1853), also served as the chairman of the finance committee of the Democratic National Committee, notably supporting Alton B. Parker for the Democratic presidential nomination in 1904.

## Evolution of the Democratic Party
The Democratic Party, the oldest existing political party in the United States, was originally known as the Democratic-Republican Party. It was organized by Thomas Jefferson in opposition to the Federalists. The party's early foundations were based on a repugnance to social and political aristocracy and a commitment to local self-government.

A significant shift in the party's composition occurred after 1801. Elements of the Federalist party that were "nationalistic"—meaning they favored a broad construction of constitutional powers to benefit manufactures and commerce—gradually moved toward the Republicans. These broad-constructionists eventually fused to form an independent party known as the National Republicans. Consequently, the Jacksonian Republicans became known simply as Democrats. 

This scission weakened the nationalistic tendencies of the Democratic Party. Following the leadership of Andrew Jackson, the party fell under the influence of Southern adherents and became virtually sectional, utilizing its states' rights doctrine to defend slavery.

## Party Principles and Development
The Democratic Party has historically been characterized by a devotion to liberty and abstract principles of democracy. In practical politics, it has often advocated for a "strict construction" of the constitution to prevent the national government from expanding its power at the expense of citizens or states.

Over time, the party has integrated various political movements:
* **Radical Democracy:** The victory of the Jacksonians in 1828 represented a major advance in radical democracy.
* **Populism:** Between 1896 and 1900, the party absorbed many members and principles of the "Populists," a radical third party.
* **Social Issues:** After 1896, the party showed a tendency to attract the "masses" and radical elements of society during a general realignment based on industrial-social issues.

Despite these shifts, the party has remained a champion for the most democratic elements of American democracy. Modern Democratic tenets include the popular election of United States senators.
