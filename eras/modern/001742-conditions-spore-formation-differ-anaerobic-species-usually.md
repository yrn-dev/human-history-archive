# Spore-Formation and Germination

Spore-formation is a characteristic method of reproduction where minute reproductive bodies serve as a resting stage for an organism. These spores are found in many forms and are highly resistant to cold, heat, and desiccation. Depending on the species, spores may be capable of immediate germination or may be preserved for years. When placed in a proper temperature and a suitable medium, germination can be completed within a few hours.

## The Process of Germination and Development
During germination, a spore swells and elongates. The contents grow outward to form a cell similar to the one that originally produced the spore. In some instances, the contents clearly break through the membrane, leaving remains attached to the young germinal rodlet; in other cases, the surrounding membrane of the spore dissolves as it swells. Once the germinal cell has grown forth, it takes on the forms typical of the specific Schizomycete involved.

## Conditions for Spore-Formation
The requirements for forming spores vary by species. Aerobic species require a free supply of oxygen, whereas anaerobic species usually require little oxygen. Additionally, many species require very specific food-media and have an optimum temperature for development.

Bacteriologists have been able to induce "asporogenous races"—where spore formation is indefinitely postponed—by systematically interfering with these conditions. This change can be achieved through the use of high temperatures or the addition of poisons and minute traces of acids. Such interference may also involve changes in virulence, vigour, and other properties.

## Types of Spores and Formation in Schizomycetes
In the study of Schizomycetes, a distinction is made between true spores (endospores) and arthrospores. An arthrospore is not a true spore, but rather an ordinary vegetative cell that separates and enters a state of rest. These can occur in species that do not form endospores, as well as in those that do, such as *B. subtilis*.

A true spore, or endospore, begins as a minute granule within the protoplasm of a vegetative cell. Over a few hours, this granule absorbs all the protoplasm and secretes a resistive, thin envelope. The resulting ripe ovoid spore is smaller than the mother-cell and lies loosely within it. Some forms, such as the simplest Schizomycetes like *Micrococcus*, have no discovered definite spores and are provisionally called "asporous," as any vegetative micrococcus may begin a new series of cells through division and growth.

## Spore-Formation in Fungi and Other Organisms
Different fungal groups exhibit diverse methods of spore production:

*   **Zygomycetes:** In this group, zygospores may be produced in different ways. Homothallic groups (such as *Spordinia grandis* and *Spinellus fusiger*) can produce zygospores from the union of branches from the same mycelium. Heterothallic groups require the association of branches from two mycelia that are different in nature, distinguished as (+) and (-) forms.
*   **Saccharomycetaceae (Yeasts):** These unicellular organisms can form endogenous spores. Under certain conditions, specifically involving temperature, the nucleus of a yeast-cell divides, and each daughter-nucleus divides again to form four spores within the mother cell. In *Schizosaccharomyces*, two cells may fuse to form a sporangium in which eight spores develop, though single cells may develop parthenogenetically to produce four spores. In *Zygosaccharomyces*, two cells also fuse to form a sporangium.
*   **Other Phycomycetes:** In some groups, protoplasm with numerous nuclei streams into the swollen end of a sporangiophore to form a sporangium. This protoplasm is then cut by clefts into smaller pieces—unicellular in *Pilobolus* and multicellular in *Sporodinia*—which are surrounded by a cell-wall to become spores.
