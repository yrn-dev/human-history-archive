# The Etymology and Early Use of "Bore"

## Definition and Physical Phenomena
The term "bore" refers to a high tidal wave that rushes up a tidal river or a narrow estuary. A notable example of this phenomenon is the bore of the Severn, which is created by a tide that rises 18 feet within a period of an hour and a half. As the body of water enters a narrowing, funnel-shaped estuary, it becomes compressed and heaps up into an advancing wave that extends from one bank to the other. This phenomenon is also well illustrated in the Bay of Fundy.

## Etymological Origins
The origin of the word "bore" in the context of tidal waves is considered doubtful. However, it is usually attributed to the Scandinavian word *bara*, meaning a billow or a wave. The phenomenon is also known by the name "eagre," though the origin of that term is unknown.

The archive clarifies that there is no connection between this tidal phenomenon and the word "bore" meaning to drill or make a hole by piercing. The latter is a common Teutonic word, related to the German *bohren* and the Latin *forare* (to pierce) and Greek *pharos* (plough). In a substantival sense, this version of "bore" is typically limited to the circular cavity of tubular objects, such as the internal diameter or "calibre" of a gun.

## Evolution of the Social Meaning
In modern usage, a "bore" describes a tiresome or wearying person, specifically one who persistently harps on a single subject regardless of the interest of their audience. While this is generally interpreted as a metaphorical extension of the word meaning "to pierce," the archive notes a different earliest sense of the word in the English language.

In 1766, as seen in certain letters printed in Jesse's *Life of George Selwyn*, the term was used to mean *ennui*. This specific usage suggests a French origin. The *New English Dictionary* conjectures that the source may be the French word *bourrer*, which means to satiate or stuff.
