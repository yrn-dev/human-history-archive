# The Court of Arbitration in the Austro-Hungarian Customs and Commercial Treaty

## Overview of the 1907 Agreement
In October 1907, Austria and Hungary reached an agreement on a new "customs and commercial treaty." This settlement followed a period of significant tension and economic dispute. The agreement was facilitated by a severe economic crisis that influenced Hungarian opinion, highlighting that separation from Austria could lead to commercial ruin or a period of distress for Hungary. Simultaneously, Austria recognized that separation would deprive its manufacturers of their primary market and increase the cost of living in Cisleithania.

## The Court of Arbitration
One of the central features of the 1907 treaty was the establishment of a court of arbitration designed to settle differences between the two states. The composition of this court was structured to ensure mutual representation:
*   Hungary was responsible for selecting four Austrian judges.
*   Austria was responsible for selecting four Hungarian judges.

The presidency of the court was to be decided by lot. Furthermore, each government was to be represented before the court by its own delegates.

## Other Provisions of the Treaty
The court of arbitration was part of a broader set of commercial and economic arrangements. The main features of the treaty included:
*   **Customs and Trade:** Both states were to maintain separate but identical customs tariffs. Additionally, impediments to the free trade of sugar were to be practically abolished.
*   **Railway Communication:** Hungary agreed to facilitate direct railway communication between Vienna and Dalmatia by the end of 1911, with each state building the sections within its own territory. Austria agreed to facilitate railway communication between Prussia and Hungary.
*   **Financial and Legal Reforms:** Hungary was required to reform its Stock Exchange and produce laws to prevent speculation in agrarian produce. Regarding the old Austrian debt, Hungary was entitled to redeem its share at a rate of 4.325% within ten years; if not redeemed in that timeframe, the capitalization rate would decrease annually by 1/12% until reaching 4.2%. This provided Hungary with a potential economy of some £2,000,000 capital compared to the original Austrian demand of 4.2%.
*   **Investments:** The securities of both governments were designated as investments for insurance companies, savings banks, and similar institutions in both countries, though they were not permitted as trust fund investments.

## Context of the Dual Monarchy
The 1907 treaty operated within the framework of the Austrian-Hungarian Monarchy (also known as the Dual Monarchy), which consisted of two independent states: the "lands of St Stephen's Crown" (Hungary or Transleithania) and the kingdoms and lands represented in the council of the empire (Austria or Cisleithania). 

Since 1868, the ruler was styled as the "Emperor of Austria, King of Bohemia, &c., and Apostolic King of Hungary." This dualist structure extended to the public service; common ministries used the terminology "Imperial and Royal" (_Kaiserlich und Koeniglich_), while the Austrian half of the monarchy retained the style _Kaiserlich-Koeniglich_ ("K.K."). This separation was evident in the financial administration, which featured three distinct ministries of finance: the _Kiralye_ for Hungary, the _Kaiserlich-Koeniglich_ for Austrian affairs, and the _Kaiserlich und Koeniglich_ for joint affairs.

## Duration and Legacy
The economic dispute was settled for a period of ten years following negotiations that had lasted more than twelve years. While the 1907 treaty provided a decade of economic unity, some issues remained, such as the future of the joint State Bank. Because the Austrian government was unable to overcome Hungarian resistance to a longer term, the Austro-Hungarian customs and commercial treaty, along with commercial treaties with other countries such as Italy and Germany, were set to lapse at the end of 1917.
