# Germany, France, and Switzerland

## Geographic and Administrative Relations
The territory of Belfort is an administrative division of eastern France, created from the southern part of the Haut-Rhin department. The remainder of Haut-Rhin was ceded to Germany via the treaty of Frankfort in 1871. Belfort is bordered by German Alsace to the east and northeast, and by Switzerland to the south and southeast. To its west and southwest lie the French departments of Haute-Saone and Doubs, while the department of Vosges borders it to the north. As of 1906, the population of Belfort was 95,421.

## Cartography and Surveys
Various geographical establishments and individuals have contributed to the mapping of these regions. In Germany, J. B. Homann founded a geographical establishment in 1702. While initially dependent on French and British copies, it eventually produced original works, including J. T. Güssfeld's map of Brandenburg (1773), Tobias Meyer's *Mappa critica* of Germany (1780), and John Majer's Württemburg (1710). Additionally, J. C. Müller produced maps of Bavaria based on trigonometrical surveys. Colonel Schmettau conducted a survey of the country west of the Weser between 1767 and 1787, though Frederick the Great prevented its publication to avoid aiding military enemies.

Switzerland's representation in the archive includes works by J. J. Scheuchzer (1712), J. Gessner (who died in 1790), G. Walser, who produced the *Atlas novus Helvetiae* (1769), and W. R. Meyer, who authored the *Atlas der Schweiz* (1786-1802). In France, the *Carte de la France* (1777) by Cassini served as a scale reference for Count Joseph de Ferrari's chorographic map of the Austrian Netherlands.

## Political and Diplomatic History
France and Germany have been involved in various conflicts and diplomatic arrangements. During the Seven Years' War (1756-63), France was allied with Russia and Austria against Britain and Prussia. In the late 18th century, the French diplomat Marc Marie, Marquis de Bombelles, served as the ambassador of France to Portugal in 1886 before being transferred to Vienna in 1789. Following the French Revolution, Bombelles emigrated in 1792 and lived in retirement in Switzerland.

In the 19th century, German diplomatic figures included Otto v. Bülow, who was appointed as the German envoy at Bern in 1882. Other notable German figures include Prince Bernhard Heinrich Karl Martin von Bülow, a statesman born in 1849 in Holstein, and his great-uncle Heinrich von Bülow, who served as the Prussian ambassador to England from 1827 to 1840.

Following a period of war, the Rhine was placed under a Central Commission meeting at Strassbourg. This commission was composed of four representatives from France (who also selected the President), four from Germany, and two each from Switzerland, the Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, and Great Britain. Under these terms, Germany was required to provide France with all rights to take water for canals on the course of the Rhine between their frontiers and was prohibited from creating canals on the right bank opposite France.

## Notable Residents and Figures
Several intellectuals and political figures spent significant time across these three nations. The Danish poet Jens Immanuel Baggesen (1764-1826) left Denmark in 1789 and spent several years in France, Germany, and Switzerland. He married in Berne in 1790 and began writing in German, publishing the poem *Alpenlied* in that language.

Mikhail Bakunin (1814-1876), a Russian anarchist, traveled to Germany to study Hegel and connect with the young German movement in Berlin. He then moved to Paris, where he met George Sand and Proudhon. Bakunin subsequently resided in Switzerland, participating in socialistic movements. After being expelled from France in 1848 and later imprisoned in Siberia, he spent the final years of his exile in western Europe, primarily in Switzerland. He retired to Lugano in 1873 and died in Bern on June 13, 1876.
