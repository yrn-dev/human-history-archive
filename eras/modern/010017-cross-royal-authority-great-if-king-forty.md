# Royal Authority and the Minority of Edward VI

The question of whether royal authority remains absolute during the minority of a monarch was a central point of legal and political conflict during the reign of Edward VI. This dispute primarily involved the government of the young king and high-ranking ecclesiastical figures, most notably Bishop Bonner and Gardiner.

## The Theory of Royal Supremacy in Abeyance

Following the accession of Edward VI, Bishop Bonner and Gardiner found themselves in a position where they could not easily repudiate the royal supremacy, as they had previously been active agents in its establishment. However, as they observed the ways in which a Protestant council utilized this supremacy, they developed a theory to limit its application. They proposed that the royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority.

This theoretical position was not considered legally or constitutionally tenable. The contradiction in their stance was evident in the fact that both Bonner and Gardiner had sought fresh licenses from the young king to exercise their ecclesiastical jurisdiction. The logic held that if the king possessed the authority to confer such jurisdiction, he likewise possessed the authority to issue injunctions and order visitations. Furthermore, it was argued that if a royal minority caused an abeyance of supremacy in ecclesiastical matters, the same would have to apply to the temporal sphere, which would result in anarchy.

## Conflict and the Requirement of St Paul's Cross

The tension over this issue led to a direct conflict between Bishop Bonner and Edward's government. In August 1547, Bonner resisted a visitation and was subsequently committed to the Fleet. Although he eventually withdrew his opposition and was released, he remained a prominent opponent of the government. During the parliamentary session from November 1548 to March 1549, Bonner emerged as a leading opponent of the Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity.

Once these measures became law, Bonner neglected to enforce them. Consequently, on September 1, 1549, the council required Bonner to maintain a specific position at St Paul's Cross: he was to declare that the royal authority was as great as if the king were forty years of age.

## Deprivation and Imprisonment

Bishop Bonner failed to comply with the council's requirement to maintain the status of the royal authority at St Paul's Cross. This failure led to a seven-day trial presided over by Cranmer in an ecclesiastical court. As a result of the trial, Bonner was deprived of his bishopric and sent to the Marshalsea.

Following the fall of Somerset the next month, Bonner appealed his deprivation to the council. However, the Protestant faction eventually prevailed. On February 7, 1550, the council, sitting in the Star Chamber, confirmed Bonner's deprivation and condemned him to perpetual imprisonment.
