# Spain During the Voyage of Commodore Anson

During the period when Commodore George Anson was conducting his voyage round the world, the Spanish state was primarily focused on the Italian policy of its king. This strategic priority led to the fitting out of a squadron at Cadiz intended to transport troops to Italy.

## Naval Conflicts and the Italian Policy
The Spanish efforts in Italy were closely monitored by the British admiral Nicholas Haddock. Due to a lack of provisions, the blockading squadron was eventually forced off, allowing the Spanish admiral Don Jose Navarro to put to sea. In December 1741, Navarro was joined by a French squadron commanded by M. de Court. The French admiral declared his support for the Spaniards in the event of an attack, which prompted Haddock to retire.

Navarro and M. de Court subsequently proceeded to Toulon, where they remained until February 1744. During this time, they were watched by a British fleet first under admiral Richard Lestock and later under Sir Thomas Mathews, who served as commander-in-chief and minister to the court of Turin.

## The Anglo-Spanish War
Great Britain had declared war on Spain on October 23, 1739. At the onset of the conflict, there was a widespread belief that Spanish colonies would fall quickly under attack. British strategy involved combined operations from both the east and west. Admiral Edward Vernon was tasked with assaulting the colonies from the West Indies, while Commodore George Anson was ordered to round Cape Horn to attack the Pacific coast.

The British efforts in the West Indies saw mixed results. On November 21, 1739, Admiral Vernon captured the Spanish harbour of Porto Bello, though this was considered a trifling success as it did not stop Spanish convoys from reaching Europe. Furthermore, Spanish privateers operated with destructive effect against British trade in European waters and the West Indies. A subsequent attack on Cartagena between March 9 and April 24, 1741, failed with a heavy loss of life for the assailants, as the Spanish admiral Don Bias de Leso had sufficient time to prepare the defenses. Following two other unsuccessful attacks on Spanish territory, the war in the West Indies diminished until 1748.

## The Expedition of Commodore Anson
Commodore Anson's expedition departed England on September 18, 1740, with six ships. The voyage was characterized by poor preparations, late sailing, and insufficient provisions. Despite these setbacks, Anson's cruise is regarded as a great feat of endurance and resolution. He harried the coasts of Peru and Chile and captured a Spanish galleon of immense value near the Philippines. 

Anson returned to England on June 15, 1744, as the sole survivor of the original fleet, arriving in his flagship, the "Centurion." The other vessels were either lost or failed to round Cape Horn. During his travels, Anson also conducted research in the archipelago of the Ladrones (later named Las Marianas in 1668), landing on the island of Tinian in August 1742.

## Broader European Context
The geopolitical situation during this era involved complex alliances. While France and Great Britain were not yet openly at war during the early stages of Anson's voyage, both were involved in a struggle in Germany. Great Britain was allied with Maria Theresa, the queen of Hungary, while France supported the Bavarian claimant of the empire. 

Avowed war between France and Great Britain did not begin until the French government issued a declaration on March 30, followed by a British reply on March 31. This period of hostility included French preparations to invade England and a confused naval battle on February 11, involving the British fleet under Sir Thomas Mathews and the allied forces.
