# Louis Philippe and the Napoleonic Successors

The political landscape of 19th-century France was marked by a succession of regimes, transitioning from the first Napoleonic Empire through the monarchy of Louis Philippe to the reign of Napoleon III. These periods were characterized by shifting alliances, revolutionary disturbances, and the influence of the Bonaparte and Orléans families across Europe.

## The Reign of Louis Philippe

Louis Philippe ascended to power in 1830 after ousting Charles X during a year of significant disturbance. His government initially included figures such as Bignon, who served briefly as minister of public instruction and minister of foreign affairs. However, Bignon's outspoken writings had offended several powers, making it impossible for Louis Philippe to make him responsible for French foreign policy, as the King needed to court the goodwill of those nations.

Queen Victoria later described Louis Philippe as possessing "great activity of mind" and "vast knowledge upon all and every subject." She characterized him as "thoroughly French," noting his liveliness and talkativeness. However, she also attributed to him a tendency toward "tricks and over-reachings," noting that he enjoyed being more cunning than others even when there was no advantage to be gained. These traits were particularly evident in the transactions regarding the Spanish marriages, which ruined his reputation in Europe and led to his downfall.

During his reign, Louis Philippe faced internal opposition from the Radical Left. Hippolyte Carnot, elected deputy for Paris in 1839 following a dissolution of the chamber by the King, became one of the leaders of the party hostile to Louis Philippe. By February 24, 1848, the political climate shifted further as figures like Carnot pronounced in favor of the republic.

## Relations with Belgium

Louis Philippe maintained a complex relationship with the newly independent kingdom of Belgium, which broke away from Holland in 1830. The Belgian congress initially sought to offer the crown to the Duke of Nemours, the younger son of Louis Philippe. This proposal was rejected by England, which threatened war if the offer were accepted, fearing Belgium would fall under direct French influence.

Consequently, the Belgian congress elected Prince Leopold of Saxe-Cobourg-Gotha on June 4, 1831. To quiet the jealousy of Louis Philippe regarding this election, Leopold married the French King's daughter, Louise-Marie. This union linked the two houses, and Louise-Marie became the first Queen of the Belgians, bearing three children: Leopold (the future King Leopold II), Philippe (the Count of Flanders), and Charlotte.

## The Napoleonic Legacy and Louis Bonaparte

The influence of the first Napoleon extended beyond France through the creation of a ring of allied states. Following the victory at Austerlitz on December 2, 1805, Napoleon proclaimed his brother, Louis, as the King of Holland on June 6, 1806. The relationship between the brothers was strained; Napoleon reproached Louis for courting popularity too much and being too lenient with his subjects.

Their rupture was further fueled by the continental system and Louis's jealousy regarding his wife. After Louis refused the throne of Spain in 1808, tensions escalated. In January 1810, Napoleon annexed the island of Walcheren, claiming Louis failed to defend French interests during the British Walcheren expedition of 1809. By July 9, 1810, Napoleon fully annexed Holland to the French empire after Louis fled his kingdom. Louis spent his remaining years primarily in Rome, focusing on philosophy and literary studies.

## Napoleon III and the Second Empire

The Napoleonic line returned to power with Napoleon III, who became Emperor of the French in 1852. His reign was marked by significant international involvement and domestic consolidation. He carried out a *coup d'etat* on December 2, 1851; while some, such as Hippolyte Carnot, protested the action, Carnot was not proscribed by Louis Napoleon.

Napoleon III's influence extended to Mexico through his patronage of Archduke Maximilian of Austria (the husband of Empress Charlotte of Belgium). Maximilian became Emperor of Mexico in 1864, supported by 2,000 Belgian volunteers. However, Napoleon III abandoned Maximilian when political troubles arose in Mexico. Maximilian was eventually captured and shot at Queretaro in 1867 by order of Juarez, an event that led to the insanity of Empress Charlotte.

The empire of Napoleon III ended following his declaration of war against Prussia in 1870. This conflict resulted in the surrender of Paris in 1871 and the subsequent Peace of Frankfort, coinciding with the King of Prussia becoming the "German Emperor."
