# Architectural Practice and Notable Architects

## The Process of Architectural Planning
In the general practice of developing land, the process begins when a land owner or freeholder consults an architect. The client provides the architect with their intentions for the building, including the required size, the intended use, and—in the case of trade—the number of employees to be hired and the necessary departments and sub-buildings. The architect uses this information to create initial rough sketches or outlines.

Once the client approves the planning and the situation of the rooms, the architect develops these sketches into formal plans, sections, and elevations. During this stage, the architect must ensure the building complies with building acts, specifically regarding frontage lines, distances from surrounding property, open spaces, light, air, and the thickness of walls. In cities such as New York and London, these building acts are extensive and subject to numerous amendments. Because the architect must consult with local authorities, such as the London County Council or a district surveyor, plans are often altered, which can lead to delays if the client disapproves of the changes.

## Legal and Technical Requirements
After obtaining approval from the client and building authorities, the architect may face disputes with adjoining owners regarding party walls, air space, or "ancient lights." In the city of London, these conflicts can cause significant financial loss to the client and long suspensions of work. If the soil is uncertain or the site is large, trial holes should be sunk immediately following the approval of sketch plans.

For leasehold properties, the architect must negotiate approval from freeholders and senior lessors before serving required notices regarding party walls. If existing buildings are on the site, they must be carefully surveyed to create accurate detail plans, particularly concerning the rights and easements of adjoining owners. Site plans must also indicate the various levels of the ground.

Once drawings are approved by the client, adjoining owners, and local authorities, copies are distributed. In London, one linen copy is typically deposited with the London County Council or the district surveyor, one is provided to the builder, and another is prepared for the freeholder if a lease is granted. In complex cases involving multiple land owners, the architect may need to prepare many complete copies for all interested parties.

## Construction Management and Contracts
Before construction begins, the builder must sign a contract, usually prepared by the architect. This document specifies the sum of money for the work, the description of workmanship and materials, and the timeline for completion, including penalties for non-completion (though allowances are made for strikes, fire, or bad weather). The contract also includes clauses to indemnify the employer against risks and accidents involving employees.

While the architect focuses on design and planning, the builder's role is to execute those designs. The builder must possess predominant constructive knowledge, including a thorough understanding of materials, their brands, and the laws regarding the pulling down of old buildings, shoring, gantries, hoardings, and scaffoldings.

On important or large works, a clerk of works may be appointed as a delegate of the architect. Unlike an architectural draughtsman, the clerk of works is a practical man who remains on-site to ensure the use of the best construction and materials. He reports difficulties to the architect for instruction. The client pays the clerk of works' salary separately from the architect's remuneration.

## Notable Architects
### European Architects
Giacomo Barocchio (also known as Barozzi or da Vignola, 1507–1573) was an Italian architect who served as the papal architect under Pope Julius III and later succeeded Michelangelo as the architect of St Peter's. He designed the Escorial and wrote works on *Practical Perspective* (1583) and the *Five Orders of Architecture* (1563). Although he built the Jesuit church in Rome, the term "baroque"—derived from the Spanish word for an imperfectly round pearl (*barrueco* or *berrueco*)—is not derived from his name, though it was used to describe the debased architecture encouraged by the Jesuits.

Sir Charles Barry (1795–1860) was an English architect known for his preference for the Italian style, seen in the Reform Club (1837) and the Travellers' Club (1831). His other works include the church of St Peter at Brighton, Bridgewater House, and the town-hall in Halifax. His masterpiece is the Houses of Parliament at Westminster (1840–1860). He was also a brilliant landscape gardener and established schools for metal-working, cabinet-making, and carving.

George Dance the elder (1700–1768) served as the architect to the city of London. His designs include the Mansion House (1739), the old excise office on Broad Street, and the churches of St Leonard, Shoreditch; St Luke's, Old Street; and St Botolph, Aldgate (1741).

### American Architects
Charles Bulfinch (1763–1844) was the first professional architect to practice in Boston. His early works include the "new" State House (1798) and the old Federal Street theatre (1793). Bulfinch also served as chairman of the board of selectmen for twenty-one years, influencing Boston's modernization through street-lighting, drainage, and the reorganization of fire and police departments. In 1818, he became the architect of the National Capitol in Washington, where he designed the new portico and western approach and constructed the rotunda.
