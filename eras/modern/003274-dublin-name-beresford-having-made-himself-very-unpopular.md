# Beresford and Banking in Dublin

## Public Unpopularity and the Beresford Incident
In the history of Dublin, a banker by the name of Beresford became significantly unpopular with the common people. This hostility manifested in a public demonstration where a large group of "ignorant country people" gathered a quantity of Beresford's bank notes and burned them publicly. As the promises to pay on demand were consumed by the fire, the crowd shouted with enthusiasm, asking, "What will he do now; his bank will surely break."

## Early Irish Banking Failures
The incident involving Beresford occurred within a broader context of extreme instability in the Irish banking sector during earlier times. Sir Robert Peel, in a speech delivered on June 9, 1845, regarding the Bank Act of that year, cited a report from a committee of Irish exchanges that had sat in 1804. According to that report, there were fifty registered banks at the time, all of which failed. These failures resulted in "the most fearful distress."

## Development and Stabilization of the System
The early development of banking in Ireland mirrored the process seen in England, where circulation preceded and fed deposits. Banks gained credit through the ready acceptance of their notes, which in turn attracted customers to their counters. This system was built gradually and surely, aided by excellent managers.

Following the legislation of 1845, the banking business in Ireland transitioned to a state of extraordinary success and steadiness. By the time of the archive's recording, the footing of Irish banking was considered fully equal to that of any other part of the United Kingdom.
