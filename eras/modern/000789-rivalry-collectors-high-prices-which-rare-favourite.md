# The Rivalry of Collectors and the Valuation of Rare Items

The pursuit of rare objects, particularly books and autographs, is driven by a combination of intellectual interest, aesthetic appeal, and the competitive nature of collecting. This rivalry often leads to high market prices for favorite or rare items, which in turn creates an environment conducive to the production of forgeries.

## The Market for Autographs and Forgeries
The high prices realized by rare or favorite autographs, fueled by the rivalry between collectors, have encouraged the activity of forgers. This is evident in the periodic appearance of false letters attributed to popular authors and heroes, such as Thackeray, Burns, and Nelson. While some of these are clever imitations, many are too obviously spurious to deceive experienced collectors. 

Some forgeries are characterized as literary inventions, such as those produced by Chatterton and Ireland, though these are described as poor performances. In the middle of the 19th century, one of the most sophisticated frauds involved the fabrication of a series of letters from Shelley and Byron. These forgeries included complete seals and postmarks and were even published as *bona fide* documents.

## Factors Influencing the Value of Books
Book-collecting is described as the gathering of books that possess an element of permanent interest in their form, contents, or the history of the individual copy, and which are rare or prospectively rare. While rarity is a significant factor in the popular view, it is subordinate to interest; a book that is rare but devoid of interest is of no concern to the collector.

Several factors contribute to the high value and imaginative appeal of specific books:
*   **Associations:** Books associated with famous events or persons—such as poets, soldiers, great statesmen, or royalty (notably Mary queen of Scots)—possess an interest that defies analysis. This includes books bearing marks of being valued by the owner or connected to a striking incident in their life.
*   **Pedigree:** Collectors often value a work more highly if its pedigree can be traced through a long succession of book-loving owners.
*   **Physical Attributes:** A book may appeal to the eye through its illustrations, the harmony of its print and paper, or its binding. In France, high prices for French first editions and Elzevirs are often due more to their 17th and 18th-century jackets than the text itself. In England, there has been an increased appreciation for embroidered bindings, blind stamped bindings, and those attributable to Samuel Mearne.
*   **Editions:** First editions are prized for their associations and their positive superiority over reprints for study. While the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces has increased—exemplified by the sale of a first folio Shakespeare (1623) in 1906—other first editions, such as the Greek and Latin classics prized during the reign of George III, are now viewed by some as mere specimens of printing since they lack textual value or associations with their authors.

## Evolution of Collecting Practices
Modern book-collecting is a relatively recent growth. Early book owners, such as Jean Grolier (1479-1565), bought books to encourage printers or provide friends with Renaissance scholarship rather than out of antiquarian instincts. Similarly, figures like Archbishop Cranmer and the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou collected the best books of their day and enriched them with heraldic badges and leather bindings.

In the era of George III, collectors like James West and John Ratcliffe focused on the products of early English presses, specifically Caxtons. However, as the cost of books grew and house-room became limited, the "cabinet theory" of collecting became popular in the United States, England, and France. This approach, exemplified by Henry Perkins (whose collection of 865 manuscripts and early printings realized nearly £26,000 in 1870), favors small, compact collections that epitomize the owner's tastes over the miscellaneous, large-scale libraries of the past.

## Other Collectibles and Market Fluctuations
Beyond books and autographs, other materials are subject to wide price ranges based on quality and fashion. Coral, for example, varies greatly in price according to size and color. The finest rose pink coral can range from £80 to £120 per ounce, while ordinary red pieces sell for about £2 per ounce, and small fragments called *collette* cost about 5s. per ounce. In China, high prices are commanded by large spheres of good colored coral used for the buttons of office worn by mandarins.
