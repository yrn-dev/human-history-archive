# The Bourbon Dynasty in Southern Italy and the Napoleonic Intervention

## Expulsion of the Bourbons from the Mainland
In early 1806, Napoleon directed a French force to Naples for the purpose of expelling the Bourbon dynasty from southern Italy. As part of this operation, Napoleon offered a promise to his brother, Joseph, that the Neapolitan crown would be his if he chose to accept it. The conquest of the Italian mainland was achieved quickly, although certain strongholds—specifically Reggio, Gaeta, and the rock of Scylla—continued to hold out for several months.

Following the loss of the mainland, the Bourbon court retired to Sicily. There, they remained under the protection of a British force.

## The Reign of Joseph Bonaparte
By a decree issued on March 30, 1806, Napoleon proclaimed Joseph king of Naples, while allowing Joseph to maintain his claims to the French throne. Napoleon advised his brother to maintain firmness in his administration, asserting that nations and peoples in Italy were disposed to mutiny and rebellion if they did not find "their masters."

Joseph's administration faced significant obstacles, as detailed in the memoirs of his minister of war, Count Miot de Melito. These difficulties included:
* A treasury that was almost bankrupt.
* Frequent attacks launched from Sicily by the British.
* Plots and intrigues orchestrated by the Bourbons.
* A populace described as degraded and fickle.

The stability of Joseph's throne was severely shaken on July 3 by General Stuart's victory at Maida. However, the subsequent surrender of Gaeta allowed Massena to march south and subdue Calabria.

Despite these challenges, Joseph implemented various improvements during his brief reign. He reorganized the educational, financial, and judicial systems, initiated public works, reformed monastic orders, and abolished the remnants of feudalism. Joseph expressed a desire to make the people happy, provided the "scourge of war" permitted it.

## Brigandage and Social Order
Prior to the French invasion, the Bourbon dynasty had succeeded in securing order on the main high-roads and significantly reducing brigandage. However, the region had been plagued by chronic brigandage due to the helplessness of the peasantry, the survival of feudal power among nobles who sheltered banditti, and a multitude of competing jurisdictions and codes.

Brigandage revived during the French invasion, a period that saw the rise of the patriot and brigand Fra Diavolo, who was eventually captured and executed by the French. Following the fall of Napoleon and the restoration of Ferdinand, the English officer General Sir Richard Church was employed to suppress the brigands. Church restored a degree of security and brought to justice figures such as Don Ciro Anicchiarico, a priest and brigand who admitted to murdering approximately seventy people.

## The Bourbon Restoration and the Hundred Days
The broader struggle between the Bourbons and Napoleon continued in France. After eleven months at Elba, Napoleon returned to France, evading British ships and landing at Cannes. This period, known as "the Hundred Days," saw Napoleon regain mastery of France, walking on white Bourbon cockades during his triumphal procession to Paris. 

During this time, the allies maintained that the only alternatives for France were Napoleon or the Bourbons, as they would not permit further experiments in republicanism. Napoleon returned professing to be a changed man who would respect the constitution régime and end despotism.
