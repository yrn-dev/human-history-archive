# Northern and Southern Baptists

The history of the Baptist denomination in the United States is characterized by a period of national organization followed by a sectional division over the issue of slavery, and a subsequent effort to maintain spiritual fellowship despite administrative separation.

## The Triennial Convention and Early Missions
At the beginning of the 19th century, Baptists in several communities, including Philadelphia, Charleston, and Boston and its vicinity, showed a strong interest in the work of the English missionary William Carey in India. A significant turning point occurred in 1812 with the conversion of Luther Rice and Adoniram Judson to Baptist views. Judson called for support for missionary work among the heathen, and Rice returned to the United States to organize societies to generate interest in this mission.

In January 1813, "The Baptist Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in India and other Foreign Parts" was formed in Boston, with similar societies quickly appearing in the Southern, Middle, and Eastern states. To create a national organization, thirty-three delegates from eleven states met in Philadelphia in May 1814 to establish the "General Missionary Convention of the Baptist Denomination in the United States of America for Foreign Missions." Because the convention met every three years, it became known as the Triennial Convention.

The Triennial Convention expanded its scope over time. In 1817, it began extensive home mission work on the frontiers, leading to the 1832 constitution of the American Baptist Home Mission Society. To address the need for educated ministers, the convention established the theological department of Columbian College in Washington in 1821, which was intended to serve as a national Baptist institution.

## Sectional Division and the Southern Baptist Convention
Before 1844, sessions of the Triennial Convention were occasionally marred by acrimonious exchanges and harsh anti-slavery statements made by Northern members against their Southern brethren. A controversy regarding slavery between Richard Fuller (1804-1876) and Francis Wayland eventually convinced the Southern members that a separate organization for missionary work was necessary.

Consequently, the Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845. This new organization established its own Foreign and Home Missionary Boards, and later added a Sunday-school Board. Following this split, Northern and Southern Baptists continued to maintain "perfect fellowship" with one another, but they determined that it was best to hold separate annual meetings and conduct their foreign and home missionary work through their respective separate boards.

## Growth and Institutional Development
Since the division in 1845, both Northern and Southern Baptists experienced significant growth in numbers, educational institutions, and literary activity. Between 1812 and 1906, the total number of Baptists increased from fewer than 200,000 to more than 5,000,000.

The institutional infrastructure of the denomination expanded rapidly:
* **Education:** While there were no theological seminaries in 1812, by 1906 there were 11, featuring over 100 instructors and 1,300 students, with endowments and equipment valued at approximately $7,000,000. In 1812, there was only one degree-conferring college; by 1906, there were more than 100 colleges and universities with an annual income of about $3,000,000 and assets valued at $30,000,000.
* **Property and Literature:** Church property, which had a small value in 1812, was estimated at $100,000,000 by 1906. Periodical literature grew from a single monthly magazine with a circulation of a few hundred to hundreds of weeklies, monthlies, and quarterlies. The American Baptist Publication Society became the denomination's single publishing concern, with assets of $1,750,000 and an annual business of nearly $1,000,000.

## Fellowship and Global Alliances
Efforts to promote denominational unity and cooperation continued into the 20th century. In 1905, a General Baptist Convention for America was established to promote comity, fellowship, and *esprit de corps*. This organization was designed not to undertake administrative work or interfere with the existing sectional organizations.

That same year, the Baptist World Alliance was formed during the first Baptist World Congress in London. The alliance's constitution stated its purpose was to manifest the "essential oneness in the Lord Jesus Christ" among Baptist churches worldwide and to promote cooperation and service while respecting the independence of each particular church. This alliance extended to every part of the globe.

By 1909, the total number of Baptist churches reached approximately 72,988, with 7,480,940 members. Within the United States, the Southern Baptist Convention accounted for 20,431 churches and 1,832,638 members, while the National Baptist Convention included 16,996 churches and 2,110,269 members.
