# Bouillon

The term Bouillon appears in the archive's records in two primary contexts: as a geographic and political entity associated with the First Crusade and as a reference to the cardinal de Bouillon, a patron of genealogical works in the early 18th century.

## Godfrey of Bouillon and the First Crusade
Godfrey of Bouillon, hailing from Belgium, was a leader during the First Crusade. Following the capture of Antioch by Crusader forces, a smaller contingent under Godfrey's command proceeded toward Jerusalem. After a siege lasting slightly more than a month, the city was captured on July 15. This victory was marked by terrible slaughter, with the blood of the conquered filling the streets. Upon the city's fall, the crusaders prayed at the Sepulchre. Godfrey of Bouillon is identified as the first king of Jerusalem.

Regarding his holdings, records indicate that upon his departure, Godfrey sold or pawned Bouillon to the church for a sum of 1,300 marks.

## Cardinal de Bouillon and Genealogical Forgeries
In the late 17th and early 18th centuries, the cardinal de Bouillon was involved in a controversy regarding the fabrication of authentic documents to support certain pretensions. This effort began with the production of spurious genealogies in the *Histoire de la maison d'Auvergne*, published by Christophe Justel in 1645. Later, in 1672, the historian of Dauphiny, Chorier, included a forged deed in the second volume of his history to connect the La Tours of Auvergne with the La Tours of Dauphiny.

A "regular manufactory" of forged documents was subsequently organized by Jean de Bar, an intimate companion of the cardinal. These forgeries were sophisticated enough to deceive prominent scholars, including Baluze, Dom Thierry Ruinart, and Dom Jean Mabillon (the founder of Diplomatics), who all provided a unanimously favorable report on July 23, 1695.

## The Role of Baluze
The cardinal de Bouillon's enemies initiated a war of pamphlets, and by 1700, Jean de Bar and his accomplices were arrested. Following a searching inquiry, they were declared guilty in 1704. Despite this, Baluze remained convinced that the documents were genuine. 

Encouraged and receiving financial support from the cardinal de Bouillon, Baluze attempted to redo the work of Justel. He produced two specific works:
*   A *Table genealogique* in 1705.
*   A *Histoire genealogique de la maison d'Auvergne* in 1709.

The latter work included "Proofs" that contained the deeds previously pronounced spurious. In 1710, Baluze fell into disgrace along with his patron, the cardinal, resulting in the loss of his pensions, appointments, and benefices, and his subsequent exile from Paris. Baluze continued his scholarly work in exile, publishing *Historiae Tutelensis libri tres* in 1717, and died on July 28, 1718, remaining unconvinced of his errors. Correspondence suggests Baluze may have been a victim of clever cheats rather than an accomplice.
