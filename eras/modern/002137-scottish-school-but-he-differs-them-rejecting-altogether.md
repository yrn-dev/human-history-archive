# Bailey and the Scottish School of Psychology

## Relationship to the Scottish School
In the study of psychology and ethics, Bailey occupies a position that is difficult to categorize strictly as either idealist or empirical, though his general inclination leans toward the empirical school. His approach to psychological method is founded entirely upon introspection. Because of this reliance on introspection, Bailey agrees to a certain extent with the Scottish school. However, he differs from the Scottish school in his complete rejection of the doctrine of mental faculties. From Bailey's perspective, what others have designated as "faculties" are actually just classified phenomena of consciousness or facts. Additionally, he is a severe critic of the habitual use of metaphorical language when describing mental operations.

## Doctrine of Perception
Bailey's theory of perception posits that the perception of external things through the organs of sense is a direct mental act or a phenomenon of consciousness. He argues that this act is not susceptible of being resolved into anything else. While he does not work this doctrine out in great detail, he supports it through subtle and elaborate criticisms of all alternative theories. He maintains that the reality of this perception can be neither proved nor disproved.

## General Ideas and Propositions
Regarding general propositions and general and abstract ideas, Bailey's opinions align with those of the empirical school. Despite this alignment, his specific analysis of these matters frequently presents them in a new light.

## Theory of Morals and Utilitarianism
In the realm of morals, Bailey advocates for utilitarianism, although he objects to the use of the term "utility" because he finds it narrow and considers it to have a sordid content to those who are unthinking. He utilizes great skill to outline the steps involved in the formation of "complex" mental facts, specifically those related to the recognition of right, obligation, and duty.

Bailey bases all moral phenomena on five specific facts:
1. Man is susceptible to pain and pleasure.
2. Man dislikes or likes the causes of these feelings.
3. Man desires to reciprocate the pain or pleasure he has received.
4. Man expects others to reciprocate in the same manner.
5. Man feels a degree of sympathy with these same feelings in his fellow human beings.
