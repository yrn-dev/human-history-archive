# BOMBARDON

The bombardon, also referred to as the bass tuba, is a brass wind instrument serving as the bass and contrabass in military bands. In an orchestral setting, it is called the bass tuba. The term is derived from *bombardone*, the Italian name for the contrabass pommer (bombard), which functioned as the bass of medieval orchestras prior to the invention of the fagotto. Additionally, the name is used to describe a 16 ft. tone bass reed stop on the organ.

## History and Development
The bombardon was the first bass wind instrument to be fitted with valves. In its early stages, it was known as the *bass horn*, *clavicor*, or *corno basso*. This original *corno basso* featured a narrow bore and horizontal pistons. It should be distinguished from the keyed bass horn, which evolved into the ophicleide.

In Prussia, the Jager military bands introduced the keyed bass horn as a bass instrument in 1829, followed by the bombardon (or valve-ophicleide) in 1831. By 1835, these were superseded in the Guards by the bass tuba invented by J.G. Moritz and Wieprecht.

The bombardon and the euphonium (of which the bombardon is the bass) resulted from applying valves to the bugle family, a process that also produced saxhorns. A primary distinction exists between saxhorns and tubas, including the bombardon: the latter possess a conical bore wide enough to produce fundamental sounds with immense power and a rich, full quality. This distinction led to a classification in Austria and Germany as "whole instruments" (*Ganze Instrumente*), while narrow-bore instruments like saxhorns were termed "half instruments" (*Halbe Instrumente*).

Historically, brass instruments with cup-shaped mouthpieces and conical bores required a tube twice as long as an organ pipe of the same note to function; for instance, a French horn sounding the 8 ft. C of an organ pipe required a 16 ft. tube, making C the second harmonic because the fundamental was unobtainable. However, once instrument-makers applied the wide conical bore proportions of the bugle to baritone and bass instruments, the fundamental sound became clear and full. Consequently, modern tubas are half the length of older instruments; a euphonium sounding 8 ft. C now requires only an 8 ft. tube.

## Forms and Construction
The modern bombardon exists in two primary forms:
*   **Upright model:** Utilized for stationary band music.
*   **Circular model (Helicon):** Worn around the body with the large bell resting on the left shoulder. This design, based on the Roman *cornu*, provides a more convenient method for carrying the heavy instrument during marches.

The instrument utilizes a cup-shaped mouthpiece. To obtain intervening notes, bombardons use pistons or valves. When depressed, these either cut off a length of tubing to raise the pitch or admit wind into additional tubing to lower it. Most bombardons feature three or four pistons that lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 1-1/2, and 2-1/2 tones, though in Belgium, the intervals are 1, 1/2, 2, and 3 tones. In Germany and Austria, a "cylinder action" is frequently used, replacing the pump or piston with a four-way brass cock operated by cranks and a key.

## Technical Characteristics
The bombardon possesses a harmonic series consisting of the 1st through the 8th harmonics, with a chromatic compass spanning 3-1/2 to 4 octaves. 

Due to the immense length of the air column, the lowest notes produced by the valves are difficult to obtain because the lips often lack the power to synchronize with such a slow rate of vibration. These lowest notes are often barely audible unless another bombardon doubles them an octave higher.

Regarding musical notation, bombardons are generally treated as non-transposing instruments, meaning the music is written as it sounds. The exception to this is in Belgium and France, where transposition is the usual practice.

## Regional Usage
The bombardon is used within the military bands of Austria. In Germany, however, it has been superseded by a bass tuba that differs slightly in construction and form from the versions used in Austria, France, Belgium, and England.
