# Rome and Italy in Historical Context

The city of Rome and the broader Italian peninsula have served as central hubs for political power, religious development, and the arts across various eras of human history. From the expansion of the Early Empire to the revolutionary movements of the 19th century, Italy has been a site of significant cultural transference and political struggle.

## Political Evolution and Expansion
The transition to the Early Empire was marked by the figure of Julius Cæsar, who established his reputation as a military leader in Gaul. While the boundaries of the Roman state continued to expand to their maximum around 100 A.D., the region experienced periods of instability, including civil war, social degeneration, and the revolt of Spartacus. 

In the sixth century, the Eastern Empire under Emperor Justinian (527-565) exhibited a phase of vigour, recovering the Vandal kingdom in 534 and expelling the Goths from Italy in 553. Following Justinian's death in 565, the Lombards settled in Lombardy, though Rome, Southern Italy, Ravenna, and North Africa remained under the rule of the Eastern Empire.

## Religious and Spiritual History
Rome served as a critical location for early Christian figures. Mark, who had been associated with Barnabas and Paul, later attached himself to Peter in Jerusalem. Tradition, preserved by Clement of Alexandria and based on records by Papias, suggests that Mark wrote his Gospel in Rome under the influence of Peter's preaching. Mark is described as having acted as a *servus servorum dei* to Peter, Paul, and Barnabas, and some suggest he served as Peter's interpreter or dragoman due to Peter's imperfect mastery of the Greek language.

In the 19th century, the "Roman question" remained a point of political contention. Massimo d'Azeglio argued that the pope should maintain full spiritual independence and nominal sovereignty over Rome, while the capital of a unified Italy should be established elsewhere, allowing Romans to be Italian citizens.

## Artistic and Musical Influence
Italy has frequently been the center of the musical and artistic worlds. The French composer Josquin Des Prés (c. 1445-1521) was invited to Rome by Pope Sixtus IV to teach musicians, as musical knowledge in Italy was then at a low ebb. Des Prés resided in Rome until 1484, and his presence marked the transference of musical art from its Gallo-Belgian birthplace to Italy, influencing later developments under Palestrina.

In the visual arts, Rome attracted international talent. Jose Benlliure y Gil, a Spanish painter born in Valencia, was sent to the Spanish school in Rome and became the leader of the Spanish art colony there, where he worked as a sculptor and painter. His notable works include the "Vision in the Coliseum." Additionally, the Italian painter Giovanni Benedetto Castiglione, known as "Il Grechetto," produced works found in Rome, Venice, Naples, and Florence, though he spent his final years in Mantua.

## 19th Century Figures and National Movements
The 19th century saw Italy embroiled in struggles for national identity and political loyalty. Lucien Bonaparte lived in Rome for several years, where he showed marked hostility toward the emperor. After a period of exile in America and England, he returned to Rome and supported Napoleon during the Hundred Days in 1815. He eventually died in Rome on June 29, 1840.

Ugo Bassi (1800-1849), an Italian patriot and Barnabite novice, lived a life of study and devotion in Rome before becoming a famous preacher. During the revolutionary movements of 1848, Bassi served as an army chaplain for General Durando's papal force. Following the proclamation of the Roman republic and the flight of the pope from Rome, Bassi joined Garibaldi's forces to fight French troops attempting to re-establish temporal power. He was eventually captured near Comacchio after following Garibaldi to San Marino.
