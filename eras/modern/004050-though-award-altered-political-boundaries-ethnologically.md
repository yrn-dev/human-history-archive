# Political and Ethnological Boundaries

The determination of boundaries often involves a distinction between political demarcations—the legal frontiers of states or spheres of influence—and ethnological distributions, which describe the placement of human varieties and population densities. While political boundaries may be altered by treaties, awards, or commissions, the underlying ethnological character of a region often remains unchanged.

## The Case of Barotseland
In 1905, the king of Italy issued an award regarding the Anglo-Portuguese Barotse Boundary dispute. This award had a significant impact on the political definition of Barotseland, effectively giving the term a second meaning. The award declared that the northern and western sections of Barotseland were outside the dominion of the paramount chief and, consequently, were not within the British sphere of influence. 

This political shift introduced a frontier based on longitudinal and latitudinal lines, which complicated existing tribal boundaries. However, the archive notes that while the award altered the political boundaries, ethnologically Barotseland remained much as it had been previously described. From an ethnological standpoint, as well as in terms of flora and fauna, Barotseland is considered to belong to Central Africa rather than South Africa. The Zambezi river serves as a definite boundary line for species distribution and has prevented the spread of cattle diseases, such as lung sickness and tick fever, from South Africa into Barotseland. The area currently under British protection is approximately 182,000 square miles.

## Regional Boundary Demarcations
Political boundaries are frequently established through arbitration, commissions, and treaties to resolve disputes between powers.

### Central America
Costa Rica's boundaries were settled through multiple international awards. In 1888, the president of the United States provided an award regarding the boundary between Costa Rica and Nicaragua, which confirmed a treaty from 1858; subsequent demarcation difficulties were resolved by a treaty in 1896. The boundary between Costa Rica and Panama (which was then a province of Colombia) was fixed on September 15, 1900, by an award from the French president.

### Asia
In Afghanistan, northern limits were established by Boundary Commissions in 1895 and between 1884 and 1886, extending from the Sarikol mountains in the east to the Hari Rud in the west. Southern and eastern boundaries were defined by minor commissions based on the Kabul agreement of 1893, ending with the Mohmand settlement in 1897.

In Burma, the eastern boundaries on the Chinese frontier are not yet fully demarcated. However, at a point level in latitude with Mogaung, the boundary is defined by the eastern watershed of the Nmaikha. The boundary then moves south to a position south-east of Bhamo (latitude 24 degrees), defines the northern edge of the Shan States, and reaches the Mekong river at approximately latitude 21 degrees 45'. For 200 miles, the Mekong river serves as the boundary between Tongking (French interests) and the Shan States (British interests). Further south, the boundary leaves the Mekong to run west toward the Salween, where the eastern watershed of the Salween basin divides Siam from the Lower Burma provinces.

## Ethnological vs. Political Terms
The distinction between ethnological and political designations is evident in the history of the Balkan Peninsula. The name "Illyria" was applied to a region extending from Durazzo to Fiume along the Adriatic shore, reaching inland to the Servian Morava and the Danube. This area includes Montenegro, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Dalmatia, the southern half of Croatia-Slavonia, the sanjak of Novibazar, part of western Servia, and the extreme north of Albania.

Originally, "Illyria" (or the Greek *hoi Illyrioi*) was an ethnological term referring to the Illyrian tribes in an indeterminate area between Epirus and Liburnia. In contrast, the Latin term *Illyricum* eventually became a political expression used for various divisions of the Roman Empire. The boundaries of *Illyricum* changed frequently and often covered a much larger area than Illyria proper, at times including cities such as Athens and Vienna, which geographers would not include in the ethnological definition of Illyria.
