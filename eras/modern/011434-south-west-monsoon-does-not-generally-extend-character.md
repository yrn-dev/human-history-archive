# The South-West Monsoon

The south-west monsoon consists of south-westerly winds that prevail north of the equator during the hot half of the year. The term "monsoon" is a corruption of the Indian word for season. These winds are the result of periodical changes in atmospheric pressure, specifically a great diminution of pressure over Asia that begins with rising temperatures in April and May. This low pressure is contrasted with relatively higher pressure over the equator and regions to its south. The pressure diminution reaches its maximum in July, shortly after the solstice, leading to the full development of the south-west monsoon.

## Atmospheric Mechanics and Seasonal Transition
Winds generally blow from areas of greatest pressure to those of least pressure, though their direction is modified by the earth's west-to-east revolution on its axis. As the barometric pressure is restored and equalized within the tropics following the October equinox, and temperatures fall north of the equator, the south-west winds subside. They are then succeeded by the north-east monsoon during the winter months, which is driven by relatively greater atmospheric pressure over Asia compared to the equatorial region.

## Regional Variations in Wind Direction
While the general law of the monsoon is consistent, local influences often disguise or modify wind directions:

*   **Arabian Sea:** The monsoon begins with west and north-westerly winds, shifting to south-west as the year progresses, and returning to north-west and north in the autumn.
*   **Bay of Bengal:** The strength of the south-west monsoon is primarily from the south and south-east. After October, these are replaced by north-east winds, which eventually give way to northerly and north-westerly winds.
*   **Malay Archipelago:** The force of the monsoons is significantly interrupted here. Because of its position on the equator, the southerly summer winds of the Asiatic seas do not reach the coasts of Java; instead, south-easterly trade winds develop.
*   **China Sea:** This region is fully exposed to both monsoons, with normal directions nearly coinciding with the channel between the eastern islands and the Asian continent.

## Progression Over Land and Topographical Barriers
The south-west monsoon does not generally maintain its character as a south-west wind once it moves over land. The air current flowing from the sea is dissipated, loses force, and is diverted toward areas of least pressure. 

As these winds move northward over India, they shift to south-easterly and easterly winds over the north-eastern Gangetic plain and become south winds as they move up the Indus. By the time they reach the northern extremity of the great Indian plain, their northward velocity is almost entirely exhausted. Consequently, they hardly penetrate the Indus basin or the ranges of the Himalaya and are not felt on the table-lands of Afghanistan. The mountains of the Himalaya and those branching into the Malay peninsula prevent the winds from continuing in their original direction.

## Impact on Rainfall and Climate
The south-west monsoon is the primary cause of summer rains in south Asia. The heaviest rainfall occurs where vapour-bearing winds face mountain ranges. Examples include the west coast of the Malay peninsula and the Western Ghats of India. The Khasi range, located about 100 miles north-east of Calcutta, records some of the heaviest rainfall in the world (at least 600 inches annually) due to its abrupt front facing moist winds from the Bay of Bengal. In the Konkan, monsoon rainfall sometimes approaches 300 inches.

Conversely, certain areas remain arid:
*   **Sind and the Indus:** Rainfall is small and irregular because there are no obstacles to force the condensation of vapour, which is instead carried to the Punjab and the outer ranges of Afghanistan and the western Himalaya.
*   **Baluchistan:** Rains cease on the southern border west of Karachi because the projection of the south-east coast of Arabia limits the length of the coastline exposed to the current and the breadth of the air current itself.
*   **Indian Desert:** This region is described as inaccessible to both main branches of the south-west monsoon.

In the Bombay Presidency, the monsoon typically begins in the first week of June, bringing heavy rain to the coast. While this makes travel difficult and unpleasant from June to October, it has little influence in Sind.

## Associated Meteorological Phenomena
The hot months in the China Sea and Indian Ocean are marked by revolving storms or cyclones. In the China Sea, these are known as typhoons. 

Cyclones in the Bay of Bengal typically originate over the Nicobar and Andaman islands between June and October (and rarely in November). They are believed to be caused by adverse northern currents meeting the south-west monsoon. These storms generally move north-west, strike the east coast of the Indian peninsula, and often pass violently across the Ganges delta. In the China Sea, cyclones originate near Formosa and advance from north-east to south-west (or occasionally east to west). In both regions, storms tend to advance toward areas of greatest heat, with winds circulating from north by west, through south, to east.
