# Plant Sensation and Discernment

The study of whether plants possess capacities for sensation, volition, and discernment has been addressed by several figures across different eras of scientific inquiry, including the works of Bonnet, Erasmus Darwin, and Francis Darwin.

## Early Theories of Plant Sensation
In 1754, Bonnet published *Recherches sur l'usage des feuilles dans les plantes*, a work resulting from several years of diligent study focused on the leaves of plants. In this text, Bonnet advanced various considerations intended to demonstrate that plants are endowed with powers of discernment and sensation. This research was conducted during a period of Bonnet's career before his eyesight failed, which eventually led him to transition from botany to the study of philosophy.

## Contributions of Erasmus Darwin
Erasmus Darwin (1731–1802), an English man of science and poet, also explored the capabilities of plants. In his 1799 publication, *Phytologia, or the Philosophy of Agriculture and Gardening*, he stated his opinion that plants possess both volition and sensation. 

Beyond his philosophical and scientific prose, Erasmus Darwin expressed a deep interest in nature through poetry. His work *The Botanic Garden* (published in full in 1791), which includes the section *The Loves of the Plants*, utilized the Linnaean system to describe plants. In this poem, he employed an extensive and sometimes excessive use of personification, applying it not only to the plants themselves but also to various parts of the plant.

## Later Research and the Darwin Family
The topic of plant sensation was revisited more recently by Francis Darwin. His work aligned with the earlier considerations of Bonnet regarding the powers of sensation and discernment in plants. 

Francis Darwin was the son of Charles Darwin (1809–1882), who also contributed extensively to botanical science. While Charles Darwin's work focused on different aspects of botany—such as the *Fertilization of Orchids* (1862) and *The Effects of Cross- and Self-Fertilization in the Vegetable Kingdom* (1876)—his research provided evidence on the vigor and number of offspring resulting from cross-fertilization compared to self-fertilization. He also authored *Different Forms of Flowers on Plants of the Same Species* (1877), which detailed how different flower forms on the same species are adapted to be fertilized by the pollen of another form.

## Comparative Plant Physiology
In the broader study of plant physiology, research by figures such as Darwin, Pfeffer, Sachs, Vines, and Klebs has been used to understand the life-actions of various plant types, including fungi. The physiology of fungi is considered to fall under the general heading of plant physiology. These organisms share several characteristics with higher plants, such as the requirement for water, oxygen, and specific salts (phosphorus, sulphur, magnesium, and potassium), as well as carbonaceous and nitrogenous materials. Their response to external factors, irritability, and general metabolism show specific variations similar to those found in ordinary plant cells.
