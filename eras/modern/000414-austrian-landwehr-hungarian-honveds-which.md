# Austrian Landwehr and Hungarian Honveds

The Austrian Landwehr (K.K.) and the Hungarian Honveds are the special national armies of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy. Established as separate national institutions, they exist alongside the common army (*K. und K.*) and the *Landsturm*, or levy-in-mass. While the common army is managed by the joint minister of war, the Landwehr and Honveds are administered by the respective ministries of national defence of Austria and Hungary. Despite this administrative separation, the total armed power of the Dual Monarchy remains a single entity under the supreme command of the monarch.

## Organization and Structure
The Austrian Landwehr is organized into eight divisions of varying strength. The "Royal Hungarian" Landwehr, known as the Honveds, is organized into seven divisions. Both national forces include their own artillery and cavalry, specifically hussars and Uhlans. 

In peacetime, the Landwehr and Honveds are distinct; however, it is probable that during wartime, a division from each will be integrated into each army corps, with the exception of the Vienna corps, which maintains three divisions during peace. Historically, the Landwehr was practically organized as part of the standing army, whereas the Honveds were kept entirely distinct from the ordinary army.

## Recruitment and Service
Since 1868, the military system has been based on the principle of universal and personal obligation for citizens to bear arms. The annual number of recruits is determined by the Austrian and Hungarian parliaments via military bills, generally based on population figures from the most recent census. 

In 1905, the annual allotment of recruits was 10,000 men for the Austrian Landwehr and 12,500 men for the Hungarian Honveds. The standard term of service consists of two years with the colours (three years for cavalry), seven or eight years in the reserve, and two years in the Landwehr. Men who are not drafted into the active army spend an equivalent total period of service in various special reserves.

## Strength and Personnel
As of 1905, the peace footing strength of these forces was as follows:

*   **Austrian Landwehr:** 2,053 officers and 25,766 men, with 1,456 horses. This total includes 1,883 officers and 23,905 men in the infantry, and 170 officers and 1,861 men in the cavalry.
*   **Hungarian Honveds:** 2,648 officers and 25,319 men, with 3,772 horses. This total includes 2,258 officers and 21,149 men in the infantry, and 390 officers and 4,170 men in the cavalry.

In terms of wartime estimates, the Landwehr and Honveds combined were expected to yield 219,000 infantry and 18,000 cavalry.

## Political and National Context
The administration of these forces has occasionally been a point of political contention, particularly within the Hungarian parliament. The Independence party, led by Francis Kossuth, sought concessions to promote the use of the Magyar language within the Hungarian part of the army to make Hungarian regiments more Magyar in character.

In 1902, a proposal to increase annual recruit contingents by 21,000 men—including contributions for the national defence troops—led to a political deadlock. The Hungarian Chamber resented the detention of several thousand supernumerary recruits in barracks pending parliamentary sanction of the Army bills. This grievance was utilized by the Opposition to obstruct ministerial measures until the supernumeraries were discharged at the end of 1902.

## Historical Note on the Honveds
The Honveds have a history associated with the Hungarian war of independence. During this period, figures such as Janos Damjanich served in the Honveds. Damjanich, a major in the third Honved regiment at Szeged, later became a commander of the 3rd army corps and led divisions during the advance on the Hungarian capital and the battle of Nagysarlo in April 1849.
