# Election and Representation

Representative government is the system adopted by modern nation states and empires as populations grew too large and distances became too great for the regular assemblies of qualified citizens characteristic of narrow parochial democracies. The essence of this system is that the people do not govern themselves directly, but instead periodically elect individuals to govern on their behalf.

## Principles of Representation and Franchise
Different democracies employ various criteria for citizenship and the right to vote. Moderate democracies often utilize a low property qualification, whereas extreme democracy extends citizenship to all adult persons, regardless of sex.

In Japan, the principle of popular representation is strictly followed across local administration levels, including prefectures (*ken*), counties (*gun*), towns (*shi*), and districts (*cho* or *son*). Local assemblies are elected with membership numbers fixed in proportion to the population. The franchise is tied to property qualifications based on annual direct national tax payments: ten yen for prefectural assemblies, three yen for county assemblies, and two yen for town and district assemblies. To ensure the representation of property, electors are divided into three classes based on their aggregate payments to the national treasury, with each class electing one-third of the assembly's members. Certain groups are denied the franchise, including salaried officials, ministers of religion, contractors for public works, and those unable to write their own names or the names of candidates.

## Electoral Systems and Voting Methods
Voting mechanisms vary significantly by region. In the United States, the large number of officials chosen during elections leads to the use of "blanket" ballots. In New York state, for example, a quadrennial presidential election ballot may be 18 to 20 inches wide and 2 to 3 feet long to accommodate lists for thirty-six presidential electors, a congressman, governor, lieutenant-governor, five other state officers, members of the state legislature, judges, a sheriff, and county-clerk. Voters may mark a full party column or select individual candidates. Other states, such as Massachusetts, arrange candidates by office rather than party lists, while Connecticut, New Jersey, Missouri, North and South Carolina, Georgia, and New Mexico use separate party ballots.

## Case Study: Belgium
Belgium's parliamentary system consists of a chamber of representatives and a senate. Until 1899, the chamber had 152 members, later increased to 166; deputies are elected for four years, with half the house re-elected every two years. The senate comprises seventy-six elected members (half of whom retire every four years) and twenty-six members nominated by provincial councils. Senators serve eight-year terms and must be at least forty years old.

The Belgian electorate underwent significant changes in 1894 when property qualifications were removed, granting one vote to every Belgian citizen aged twenty-five with one year of residence in their commune. However, a system of plural voting was introduced:
*   **Second vote:** Granted to citizens aged thirty-five who are married (or widowers with legitimate offspring) and pay five francs in direct taxes.
*   **Additional votes:** Up to two extra votes are granted for university diplomas, official status, or property qualifications.
The maximum voting power for any individual is three votes. In 1904, there were 1,581,649 voters holding 2,467,966 votes.

In 1894, elections gave the Catholic party an overwhelming majority, while the Liberal party nearly disappeared and Socialists were returned in Walloon provinces. Because the Liberals received fewer seats than their vote count suggested they deserved, a movement for proportional representation grew. This was implemented in August 1899 for all arrondissements to protect minorities. By 1902, communal elections—where burgomasters are appointed by councils chosen by electors—also utilized proportional representation and multiple votes (up to a maximum of four).

## Regional Variations in Representation
In Switzerland, the *Grossrath* (legislature) of certain cantons consists of members elected in electoral circles at a ratio of one member per 1,000 souls. Proportional representation was adopted for these elections on April 4, 1909. The executive and federal representatives (*Ständerath* and *Nationalrath*) are elected by popular vote every four years. Swiss citizens also possess the right of "initiative" (requiring 4,000 citizens) for legislative projects or constitutional amendments, and the right to a facultative referendum or the revocation of the legislature's mandate (requiring 5,000 citizens).

In Canada, the mid-19th century was marked by disputes over "representation by population." Upper Canada, having increased in population and revenue, demanded proportionate representation. Opponents, such as La Fontaine, argued that this would allow the stronger province to control the weaker one without constitutional restraints. During this era, figures like William Lyon Mackenzie advocated for "responsible government" as a remedy for grievances. Mackenzie's career illustrated the volatility of representation; he was elected member of parliament for York in 1828 but was expelled five times for publishing house proceedings without authorization, being re-elected by his constituents each time.

## The Role of the Deputy
In representative government, a deputy is a member of an elected chamber appointed to act or govern in place of another. A deputy's powers are delegated by their principal. While some are general deputies exercising the whole office, others are special deputies for specific matters. In the United Kingdom, statutes allow certain officials, such as returning officers at elections or county court judges, to appoint deputies during illness or absence.
