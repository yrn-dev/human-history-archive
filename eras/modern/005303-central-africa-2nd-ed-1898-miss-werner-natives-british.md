# British Central Africa

British Central Africa is a general designation for the British protectorates located in South Central Africa north of the Zambezi river. More specifically, the term refers to a large territory bounded by Lake Tanganyika at 8° 25' S. and the river Shiré near its confluence with the Zambezi at 17° 6' S., and extending between 26° 30' E. (the river Luengwe-Kafukwe) and 36° 10' E. (the district of Mlanje).

## Administrative Evolution
The term "British Central Africa" was originally used by Sir H.H. Johnston to describe all territories under British influence north of the Zambezi that were intended for a single administration. However, because the Rhodesian territories and Barotseland could not be connected with the direct British administration north of the Zambezi, the official use of the name was confined in 1893 to the British protectorate surrounding Lake Nyasa and the Shiré. 

In 1907, the official title of this protectorate was changed to the Nyasaland Protectorate. Simultaneously, the territories of the British South Africa Company north of the Zambezi were divided into North Western Rhodesia (Barotseland) and North Eastern Rhodesia. Despite these official changes, "British Central Africa" remains a convenient designation for the region, which includes the Nyasaland Protectorate, North Eastern Rhodesia, and part of North Western Rhodesia. The western boundary of this region is defined by a line from the southern boundary of the Belgian Congo near the source of the Lualaba to the western source of the Luanga river, following the Luanga to its junction with the Luengwe-Kafukwe, which then delimits the territory down to the Zambezi.

## History and Exploration
European penetration into the region was limited during the 16th and 17th centuries to vague Portuguese wanderings. The first educated European explorer to enter the country was the Portuguese official Dr. F.J.M. de Lacerda e Almeida, who traveled from Tete on the Zambezi toward Lake Mweru.

The modern history of the region began with David Livingstone, who discovered Lake Nyasa in 1859 after penetrating up the Shiré river. Livingstone's subsequent explorations of Lake Mweru, Lake Bangweulu (where he died in 1873), and the south end of Tanganyika established British interests in South Central Africa. Following his death, missionary societies entered the region to begin evangelization. These missionaries, along with a small number of Scottish settlers, resisted Portuguese attempts to expand their influence from the Zambezi and the provinces of Moçambique.

From these missionary efforts emerged the African Lakes Trading Corporation, which entered into a conflict with Arabs established at the north end of Lake Nyasa. A struggle for possession of the country between Britons and Arabs lasted from approximately 1885 until 1896. During this unofficial war, the African Lakes Corporation utilized volunteers, including Mr. (later Sir) Alfred Sharpe and Captain (later Sir F.D.) Lugard, both of whom were wounded. In 1889, Sir H.H. Johnston was dispatched to arrange a settlement between the Corporation and the Arabs and to protect friendly native chiefs from Portuguese aggression. These efforts and subsequent treaties resulted in the creation of the British sphere of influence and protectorate north of the Zambezi.

## Geography and Population
The region's hydrology involves complex nomenclature. The Luangwa (or Loangwa) is an independent affluent of the Zambezi, whereas the Luanga (also known as the Lunga) is a tributary of the Luengwe-Kafukwe, which is often referred to as the Kafue.

The native population of British Central Africa is estimated at 2,000,000. While the tribes of Central Africa are less known than those of South Africa, many exhibit racial characteristics similar to South African tribes, though some possess an admixture of Arab blood.

## Regional Context and European Influence
The broader exploration of Africa by 1900 resulted in the continent being mapped and divided among European powers. This "scramble" often ignored the welfare of native populations. In the Belgian Congo, the compulsory collection of wild rubber led to horrible atrocities. In 1898, tensions between France and Great Britain nearly led to war when Colonel Marchand attempted to seize the Upper Nile at Fashoda while crossing Central Africa from the west coast.
