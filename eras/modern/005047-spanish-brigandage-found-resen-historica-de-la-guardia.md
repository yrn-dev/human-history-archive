# Brigandage in Spain

Brigandage in Spain has historically been characterized by its persistence in specific geographic regions and its relationship with the strength of the central government. It has been particularly endemic in areas south of the Sierra Morena, while in northern regions, it typically flourished during periods of civil war, foreign invasion, or weak government administration.

## Geographic and Social Factors
The development of brigandage in Spain was favored by the presence of convenient hiding places, specifically the Sierras. In these regions, the outlaws were known as *bandoleros* (meaning banished men) and *salteadores* (raiders). 

The persistence of these activities was often linked to bad administration and a lack of native help for law enforcement, as officers of the law were frequently viewed as dangers or deliberate enemies. Furthermore, local potentates sometimes protected brigands for their own interests. In the south, *bandoleros* often received sympathy from the population and utilized Spanish equivalents of the Italian *manuténgoli* (maintainers). Additionally, the lawless class associated with tobacco smuggling from Gibraltar has contributed to the continuation of pure brigandage.

## Brigandage in Catalonia
Catalonia experienced some of the highest levels of brigandage, which originated from peasant strife against the feudal exactions of landlords. This era produced the traditional hero Roque Guinart, who is featured in the second part of *Don Quixote*. The movement received further stimulus from the War of the Succession (1700–1714) and the 1640 revolt against the house of Austria.

The suppression of Catalan brigandage involved unique methods. Pedro Veciana, a country gentleman and hereditary *balio* (civil and military lieutenant) of the archbishop of Tarragona in Valls, armed his own farm-servants to resist brigand attacks. Alongside other neighboring gentlemen, he formed a strong band called the Mozos (Boys) of Veciana. After the brigands were repulsed in an attack on the town of Valls, the government of Philip V commissioned Veciana to establish a special police corps known as the *escuadra de Cataluna*. The position of colonel of this corps remained within the Veciana family for five generations.

## Law Enforcement and Suppression
Historically, brigandage in Spain has been easily suppressed by capable administrations. In central and northern Spain, the rural population generally supported the police provided the government acted with firmness. 

A significant turning point occurred around 1844 with the organization of the *Guardia Civil*, an excellent constabulary established by the duke of Ahumada, which kept brigandage well under control. Even after the Carlist War ended in 1874, remaining bands in Catalonia were vulnerable; for example, one of the worst bands was surprised and killed with boxwood cudgels by a gang of charcoal-burners at the ruins of the castle of San Martin de Centellas.

## National Resistance and Terminology
The definition of a "brigand" has often been influenced by political conflict. Between 1808 and 1814, Spanish irregular levies maintained national resistance against the French. While these fighters were called brigands by their enemies, such activities are often the last resource for a people subject to invasion.
