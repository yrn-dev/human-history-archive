# Chile and the Secret Treaty of 1873

The diplomatic and military history of Chile in the late 19th and early 20th centuries was significantly shaped by its relations with Bolivia and Peru, specifically regarding territorial boundaries, nitrate deposits, and a secret agreement between the latter two nations.

## The Secret Treaty and its Origins
On February 6, 1873, Bolivia and Peru entered into a secret agreement. The ostensible purpose of this treaty was the mutual defense of their territorial integrity and protection against exterior aggression. In practice, both countries viewed further encroachment by Chile as the primary threat.

The motivations for this agreement were rooted in economic and territorial disputes. Under a treaty from 1866, Bolivia had conceded the 24th parallel as the boundary of Chilean territory and agreed that Chile would receive a half share of customs and full trading facilities on the coast between the 23rd and 24th parallels. Additionally, Chile was permitted to mine and export products from this district without hindrance or tax from Bolivia. In 1870, Bolivia further granted an Anglo-Chilean company the right to work nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel for a sum of $10,000. The resulting flow of wealth into Chilean hands caused discontent in Bolivia, while Peru was displeased by the establishment of Chilean capital in the rich Tarapaca district.

## Escalation to War
Tensions rose during the presidency of General Hilarion Daza, who succeeded Dr. Tomas Frias in 1876. Daza demanded a tax of 10 cents per quintal on all nitrates exported from Bolivia, threatening to seize nitrates held by exporters if the levy was not paid. Chile responded by sending its fleet to blockade the ports of Tocapilla, Cobija, and Antofagasta to protect the property of Chilean subjects. Colonel Sotomayor occupied Antofagasta on February 14, 1879, and the Bolivian government declared war on March 1.

As the conflict expanded, Peru offered to act as a mediator. However, the Chilean government, which was well aware of the existence of the secret treaty between Peru and Bolivia, viewed this offer of intervention as questionable. Chile's distrust was further fueled by a Peruvian law passed in 1875 that created a monopoly of the Tarapaca nitrate beds, which seriously prejudiced Chilean enterprise. Chile demanded an assurance of Peruvian neutrality and the annulment of the secret treaty; when Peru refused both demands, Chile declared war.

## The War of the Pacific
Chilean superiority at sea eventually allowed them to land troops to face allied forces concentrated at other points in the south and Arica. The Chileans quickly took control of Bolivian ports. A sea attack on Pisagua routed troops under the Peruvian General Buendia, opening the way into southern Peru. Following this defeat, General Daza led Bolivian troops in a disorderly retreat to Tacna. The allies evacuated Iquique without a struggle, and after severe fighting, the province of Tarapaca surrendered by the end of 1879.

The war continued with significant naval operations, including a destructive raid led by the Chilean naval officer Patricio Lynch. In January 1881, the battles of Miraflores and Chorillos took place, characterized by heavy slaughter and savage excesses by Chilean troops. These events led to the surrender of Callao and Lima, leaving Chile as the practical master of Peru. While a provisional government under Admiral Montero lingered in the interior, the Peruvians eventually concluded a peace treaty on October 20, 1883.

## Post-War Settlements and Boundary Disputes
A peace treaty was signed between Chile and Bolivia on December 11, 1883, in which Bolivia ceded its entire sea-coast, including the port of Cobija, to Chile. However, disputes continued regarding the occupation of Arica and Tacna under the Pacto de Tregna of April 4, 1884.

In 1895, Chile considered conceding a sea outlet to Bolivia in exchange for the recognition of Chilean ownership of Arica and Tacna. This conciliatory attitude changed when Chile discovered secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina. Deprived of Argentine support and unable to compel concessions, Bolivian presidents Pando and Ismail Montes eventually yielded.

A treaty ratified in 1905 settled the boundary dispute. Bolivia ceded the maritime provinces occupied by Chile since the war of 1881, leaving the republic without a seaport. In return, Chile agreed to:
* Construct a railway from Arica to the Bolivian capital, La Paz, at its own cost.
* Provide Bolivians with free transit through Chilean territory to specific coastal towns.
* Pay a cash indemnity to Bolivia.
* Provide pecuniary assistance for the construction of other interior railways.
