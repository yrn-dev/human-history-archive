# The Himalaya: Zoological and Botanical Relations

The Himalaya serves as a significant geographical and biological boundary in Asia, marking the transition between various zoological and botanical regions. Its distribution of life is heavily dependent on climatic conditions, showing a marked affinity with the flora and fauna of Europe and other Asian territories.

## Zoological Classifications
The northern portion of Asia, extending as far south as the Himalaya, is not zoologically distinct from Europe. Together with the strip of Africa located north of the Atlas mountains, these areas comprise the Palaearctic region, a primary division of the earth established by Dr. Sclater. This classification has received general approval from naturalists. The Palaearctic Subregion broadly encompasses Europe and Asia, excluding China and India. While not as homogeneous as the Nearctic area, it maintains a consistency in its bulk of birds; for instance, there is a notable similarity between the avifauna of western Europe and Japan, where species such as the hedge-sparrow and redbreast are repeated with slight modifications.

In contrast, the southern ranges of the Himalayas mark a different boundary. The equatorial zoological zone, which generally coincides with the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, extends northwards through Persia and Afghanistan to reach the southern ranges of the Himalayas.

## Botanical Affinities with Europe
The flora of northern Asia is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, with differences appearing primarily in species rather than genera. A warm temperate zone extends from the Mediterranean to the Himalaya, characterized by a flora that is essentially European in character. While few European species are known in the eastern parts of the Himalaya, many reach the central chain. Genera common to both Europe and the Himalaya are abundant and extend across the entire chain at all elevations.

Specific examples of this botanical connection include:
*   **The Maple:** The genus *Acer* includes about sixty species native to Asia (especially the Himalayas, China, and Japan), Europe, and North America.
*   **Evergreen Oak:** *Quercus Ilex*, the evergreen oak of southern Europe, is found in forests as far east as the Sutlej.
*   **General Forestation:** In areas of abundant rain, mountains in Asia Minor and northern Persia are clothed in forests of *Ulmus*, *Acer*, *Carpinus*, *Corylus*, *Fagus*, and *Quercus*. Of these, *Fagus* is the only genus not found on the Himalaya.

## Regional Flora and Fauna
The biological character of the region varies by geography:
*   **Afghanistan and Persia:** The flora of northern Afghanistan approximates that of the contiguous western Himalaya. Higher parts of Persia and Afghanistan are characterized by thistles, Boraginaceae, and gigantic Umbelliferae (including *Dorema*, *Bubon*, *Ferula*, *Prangos*, *Galbanum*, and *Peucedanum*), some of which extend into Tibet.
*   **Northern Asia:** In the mountains, the principal trees are birch, larch, and pines, while poplars, alders, and willow grow on lower ground. In Siberia, the northern limit of the pine is approximately 70 deg. N. It is noted that all heaths and the oak are absent east of the Ural.
*   **Eastern Asia:** There is a diffusion of Chinese and Japanese forms along the temperate zone, which are most numerous in the eastern Himalaya. Additionally, temperate forms from the American continent extend to the Himalaya, reaching almost as far as Kashmir.

## Notable Species
The eastern Himalaya to north-western China is the habitat of the panda (*Aelurus fulgens*), a bright golden red animal with black under-parts and thick fur. The panda is a representative of the raccoon tribe (*Procyonidae*). It is characterized by a cat-like face, blunt claws, and a cylindrical ringed tail. Its dentition totals 38, with broad upper molars featuring complicated cusps. Fossil species of the panda have been found in the later Tertiary deposits of Europe.
