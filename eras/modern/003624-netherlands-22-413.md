# Netherlands

## Early History and Development
In the twelfth century, the lower Rhine country was divided among various small rulers. The population was primarily Low German with a Keltic basis, which was later mixed with Danish elements. Linguistically, the bulk of the region spoke Dutch, Frisian, and other Low German languages, while French dialects were spoken along the southeastern fringe.

During the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, several significant towns emerged, including Haarlem, Leyden, Utrecht, Ypres, Bruges, and Ghent. These urban centers developed a class of educated townsmen and quasi-independent municipal governments. The region also played a notable role in the crusades; Baldwin of Flanders founded the Latin Dynasty of emperors in Constantinople during the Fourth Crusade, and Godfrey of Bouillon, a Belgian, took Jerusalem during the First Crusade.

## Political Consolidation and Spanish Rule
The territorial concentration of the provinces of the Netherlands was initiated by the dukes of Burgundy. By 1515, Archduke Charles (later Emperor Charles V) assumed control of the region, which was commonly referred to as the Seventeen Provinces and included Holland and Belgium, as well as the county of Artois. Charles V's reign was marked by continuous strife with France for European hegemony, during which the Netherlands were frequently attacked by the French King Francis I and his allies, including the La Marcks (Lords of Bouillon and Sedan) and the Duke of Gueldre.

Following Charles V, the region came under the rule of Philip II. The administration of the Netherlands saw a succession of regents: Margaret of Austria served from 1507 to 1530, followed by Maria of Hungary from 1530 to 1555. From 1559 to 1567, Margaret of Austria (the natural daughter of Charles V and Margaret van Ghent) served as regent.

## The Revolt of the Netherlands
The break from absolutist monarchy in the Netherlands was characterized as a national and religious affair. Discontent rose against Spanish despotism and the Inquisition. While Margaret of Austria held the position of regent, Philip II maintained an arbitrary course and left her with only nominal authority. 

Opposition to the policies of Cardinal Granvella was led by three great nobles: the prince of Orange, Count Egmont, and Philip de Montmorency, Count of Horn. Horn was a man of great possessions in the Netherlands who had served as knight of the Golden Fleece, admiral of Flanders, and stadtholder of Gelderland. These nobles resisted the introduction of Spanish despotic rule and the Spanish Inquisition.

In response, Philip II replaced Margaret with the duke of Alva, who entered the Netherlands with a veteran army to crush opposition. While the prince of Orange fled, Egmont and Horn remained. They were tried as traitors and executed on June 5, 1568, in the square before the town hall at Brussels. Eventually, Alexander Farnese, son of Margaret of Austria, served as governor-general of the Netherlands.

## Diplomatic Treaties and the Barrier
In the early eighteenth century, the states-general of the United Netherlands entered into several "Barrier Treaties" with Great Britain to secure Holland against French aggression. 

The first treaty, signed October 29, 1709, saw the United Netherlands guarantee the Protestant succession in England for the house of Hanover. In return, Great Britain undertook to procure a "barrier" of towns for the Dutch, including the citadel of Ghent, Ypres, Lille, Namur, Tournai, Maubeuge, Charleroi, Valenciennes, Conde, Halle, Damme, Dendermond, Nieuport, and Furnes. 

A second treaty on January 29, 1713, reduced these strong places to Menin, Tournai, Ypres, Mons, Charleroi, Furnes, the fort of Knocke, and the citadel of Ghent, along with certain fortresses near Bruges and Ghent. This was included in the treaty of Rastatt (March 7, 1714). A third Barrier Treaty followed in November 1715.
