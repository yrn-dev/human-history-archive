# The Bahrein Islands

The Bahrein islands are a group of islands located in the Persian Gulf, situated approximately 20 miles east of the coast of El Hasa and slightly south of the port of El Katif. The largest island in the group is Bahrein, which measures about 27 miles from north to south and 10 miles in width. It is characterized as a low, flat area of sandy waste featuring luxuriously beautiful palm groves and cultivated oases. A rocky hill known as Jebel Dukhan, or the "mountain of the mist," rises to a height of 400 feet in the center of the island.

The other islands in the group are of coral formation. Moharek is the next largest, measuring about 5 miles long by 1/2 mile in breadth and located 1 mile north of Bahrein. Other islands in the group include Sitrah (4 miles long), as well as Nebbi, Saleh, Sayeh, Khasifeh, and Arad (3/4 mile long), the latter of which is the only minor island to retain its classical name.

## Governance and History

The present Sheik of Bahrein, who resides primarily at Moharek, belongs to the family of El Kalifa. This ruling race held great possessions on the mainland before being driven away by the Turks around 1850. 

The islands have a history of foreign contention and occupation. The Portuguese took possession of the islands in 1507 but were driven out by Shah Abbas in 1622. Following this, the islands were contested by Arabs and Persians until 1784, when the Arabian tribe of the Athubis became their masters. In 1867, the Persians threatened Bahrein, and in 1875, the Turks seized it. In both instances, British interference successfully maintained Arab rule, and the islands currently exist under British protection.

## Geography and Environment

The climate of the islands is mild and humid, though it is considered rather unhealthy. The soil is generally fertile, supporting the production of fruits—specifically high-quality citrons—as well as pot herbs and rice. Water is abundant. On Moharek, the water supply is unique, derived from springs that burst through beds below sea level with enough force to remain fresh despite the surrounding salt water.

The landscape is dotted with approximately fifty villages, most of which are walled for protection. Each village possesses its own cultivation and date groves. To the south-west of the palm belts on the northern coast of Bahrein lies a wide sandy plain containing gigantic earth mounds or tumuli. These mounds consist of two-chambered sepulchres built from huge limestone slabs; examinations by Durand, Bent, and others have revealed relics of Phoenician design within them. There is significant belief that the Puni, or Phoenicians, emigrated northwards to the Mediterranean from these islands.

## Economy and Population

The inhabitants of the islands are a mixed race of Persian, Omanite, and Arab blood. They are described as small and slender in physical appearance, possessing intelligence and great activity. They are recognized throughout the ports of the Persian Gulf for their industrial and commercial abilities.

The economy is heavily reliant on the sea. Fish and sea-weed are the staple foods for the islanders, and various kinds of fish are cheap in the markets. The sea surrounding the islands is very shallow, which limits access to native craft and results in a harbor closely shut in by reefs.

Bahrein has served as the center of the Persian Gulf's pearl fishing industry. Currently, about 400 boats are employed in the fisheries, and each boat pays a tax to the Sheik. The annual value of pearl exports from Linja is estimated between L30,000 and L35,000.

The capital town is Manameh, a narrow, straggling town with approximately 8,000 inhabitants, most of whom belong to the Wahabi sect. Manameh is located at the northernmost point of the island, overlooking the narrow strait that leads to Moharek.
