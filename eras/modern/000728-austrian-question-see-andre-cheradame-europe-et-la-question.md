# The Austrian Question

The "Austrian question" refers to the complex set of political, racial, and diplomatic challenges facing the Habsburg monarchy and its relationship with the rest of Europe. This issue encompassed the internal stability of the empire, the aspirations of the various nationalities under its crown, and the broader European balance of power.

## Metternich and the Policy of Stability
During the early 19th century, the Habsburg monarchy was characterized as a "rotten structure" that survived primarily due to the apathy of the populations it oppressed. Because the empire's system was considered artificial and unsound, its foreign policy was dedicated to preventing any movements that might threaten its existence. 

Prince Metternich imposed a system of "stability" upon Europe for thirty years to protect the monarchy from the triumph of national aspirations or Liberal principles. This policy manifested in several ways:
* **Germany:** The Vienna Final Act and the Carlsbad Decrees were implemented to keep Germany quiet to ensure the "sleep of Austria" was not disturbed. Metternich encouraged Frederick William III to reject a popular constitution, believing such a grant would be fatal to the Prussian monarchy.
* **International Diplomacy:** The Troppau Protocol served as a cover for Austrian aggressions pursued for purely Austrian ends.
* **The Eastern Question:** Austria provided moral support to the "legitimate" authority of the sultan over "rebel" Greeks to maintain the integrity of Turkey.

## The Revolutions of 1848 and National Conflict
The tensions within the empire reached a breaking point in February 1848, triggered by the fall of Louis Philippe in France. On March 3, Kossuth delivered a speech in the diet at Pressburg, declaring the war of Hungarian Liberalism against the Austrian system, which he described as a "charnel-house" emitting "pestilential air."

Kossuth envisioned a liberated Hungary as the center of freedom for all races under the Austrian crown, proposing a "fraternization of the Austrian peoples." However, this vision clashed with the role of "Germanism." Previously, Germanism had functioned as an "unnational mediating" element among contradictory racial tendencies. As the ideal of German unity grew, its center shifted beyond the boundaries of the Austrian monarchy, creating an antagonism between the traditional German ascendancy and the aspirations of other races. This conflict ultimately led to the failure of the revolution in both Austria and Germany.

## Territorial and European Relations
The Austrian question also extended to the empire's territories and its interactions with other great powers. In Belgium, the Habsburgs claimed to be the natural heirs of the Spanish Habsburgs. Under Charles VI, the Treaty of the Barriers established that Austrian rule was subject to restrictions, including the recognition of the rights of cities and states and the predominance of the Catholic church. This limited sway was required by the theory of the European balance of power, as a strong, universal monarchy was no longer possible in Europe.

Later, the "République des États Belgiques unis" was encouraged by England, Holland, and Prussia to weaken Austria and hinder its policy of extension toward Constantinople in eastern Europe. However, these powers eventually prioritized the balance of power over Belgian independence, leading to the Treaty of the Hague (1790), which restored Austrian rule.

## Diplomatic and Scholarly Context
The "Austrian question" has been the subject of extensive study at the turn of the 20th century. Key developments were analyzed by Andre Cheradame in *L'Europe et la question d'Autriche au seuil du XX^e siecle* (1901) and *L'Allemagne, la France et la question d'Autriche* (1902). Other perspectives include Rene Henry's work on Austria-Hungary and the "question d'orient" (1903) and the analysis provided by "Scotus Viator" regarding the future of Austria-Hungary (1907).

Additionally, the "racial question" regarding languages and race in Austria has been documented in legal terms by Josef Ulbrith and Ernst Mischler in the *Oesterr. Staatswoerterbuch*.
