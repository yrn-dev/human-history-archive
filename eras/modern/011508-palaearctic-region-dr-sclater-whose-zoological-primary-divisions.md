# Palaearctic Region of Dr. Sclater

The Palaearctic region is one of the primary zoological divisions of the earth first proposed by Dr. P.L. Sclater. Sclater's system of dividing the world into a few great regions was based primarily on the distribution of birds, specifically the mass of arboreal birds known at the time as the order Insessores or Passeres (which includes the orders Cuculiformes, Coraciiformes, and Passeriformes). Sclater excluded groups of birds with universal occurrence to establish these boundaries.

## Geographic Scope and Boundaries
The Palaearctic region broadly encompasses Europe and Asia, with the exception of China and India. Specifically, it includes the northern portion of Asia as far south as the Himalaya, as well as the strip of Africa located north of the Atlas mountains. 

Dr. Sclater grouped the Palaearctic together with the Ethiopian, Indian, and Australian regions to form the "Old World" (Palaeogaea). In contrast, the Nearctic and Neotropical regions were grouped as the "New World" (Neogaea).

## Zoological Characteristics and Avifauna
The propriety of treating this vast tract as a single zoological region is widely accepted by naturalists. While it is not as homogeneous as the Nearctic area, the bulk of its birds show a lack of striking differences even at extreme points. For example, there is an "astonishing similarity" between the birds of western Europe and those of Japan. While some families and generic forms are unique to one or the other, many species are repetitions of one another with slight modifications in plumage. Examples include the hedge-sparrow and redbreast, as well as the relationship between *Cyanopica cooki* (found in Spain and Portugal) and *C. cyana* (found in Japan and Amoorland).

According to Newton, the Palaearctic contains 67 families and 323 genera of land birds. Only one family of land birds, the *Panuridae* (represented by the bearded tit-mouse, *Panurus biarmicus*), is considered peculiar to the region.

## Relations to Other Regions
The Palaearctic shares a strong affinity with other regions, particularly the Nearctic. Of the 323 Palaearctic genera, 128 are common to the Nearctic subregion. This relationship is so close that the term "Holarctic region" is generally accepted to describe the combined unit of the Nearctic and Palaearctic areas. T.H. Huxley further categorized these, along with the Indian and Ethiopian regions, under the term "Arctogaea."

The Palaearctic also shares genera with the Ethiopian and Indian regions:
* 51 genera are native to both the Ethiopian and Indian regions.
* 18 genera are common to the Ethiopian region but not the Indian.
* 71 genera are common to the Indian region but not the Ethiopian.

Conversely, the Palaearctic has extremely few points of resemblance to the Neotropical and Australian regions.

## Mammalian and Ichthyological Distinctions
In terms of mammals, the Palaearctic is distinct from the Indian region. The Indian region's mammal fauna is more highly developed, featuring peculiar genera of Quadrumana (such as *Simia*, *Hylobates*, and *Semnopithecus*) and the Indian elephant. While the tiger is characteristic of the Indian region, it has wandered north into the Palaearctic division of Asia.

In ichthyology, the Europaeo-Asiatic or Palaearctic region is characterized by the abundance of *Barbus* and *Cobitinae*, and is specifically noted for the absence of osseous Ganoidei.

## Taxonomic Revisions
Following Sclater's initial work, which was practically adopted by A.R. Wallace, T.H. Huxley introduced a system of co- and sub-ordination. Under this revised framework, some of Sclater's "regions" were redesignated as subregions; consequently, the Palaearctic is often referred to as the Palaearctic Subregion.
