# Suppression and Overthrow in History

The archive's records document various instances of suppression, ranging from the elimination of religious liturgical practices and political conspiracies to the military defeat of city-states and the eradication of piracy.

## Liturgical Revision and Suppression in France
During the 17th and 18th centuries, a movement of revision emerged in France that modified approximately half of the country's Breviaries. This movement was driven historically by Louis Sébastien le Nain de Tillemont and Jean de Launoy (1603-1678), known as "le dénicheur des saints," who demonstrated the falsity of many lives of the saints. Theologically, the movement was influenced by the Port Royal school, which emphasized communion with God over the invocation of saints.

These reforms led to the adoption of a rule requiring all responses and antiphons to use the exact words of Scripture, effectively removing appeals to created beings. The services were shortened and simplified, and the weekly use of the entire Psalter was restored. Examples of these reformed texts include the 1680 Paris Breviary by Archbishop François de Harlay (1625-1695) and the 1736 version by Archbishop Charles Gaspard Guillaume de Vintimille (1655-1746).

However, during the pontificate of Pius IX, an Ultramontane movement arose to oppose these Breviaries. This movement was inaugurated by Montalembert and championed by Louis François Veuillot of the *Univers* and Dom Gueranger, a Benedictine monk and abbot of Solesmes. This movement succeeded in suppressing the reformed Breviaries everywhere; the diocese of Orleans was the last to surrender in 1875.

## Political Suppressions in Revolutionary France
In the late 18th century, France experienced several violent political shifts. In the summer of 1794, Robespierre was overthrown and guillotined, succeeded by a Directory of five men. While the Directory's armies exported revolutionary zeal to north Italy, south Germany, Switzerland, Belgium, and Holland—expelling kings and establishing republics—they also looted the treasures of these liberated peoples to address French financial difficulties.

Later, a *coup* was successfully executed against other factions. Following this, the *Tribun* ceased publication on April 24. Despite attempts by Lebois in the *Ami du peuple* to incite a military revolt, and tentative riots on Fructidor 10 and 11 (August 27 and 28) to rescue prisoners being moved from Paris, these efforts were easily suppressed. A further attempt by several hundred Jacobins to rouse soldiers at Grenelle on September 7 also failed. This period culminated in the trial at Vendome of the socialist Babeuf and his accomplices; Babeuf and Darthe were executed on Prairial 8, 1797.

## Military Conquests and the Fall of Cities
The archive records the violent suppression of cities and regimes in antiquity and the Middle Ages. In 1333, the city of Kamakura fell on July 5. After the Hojo had equipped an army of reportedly one hundred thousand men, the Imperialist forces under Yoshisada launched a three-pronged attack. Yoshisada's western column succeeded by crossing sands during a low tide to bypass a steep cliff, carrying the city by storm and setting fire to buildings. The Hojo troops were slaughtered, and Takatoki committed suicide along with his family and approximately eight hundred officers and men.

In Syracuse, a fleet sent by Dionysius under the command of Nypsius arrived with money and corn for the besieged. Although the Syracusans initially won a sea-fight, they abandoned discipline for reckless drinking and feasting. Nypsius took advantage of this confusion to attack the Syracusan wall of investment. His attack succeeded, and his foreign mercenaries sacked the city, slaughtering the men and forcing women and children into the citadel.

## Maritime Suppression and Piracy
In 270 B.C., the Greek king of Syracuse, Hiero, was forced to take measures to suppress a band of mercenaries who had seized Messina in 289 B.C. and were raiding Syracusan trade. Carthage, which viewed the suppression of piracy as its task by habit and tradition and was the only capable guardian of Mediterranean sea law after the destruction of Tyre, assisted Hiero by placing a garrison at Messina. This action eventually led to Roman intervention when the pirates of Messina appealed to Rome.
