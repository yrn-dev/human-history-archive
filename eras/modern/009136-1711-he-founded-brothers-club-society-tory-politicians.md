# The Brothers' Club

The Brothers' Club was a society established in 1711. It was founded by St John and consisted of a membership composed of men of letters and Tory politicians.

## Context of Foundation
The club was founded during a period of significant political activity involving St John, who had a long career in the English parliament. St John first entered parliament in 1701 representing the family borough of Wootton Bassett in Wiltshire. A declared Tory, he aligned himself with Harley (later Lord Oxford), whom he referred to as "dear master." St John became known for his eloquence in debate, which allowed him to gain an extraordinary ascendancy over the House of Commons and eclipse his schoolfellow, Walpole.

During his early parliamentary career, St John was involved in several key legislative and political actions:
* In May 1701, he was in charge of the bill to secure the Protestant succession.
* He participated in the impeachment of Whig lords regarding their conduct with the Partition treaties.
* He opposed the oath abjuring the Pretender.
* In March 1702, he was chosen as a commissioner for taking public accounts.

Following the accession of Queen Anne, St John supported bills against occasional conformity in 1702 and 1704 and played a leading role in disputes between the two Houses of Parliament.

## Political Career of St John
St John's political trajectory saw him move in and out of office. In 1704, he became secretary at war alongside Harley, a position that brought him into a relationship with Marlborough, who treated him with paternal partiality. However, St John left office with Harley in 1708 following the failure of Harley's intrigue. He retired to the country until 1710, when he returned to serve as secretary of state and a privy councillor in Harley's new ministry, representing Berkshire in parliament. During this time, he supported a bill requiring a real property qualification for those holding a seat in parliament.

In the year he founded the Brothers' Club (1711), St John also promoted two expeditions—one to Canada and one to the West Indies—both of which failed. In 1712, he authored a bill that taxed newspapers.

## The Peace with France
A primary focus of the government during this era was establishing peace with France. The ministry, supported by the people, parliament, and the queen, sought to terminate hostilities. This design was motivated by the belief that the war was not being continued for the national interests of England, citing the fact that the Whigs had refused to grant terms in 1706 and 1709, even when Louis XIV offered to yield every point the allies claimed to be fighting for. Despite this, St John was induced to enter into agreements contrary to the most solemn obligations due to the diverse aims of the allies.

## Contemporary Political Clubs
The Brothers' Club existed alongside other factional and social organizations in London. The Kit-Cat Club, founded around 1703, served as a gathering for Whig politicians, painters, men of letters, and wits. Its members included Sir Robert Walpole, the duke of Marlborough, and writers such as Addison and Steele. The club met at various locations, including the Fountain tavern in the Strand and Barn Elms.

In contrast to these societies, the Green Ribbon Club served as the headquarters for the Whig opposition to the court. It functioned as both an intelligence department and a debating society where members discussed parliamentary questions over ale and tobacco. The Green Ribbon Club was associated with the promotion of sedition, conspiracy, and the Exclusion Bill, and its members were known to spread slander against the Tories and the court.
