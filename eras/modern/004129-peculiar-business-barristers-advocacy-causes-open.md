# Barristers

In England and Ireland, a barrister is a member of the highest class of lawyers, characterized by their exclusive audience in all superior courts. The term is derived from the "bar" found in law courts. In England, every barrister must belong to one of the four ancient societies known as the Inns of Court: Gray's Inn, Lincoln's Inn, the Middle Temple, and the Inner Temple. In Ireland, they must be members of the King's Inns. These English societies originated in the 13th century, a development attributed to the Magna Carta's clause fixing the Common Pleas at Westminster, which concentrated law professors in London.

## Professional Duties and Specializations
The primary business of barristers is the advocacy of causes in open court. However, in England, their work extends to other areas. They serve as the chief conveyancers and typically draft pleadings, which are the counter statements made by parties before joining an issue. While conveyancing and special pleading were once handled by a separate class of licensed practitioners, these tasks are now part of the ordinary work of a junior barrister.

The profession is divided into several specialized branches, most notably between those practicing in the courts of common law and those practicing in chancery. Although the Judicature Acts of 1873 and 1875 aimed to fuse law and equity to break down this distinction, the separation largely remains. Common law counsel attach themselves to specific circuits in England and generally cannot practice elsewhere except under special conditions. In chancery, king's counsel typically restrict their practice to specific courts of the chancery division. Other areas of business include parliamentary committees, the privy council, and the courts of admiralty, divorce, and probate.

## Hierarchy and Rank
The profession is headed by the solicitor-general and the attorney-general, who hold office at the will of the current government. Below them is the higher rank of queen's or king's counsel. These senior barristers lead in court and provide opinions on submitted cases, but they do not admit pupils to their chambers, nor do they accept pleading or conveyancing work. Ordinary practitioners are sometimes technically referred to as "utter barristers." Precedence among both outer barristers and king's counsel is determined by seniority. The former order of serjeants-at-law, who once ranked after king's counsel, is now extinct.

## Education and Admission
Admission to the profession begins with membership in an Inn of Court, which requires the payment of fees and passing an elementary examination or providing evidence of a university public examination. To be "called to the bar," a student must pass further examinations and "keep" twelve terms (four per year). A term is kept by dining in hall six times, or three times for university students. This dining requirement is a relic of an older system where a certificate from a barrister regarding a student's twelve months of reading in chambers was the only requirement.

Students typically read for a year or more as pupils in the chambers of a practicing barrister, though in the 18th century it became common to read with attorneys, special pleaders, equity draftsmen, or conveyancers. 

The profession is open to most, with specific restrictions:
* Individuals connected with the law in an inferior capacity or chartered/professional accountants cannot enter an Inn until they have entirely ceased to practice in that capacity.
* Some Inns prohibit members from engaging in trade.
* Applicants must declare they are not in holy orders and have not performed clerical functions or held clerical preferment in the preceding year.

Certain exceptions exist for experienced practitioners: solicitors of at least five years' standing may be called to the bar without keeping terms if they pass the necessary examinations. Conversely, a barrister of five years' standing may become a solicitor upon passing the final solicitor's examination. Irish barristers or barristers from colonies where the professions remain distinct may be called to the English bar after keeping three terms without further examination.

## Legal Status and Regulations
Barristers hold the rank of esquires and enjoy specific privileges, including exemption from serving on juries while in active practice and immunity from arrest while on circuit or attending superior courts. Additionally, "revising barristers"—those with at least seven years' standing—may be appointed to revise parliamentary voter lists.

The benchers of the Inns of Court maintain strict disciplinary authority. They may reject applicants for membership without assigning a cause, refuse to call a student to the bar for sufficient reason, or "dis-bar" (expel) barristers and benchers for misconduct, whether professional or otherwise.

Regarding financial and professional conduct:
* Barristers cannot maintain actions for their fees, as these are viewed as gratuities.
* They are prohibited from entering contracts for payment with clients regarding litigation.
* Except in criminal cases—where a barrister may be engaged directly via a fee given in open court—they cannot undertake a case without a solicitor's intervention.

In court, counsel is not liable for statements made regarding a cause if those statements were suggested in the client's instructions, even if they are groundless or reflect poorly on another's character. However, counsel is liable for untruths of their own invention or instructions that are impertinent to the matter. They may also be punished for contempt by the judge or by their inn's benchers.

## Relation to the Judiciary
The rank of barrister is a necessary qualification for nearly all judicial offices, including magistracies of minor rank (such as county court judgeships and recorderships) and judgeships in the superior courts of equity and law in England and its colonies. This creates a harmony of interest and opinion between the judicial bench and the legal profession.
