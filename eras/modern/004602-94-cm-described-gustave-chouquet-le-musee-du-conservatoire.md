# The Phagotus and Related Wind Instruments

The phagotus is a rare musical instrument of which only three examples are known to exist. These instruments are identified by a trefoil used as the maker's mark. Of these three, one is located in Brussels and two are held in the museum of the Conservatoire in Paris.

## The Paris and Brussels Instruments
The two phagotuses preserved in Paris differ in size, measuring 73 cm and 94 cm respectively. These specific instruments are described in the work *Le Musee du Conservatoire National de Musique--Catalogue descriptif et raisonne* (1884) by Gustave Chouquet, appearing as Nos. 409 and 410 on page 106.

The third known instrument is the smallest of the group and is tuned in F. This instrument is located in Brussels and is described in the *Catalogue* by Victor Charles Mahillon on page 201 (No. 189). Additionally, this smaller instrument was reproduced in the *Catalogue of the Musical Instruments exhibited at the Royal Military Exhibition, London*, 1890, by Capt. C. R. Day.

## Documentation and Illustrations
Various scholars and catalogs have provided further details on the phagotus. Count L. F. Valdrighi provided an Italian translation of the instrument's description in *Musurgiana*, No. 4 (1881), in a section titled "Il Phagotus di Afranio." While Valdrighi's account did not include illustrations, W. J. von Wasielewski provided visual representations of the phagotus in *Gesch. d. Instrumentalmusik im XVI. Jahrh.* (1878), specifically on plates v and vi.

## Related Wind Instrument Research
The study of wind instruments in the archive's collections extends to other historical and technical analyses. Victor Charles Mahillon has provided descriptive and analytical catalogs for the instrumental museum of the Conservatoire Royal de Musique in Brussels, including a description of the modern bassoon (vol. ii, pp. 275-276, No. 999). 

Other research into wind instruments includes:
* **Acoustics:** Mahillon has conducted practical demonstrations regarding conical pipes, arguing that given a certain calibre, a conical pipe must overblow the harmonics of an open pipe, regardless of how the sound is produced. This contrasts with the views of Chladni, who focused on standard rectangular and cylindrical organ-pipes.
* **Ancient Instruments:** The museums of the conservatoires in Brussels and Paris, as well as the Collection Kraus in Florence, possess facsimiles of Pompeian buccinas. Furthermore, the British Museum's department of Greek and Roman antiquities holds two bronze Etruscan cornua (No. 2734) that are semicircular in shape and measure 2 ft. 1 in. and 2 ft. 2 in. from end to end.
* **Evolutionary Evidence:** The evolution of three specific wind instruments is evidenced by the various manuscript and printed editions of *De re militari* (iii. 5) from the middle ages.
