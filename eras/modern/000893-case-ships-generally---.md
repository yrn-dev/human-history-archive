# Ships Generally

The history and administration of ships encompass a wide range of applications, from ancient naval warfare and the logistics of invasion to the complex legal and financial regulations governing maritime accidents and insurance.

## Ancient Naval Warfare and Logistics
In ancient Greece, the command and equipment of warships were often a blend of state provision and private initiative. The state provided the trireme's essential oars and tackle, as well as the regular pay for the crew. However, the trierarchs—wealthy citizens who took on the duty of commanding a ship of war—often spent their own funds to ensure their ships were in the most finished state of equipment. This additional outlay was driven by custom and general opinion rather than law.

Naval operations in this era involved diverse tactics. During the siege of Tyre, Alexander the Moor moored ships against the city walls, equipping them with rams and towers. The Tyrians countered these flotillas with fire-ships and raids. In one such engagement, the Tyrians lost several ships to ramming, including a galley with four banks of oars and another with five. Eventually, Macedonian forces used their ships as platforms to clamber up debris and storm the city.

In other Athenian naval contexts, the division of duty was critical. During one operation, generals assigned forty-eight triremes and specific trierarchs, including Thrasybulus and Theramenês, the task of rescuing drowning men. While the generals led the main fleet to relieve Mitylênê and attack Eteonikus, the trierarchs were responsible for the rescue operation. Disputes later arose regarding whether a storm had rendered such rescues impossible.

## Maritime Law and General Average (G.A.)
Under English law and various maritime rules, the concept of General Average (G.A.) governs how losses and expenses are shared following a maritime sacrifice. Generally, expenses incurred after a ship and its cargo have reached a port of refuge are not treated as G.A. if the port was entered simply to avoid danger, such as a hostile cruiser or a storm. In such cases, subsequent expenditures are viewed as the shipowner's obligation to complete the voyage.

However, if the entry into port was necessitated by a G.A. sacrifice—such as the cutting away of masts—the expenses for unloading, storing, reloading cargo, and repairing the damage are treated as consequences of that sacrifice and are subject to contribution. 

The issue of fire on board presents specific legal complexities:
* **Public Authority vs. Master:** In the United States, the Supreme Court ruled in *Ralli v. Troup* (1894) that if a public authority scuttles a ship to extinguish a fire in port, the resulting damage is not a G.A. contribution because the act was compulsory and not performed by someone charged with the safety of the adventure.
* **Port of Refuge:** Conversely, in the English case of *Papayanni v. Grampian S.S. Co.*, the scuttling of a ship at a port of refuge in Algeria by the captain of the port was held to be a G.A. act, as it was done in the interest of the ship and cargo.

Regarding crew costs, Rule XI allows for the wages and maintenance of the master, officers, and crew during the detention of a ship for repairs. While this is a concession to shipowners, English common law generally does not allow these costs if the detention is to repair accidental damage. Furthermore, these rules do not protect shippers; for example, a shipper of cattle cannot claim G.A. for the extra fodder or wages for cattlemen during a stay at a repairing port.

## Ship Maintenance and Valuation
For the purpose of calculating deductions following an accident, the age of the ship is generally used rather than the age of specific parts. For wooden or composite ships under one year old from the date of original register, no "new for old" deduction is made. After one year, a deduction of one-third is typically applied, with specific exceptions:
* **Anchors:** Allowed in full.
* **Chain cables:** Subject to a deduction of one-sixth.
* **Metal sheathing:** Allowed in full for the cost of a weight equal to the gross weight stripped off, minus the proceeds of the old metal.
* **Nails, felt, and labour metalling:** Subject to a one-third deduction.

Regardless of ship type, the expenses for graving dock dues (including removals, cartages, and use of stages) and the labor for straightening and replacing bent ironwork are allowed in full. Additionally, no deduction is made for provisions and stores that have not been in use. Painting of the bottom is only allowed if it occurred within six months prior to the accident.

## Shipping in Global Conflict
Shipping availability is a primary limiting factor in military invasions. In the context of war with Germany, the ability to launch an oversea attack is governed by the amount of suitable shipping available instantly at the correct ports, and subsequently what becomes available after twenty-four hours. Once a war has officially begun, the primary difficulty shifts from finding ships and men to the logistics of transporting them across the sea.

Historically, the use of privateer ships has also played a role in naval conflict. During the American Civil War, the English government permitted Confederate agents to launch and man three swift privateer ships, most notably the *Alabama*, which were used to chase United States shipping.

## Technical Terminology
A cable is defined as a large rope or chain used generally with ships. The term is also applied by analogy to other engineering attachments, including submarine electric cables used for transmitting telegraphic messages.
