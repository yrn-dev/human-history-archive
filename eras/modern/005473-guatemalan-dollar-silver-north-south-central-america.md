# The Guatemalan Dollar and American Silver

## The Guatemalan Dollar and Monetary Transition
In the history of currency in British Honduras, the Guatemalan dollar served as a standard of exchange until the late 19th century. On October 14, 1894, a significant monetary shift occurred when the American gold dollar was adopted as the standard coin to replace the Guatemalan dollar. 

Following this adoption, the silver of Central, South, and North America ceased to be legal tender in the region. The current monetary system includes government notes issued in denominations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 50, and 100 dollars. Additionally, the local currency consists of bronze pieces valued at one cent and silver pieces valued at 5, 10, 25, and 50 cents. The British half sovereign and sovereign also maintain status as legal tender.

## Origins and Nature of the Dollar
The term "dollar" is a modified version of *thaler*, which itself is a shortened form of *Joachimsthaler*. This name originated from a silver mine discovered in 1516 in Joachimsthal (Joachim's dale), a mining district in Bohemia. The count of Schlitz appropriated the mine and struck silver coins beginning in 1518, which featured an effigy of St Joachim. These coins were intended to be the silver equivalent of the gold gulden used in Germany since the 14th century.

The *Joachimsthaler* was also known as the *Schlickenthaler*. In English, the word "dollar" was first applied to this silver thaler, which circulated in Germany at various values from the 16th century onward. Later, from 1857 to 1873, the thaler served as the unit of the German monetary union before being replaced by the mark.

## Silver Currency in the Americas
Silver coins, specifically the Spanish piece-of-eight (*reals*), were commonly referred to as dollars. These coins circulated widely throughout the Spanish-American colonies and were used extensively in the British North American colonies. Because the British-American colonies utilized a reckoning system of pounds, shillings, and pence, the use of these coins created inconveniences; these were partially mitigated by the use of a tariff list to rate the circulating gold and silver coins.

In other regions, silver remains a central component of the monetary unit. In Honduras, for example, the monetary unit is the silver *peso* or dollar, consisting of 100 cents. This silver coin weighs 25 grammes and is .900 fine, with a value of approximately 1s. 8d. Honduras utilizes a metallic currency including 1 peso silver pieces, as well as silver coins of 5, 10, 20, 25, and 50 cents.

## Regional Economic Context
The movement of specie is a noted component of trade in certain American ports. In British Honduras, between 1901 and 1905, specie was listed as one of the principal imports, alongside hardware, groceries, spirits, beer, wine, and cotton goods. During this same period, the tonnage of vessels at these ports rose from 300,000 to 496,465, and exports—which included tortoiseshell, rum, timber, sponges, turtles, and fruit—increased from £285,500 to £377,623.
