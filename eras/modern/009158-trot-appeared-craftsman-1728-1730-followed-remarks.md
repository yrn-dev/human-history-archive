# Trot and Trotting

The term "trot" refers to a specific gait of horses, most notably associated with the Hackney breed and the specialized sport of trotting races. In the context of horse movement, the "action" of a Hackney during a trot is characterized by a knee that is well advanced and elevated, with the leg well extended before the foot is placed on the ground. This movement is supported by hock action, which provides the necessary propelling power.

## The Trotting Turf and Racing
Historically, the "trotting turf" was viewed poorly, and prior to 1866, purses for trotters were small, with match races being more common. This changed in 1866 when an association in Buffalo, N.Y., offered an unprecedented $10,500 for a four-day trotting meeting. The success of this event led other cities to follow suit, resulting in a significant increase in prize money; by 1872, prizes at Buffalo reached $70,000. In the United States and Canada, annual offerings have since reached $1,500,000.

The National Trotting Association was established in 1869 and published an elaborate code of rules. Unlike running races, where time is of less consequence, the time test is the supreme measure in trotting races. The horse with the fastest one-mile record in harness is regarded as the king or queen of the trotting turf.

## Notable Trotters and Records
Records for the one-mile trot have been reduced over time through improved breeding and methods. Early records include:
* **Lady Suffolk (1843):** 2 m. 26(1/2) s.
* **Tacony (1853):** 2 m. 25(1/2) s. (achieved under saddle).
* **Flora Temple (1856):** 2 m. 24(1/2) s. (in harness), later reducing the record to 2 m. 19(3/4) s. in 1859.
* **Dexter (1867):** 2 m. 17(1/4) s.
* **Goldsmith Maid (1871–1874):** A mare by Alexander's Abdallah (son of Rysdyk's Hambletonian). She began trotting in 1866 and retired in 1878 at age twenty-one with winnings over $200,000. Her mile record progressed from 2 m. 17 s. in 1871 down to 2 m. 14 s. in 1874.
* **Rarus (1878):** 2 m. 13(1/4) s.
* **St Julien (1879):** A bay gelding by Volunteer (son of Rysdyk's Hambletonian), who trotted a mile in California in 2 m. 12(3/4) s.

Further reductions in time were achieved by Maud S. (2 m. 8(3/4) s. in 1885), Sunol (2 m. 8(1/4) s. in 1891), Nancy Hanks (2 m. 4 s. in 1892), Alix (2 m. 3(3/4) s. in 1894), Cresceus (2 m. 2(1/4) s. in 1901), and Lou Dillon (1 m. 58(1/2) s. in 1905).

## Training and Equipment
The improvement in times is attributed to breeding and refined methods. Older training rules, observed around 1870, were severe and often overtaxed the animal's strength, such as the practice of denying a horse water the night before a race. A critical technical aspect of trotting is the use of toe-weights. These are fastened to the horses' feet to equalize their action, and proper shoeing can improve a horse's time by several seconds.

## The Hackney and Related Terms
The term "Nag," used for a trotting or active riding horse, comes from the Anglo-Saxon *hnegan* (to neigh). The word "Hackney" is derived from the Norman *haquenee* or *hacquenee*, which comes from the Latin *equus*. Both terms are used synonymously. Records from the 14th century frequently mention trotters and hackneys in farm accounts.

The modern trotting hackney can be traced back to a stallion foaled around 1755, known as the Shales, Shields, or Schales horse. Breeding is primarily pursued in the counties of York, Lincoln, Huntingdon, Cambridge, and Norfolk. The ideal Hackney type is a powerfully built, short-legged horse (typically 15 hands 2 in. to 15 hands 3(1/2) in.) with a strong back, powerful loins, and an intelligent head. While the high action of the Hackney is uncomfortable for riding, using hackney sires on half-bred mares (the offspring of trotting mares and thoroughbred stallions) has sometimes yielded excellent results.
