# BARBEYRAC, JEAN (1674-1744)

Jean Barbeyrac was a French jurist born on March 15, 1674, in Beziers, located in Lower Languedoc. He was the nephew of Charles Barbeyrac, who was a distinguished physician in Montpellier.

## Early Life and Academic Career
Following the revocation of the edict of Nantes, Barbeyrac moved to Switzerland with his family. While there, he pursued the study of jurisprudence. His early professional path included time spent in Frankfort-on-Main and Geneva. He subsequently served as a professor of belles-lettres at the French school in Berlin.

In 1711, Barbeyrac was appointed to a professorship of civil law and history at Lausanne. He eventually settled into a position as a professor of public law at Groningen, where he remained until his death on March 3, 1744.

## Legal and Philosophical Contributions
Barbeyrac is most famous for the notes and preface he provided for his translation of Pufendorf's treatise *De Jure Naturae et Gentium*. In terms of fundamental principles, he followed the works of Pufendorf and Locke almost entirely. 

He is noted for his skillful development of the theory of moral obligation, which he attributed to the will or command of God. Additionally, Barbeyrac indicated a distinction between the moral and legal qualities of an action, a concept that was later developed more extensively by Kant and Thomasius.

In the realm of international law, Barbeyrac reduced its principles to those of the law of nature. In doing so, he contested many of the positions established by Grotius. He specifically rejected the idea that property resembles sovereignty in any way. Furthermore, he viewed marriage as a matter of civil contract.

## Published Works and Translations
Barbeyrac was a prolific translator of legal texts. In addition to his work on Pufendorf's *De Jure Naturae et Gentium*, he translated:
* Pufendorf's smaller treatise, *De Officio Hominis et Civis*
* Cumberland's *De Legibus Naturae*
* Grotius's *De Jure Belli et Pacis*

His own original writings include a history of ancient treaties found in the *Supplement au grand corps diplomatique* and a treatise titled *De la morale des peres*. In 1709, he published the *Traite du jeu*, a work in which he defended the morality of games of chance.
