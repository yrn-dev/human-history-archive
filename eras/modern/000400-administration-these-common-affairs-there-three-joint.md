# Administration of Common Affairs in the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy

The administration of common affairs in the Austro-Hungarian monarchy was designed to manage interests affecting both halves of the Dual Monarchy while maintaining the independence of the two separate states, Austria and Hungary. This system was established through a series of legal foundations, including the Pragmatic Sanction of Emperor Charles VI (promulgated April 19, 1713), the diploma of Emperor Francis Joseph I (October 20, 1860), and the *Ausgleich* or "Compromise" concluded on February 8, 1867.

## The Joint Ministries
To manage common affairs, three joint ministries were established. The authority of these ministers was strictly restricted to common affairs; they were prohibited from exercising influence over government matters that affected only one half of the monarchy.

*   **Ministry of Foreign Affairs:** This ministry conducted the international relations of the Dual Monarchy and held the power to conclude international treaties. However, treaties involving commercial matters, changes of territory, or state burdens required the parliamentary assent of both states. This office also held the position of the ministry of the imperial house.
*   **Ministry of War:** This ministry served as the head of administration for all military affairs, with the exception of the Hungarian *Honveds* and the Austrian *Landwehr*, which remained under the administration of the respective states' ministries for national defence. While the minister controlled the common army, the supreme command of the army was vested in the monarch, who possessed the power to take all measures regarding the entire force.
*   **Ministry of Finance:** This ministry was responsible for the finances of common affairs, the administration of the joint state debt, and the preparation of the joint budget. Until 1909, the joint minister of finance also administered the provinces of Bosnia and Herzegovina, except for matters dependent on the minister of war.

## The Delegations and Oversight
The administration of these common institutions followed the principle of dualism, ensuring parity between Hungary and the other territories of the king. Responsibility for the common ministers rested with a *Delegation* consisting of sixty members elected from both the Hungarian and Austrian parliaments.

To prevent the Delegations from overshadowing the national parliaments, they did not sit together. Instead, each met separately and communicated via written documents translated into German or Magyar. A common session occurred only if agreement could not be reached after three exchanges of notes; in such sessions, voting was done in silence by simple majority, and no discussion was permitted.

The Delegations lacked legislative power. For example, while the minister of war controlled the common army, the laws for army recruitment had to be voted on separately in each parliament. Similarly, while the minister of finance presented the common budget to the Delegations, the Delegations could not vote taxes or raise money; the necessary funds had to be provided by the separate parliaments.

## The Common Budget and Finance
The Dual Monarchy maintained a common budget alongside the individual budgets of each state. This joint budget covered expenditures for the army, the ministry of finance, and the conduct of foreign affairs. Revenues for this budget were derived from the joint ministries, the net proceeds of customs, and proportional contributions (the quota) from the two states.

The quota was fixed for several years, typically coinciding with the duration of commercial and customs treaties. The contribution ratios shifted over time:
*   Until 1897: Austria contributed 70% and Hungary 30%.
*   1897 to July 1907: Austria contributed 66-46/49 and Hungary 33-2/49.
*   From 1907: Hungary's contribution was increased to 36.4%.

Additionally, 2% of total charges were debited to Hungary due to the incorporation of the former military frontier. In 1905, the total revenue for the common administration was L20,762,410, with the largest portions coming from proportional contributions (L15,650,448) and customs (L4,780,000). A joint supreme court of accounts was appointed to audit the accounts of the joint ministries.

## Military Structure
The military system, based on the principle of universal and personal obligation to bear arms since 1868, was divided into three components: the common army (*K. und K.*), the *Landsturm* (levy-in-mass), and the special national armies (the Austrian *Landwehr* and Hungarian *Honveds*).

The yearly contingent of recruits was determined by the population based on the last census and voted on by the Austrian and Hungarian parliaments. In 1905, the common army's recruits totaled 103,100 men (59,211 from Austria and 43,889 from Hungary), while 10,000 men were allotted to the Austrian *Landwehr* and 12,500 to the Hungarian *Honveds*. Service terms were generally two years with the colours (three for cavalry), seven or eight in the reserve, and two in the *Landwehr*.

## Terminology and Titles
The principle of Dualism was reflected in the titles of the sovereign and the administration. In 1868, the emperor changed his style to "Emperor of Austria, King of Bohemia, &c., and Apostolic King of Hungary" to signify the separation of the two districts. The realm was referred to as "The Austrian-Hungarian Monarchy" or "The Austrian-Hungarian Realm."

The term "Imperial and Royal" (*Kaiserlich und Koeniglich*) was applied to branches of public service belonging to the common ministries. This was first applied to the diplomatic service, then to the army in 1889, and to the ministry of the imperial house in 1895. In contrast, the Austrian half of the monarchy retained the style *Kaiserlich-Koeniglich* ("K.K."). This distinction is evident in the finance ministries: the *Kaiserlich und Koeniglich* for joint affairs, the *Kaiserlich-Koeniglich* for Austrian affairs, and the *Kiralye* for Hungary.
