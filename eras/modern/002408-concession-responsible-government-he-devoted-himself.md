# Robert Baldwin

Robert Baldwin (1804–1858) was a prominent Canadian statesman born on May 12, 1804, in York, which is now known as Toronto. He was the son of William Warren Baldwin, an Irishman who arrived in Canada in 1798. Although his father was a man of wealth, good family, and a devoted member of the Church of England, he opposed the political and religious oligarchy then leading Canadian affairs and raised Robert in those same principles.

## Early Political Career
Baldwin was called to the Bar in 1825 and entered into a partnership with his father. His entry into politics began in 1829 when he was elected as a member of the parliament of Upper Canada for the town of York. However, he was defeated the following year and spent a period in private life. During the subsequent six years, Baldwin became known as "the man of one idea" due to his constant advocacy for a responsible executive, which he viewed as the sole cure for the economic and political evils of the era.

In 1836, the lieutenant-governor, Sir Francis Bond Head, called Baldwin to the executive council. Baldwin resigned within a month after finding he lacked influence and was forced to support measures he opposed. Despite his identity as a reformer, Baldwin strongly disapproved of the rebellion that took place between 1837 and 1838.

## Pursuit of Responsible Government
Following the union of the two Canadas in 1841, Baldwin served as a member of the executive council under Lord Sydenham, though he soon resigned over the issue of responsible government. In 1842, in connection with Mr. (later Sir) L. H. Lafontaine, Baldwin formed the first Liberal administration. This administration ended the following year after a quarrel with the governor-general, Sir Charles Metcalfe, regarding a question of patronage that Baldwin believed involved the principle of responsible government.

The Liberals returned to power in 1848. Under Lord Elgin, Baldwin and Mr. Lafontaine formed their second administration. During this term, they implemented several significant reforms, including the introduction of an important municipal system into Upper Canada and the removal of the Provincial University from sectarian control.

## Later Career and Resignation
After the concession of responsible government, Baldwin devoted himself to fostering a good understanding between the French-speaking and English-speaking inhabitants of Canada.

Internal conflicts eventually emerged within the Liberal party. In 1849, Baldwin introduced a measure establishing the constitution of the court of chancery of Upper Canada. In 1851, an attempt was made to abolish this court; although the attempt failed, it was supported by a majority of representatives from Upper Canada. Baldwin, possessing a fastidious conscience, interpreted this as a vote of want of confidence and resigned. Additionally, Baldwin found himself unable to approve of the advanced views held by the Radicals, who became known as "Clear Grits."

When seeking re-election in York, Baldwin was defeated after declining to provide a pledge regarding the burning issue of the Clergy Reserves. In 1854, a coalition formed the Liberal-Conservative party. In 1858, this party attempted to recruit Baldwin as a candidate for the upper house, which was elective at the time. Baldwin refused to stand, as he could not approve of the tactics used by the opponents of the advanced reformers, despite having broken with the reformers himself.

## Death and Legacy
Robert Baldwin died on December 9, 1858. He is remembered for the purity and unselfishness of his motives, qualities admitted even by those who most bitterly attacked his measures. Because of his efforts to bridge the divide between the English and French-speaking populations, his memory is held as dear in his native province of Ontario as it is among French Canadians.
