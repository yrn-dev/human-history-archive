# Legislative and Administrative Systems of Barbados and Bermuda

## Legislative Structure
In certain colonies that possess representative institutions but lack responsible government, the legislative process is managed by the governor with the concurrence of the legislative body or bodies. In cases where a second chamber exists that possesses a preponderance of elected representatives, the concurrence of at least one of these bodies is required for laws to be made. Barbados, Bermuda, and the Bahamas are characterized by this structure, possessing two legislative bodies: one that is elected and one that is nominated by the crown. This differs from the systems in Malta and the Leeward Islands, which utilize a single body that is partly nominated and partly elected.

## Administration of Barbados
Barbados is administered by a governor who is assisted by an executive council. The legislative framework consists of a house of assembly comprising 24 members elected via a limited franchise, and a legislative council consisting of 9 nominated members. While the colony has representative institutions, it does not have responsible government. Consequently, the home government appoints public officials, with the exception of the treasurer, and the crown maintains a veto over legislation.

## History and Settlement of Barbados
The name of Barbados may derive from a species of bearded fig-tree or the Spanish word for hanging branches of a vine that strike root in the ground. Early 16th-century maps recorded the name in various forms, including Barnodo, Barnodos, Barbudoso, Bernados, and St Bernardo. The island shows more numerous traces of Carib Indians than any other of the Antilles.

British involvement began in 1605 when the ship "Olive Blossom" touched the island; the crew found it uninhabited and took possession in the name of James I. The first actual settlement occurred in 1625 under the patent of Lord Leigh (later the earl of Marlborough) and the direction of Sir William Courteen. By 1627, a grant was obtained by the earl of Carlisle. In 1628, Charles Wolferstone, a native of Bermuda, was appointed governor, and the present capital was founded following the arrival of sixty-four settlers at Carlisle Bay.

During the English Civil War, Barbados became a refuge for Royalists under Lord Willoughby, who leased the island from the earl of Carlisle. After Willoughby was defeated and exiled, and following the Restoration, the crown took over the government of the island to appease planters. To satisfy the claims of the patentees, a duty of 4-1/2% was imposed on all exports.

## Demographics and Society in Barbados
Barbados is one of the most densely populated areas in the world. In 1901, the population was 195,588 (1,178 per sq. m.), increasing to 196,287 by 1906. A census taken in 1684 under Governor Sir Richard Dutton recorded a population of 20,000. The majority of the population consists of negroes, who outnumber whites by a ratio of 9 to 1.

The inhabitants primarily belong to the Church of England, which is larger than all other denominations combined. The government pays the clergy of all creeds as well as the bishop, as the island serves as a see.

## Education and Development in Barbados
The primary educational establishment is Codrington College, founded via a 1710 bequest of two estates by Colonel Christopher Codrington to the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel. Affiliated with the university of Durham, it trains young men for holy orders. Secondary education is provided by The Lodge and Harrison College for boys, and Queen's College for girls. The island also maintains several second-grade and numerous primary schools.

In terms of economic development, Barbados serves as the headquarters of the Imperial Agricultural Department of the West Indies. Under Sir Daniel Morris, this department contributed to the development of cotton growing on the island. The island contains no forests or crown lands.
