# Boileau and the 10th Satire

## Literary Production and Royal Appointment
Boileau served as the historiographer to the king beginning in 1677. Following this appointment, the volume of his literary production decreased. During this specific period of his life, he authored several notable works, including the ode *Sur la prise de Namur*, the epistles *Sur l'amour de Dieu* and *A mes vers*, and the satire *Sur l'homme*. He also wrote the satire *Sur les femmes*.

## The 10th Satire and Resulting Controversies
Boileau's satires generated a significant number of enemies. Specifically, his 10th satire, which focused on women, provoked a response from Charles Perrault in the form of a work titled *Apologie des femmes*.

The 10th satire also drew criticism from religious figures. In the year of his death, Antoine Arnauld wrote a letter defending Boileau. However, when Arnauld submitted this reply to Bossuet, the bishop declared that the 10th satire was subversive of morality. Furthermore, Bossuet pronounced that all satire was incompatible with the spirit of Christianity. In response to these types of criticisms, the friends of Arnauld argued that it was inconsistent with the dignity of a churchman to write about a subject as trivial as poetry. Boileau later provided a triumphant vindication of the dignity of his art through his epistle *Sur l'amour de Dieu*.

## Later Life and Academic Career
Boileau was not admitted to the Academy until April 15, 1684, an admission that occurred only because of the wish of the king. In 1687, he moved to a country-house in Auteuil. This residence was referred to by Racine as his *hotellerie d'Auteuil* due to the large number of guests who visited.

Boileau returned to Paris in 1705 after selling his house, subsequently living with his confessor within the cloisters of Notre Dame. Around 1705, he wrote the 12th satire, *Sur l'equivoque*, in which he attacked the Jesuits. This work was described by Sainte-Beuve as a recapitulation of Pascal's *Lettres provinciales*.

## Final Years and Death
In his final years, Boileau focused on organizing a definitive and complete edition of his works. However, he faced opposition from the Jesuit fathers, who successfully petitioned Louis XIV to withdraw the publication privilege that had already been granted. The Jesuits further demanded that the 12th satire be suppressed. These annoyances are cited as having hastened Boileau's death, which occurred on March 13, 1711.
