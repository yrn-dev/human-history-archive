# Geography and Populations of the Tibeto-Himalayan and Central Asian Regions

## The Tibetan Plateau and Surrounding Ranges
The Tibetan plateau serves as a central geographical hub from which several major mountain systems and river networks originate. To the north, the Astin-tagh forms the outermost border-ridge of the plateau. Rather than a single chain, it consists of parallel main ranges—two in most areas, three in the east, and up to four northeast of Tsaidam—flanked by spurs and offshoots. This system includes the massif of Anambaruin-ula and the Humboldt Range, the latter of which features a pass at 13,200 ft. and likely continues as the south Koko-nor Range. The northern branch of this system merges into the Nan-shan, or Southern Mountains.

The elevation of the Astin-tagh decreases toward the east. Passes in the Lower Astin-tagh range from 10,150 to 10,700 ft., while those in the Upper Astin-tagh range from 11,770 to 15,680 ft. (Tash-davan), though one pass near the Charkhlik-su is only 9,660 ft. The mountains of the Astin-tagh are characterized by hard, bare rock and rugged, pinnacled peaks, contrasting with the rounded ranges found on the Tibetan plateau itself.

## Hydrography of the Region
The Tibetan plateau is the source of several of Asia's most significant rivers. The Tsanpo, a great river of eastern Tibet, supplies the main stream of the Brahmaputra. China's two primary rivers also originate here: the Hwang-ho rises from the north-east angle and the Yang-tsze-kiang from the south-east. The latter is known as the Dichu in Tibet, and its chief feeder, the Ya-lung-kiang, is regarded as the territorial boundary between Tibet and China.

From the eastern extremity of the Tibetan mountains, between the 95th and 100th meridians, high ranges extend southwards from approximately 35 deg. N. These ranges reach the sea in the Malay peninsula, Cochin-China, and the east flank of Bengal. Between these ranges flow the Irrawaddy, Salween, Menam, and Mekong rivers. The valleys of these rivers form the primary portions of Burma, Siam (including Laos), and Cochin-China (including Cambodia and Tongking).

## Central Asia and the Gobi Desert
The great desert of central Asia, the Gobi, is bounded by the Khingan range to the east, the northern face of the Tibetan plateau to the south, and the Yablonoi Mountains and Tian-shan to the north and north-west. The Gobi's eastern portion is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia, while its western portion forms eastern or Chinese Turkestan. 

The region is characterized by high elevations; no part of the Gobi is likely below 2,000 ft. The plains around Yarkand and Kashgar, as well as northern Mongolia, range between 4,000 and 6,000 ft. Some mountain ridges in this central area exceed 10,000 or 12,000 ft. However, a central depression exists between the Sarikol range and the sources of the Hwang-ho, which probably never exceeds 2,000 ft. and may be below sea-level near Lop Nor.

## The Pamir and Western Plateaus
The north-western extremity of the Tibeto-Himalayan plateau is the Pamir, located at approximately 39 deg. N. and 73 deg. E. This region, described by Russians as a steppe, is the source of the Jaxartes (Syr), which flows north through the Turki state of Khokand, and the Oxus (Amu), which flows west. To the east, waters run past Kashgar toward the Gobi, joining streams from the Tibetan plateau that pass through the Turki principalities of Khotan and Yarkand.

West of British India lies another extensive plateau of similar area to Tibet but lower altitude. This region encompasses Armenia, Asia Minor, Iran (Persia), Baluchistan, and Afghanistan. Its eastern flank is marked by hills near the 72nd meridian that generally do not exceed 5,000 ft., though some summits reach over 10,000 ft.

## British India and the Northern Plains
British India occupies the area between the 70th and 95th meridians, extending from the Tibetan table-land to the Indian Ocean. The region is defined by the great plain of northern India, which lies between the Tibetan plateau's Himalayan slope and the peninsula's table-land. This plain, including the valley of Assam, covers about 500,000 sq. m. and is composed of deposits so fine that one could travel over 2,000 m. from the Bay of Bengal through the Punjab to the Indus without finding a pebble.

South of 25 deg. N., the Indian peninsula becomes a table-land. Its highest points in the west exceed 8,000 ft., though ordinary higher hills rarely exceed 4,000 ft., with the general level between 1,000 and 3,000 ft.

## Populations and Culture
The regions between India and Mongolia are inhabited by populations of Chinese and Mongolian types, including the Siamese, Burmese, and Tibetans. These groups use fundamentally monosyllabic languages and have adopted Buddhism and Indian-derived letters from the Indians. The people of Cochin-China, called Anam, are more nearly Chinese in all respects. 

The Burmese and eastern Burmese tribes (such as the Shans, Kachins, and Chins) belong to Tibeto-Chinese modifications of the Mongolian type. Similarly, a cognate race occupies the Himalaya to the eastern limits of Kashmir. Evidence suggests Mongolian settlements extend into north-west India, including the Mingals (who occupy hills south of Kalat) and a colony in Makran. There is also a suggested connection between the Tibetan race and central Indian tribes, the Kols and Bhils.
