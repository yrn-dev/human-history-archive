# Psychophysical Parallelism and Related Theories

## Overview of Psychophysical Parallelism
Psychophysical parallelism, also referred to as the "double aspect theory," is a philosophical and psychological concept regarding the relationship between the mind and the body. According to this theory, both physical phenomena and mental states result from a single substance or cause termed "mind stuff." 

In the context of materialism, this theory is used to argue against the belief in immortality. Materialism posits that life is dependent upon a material organism and that thought is a function of the brain. Under the framework of psychophysical parallelism, physical changes always correspond to mental states; consequently, materialists argue that the dissolution of the physical body necessarily results in the cessation of consciousness.

## Distinctions from Automatism
Psychophysical parallelism must be distinguished from the theory of automatism. In psychology, automatism refers to actions that do not result from conscious endeavour or conation. Some theorists, including T. Huxley in *Science and Culture* and Shadworth Hodgson in *Metaphysic of Experience and Theory of Practice*, maintain a view where physical changes in the brain result in specific actions. In this view, any accompanying volition or mental desire is not prior to the physical change, nor is it causally connected to it. This differs from the double aspect theory, which views both the mental and physical as resulting from the same underlying "mind stuff."

## Critiques and Counter-Arguments
The theory of psychophysical parallelism and the materialist inferences drawn from it have been subjected to rigorous examination. In *Naturalism and Agnosticism* (part iii), James Ward presents arguments asserting that the mind cannot be derived from matter. 

Further objections to the materialist view—which relies on the correspondence of physical changes to mental states—include the following considerations:
* The ability of humans to distinguish themselves from their own bodies.
* The consciousness of personal identity despite changes in the physical body.
* The experience of the will controlling the body rather than being controlled by it.
* The fact that consciousness allows an individual to deny the absolute identification of the self with the body.

Additionally, it is argued that materialism is an inadequate philosophy for explaining the physical universe, as such an explanation requires the assumption of a mind that is distinct from and directive of matter. Sir Oliver Lodge, in his response to E. Haeckel's *Riddle of the Universe*, suggests that life may be immaterial and exist outside current categories of energy and matter. Lodge rejects the notion that human personality is generated solely by the latent power of a material molecular aggregate.

## Related Psychological and Physical Perspectives
Other thinkers have explored the intersection of the physical and the mental. Ernst Mach, an Austrian physicist and psychologist, initially focused on the physical side of psychology. After a period of noümenalism influenced by Fechner's *Psychophysics*, Mach adopted a universal physical phenomenalism. He concluded that all existence is sensation and viewed the Ego not as an entity distinct from the Non-ego, but as a medium of continuity for sensory impressions.

In the study of association, William James posits that while association is between "things thought of" (objects) as an effect, it is between processes in the brain when viewed as a cause. James attributes the law of Contiguity to a physiological cause—specifically the laws of habit in the nervous system—noting that a nerve current passes more easily through a path it has previously taken. He concludes that the ultimate law of association is that of neural habit, which is a physical fact.
