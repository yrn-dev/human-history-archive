# Charles Bonnet

Charles Bonnet (1720–1793) was a Swiss philosophical writer and naturalist born in Geneva on March 13, 1720. Born into a French family that had been driven into Switzerland due to religious persecution during the 16th century, Bonnet initially pursued a profession in law. However, his primary interest lay in natural science.

## Scientific Contributions
Bonnet's interest in insect life was sparked at the age of sixteen after reading an account of the ant-lion in N.A. Pluche's *Spectacle de la nature*. He furthered his studies using R.A.F. de Reaumur's work on insects and live specimens to add his own observations. In 1740, he presented a paper to the academy of sciences regarding experiments on parthenogenesis in tree-lice (*aphides*), which led to his admission as a corresponding member of the academy.

His subsequent research included the study of regeneration of lost parts and reproduction by fusion in other animals and the freshwater hydra, beginning in 1741. In 1742, he discovered that butterflies and caterpillars respire through pores, which were later named *stigmata*. Bonnet was admitted as a fellow of the Royal Society in 1743, the same year he earned his doctorate in laws, marking the end of his involvement in a legal profession he found distasteful.

## Philosophical System
Bonnet's philosophical framework posits that man is a compound of two distinct substances: a material body and an immaterial mind. He argued that all knowledge begins with sensations, which are caused by external physical stimuli that make the nerves vibrate. Bonnet noted that once a nerve is set in motion by a specific object, it tends to reproduce that motion; consequently, when the same object provides a second impression, the nerve vibrates with less resistance. This increased flexibility in the nerve is what Bonnet identified as the condition of memory.

Abstract ideas are formed when reflection—the active element of the mind—is used to combine and acquire these sensations. According to Bonnet, the mind is put into activity by pain or pleasure, and the ultimate end of human existence is happiness.

## Metaphysical Theory
Bonnet's metaphysics are based on two principles derived from Leibnitz:
1. The universe was completed by a single original act of the divine will and moves by its own inherent force, rather than through successive acts of creation.
2. There is no break in the continuity of existence.

Bonnet taught that the divine Being created a graduated scale of germs, each possessing an inherent power for self-development. As the universe progresses, these germs are modified and advance toward perfection. This continuity ensures there are no gaps in the chain of existence, leading to the conclusion that all forms of existence, not just man, are immortal. Furthermore, Bonnet asserted that man's body is also immortal; while the current body will not persist, it will pass into a finer higher stage, the germ of which currently exists within him. However, Bonnet postulated that absolute perfection is unreachable because the distance to it is infinite, a point that contradicts his own principle of continuity by creating an interval.

## Personal Life and Works
Bonnet's life was largely uneventful and spent within Switzerland. Between 1752 and 1768, he served as a member of the council of the republic. He spent the final twenty-five years of his life in the country at Genthod, near Geneva. He was married to a woman from the De la Rive family; they had no children, but they raised Madame Bonnet's nephew, H.B. de Saussure, as their son. Bonnet died on May 20, 1793, following a long and painful illness.

His complete works were published in Neuchatel between 1779 and 1783, featuring some of his own revisions. A partial English translation of his *Palingenesie philosophique* was published in 1787 under the title *Philosophical and Critical Inquiries concerning Christianity*.
