# The Amur and Usuri Regions

The Amur and Usuri rivers define a significant geographical and political area in the eastern reaches of Siberia, specifically within the Maritime Province. This region is characterized by a diverse landscape ranging from deep depressions to impenetrable mountain ranges and a complex history of territorial acquisition.

## Geography and Climate
The southern portion of the province is divided into two distinct regions. A wide, deep depression, situated less than 500 feet above sea level, extends from the north-eastern extremity of the Little Khingan range (or Bureya)—which continues into the Shantar Islands—south-west to the confluence of the Amur and Usuri rivers, and further to the lower Sungari lowlands. To the right banks of the Amur and Usuri, between the rivers and the coast, lies the Sikhota-alin, a system of mountains covered in impenetrable forests. The highest summit is Golaya Gora, reaching 5,150 feet, while mountain passes average 2,500 feet. Lake Kidzi is located within a depression in this range and may have once served as an outflow for the Amur river to the sea.

The climate is generally harsh. In the southern Maritime Province, the combination of the Sikhota-alin mountains and monsoons from the Chinese Sea results in wet summers and cold winters. At Vladivostok, the average yearly temperature is 39.5° F, and the harbor freezes for nearly three months annually. Further north, the climate is arctic and continental; Nikolayevsk records winter temperatures as low as -41.5° F and summer highs of 94.6° F. The Amur and Usuri rivers typically freeze in November. Late summer monsoons often cause heavy rains, leading to serious inundations of the Amur.

## Flora, Fauna, and Natural Resources
The Usuri district is noted for a striking combination of subarctic and warm-climate species. Larch and cedar-pine trees are often entwined with wild vines, and the region's fauna includes the tiger, bear, and sable. The rivers are exceptionally rich in fish, particularly in August when salmon swarm the Amur and Usuri.

Economic activity is tied to these resources. The Russians engage in agriculture and the fur trade, while gold-mining has been established on the Amguñ, a tributary of the Amur. Coal deposits are found in Kamchatka and near Vladivostok.

## Political History and Boundaries
The southern boundary of Siberia follows an irregular course. After passing the Sayan range and touching the Kerulen affluent of the Amur, the boundary follows that affluent to its junction with the Amur, and then follows the Amur river to its junction with the Usuri. From there, it follows the Usuri to its head, eventually striking the Pacific coast at the mouth of the Tumen river, 100 meters south of the Amur bay.

Historically, the strip of seacoast from the mouth of the Amur to the Korean frontier—the Usuri region—belonged to China. While China was initially reluctant to make concessions regarding the Usuri after conceding much of the Amur to Russia, the region was temporarily regarded as common property. In 1860, following China's defeat by France and England, Russia secured the final cession of the Usuri region, which became the Maritime Province of Siberia. This expansion led Russia to shift its Pacific naval basis from Nikolaievsk to Vladivostok, located ten degrees to the south.

## Population and Infrastructure
As of 1897, the total population of the province was 209,516. Russians made up 57.7% of the population, with the remainder consisting of various groups including the Golds, Tunguses, Orochons, Lamuts, Chuvantses, Chukchis, Koryaks, Ghilyaks, and Kamchadales. While many settlements are merely administrative centers, active measures between 1883 and 1897 increased the Russian population in the South Usuri district through the immigration of over 29,000 people, primarily Little Russian peasants, as well as Cossacks from Orenburg and the Don.

Infrastructure is concentrated in the South Usuri district, the only area with roads. A railway connects Vladivostok to Nikolsk (69 m), and continues to Khabarovsk (412 m from Nikolsk) along the right bank of the Usuri. The Manchurian railway begins at Nikolsk.

## Major Settlements
Khabarovsk (known as Khabarovka until 1895) serves as the capital of both the Amur region and the Maritime Province. Founded in 1858 on a high cliff on the right bank of the Amur at its confluence with the Usuri, it is a center for trade, particularly in sables, and an entrepôt for goods from the Usuri and the Sungacha. The town is constructed of wood and features a large cathedral, a technical railway school, a branch of the Russian Geographical Society with a museum, a cadet corps, and a monument to Count Muraviev-Amurskiy.

Vladivostok, situated on Peter the Great Bay, is the chief Russian naval station on the Pacific, a role it resumed after the loss of Port Arthur to the Japanese in 1905. Trade in the area is largely managed by Germans, who import spirits and groceries, and the Chinese, who export mushrooms, seaweed, and stags' horns.
