# Post Obit Bonds

In English law, a post obit bond is a specific type of obligation by deed. It is a bond given by a legatee or an expectant heir. The terms of such a bond dictate that it is payable either on or after the death of the person from whom the obligor (the person giving the bond) has expectations.

## Legal Nature and Validity
A post obit bond falls under the broader category of bonds, which are designed to ensure that an obligor will either perform a specific act, refrain from doing an act, or pay a sum of money. To secure this, the obligor binds himself to the obligee through a penalty. 

While many bonds are valid, English law renders void any bond given to secure actions that are contrary to the policy of the law. Examples of such void bonds include marriage brocage bonds (used to procure a marriage between parties) or bonds given to a woman for future cohabitation.

Regarding post obit bonds specifically, the law provides a safeguard against exploitation: if the obligee has exacted terms that are unconscionable, the bond may be set aside.

## General Principles of Bonds
To understand the function of a post obit bond, it is necessary to understand the general structure of bonds in English law. A bond typically includes a "defeasance," which is a condition that defeats or undoes the bond if the obligor fulfills their requirement. For example, in a common money bond, the obligor may bind himself to a penalty (such as £2000), but the defeasance stipulates that if a smaller sum (such as £1000) is paid by a certain date, the bond becomes void.

If the condition is not performed—meaning the obligor fails to refrain from an act, perform an act, or pay the money by the stipulated day—the bond becomes absolute at law or forfeit. This then charges the estate of the obligor as well as the obligor himself.

## Equitable Relief and Statutory Regulation
Historically, when a bond was forfeit, the entire penalty was recoverable at law, and payment made after the stipulated date (*post diem*) could not be used as a defense. However, the court of chancery intervened to prevent oppression, viewing the penalty as a form of pledge to secure the bona fide repayment of a sum rather than the substance of the debt. The court of chancery would not allow an obligee to take more than was conscientious, which in a common money bond included the principal, interest, and expenses.

This equitable approach was given statutory recognition in 1705, which provided that for common money bonds, payment of the lesser sum plus costs and interest would be considered full satisfaction. Furthermore, since the date of the Judicature Act, an obligee of a common money bond can obtain a summary judgment by specially endorsing his writ.

## Other Types of Bonds
Post obit bonds are distinct from other specialized bonds used in English law:
*   **Bottomry Bonds:** These are contracts of hypothecation where a ship's owner or master borrows money for ship emergencies (such as necessary repairs) by pledging the ship, or its keel/bottom (*partem pro toto*), as security. If the voyage is completed safely, the money is repaid with interest; if the ship is totally lost, the obligee loses the money.
*   **Performance and Guarantee Bonds:** Bonds may be used to guarantee the fidelity of a rent collector, a clerk, or a public trust official. They can also ensure a building contract is completed, that a husband settles a sum on his wife should she survive him, or that a rival business is not operated within certain limits of space and time.

In many instances, covenants have superseded bonds for these purposes. However, bonds remain preferable in certain cases, such as securing an annuity. In such cases, if the obligor defaults, a judgment for the penalty can be entered to serve as security for future payments, removing the need for a new action for every single payment.
