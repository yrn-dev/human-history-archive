# Vorarlberg

## Overview and Geography
Vorarlberg is an Austrian province. Within this province lies the township of Dornbirn, which serves as the region's chief industrial center. Dornbirn is situated on the right bank of the Dornbirner Ach at the point where the river exits the hilly Bregenzerwald region and enters the broad valley of the Rhine on its way toward the Lake of Constance. The town is located 15 miles north of Feldkirch and 7-1/2 miles south of Bregenz by rail.

The name Dornbirn refers to a collective of four villages—Oberdorf, Hatlerdorf, Haselstauden, and Dornbirn—which extend over a distance of approximately three miles. As of 1900, Dornbirn was the most populous town in Vorarlberg, with a population of 13,052.

## Economy and Industry
The industrial activity of Vorarlberg is centered in Dornbirn, where the regulated Dornbirner Ach provides the necessary motive power for various factories. These industrial operations include iron-founding, dyeing, the production of worked muslin, and cotton weaving and spinning.

## Political and Administrative History
The administrative structure of Vorarlberg included a system of provincial diets and estates. This system was revived in 1816. These bodies were not representative in nature, as eligibility was restricted to the nobility and clergy, with a small number of delegates from the towns. Their functions were largely limited to dealing with local police matters and registering imperial decrees concerning taxation or recruiting.

In the context of the Austrian empire's racial and political divisions, Vorarlberg was part of the purely German district, which also included Salzburg, Upper and Lower Austria, most of Styria, a large part of Carinthia, and Tirol (excluding the south). This region formed the kernel of the empire.

## Religious and Social Influence
Vorarlberg, along with Tirol, maintained a clerical majority within its diet. This religious influence manifested in political resistance to centralized government mandates. Specifically, the diet of Vorarlberg refused to implement the school law due to the influence of the clergy and the support of the peasants in rural districts. This resistance was part of a broader struggle where Conservative and Clerical parties supported local institutions as a means of protection against the influence of a centralized bureaucracy and the Liberal party.
