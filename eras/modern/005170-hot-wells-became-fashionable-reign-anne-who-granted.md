# The Reign of Queen Anne

The reign of Queen Anne (1702–1714) was a period marked by specific developments in English social customs, equestrian breeding, and the arts.

## Social Customs and Fashion
During the reign of Anne, the hot wells became fashionable, and the Queen granted a charter in 1710. In the realm of attire, the 18th century was characterized by the three-cornered cocked hat, a style derived from the French court. While wigs were generally powdered and worn by middle- and upper-class men, the large and costly full-bottomed periwig was gradually replaced by the lesser or "Ramillie" wig. 

The transition to the tie or Ramillie wig was not without social friction; Lord Bolingbroke once attended Queen Anne while wearing one, prompting Her Majesty to express her displeasure by remarking that his lordship would next appear at court wearing a night-cap. Despite this, the tie-wig eventually became acceptable court wear.

## Equestrian Breeding
The era of Queen Anne is inseparably linked to the development of the thoroughbred race-horse, primarily due to the fame of the Darley Arabian. A bay stallion, the Darley Arabian was brought to England by a brother of Mr. Darley of Yorkshire, who had acquired the horse through his membership in a hunting club while serving as a merchandise agent abroad.

While sources differ on the exact date of the stallion's arrival—with some suggesting the year 1700 and others a later date—it is generally believed he was imported during Anne's reign. The Darley Arabian is the ancestor of the finest horses and helped eliminate public prejudice against Eastern blood, which had been fostered by the duke of Newcastle's denunciation of the Markham Arabian. The importation of many valuable stallions during this period was aided significantly by the intervention of Prince George of Denmark, the consort of Queen Anne, who was a large horse-owner himself. Notable offspring of the Darley Arabian include Manica (foaled 1707), Aleppo (1711), Almanzor (1713), Flying Childers (1715), and Bartlett's Childers.

## Drama and Literature
The reign of Queen Anne marked the end of the "hey-day" of the epilogue in English drama. From 1660 until the decline of the drama during Anne's reign, nearly every play produced on the London stage—whether comic or serious—featured a prologue and an epilogue. 

These components were almost always written in verse, regardless of whether the play itself was written in prose, to provide a literary finish. Following the fashion established by Ben Jonson, epilogues typically took the form of short, complete pieces written in heroic couplets. These works often served as satires or elaborate essays on politics, criticism, or fashion, and were not always limited to the subject of the play. It was common for playwrights to have friends write these poems, or for publishers to pay prominent poets to supply them.
