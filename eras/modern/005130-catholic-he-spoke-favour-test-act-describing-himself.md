# The 2nd Earl of Bristol

The 2nd Earl of Bristol was a prominent and contradictory figure of his era, recognized for his brilliant abilities and oratorical skill. Despite his natural gifts, his career was marked by instability of character and a restless ambition that hindered his ability to achieve lasting greatness. He was described by Clarendon as a man of incomparable parts who failed to gain wisdom from experience or misfortune, and Horace Walpole characterized his life as "one contradiction."

## Political Career and Conflict with Clarendon
Digby succeeded to the peerage as the 2nd Earl of Bristol in January 1659 following the death of his father and was made a Knight of the Garter (K.G.) that same month. Upon returning to England during the Restoration, he found himself relegated to secondary importance and excluded from office due to his religion. This marginalization fueled an ambitious and restless activity within parliament, where he adopted a stance of violent hostility toward Clarendon.

Bristol's involvement in foreign affairs was characterized by a strong inclination toward Spain. He opposed the marriage of the king to Catherine of Portugal and attempted to persuade Charles to send him to Italy to evaluate the Medici princesses; however, the treaty and marriage with Portugal were finalized while he was absent.

In June 1663, Bristol attempted to undermine Clarendon's management of the House of Commons. When this intrigue was exposed by Charles, Bristol was forced to appear before the House to exonerate himself. During this appearance, he admitted to having "taken the liberty of enlarging," delivering a "comedian-like speech" that resulted in general amusement.

## Fall and Temporary Exile
The relationship between Bristol and the king deteriorated in early July, culminating in a violent scene where Bristol leveled fierce and disrespectful reproaches at Charles. He threatened that if his requests were not granted within twenty-four hours, he would do something to "awaken him out of his slumbers, and make him look better to his own business." 

Following this, on the 10th of July, Bristol impeached Clarendon in the Lords on charges of high treason. After the charge was dismissed, he renewed the accusation, which led to his expulsion from the court. He avoided a warrant for his apprehension by remaining concealed for two years.

## Religious Transitions and the Test Act
Bristol's religious identity was a source of significant public and legal fluctuation. In January 1664, he caused a sensation by appearing at his house in Wimbledon to publicly renounce Roman Catholicism before witnesses and declare himself a Protestant. This move was likely motivated by a desire to secure immunity from charges of recusancy.

Despite these shifts, Horace Walpole noted that Bristol spoke in favor of the Test Act even while he was a Roman Catholic. Walpole further highlighted the contradictions of his life, noting that he "wrote against popery and embraced it."

## Return to Court and Personal Life
Bristol was welcomed back to court when the fall of Clarendon became a desired outcome. He resumed his seat in the Lords on July 29, 1667. 

Beyond his political activities, Bristol was a writer. His works include an *Apologie* (1643), which justified his support for the king's cause, and a comedy titled *Elvira* (1667). He also wrote an adaptation from the Spanish titled *Worse and Worse*. Additionally, he is credited with co-authoring *The Adventures of Five Hours* (1663) with Sir Samuel Tuke. His library's catalogue was published in 1680.

Bristol married Lady Anne Russell, the daughter of Francis, 4th Earl of Bedford. They had two daughters and two sons: Francis, who died unmarried before his father, and John, who succeeded him as the 3rd Earl of Bristol.
