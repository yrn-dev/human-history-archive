# Trente-six ballades joyeuses and Related Works

## The Ballade and Theodore de Banville
The ballade is a typically French poetic form that experienced a significant decline in popularity, falling entirely out of fashion for a period of two hundred years. It was resuscitated in the middle of the 19th century by Theodore de Banville. In 1873, Banville published a volume titled *Trente-six ballades joyeuses*, a work that subsequently found many imitators.

While the form is primarily French, it has been employed extensively in English. During the 15th and 16th centuries, leading English poets such as Lydgate, Gower (whose surviving ballades are all in French), and Chaucer wrote ballades with varying degrees of adherence to French rules. Chaucer's work demonstrates that the specific rhyme arrangement and strophe type of the ballade were present in medieval English.

## Other Works of the Period
The archive records several other literary contributions from the late 19th century. In 1871, the poems *Idylles prussiennes* were produced. Other works associated with this literary circle include the plays *Le Feuilleton d'Aristophane* (1852), *Gringoire* (1866), and *Deidamia* (1876). 

Additionally, the archive notes several prose tales, including *Les Saltimbanques* (1853), *Esquisses parisiennes* (1859), and *Contes feeriques*. ## Historical Context of the Ballade
Beyond the 19th-century revival, the ballade appeared in earlier collections of didactic and satirical poetry. For instance, the works of Gringoire, edited by C. d'Hericault and A. de Montaiglon in 1858 and supplemented in 1877 by Montaiglon and M. James de Rothschild, include *Les Folles Entreprises* (1505). This collection consists chiefly of rondeaux and ballades, with specific sections dedicated to the vices of the clergy and the tyranny of the nobles.
