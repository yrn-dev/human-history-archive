# AUTOMATIC WRITING

Automatic writing is a term used by students of psychical research to describe the act of writing performed without the volition of the agent. This phenomenon may occur while the agent is in a state of induced or spontaneous trance, or during other morbid states such as hystero-epilepsy. However, it can also take place in a condition that is not distinguishable from normal wakefulness.

## Nature of the Phenomenon
The level of consciousness during automatic writing varies. In some instances, the writing occurs without any consciousness of the words being produced. In other cases, automatists may be aware of the specific word they are writing, and perhaps two or three words surrounding it, though they rarely possess a clear perception of the meaning of the entire text.

Within the broader study of automatism, automatic writing is categorized as a form of motor automatism. Motor automatism is defined as a non-reflex movement of a voluntary muscle that is executed in a waking state but is not controlled by ordinary waking consciousness. While lower levels of motor automatism include vague muscle movements (such as those seen in "bier-divination" or witch seeking) or more specialized actions (such as the use of a divining rod), automatic writing and automatic drawing represent a higher stage of these delicate movements. A parallel to this is found in the speech centers, which can produce everything from unintelligible babblings to trance speeches in the speaker's ordinary language, including "Glossolalia."

## History and Spiritualism
Automatic writing played a significant role in the history of modern spiritualism, appearing on a large scale in America between approximately 1850 and 1860. During this period, numerous reports emerged of lengthy writings purportedly produced under spirit guidance, some of which were written in "unknown tongues." Notable published works from this era include J. Murray Spear's *Messages from the Spirit Life*, Charles Linton's *The Healing of the Nations*, and Andrew J. Davis's *Great Harmonia*.

In England, early spiritualist newspapers featured "inspirational" writing, such as the *Pages of Ike Paraclete*. One of the most notable English series of automatic writings is the *Spirit Teachings* of the Rev. W. Stainton Moses. To facilitate the writing process during the early years of the movement, a "planchette"—a small, heart-shaped board on wheels—was frequently employed.

## Psychological and Experimental Applications
While the theory that external inspiration causes the phenomenon has generally been discredited in recent years, automatic writing has become a method for experimentally investigating subconscious mental processes. It is frequently used to reveal knowledge that has lapsed from primary consciousness, such as foreign languages or forgotten fragments of poetry.

Edmund Gurney devised an experimental parallel to this by demonstrating that information given to a subject in a hypnotic trance could later be reproduced through handwriting while the subject was occupied with reading aloud or conversing. Similarly, arithmetical problems set during a trance could be solved through handwriting without the subject's apparent consciousness.

Furthermore, Pierre Janet and others have utilized the communication with subconscious strata of the personality provided by automatic writing to treat cases of dissociation of consciousness and hystero-epilepsy. For example, a patient experiencing hysterical convulsions who cannot respond to oral appeals may be induced to answer questions in writing, allowing them to accept therapeutic suggestions or reveal the secret of their malady.

## Telepathy and Communication
Some automatic writings are thought to exhibit indications of telepathy. The most remarkable examples of this are the writings of the American medium Mrs. Piper, produced in a state of trance. Some observers believe these writings indicate communication with the spirits of the dead.
