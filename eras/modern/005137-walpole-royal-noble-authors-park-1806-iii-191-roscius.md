# Walpole's Royal and Noble Authors and Roscius

## Royal and Noble Authors
*Royal and Noble Authors* is a work by H. Walpole, with an edition published by Park in 1806. The text serves as a source for various biographical and literary details regarding noble figures. In volume III, page 191, it is cited as an authority regarding certain historical figures, and on page 49, it provides information concerning Bristol. Volume III, page 304, contains material regarding the life of Buckingham.

In volume IV, Walpole discusses the intellectual capacities of various contemporaries. He notes that Dorset possessed as much wit as his first master, as well as contemporaries such as Rochester and Buckingham. Walpole further characterizes Dorset by contrasting him with these figures, stating that Dorset lacked the "royal want of feeling" associated with his master, the "want of principles" seen in the duke (Buckingham), and the "want of thought" attributed to the earl (Rochester). Additionally, volume IV, page 209 and following, contains references to the attempts at verse made by Bolingbroke.

## Roscius
The name Roscius appears in historical records associated with several different individuals in Roman history.

### Quintus Roscius
Quintus Roscius was a master of the art of acting whose name became a proverbial expression among Romans to describe a perfect master of any art. According to Cicero, Roscius was a perfect master of his craft, and Cicero himself was intimate with him, learning lessons from Roscius that were useful for his own work as an orator. Roscius authored a work that compared the arts of acting and oratory. He was known to have earned immense professional gains and possessed a sharp eye for his own interests.

In the title of Cicero's oration, Roscius is called *Comœdus*, a term also used by Plutarch. While the general Roman term for an actor was *histrio*, Cicero contrasted the *histrio* with the *comœdus*, viewing the latter as the higher professor of the art. However, Roscius is sometimes referred to as a *histrio*, and evidence from Cicero suggests he also performed tragedy. Cicero delivered a speech in defense of Roscius, which remains extant.
