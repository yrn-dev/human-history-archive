# Disciplinary Measures and Systems

Disciplinary measures encompass a wide range of regulatory actions across various institutional frameworks, including religious governance, legal professions, correctional facilities, and judicial systems. These measures are designed to regulate conduct, maintain order, and enforce professional or institutional standards.

## Ecclesiastical and Professional Discipline

In the history of the papacy, disciplinary measures have been used to regulate the internal operations of the church. During a period of conflict between the papacy and a council, the "fathers" of the council sought to curtail the power and resources of the papacy. They implemented disciplinary measures to regulate the celebration of divine service, the periodical holding of provincial councils and diocesan synods, and the conduct of elections. These efforts included abolishing annates, limiting the pope's "reservation" of the patronage of benefices, and eliminating the right of "next presentation" to benefices not yet vacant, known as *gratiae expectativae*. Additionally, rules were established for the constitution of the Sacred College and the election of popes, while the jurisdiction of the court of Rome was limited.

In the English legal profession, the Inns of Court hold the exclusive privilege of "calling to the bar," which converts a law student into a barrister. These bodies also exercise disciplinary power over their members. Due to concerns that the self-elected governing bodies (benchers) were not sufficiently watchful of professional minutiae, a bar committee was formed in 1883 to address professional etiquette, conduct, and practice. This was succeeded in 1894 by the general council of the bar. While the general council's powers are not disciplinary, it serves to alert the benchers to any gross violations of professional etiquette.

## Correctional Disciplinary Processes

Within the Bureau of Prisons (BOP), disciplinary programs are governed by policies that identify prohibited acts, describe adjudication processes, and list penalties. The process typically begins when a staff member reports an observed prohibited act, followed by an investigation by a BOP supervisor that includes taking a statement from the inmate.

For inmates in disciplinary segregation or administrative detention, such as those in a Special Housing Unit (SHU), additional accountability measures are required. Correctional staff must conduct rounds at least twice an hour—once in the first 30 minutes and once in the second 30 minutes—on an irregular schedule with no more than 40 minutes between rounds.

If an inmate appears mentally ill during the process, a mental health staff member must assess their competency to participate. Competency is determined by a clinical assessment of the inmate's ability to assist in their own defense and understand the nature of the proceedings. If found competent and the act is categorized as one of the two most severe, the matter is referred to a discipline hearing officer for a determination and the imposition of corresponding sanctions.

## Judicial Corporal Punishment

Corporal punishment has been utilized as a disciplinary or legal measure in various jurisdictions. In Scotland, the whipping of male offenders under 14 is regulated by the Whipping Act 1862, the Prisons (Scotland) Act 1860, and the Burgh Police (Scotland) Act 1892; however, those over 16 cannot be whipped for offenses against property or person. Similar laws exist in Ireland. In British colonies, corporal punishment is generally regulated by modern English legislation, though some, such as Queensland under the Criminal Code 1899, have a larger list of punishable offenses. The flogging of women is prohibited in the British colonies and throughout British India under the Code of Criminal Procedure, Act v. of 1898.

In the United States, whipping is not a legal punishment under Federal Law. However, it is inflicted under statute in some states and is not considered cruel or unusual under the Federal Constitution. For example, in Maryland and Delaware, flogging is a punishment for wife-beating, with Delaware also applying it to certain property offenses committed by males. Additionally, flogging is employed as a disciplinary measure in some penal institutions.
