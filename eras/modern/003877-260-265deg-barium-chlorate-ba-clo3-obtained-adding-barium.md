# Barium

Barium (symbol Ba) is a metallic chemical element belonging to the alkaline earth group. Its name is derived from the Greek word *barus*, meaning heavy, due to its presence in heavy spar or barytes. The element has an atomic weight determined by C. Marignac as 136.84 and by T. W. Richards as 137.44; the archive lists its atomic weight as 137.37.

## Physical Properties and Isolation
In its pure form, the metal possesses a silver white lustre and is slightly harder than lead. It is extremely easy to oxidize when exposed to air and readily attacks alcohol and water. Barium is soluble in liquid ammonia.

The metal is difficult to isolate. Sir H. Davy attempted to electrolyse baryta without success, but later produced a barium amalgam using barium chloride and mercury, from which the metal was obtained as a silver white residue via distillation. In 1854, R. Bunsen electrolysed a mixture of dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride with mercury at 100°C to obtain an amalgam. A further method developed by A. N. Guntz involves the electrolysis of a saturated barium chloride solution using a mercury cathode to create a 3% amalgam; when heated electrically in a porcelain tube, pure barium is yielded at approximately 1000°C.

## Natural Occurrence and Detection
Barium occurs primarily as witherite ($\text{BaCO}_3$) and barytes or heavy spar ($\text{BaSO}_4$), and to a lesser extent in baryto-celestine, baryto-calcite, and various complex silicates. Barytes is common in metalliferous veins, particularly those containing silver and lead ores, and is found in iron ore beds, Triassic sandstones, and London Clay.

Barium and its salts can be detected by heating them in a Bunsen flame after moistening with hydrochloric acid, which produces a yellowish-green colour, or by observing two characteristic green lines in their spectra. In solution, they can be identified by the yellow precipitate formed upon adding potassium chromate, or by the immediate precipitate produced by the addition of calcium sulphate.

## Barium Oxides and Hydroxide
Three oxides of barium are known:
*   **Monoxide ($\text{BaO}$):** A greyish solid formed when the metal burns in air or through the ignition of the nitrate, iodate, or a mixture of carbon and carbonate. It combines energetically with water to form the hydroxide.
*   **Dioxide ($\text{BaO}_2$):** A grey powder produced by heating barium monoxide with air under pressure (the Brin process) or by adding excess baryta-water to hydrogen peroxide solution. It decomposes with dilute acids to produce hydrogen peroxide.
*   **Suboxide:** Obtained by heating barium monoxide with magnesium in a vacuum at 1100°C.

Barium hydroxide ($\text{Ba(OH)}_2$) is a white powder. On a large scale, it is produced by heating heavy spar with small coal to create barium sulphide, which is then converted to barium carbonate using moist carbon dioxide, and finally decomposed by superheated steam. Its solution, known as baryta-water, is used in organic chemistry as a condensing or hydrolysing agent and in gas-analysis to determine atmospheric carbon dioxide.

## Barium Salts
Barium forms a wide variety of salts with distinct properties and preparation methods:

**Chlorides, Bromides, and Iodides:** Barium chloride ($\text{BaCl}_2 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is obtained from witherite via dilute hydrochloric acid or from heavy spar using a reverberatory furnace with limestone, coal, and calcium chloride. It is a strong poison with a bitter taste. Barium bromide ($\text{BaBr}_2 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is prepared by decomposing barium carbonate with hydrobromic acid or saturating baryta-water. Barium iodate ($\text{Ba(IO}_3)_2 \cdot \text{H}_2\text{O}$) is produced by adding sodium iodate to barium chloride or using iodic acid on hot caustic baryta solution.

**Oxysalts:** 
*   **Barium Bromate ($\text{Ba(BrO}_3)_2$):** Prepared by the action of excess bromine on baryta-water; it decomposes when heated to 260-265°C.
*   **Barium Chlorate ($\text{Ba(ClO}_3)_2$):** Obtained by adding barium chloride to sodium chlorate solution or by passing chlorine through barium carbonate suspended in boiling water; it decomposes at 250°C.
*   **Barium Nitrate ($\text{Ba(NO}_3)_2$):** Prepared by dissolving the sulphide or carbonate in dilute nitric acid; it is used in pyrotechny to create green fire.
*   **Barium Sulphate ($\text{BaSO}_4$):** An amorphous white powder and the most abundant natural barium compound. It is used as a pigment called "permanent white" or *blanc fixe*.

**Other Compounds:** Barium carbonate ($\text{BaCO}_3$) is found as witherite. Barium sulphide ($\text{BaS}$) is a white powder produced by fusion of the sulphate with small coal. Barium nitride ($\text{Ba}_3\text{N}_2$) is a brownish mass obtained by passing nitrogen over heated barium amalgam. Barium carbide ($\text{BaC}_2$) can be prepared by distilling a mixture of carbon and barium amalgam in a hydrogen stream.
