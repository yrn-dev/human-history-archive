# The Dobrudja

The Dobrudja is a peninsula located in the Balkan region. As of 1909, the area under Rumanian administration comprised 5,896 square miles with a population of 258,242, resulting in a population density of 43.9 people per square mile.

## Geography and Local Centers
The northern Dobrudja contains densely wooded highlands. Within this region, in the department of Tulcea, lies the town of Babadag (also referred to as Babatag). Babadag is situated on a small lake formed by the river Taitza; this lake is separated from Lake Razim—a broad, landlocked body of water that opens into the Black Sea—by a strip of marshland. As of 1900, Babadag had a population of approximately 3,500 and served as a market for the mutton and wool produced in the Dobrudja.

## Early History and Turkish Influence
Babadag was founded by the Turkish sultan Bayezid I, who reigned from 1389 to 1403. The town served as winter headquarters on several occasions during Turkish wars with Russia and was subjected to Russian bombardment in 1854.

## Territorial Disputes and Treaties
The Dobrudja has been a subject of significant territorial contention between Bulgaria and Rumania. Following the Russian advance to Constantinople, the Treaty of San Stefano (March 3, 1878) created an autonomous Bulgarian principality. Although the Dobrudja possessed a Bulgarian population, it was excluded from this new state and reserved as compensation for Rumania following the Russian annexation of Bessarabia. This transfer of the Dobrudja to Rumania was later sanctioned by the Treaty of Berlin on July 13, 1878.

In 1913, Rumania annexed the southern Dobrudja. By the autumn of 1915, Bulgaria sought to reclaim these populations.

## World War I and German Intervention
During World War I, Bulgaria entered the war on the Teutonic side in 1915. Following Rumania's intervention on the Allied side in the autumn of 1916, Bulgarian troops defeated the Rumanians and overran the entire Dobrudja, advancing as far as the mouth of the Danube.

Despite Bulgaria's expectation to acquire the entire peninsula, Germany intervened due to the strategic importance of the region. The Dobrudja controlled the mouth of the Danube and contained the port terminus for the primary railroad trunk-line connecting Central Europe to the Black Sea. Consequently, Germany did not intend to place these assets solely in Bulgarian hands.

As a result, Bulgaria was granted only the southern Dobrudja, while the remainder of the peninsula was held "in common" as a condominium. When the collapse of Russia in the spring of 1918 forced Rumania to make peace with the Central powers, Rumania ceded the Dobrudja prize to the Central powers rather than to Bulgaria. Germany maintained the condominium arrangement until the final peace settlement, leaving Bulgaria dissatisfied with its share of the territory.
