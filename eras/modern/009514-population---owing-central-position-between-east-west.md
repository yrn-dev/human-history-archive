# Population and Demographics of Selected Global Regions

The distribution and composition of human populations vary significantly across the globe, influenced by geography, trade, colonial history, and environmental conditions. From the dense urban centers of Asia to the sparse settlements of the Arctic, population patterns reflect the intersection of economic opportunity and natural constraints.

## Urban Diversity and Influence in Bombay
Due to its central position between the East and the West and the inherent diversity of races in India, Bombay exhibits a greater variety of types than any other city in the world. While European rulers and the Mahratta race represent the dominant elements, the city serves as a primary meeting-place for the Eastern world. Its population includes a wide array of groups, such as Arab traders from the Gulf, Afghans and Sikhs from northern India, Bengalis, Rajputs, Chinese, Japanese, Malays, negroes, Tibetans, Sinhalese, and Siamese.

The city's population is divided into several large sections, including Hindu, Mahommedan, Parsee, Jain, and Christian. Among these, the Parsees are one of the smallest groups, numbering approximately 46,000. Despite their small size, they are among the most influential; Parsee millionaires own most of the great business houses and have founded most of the large charities.

## Regional Populations in Asia
In China, the central province of Ho-nan covers an area of 81,000 square miles with a population of about 22,100,000. The province is characterized by extreme population density in certain areas, such as the Hwai-k'ing prefecture—a fertile plain where cereals grow luxuriantly—and the extensive plain running north and south from the Fu-niu Shan in the valley of the Tang-ho and Pai-ho.

In the independent state of Las Bela, located south of Kalat in Baluchistan, the population was 56,109 in 1901. Of this total, 54,040 were Mussulmans. The state's population is supported by highly irrigated and fertile alluvial plains provided by the Purali and Kud rivers.

## Colonial and Settler Populations
Population dynamics in colonial territories vary by the goals of the governing power and the nature of the land.

### Africa and the West Indies
In the West Indies, populations are diverse and largely composed of imported African negroes, who make up four-fifths of the population in Jamaica. The region also includes Chinese and imported East Indian coolies, as well as aboriginal races including Caribs and American Indians of the mainland. There has been intermixture of Portuguese and Spanish blood. The European population in these colonies exceeds 80,000, with an additional 15,000 in Bermuda. In Africa, the population is estimated at 5,211,329 in the south and 2,000,000 in the central region, and is described as chiefly black.

### German and British Territories
In contrast to the English model, German colonies in East and West Africa had no significant white population before the war. German policy favored the concentration of population in the fatherland for industrial and military reasons. Baron von Rechenberg, former governor of German East Africa, noted a shortage of labor in German agriculture and a trend where immigration into Germany exceeded emigration, making the acquisition of territory for settlement impractical.

In southern Rhodesia, South Africa, the capital of Matabeleland, Bulawayo, had a white population of 3,840 in 1904.

### Danish Greenland
The Danish colony in Greenland had an estimated population of 11,893 in 1901, including approximately 300 Europeans. The Eskimo population on the west coast appears to have decreased since the mid-18th century, when Hans Egede estimated it at 30,000 (though this is considered a likely over-estimate), potentially due to infectious diseases like smallpox. While the native population saw a slight increase in the latter half of the 19th century, numbers fluctuate due to immigration from the east coast to the west. The east coast population is generally decreasing, with several hundreds living at Angmagssalik. In the north, settlements in the Franz Josef and Scoresby Fjord regions have either wandered south or died out. A small group known as the "Smith Sound Eskimo" or "Arctic Highlanders" near Cape York numbers about 240.
