# Leipzig and the Career of Johann Sebastian Bach

## Appointment and Professional Life
In 1723, Johann Sebastian Bach moved to Leipzig after finding his previous position at Coethen uninspiring for choral music. In Leipzig, he served as the cantor of the Thomasschule. Despite this new role, he was able to maintain his position as visiting *Kapellmeister* at Coethen, while also holding a similar position at Weissenfels. 

Bach's tenure in Leipzig was the period during which he produced his most colossal achievements, including the *B Minor Mass* and the *Passion according to St Matthew*. While his position was congenial and important, it was not without difficulty. Bach was frequently ruffled by the town councillors of Leipzig, who were shocked by his independent bearing and the "unecclesiastical style" of his compositions.

## Family Life in Leipzig
Bach's personal life during this period was marked by both growth and loss. His first wife died in 1720, leaving him with seven children. In December 1721, he married again. His second wife was a significant help in his work; her musical handwriting eventually became so similar to Bach's that her copies are difficult to distinguish from his autographs. Bach wrote many of his most inspired arias for her beautiful soprano voice.

Together with his second wife, Bach had thirteen children. Of the six sons from this second marriage, he lost four. Of the seven children from his first marriage, only three survived him. Despite the size of his family, his post in Leipzig was more dignified than lucrative, and an inventory of his possessions after his death revealed a history of uncomplaining thrift.

## Musical Relations and Influence
While residing in Leipzig, Bach held a deep admiration for Handel. In 1729, upon hearing that Handel was visiting Halle for a second time on his way from Italy to London, Bach attempted to arrange a meeting. Because Bach was too ill to travel, he sent his son to Halle to invite Handel to Leipzig. However, the errand was not successful, and Bach never met the man he considered his only compeer. Bach's admiration for Handel is further evidenced by his manuscript copy of Handel's *Passion nach Brockes*.

Bach's own approach to music evolved over time. He faced the prospect of his polyphonic art being lost to a new style—one championed by his son Philipp Emanuel—with equanimity, noting that the art had advanced to great heights and the old style no longer pleased modern ears.

## The Bach Sons and Leipzig Education
Leipzig served as a center of education for several of Bach's children:
* **Wilhelm Friedermann Bach** (1710–1784), the eldest and reputedly most gifted son, was educated at Leipzig before becoming an organist in Dresden and a musical director in Halle.
* **Johann Gottfried Bernhard Bach** (1715–1739), the fourth son, was born at Weimar but educated at Leipzig.
* **Johann Christoph Friedrich Bach** (1732–1795), the ninth son, was born at Leipzig and studied at both the university and the Thomasschule.
* **Johann Christian Bach** (1735–1782), the eleventh son, was also born at Leipzig.

## Later Years and Legacy
Bach's career reached a culmination in 1747 when he was summoned to Potsdam to visit Frederick the Great, an invitation resulting from the successes of his son Philipp Emanuel. During this visit, Bach played on Silbermann's newly invented pianofortes and tried the organs of Potsdam's churches. A theme provided by Frederick the Great served as the basis for Bach's *Das musikalische Opfer*.

Following this event, Bach's sight began to fail, and he eventually became perfectly blind. He died of apoplexy on July 28, 1750. At the time of his death, he was regarded as one of the greatest clavier players and organists of his era, though relatively little of his compositions were known. His manuscript works were divided among his sons, and many were lost. It was not until nearly a century later that his work was rediscovered, notably by Mendelssohn, and later systematically published by the *Bach-Gesellschaft*.

## Other Academic Connections to Leipzig
Beyond the Bach family, Leipzig remained a destination for musical study in later years. Francis Edward Bache, an English composer, went to Leipzig in 1853 to study with Plaidy and Hauptmann. His younger brother, Walter Bache, also attended the Leipzig Conservatorium, where he became an excellent pianist.
