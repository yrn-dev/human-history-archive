# Excellency of Moral Virtue, and the Support and Improvement which it Receives

The concept of moral virtue, its foundations, and the mechanisms by which it is supported and improved have been explored across diverse philosophical, religious, and cultural traditions. These perspectives range from the role of discipline and reason to the influence of divine revelation and social codes of honor.

## Philosophical Foundations and Development
In Greek thought, the acquisition of virtue was often linked to study and practice. Xenophon noted that all virtues found among humans increase through learning and discipline. Sokratês specifically identified continence, or self-control, as the very basis of virtue and a prerequisite for the acquisition of knowledge. He characterized the qualities of a good friend not merely by superior knowledge, but by moral excellence, including a grateful disposition, mildness, a self-sufficing temper, and continence.

Other philosophical traditions have linked virtue to reason and the nature of the deity. John Balguy, an English divine and philosopher, sought to establish the theory that virtue consists of conformity to reason. In this view, virtue is the act of following "fitnesses" that emerge from the eternal and immutable relations between agents and objects. Balguy further proposed that this same moral principle directing human life underlies the works of God, suggesting that divine goodness is a regard for harmony, beauty, and order.

## Religious Support and Sanctions
The role of religion in supporting moral virtue has been a subject of significant inquiry. John Balguy argued in his 1726 work that while loving virtue for its own sake is the highest moral principle, religious rewards and punishments serve as valuable and sometimes indispensable sanctions for conduct.

Similarly, during the decline of the Roman Empire, the Emperor Constantine viewed the propagation of the gospel as a means to inculcate public and private virtue. While the laws of the state were often insufficient to restrain vice or inspire virtue, and Pagan superstition provided feeble support for the cause of virtue, Christianity offered a universal system of ethics. This system was recommended as the reason and will of the supreme Deity and was enforced through the sanction of eternal punishments or rewards. The apologist Lactantius suggested that the establishment of Christianity could restore the felicity and innocence of the primitive age.

## Cultural Codes and Social Virtue
Different cultures have identified specific "corner-stones" or "pedestals" upon which moral structures are built. In the context of *Bushido* and the soul of Japan, the duty of loyalty—homage and fealty to a superior—is the distinctive feature and the key-stone of feudal virtues. While personal fidelity exists among various classes of men, it assumes paramount importance in the code of chivalrous honor. Additionally, the philosopher Mencius taught that shame serves as the "soil" for all good morals, manners, and virtue.

In Europe, the ideal of knighthood was infused with spiritual data through Christianity, with Lamartine noting that glory, war, and religion were the three souls of a perfect Christian knight. Even primitive instincts, such as the childhood desire to avoid bullying others or fleeing from challenges, are viewed as the root of civic and military virtues.

## Exemplars of Virtue in History
Historical records highlight individuals whose lives served as models of moral improvement and excellence. Scipio is cited as a model of wisdom and discretion after five years in a school where Polybius inspired in him an aversion to the luxury and licentiousness common among Roman youth. This education led Scipio to a noble disregard for riches and a generosity that he carried to an exalted pitch.

In the Roman era, Aelius Tubero was noted for supporting his poverty gloriously, and his wife was described as being proud of the virtue that kept her husband poor. Other figures who exalted the dignity of human nature through the practice of virtue and the pursuit of truth included Seneca, Tacitus, Plutarch, Galen, Epictetus, and Marcus Antoninus.

Furthermore, the Athenian audience demonstrated a high reverence for morality, justice, and decency. This was evident in their willingness to reprove poets like Euripides when his works seemed inconsistent with these virtues, despite the poet's use of excellent maxims of morality.
