# Managers of the Impeachment of Simon, Lord Lovat

In the English judicial parliamentary procedure known as impeachment, the House of Commons acts as the prosecutor while the House of Lords serves as the judges. To conduct the prosecution, the House of Commons appoints specific individuals known as "managers." One such manager during the impeachment of Simon, Lord Lovat, was William Wildman Shute, 2nd Viscount Barrington.

## The Role of Managers in Impeachment
Impeachment is a strictly judicial process that differs from bills of attainder. The procedure begins when the House of Commons accepts a motion for impeachment; the mover then proceeds to the bar of the House of Lords to impeach the accused in the name of the House of Commons and all the Commons of the United Kingdom. 

The charges are formulated into articles, and the accused is permitted to deliver written answers to these articles. While the entire House in committee attends the trial, the managers appointed by the Commons are responsible for conducting the prosecution. The prosecution is required to confine itself to the charges contained within the articles, although additional articles may be added over time.

## The Trial Process and Judgment
During the trial, the defendant may be represented by counsel. If a peer is impeached for high treason, the president of the House of Lords is the lord high steward; in other instances, the lord chancellor presides. The hearing follows the format of an ordinary trial, allowing the defense to call witnesses and granting the prosecution a right of reply.

At the conclusion of the case, the president puts questions regarding the first article to each peer, starting with the junior baron. Each peer stands uncovered, places his right hand on his breast, and answers "Guilty" or "Not guilty" upon his honour. This process is repeated for each article separately, with the lord high steward providing his own opinion last. If the accused is found guilty, judgment is only passed if the Commons move for it, meaning the Commons retain the power of pardon.

## Simon, Lord Lovat
Simon Fraser, 12th Baron Lovat (born approximately 1667), was a Scottish chief and a Jacobite intriguer. Educated at King's College, Aberdeen, he was proficient in English, idiomatic French, and the Latin classics, frequently quoting Horace and Virgil. 

Lovat's history was marked by deception and betrayal. Early in his career, he recruited three hundred men from his clan for a regiment serving William and Mary, intending to use these trained soldiers for King James. He was also involved in the rape and forced marriage of the widow of the 10th Lord Lovat to secure his succession to the estates. Following a prosecution by Lady Lovat's family, he fled to his Highland strongholds and later to France in July 1702, where he joined the court of St Germain and announced his conversion to the Catholic faith to gain influence.

Simon, Lord Lovat, was ultimately beheaded on April 9, 1747. He is noted as the last person to be beheaded in England.

## William Wildman Shute, 2nd Viscount Barrington
William Wildman Shute, 2nd Viscount Barrington (1717–1793), served as one of the managers for the impeachment of Lord Lovat. The eldest son of the 1st Viscount Barrington, he succeeded to his title in 1734. 

Barrington had a lengthy career in British governance and parliament. He was returned to parliament as a member for Berwick-upon-Tweed in March 1740 and took his seat in the Irish House of Lords in 1745. In 1746, he was appointed as one of the lords commissioners of the admiralty. His subsequent political appointments included:
* Member of parliament for Plymouth (1754)
* Privy councillor and secretary at war (1755)
* Chancellor of the exchequer (1761)
* Treasurer of the navy (1762)
* Secretary at war (returning to the position in 1765 and serving until December 1778)
* Joint postmaster-general (for four months in 1782)

Barrington married Mary Lovell in 1740 but had no children. He died on February 1, 1793, at Becket and was buried in Shrivenham church.
