# Bolivar

The term Bolivar refers both to a former administrative department of Colombia and to the historical figure Simon Bolivar, the liberator of several South American states.

## The Department of Bolivar
Until 1908, Bolivar was a department of Colombia. It is bounded to the north and west by the Caribbean Sea, to the east by the departments of Santander and Magdalena, to the south by Antioquia, and to the southwest by Cauca. The department covers an area of 27,028 square miles, consisting largely of densely wooded low, alluvial plains. These lands are slightly cultivated and are considered unsuited for north European labour.

The population of the department was estimated at 323,097 in 1899. This population is composed largely of mixed races, who in some localities are estimated to constitute four-fifths of the inhabitants. The capital is Cartagena, located on the Caribbean coast, which previously served as Colombia's principal commercial entrepot. Other significant towns include Corozal (9,000 inhabitants) and Lorica (10,596 in 1902) near the western coast, as well as Mompox (8,000) and Barranquilla on the Magdalena river.

## Cartagena
Cartagena is the seaport capital of the department of Bolivar. Its population, by an official estimate in 1905, was 14,000, consisting predominantly of mixed races and blacks. The city's well-to-do white residents typically maintain country houses on the Turbaco hills to escape the coastal heat, as the mean annual temperature in the city is 82 degrees. The port is regarded as very unhealthful for foreigners who are not acclimatized.

The harbour is considered the best on the north coast of South America, featuring an area of about 62.5 square miles with deep, secure anchorages. While the city lacks modern quays, a railway pier on the mainland (lengthened by 120 feet in 1898) allows vessels to moor alongside. Historically, the harbour had two entrances: the Boca Grande and the Boca Chica. After the city was captured three times, the Boca Grande was filled with stone due to the ease with which enemy ships could enter. Consequently, the narrower and more defensible Boca Chica, located seven miles to the south, became the primary entrance.

## Simon Bolivar: The Liberator
Simon Bolivar was the central figure in the independence of three states: Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia. Following his return to Angostura, he obtained the enactment of the fundamental law of December 17, 1819, which united the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar as president. The government seat was provisionally moved to Rosario de Cucuta.

Bolivar led republican armies against the Spaniards. After a six-month armistice concluded at Truxillo on November 25, 1820, hostilities resumed under General Torre. Bolivar achieved a decisive victory at Carabobo, routing the Spaniards and forcing them to retreat to Puerto Cabello, where they surrendered to Paez two years later. On June 29, 1821, Bolivar entered Caracas. On August 30, 1821, the constitution of Colombia was adopted, naming Bolivar president and Santander vice-president.

## Campaigns in Peru and Bolivia
Following the expulsion of Spaniards from Colombia, Bolivar sought to liberate Ecuador and Peru. In June 1822, after a battle at Pichincha led by Sucre, republicans entered Quito. Bolivar then entered Lima in triumph and was invested as dictator with absolute power. Despite temporary setbacks and the re-occupation of Lima by the Spaniards under Canterac, Bolivar reorganized the liberating army. He defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin on August 6, 1824, while Sucre gained a decisive victory at Ayacucho.

In June 1825, Bolivar visited Upper Peru. This region had detached itself from Buenos Aires to form a separate state named Bolivia in Bolivar's honour. The first congress of Bolivia declared Bolivar its perpetual protector in August 1825.

## Political Challenges and Later Life
Bolivar's administration of the freed provinces was often met with slander. In May 1826, he presented a constitution for Bolivia that proposed a president for life with the power to nominate a successor. This proposal alarmed republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires. In Peru, he was accused of attempting to unite Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia into a single state under his perpetual dictatorship.

Internal strife also plagued Colombia. While Santander administered the government ably, Paez in Venezuela entered a state of open rebellion, encouraged by parties in the northern departments seeking separation. Bolivar returned to Bogota on November 14, 1826, assuming extraordinary powers to address the rebellion. He issued decrees for a general amnesty, a national convention at Ocana, and the establishment of constitutional order. Although he formally resigned the presidency in February 1827 to retire to his patrimonial estate, he was urged by Santander and a resolution of congress to resume his functions.

Bolivar spent nine-tenths of his personal fortune in service to his country. Despite having control over the revenues of three nations, he died without any public money in his possession. He is remembered for purifying the administration of justice, fostering national interests, and encouraging the arts and sciences. Monuments to his memory were erected in Caracas (1842), Bogota (1846), Lima (1858), and New York's Central Park (1884).
