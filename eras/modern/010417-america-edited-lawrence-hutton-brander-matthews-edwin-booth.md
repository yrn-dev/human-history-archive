## Lawrence Barrett
Lawrence Barrett (1838–1891) was an American actor born in Paterson, New Jersey, to Irish parents; his original family name was Brannigan. Barrett began his stage career in 1853 in Detroit, appearing as Murad in *The French Spy*. He made his New York debut in December 1856 at the Chambers Street theatre as Sir Thomas Clifford in *The Hunchback*, and by 1858, he was part of the stock company at the Boston Museum.

Beyond the stage, Barrett served as a captain in the 28th Massachusetts infantry regiment during the Civil War. Between 1867 and 1870, he co-managed the California theatre in San Francisco with John McCullough. Barrett's repertoire was extensive, featuring roles such as Hamlet, Macbeth, Lear, Richard III, Shylock, Richelieu, Wolsey, and Benedick. He also performed as Lanciotto in George Henry Boker's *Francesca da Rimini* and James Harebell in *The Man o' Airlie*. 

Barrett had a professional relationship with Edwin Booth, playing Cassius to Booth's Brutus and Othello to Booth's Iago. He performed in London during 1867, 1881, 1883, and 1884, where his portrayal of Richelieu in Bulwer Lytton's drama was regarded as his best part. In addition to his acting, Barrett was a writer; he authored a life of Edwin Forrest for the *American Actors Series* (1881) and a sketch of Edwin Booth in *Edwin Booth and his Contemporaries* (1886). He died on March 20, 1891.

## Edwin Booth
Edwin [Thomas] Booth (1833–1893) was a prominent American actor born in Belair, Maryland. He was the second son of Junius Brutus Booth, a London-born actor who moved to America in 1821. Edwin Booth made his first regular appearance on September 10, 1849, at the Boston Museum, playing Tressel in Colley Cibber's version of *Richard III*. His New York debut occurred on September 27, 1850, at the National theatre in Chatham Street as Wilford in *The Iron Chest*.

Booth's career gained significant momentum after his father's death in 1852. Between 1852 and 1856, he performed in the Sandwich Islands, Australia, and California. He eventually achieved great acclaim for his roles as Richelieu, Richard, and Hamlet—the latter being his most famous part, which he likely played more often than any other actor. He also surpassed his father's success as Sir Giles Overreach in *A New Way to Pay Old Debts*.

In 1862, Booth became the manager of the Winter Garden theatre in New York, producing magnificent Shakespearian productions from 1864 to 1867. In 1868–1869, he established Booth's theatre at 6th Avenue and 23rd Street in New York. He was noted for using the true text of Shakespeare in his productions, a reform that preceded similar movements in England. Although successful artistically, his generous nature led to uneconomical management, and he lost his theatre and fortune in 1874.

Booth later recovered his wealth and converted his Gramercy Park residence into The Players' club for members of the acting profession and other elect individuals. He died in a single apartment within that residence on June 7, 1893. He was married twice: first to Mary Devlin (1860–1863) and later to Mary F. McVicker (1869–1881). His daughter from his first marriage, Edwina Booth Grossman, published *Edwin Booth: Recollections* in 1894.
