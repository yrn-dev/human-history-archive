# Philosophical and Critical Inquiries concerning Christianity

The intersection of philosophical inquiry and Christianity has manifested through various forms of critical analysis, theological synthesis, and historical debate, ranging from the early Roman Empire to the modern era. These inquiries have addressed the validity of sacred texts, the relationship between faith and reason, and the influence of classical philosophy on Christian doctrine.

## Early Roman and Philosophical Critiques
In the early centuries of the Roman Empire, Christianity faced significant scrutiny from philosophers and Roman authorities. Emperors such as Antoninus and Hadrian encountered the apologies of Aristides, Melito, and Justin Martyr. While some, such as Apollonius the Martyr, defended the cause of Christianity before the senate with elegant discourse, other contemporary figures expressed irreverence. Lucian and Celsus, as cited by Origen, expressed such views concerning the Hebrew prophets.

Critical philosophers of the era also questioned the authenticity of predictions. Those who derided the ancient predictions of the Sibyls were seen as capable of detecting "Jewish and Christian forgeries" that had been quoted by church fathers from Lactantius to Justin Martyr. One such example was the "Christian Sybil," who had predicted the ruin of Rome for the year 195 A.U.C. (948). Furthermore, Roman philosophers may have viewed prophecies, such as the prophecy of the Seventy Weeks, with skepticism, as reflected in the questioning tone of Cicero regarding augurations of years.

## Synthesis with Classical Philosophy
The development of Christian theology was deeply influenced by Greek philosophy, particularly that of Plato. The Apostle's endorsement of a fundamental principle of Platonic theology encouraged learned proselytes in the second and third centuries to study the Athenian sage, who was seen as having anticipated discoveries of the Christian revelation. 

This relationship with Plato was complex; his name was used by the orthodox to support truth and by heretics to support error. Dialectics and the work of Plato's commentators were employed to fill the "discreet silence" of inspired writers. In Alexandria, both Christian and philosophical schools debated profound questions regarding the "mysterious Triad" or Trinity, specifically concerning the equality, distinction, generation, and nature of the three divine persons. 

These inquiries often reached the limits of human comprehension. Athanasius confessed that meditating on the divinity of the *Logos* resulted in toilsome and unavailing efforts, where increased thought led to less comprehension and writing led to a decreased ability to express thoughts. Such difficulties are attributed to the nature of the subject—reasoning about spiritual generation and infinite substance—which often leads to contradiction and perplexity for both the theologian and the philosopher.

## Later Intellectual and Critical Developments
In later periods, the inquiry into Christianity shifted toward historical criticism and the defense of faith against new intellectual movements. In the context of biblical studies, critical inquiries into the Gospel of St. Mark led to the conclusion that the other two synoptic Gospels are based upon it, or a document it represents, positioning Mark as an earlier source of information.

By the 19th century, philosophers like Elme Marie Caro (1826–1887) focused their efforts on defending Christianity against modern Positivism. Caro, a professor of philosophy and member of the French Academy, utilized exposition and criticism to maintain the faith against these modern currents.

Additionally, the 18th century saw the publication of works such as the *Palingenesie philosophique* by Charles Bonnet. Portions of this work were translated into English in 1787 under the title *Philosophical and Critical Inquiries concerning Christianity*.

## Cross-Cultural Philosophical Exchange
The intellectual atmosphere of certain courts, such as that of Frederick in Sicily, fostered an environment of unprejudiced detachment and curiosity. Frederick gathered Christian, Jewish, and Moslem philosophers, facilitating the introduction of algebra and Arabic numerals to Christian students. This period of inquiry included the translation of Aristotle and the commentaries of the Arab philosopher Averroes by Michael Scott, demonstrating a broader philosophical openness that transcended strict religious boundaries.
