# Metternich's System of Stability and National Movements

## The Policy of Stability
Following the Congress of Vienna in September 1814, Count Metternich implemented a system of "stability" that he imposed upon Europe for thirty years. This diplomacy was designed to maintain the Habsburg monarchy, a structure described as artificial, unsound, and "rotten," which survived primarily due to the apathy of the populations it oppressed. Metternich believed that the triumph of national aspirations or Liberal principles, whether in Germany or elsewhere in Europe, could shatter the monarchy.

To preserve this status quo, Metternich utilized various diplomatic and political tools. He persuaded Frederick William III that granting a popular constitution would be fatal to the Prussian monarchy. He also utilized the Vienna Final Act and the Carlsbad Decrees specifically to keep Germany quiet to ensure the "sleep of Austria" was not disturbed. Furthermore, the Troppau Protocol served as cover for Austrian aggression for Austrian ends, and Metternich provided moral support to the sultan's "legitimate" authority over "rebel" Greeks to maintain the integrity of Turkey.

## Internal Control and Censorship
To prevent the infiltration of "revolutionary" ideas from outside the empire, the government erected a "Chinese wall" consisting of tariffs and censors. Internally, the growth of Liberalism was countered through a rigid supervision of the press and the re-establishment of clerical control over education. 

The government also managed racial rivalries through the strategic deployment of the army; for example, Italian regiments were stationed in Galicia, Hungarian regiments were garrisoned in Italy, Poles were placed in Austria, and Austrians were stationed in Hungary. While political action was rendered impossible by Metternich's arbitrary and absolutist government, some movements for the revival of national languages and literatures in Croatia and Bohemia were encouraged by exalted circles. This was done in the hope that such interests would divert attention from political goals toward purely scientific ones.

## National Movements in Germany and the Empire
Despite the presence of Metternich's police and censors, national movements in Germany gained an entrance. The revolution of 1848 in Vienna eventually demonstrated that advanced revolutionary views had been making headway. In Germany, the settlement following the Congress of Vienna had seen the Holy Empire replaced by a loose confederation of sovereign states (*Staatenbund*) under Austrian presidency. Metternich viewed this body as a guarantee of peace in central Europe and a means of ensuring Austrian preponderance, as it was "powerful for defence, powerless for offence." However, the refusal to revive the Empire shattered many patriotic hopes within Germany.

## Agitation in the Habsburg Dominions
Popular discontent grew rapidly following the death of Francis I on March 2, 1835. His successor, Ferdinand I, was personally amiable but incapable of ruling due to epilepsy, leading to a veiled regency characterized by divided counsels and administrative weakness.

This environment allowed political agitation to spread:
*   **Hungary:** The diet showed signs of vigorous life, and Magyar ascendancy was roused by growing Slav separatist movements in the south.
*   **Bohemia:** The Czech literary movement evolved into an organized resistance. Nationalists adopted "Repeal" as their watchword, disguising their attacks on the established order as criticisms of English administration in Ireland. By 1845, the estates of Bohemia began opposing the Vienna government, asserting their right to vote on taxes based on the constitution of 1627.
*   **Southern Slavs:** Starting in 1836, the "Illyrian" movement was voiced through Ljudevit Gaj's *Illyrian National Gazette*. Initially seeking a Pan-Slav union, the movement shifted in 1844—following Austrian government interference—toward the revival of a "Triune Kingdom" (Dalmatia, Croatia, and Slavonia) independent of the Hungarian crown.

## Administrative and Economic Stagnation
The government maintained an old system of provincial diets and estates (revived in various regions between 1816 and 1828), but these were not representative. Eligibility was limited to nobles and clergy, with a few town delegates, and their functions were largely restricted to local police matters and registering imperial decrees regarding taxation or recruiting. The right of petition was rarely used and typically met with imperial disfavour.

This political stagnation was mirrored by economic decline. Trade was strangled by internal *octrois* and high frontier tariffs, while agriculture was hampered by the feudal privileges of a noble caste. Additionally, public credit was shaken by the neglect of funding and lavish issues of paper money.
