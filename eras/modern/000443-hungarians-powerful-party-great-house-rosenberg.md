# Hungarians and the House of Rosenberg

## The House of Rosenberg
The Rosenberg family, also known as the *pani z ruze* (lords of the rose), was a powerful lineage whose stronghold was the ancient castle at Krumau (Czech: *Krumlov*), a town in Bohemia situated on the banks of the Moldau (Vitava). Henry II. of Rosenberg, who died in 1310, was the first member of the family to reside at Krumau. His son, Peter I. (d. 1349), subsequently raised the settlement to the rank of a city.

The family's influence in Bohemian history was significant, particularly during the era of the last two members of the line: two brothers named William and Peter Vok. William was created prince of Ursini-Rosenberg in 1556 and died in 1592. Peter Vok was a strong adherent of the Utraquist party. Shortly before his death in 1611, Peter Vok sold Krumau due to the establishment of Jesuits in the surrounding neighborhood. The annals of the family were documented by their librarian, Wenceslas Brezan, whose historical works include biographies of William and Peter of Rosenberg.

Following the Rosenberg era, the lordship of Krumau—one of the most extensive in the monarchy—was purchased by Emperor Rudolph II for his natural son, Julius of Austria. It later passed to Hans Ulrich von Eggenberg in 1622, who was granted an hereditary duchy in 1625. In 1719, the estate passed to Prince Adam Franz Karl of Schwarzenberg, who was created duke of Krumau in 1723; the head of the Schwarzenberg family continues to bear this title.

## Hungarian Political and Military History
Hungarian history during the 19th and early 20th centuries was marked by struggles for independence and internal political divisions. Georg Klapka (1820–1892), born in Temesvár, was a prominent soldier who joined the patriot party during the Hungarian revolution of 1848. Klapka held various staff appointments and commanded an army corps, contributing to victories at Isaszeg, Waitzen, Nagy Sarlo, Komárom, and Kapólna. He later served as minister of war and defended the fortress of Komárom for two months before surrendering on honourable terms following the capitulation of Világos in August. After years of exile in England and Switzerland, and serving as a Prussian major-general in 1866, Klapka returned to Hungary. In 1867, he was elected to the Hungarian Chamber of Deputies as a member of the Deák party.

Political leadership also featured Ferencz Lajos Akos Kossuth (born 1841), the son of Lajos Kossuth. Educated in London and Paris, he spent thirty-three years in Italy working on railway construction while writing pamphlets and articles to maintain the question of Hungarian independence. Upon returning to his native land and taking the oath of allegiance in 1895, he became an active politician. By the autumn of 1898, he led the "Independence Party" (also known as the obstructionists), opposing the administrations of Stephen Tisza, Szápáry, Khuen-Haderváry, and Szell. Following the 1905 elections, which provided his party with a large majority, he helped construct the Wekerle ministry.

## Governance and the Austro-Hungarian Relationship
The relationship between the crown and the Hungarians involved complex negotiations regarding national identity and military control. In October 1903, Count Stephen Tisza formed a cabinet based on a "programme of the nine," which offered concessions to Magyar nationalist feeling. These included the establishment of military scholarships for Magyar youths, the introduction of a two-year service system, and the transfer of Hungarian officers from Austrian to Hungarian regiments. While the programme asserted that the cabinet's responsibility extended to the crown's military prerogatives, it excluded the specific issue of military language for "weighty political reasons." The Liberal party adopted an addendum stating that the king retained the right to determine the language of command and service in the Hungarian army.

In the broader Austrian political landscape, figures like Dr. Lueger, a leader of the Christian Socialists in Vienna, exerted influence through violent attacks on Hungarians and Jews. Meanwhile, minister-president Count Badeni sought to carry through the *Ausgleich* (composition) with Hungary, viewing this mission as the primary objective to which all other political matters were subordinated.

## Royal Successions in Hungary and Bohemia
The crowns of Hungary and Bohemia were frequently linked through royal succession. Albert II (Duke Albert V of Austria) married Elizabeth, the daughter of Sigismund, king of Bohemia and Hungary; upon Sigismund's death, Albert assumed both crowns. Their son, Ladislaus Posthumus, succeeded to the kingdoms of Hungary and Bohemia and the duchy of Austria.

Later, Vladislav was elected king of Hungary in 1490 and died there in 1516. He was succeeded by his son, Louis, who had been crowned king of Bohemia at age three. Louis arrived in Bohemia from Hungary in 1522, at which time he dismissed several state officials, including the powerful burgrave Zdenek Leo, lord of Rozmital.
