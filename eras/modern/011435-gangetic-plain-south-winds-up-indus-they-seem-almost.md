# The Gangetic Plain and the Indus Region

The Gangetic plain and the Indus basin represent two of the primary geographical and hydrological systems of the Indian subcontinent, characterized by distinct climatic patterns, varied topography, and significant historical movement of peoples.

## Climate and Wind Patterns
The region is heavily influenced by the south-west monsoon. While the south-west monsoon does not generally extend over the land as a south-west wind, the air currents flowing from the sea are diverted toward areas of least pressure, losing much of their original force and dissipating. As these winds move northward over India, they transition into easterly and south-easterly winds over the north-eastern portion of the Gangetic plain and blow as south winds up the Indus.

By the time these winds reach the northern extremity of the great Indian plain, their northward velocity is almost entirely exhausted. Consequently, they hardly penetrate the Indus basin or the ranges of the Himalaya, nor are they felt on the table-lands of Afghanistan. The progress of these winds is further obstructed by the Himalaya and the mountains branching into the Malay peninsula.

Rainfall varies significantly across these regions. At the head of the Gangetic plain, rainfall is reduced to approximately 25 or 30 inches. In contrast, the eastern part of the Ganges delta averages between 75 and 80 inches, with Calcutta recording about 64 inches. The Khasi hills, located about 100 miles north-east of Calcutta, experience some of the heaviest rainfall in the world due to their abrupt front facing moist winds from the Bay of Bengal, with annual records reaching at least 600 inches. Conversely, the tract along the Indus is almost rainless until it comes within 60 or 80 miles of the Himalaya. The irregular and small rainfall in Sind and along the Indus is attributed to a lack of obstacles in the path of vapour-bearing winds, which carry uncondensed rain further north to the western Himalaya and Afghanistan.

## The Indus Basin and Surrounding Territories
The Indus River serves as a major geographical marker and transport route. In the District of Dera Ismail Khan, which covers 3403 square miles, the Indus is navigable by native boats for its 120-mile course within the district. This area was formerly divided by the river into two portions; the trans-Indus tract was allotted to the North-West Frontier Province in 1901, while the cis-Indus tract remained under Punjab jurisdiction. The cis-Indus portions of the Bannu and Dera Ismail Khan districts now form the Punjab district of Mianiwali.

To the east of the Indus in Dera Ismail Khan lies the *Kachi*, a wide tract exposed to river action, beyond which is a barren, sparsely cultivated desert plain inhabited by nomadic herdsmen. The population of this district in 1901 was 252,379, consisting primarily of Baluch and Pathan Mahommedans, with the Pathan element predominating.

Further south, the plain of Sind is covered by wind-blown sand and alluvium. The Manchar Lake is located on the right bank of the Indus, reaching a length of 20 miles and a breadth of 10 miles during inundations.

## The Gangetic Plain and Bengal
The Gangetic plain extends into districts such as Birbhum in the Burdwan division of Bengal. Birbhum, covering 1752 square miles, is situated partly on the hills and bounded to the south by the river Ajai. The district is divided between an ordinary alluvial plain of the Gangetic delta in the east and undulating beds of laterite covered with small scrub jungle in the west. The principal rivers—the Ajai, Bakheswar, and Mor (or Maurakshi)—are hill streams navigable only during the rains. As of 1901, the population of Birbhum was 902,280. The region's primary industries include lac factories and the weaving and spinning of silk, specifically from jungle or tussur silkworms.

## Frontier Geography and Migration
The plains of India are separated from the Afghan highlands by mountain barriers. The Khyber Pass is the most strategic of these, a narrow defile of limestone and shale cliffs between 600 and 1000 feet high. For 2000 years, it has served as a gateway for various conquerors, including Alexander the Great, Genghiz Khan, Timur, Baber, and Mahmud of Ghazni.

To the west of the Indus plains lies Baluchistan, a sparsely populated area. It is divided by a railway running from the Indus to Quetta. North of this line are the highlands of the Suliman system, the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander. South of the railway is a block of territory roughly 300 miles square, primarily the home of the Baluch and Brahui.

Historical accounts from Sanscrit epics describe a fair, beef-eating people who migrated from Persia into the plain of North India, conquering their way toward the Indus before spreading across India. During this expansion, they conquered the dark Dravidians and acquired much from them, though they appeared to lose their bardic tradition.
