# Nicolas Boileau-Despreaux

Nicolas Boileau-Despreaux (1636–1711) was a prominent French poet and critic who is credited as the first figure to reduce French versification to a set of rules and to emphasize the importance of workmanship for its own sake. Born on November 1, 1636, in Paris, Boileau was the fifteenth child of Gilles Boileau, a clerk in the parlement. Following the death of his father in 1657, which left him a small fortune, Boileau devoted himself to letters, driven by a lifelong passion described as "the hatred of stupid books."

## Early Life and Career
Boileau's early education took place at the College de Beauvais, followed by studies in theology at the Sorbonne. He eventually transitioned to the study of law and was called to the bar on December 4, 1656. However, he quickly recoiled from the legal profession in disgust, citing the prevalence of chicanery. 

His literary career gained momentum in 1666 with the publication of *Satires du Sieur D....*, which included seven satires and the *Discours au roi*. Beginning in 1669, he published epistles that were more polished in style and maturer in thought than his earlier satires. These *Epitres* earned him the favor of King Louis XIV. When the king inquired which of his verses were his best, Boileau diplomatically identified some unprinted lines written in honor of the monarch as his "least bad." Consequently, he was granted a pension of 2,000 livres.

## Major Works and Literary Theory
In 1674, Boileau published two of his masterpieces, *Le Lutrin* and *L'Art poetique*. *Le Lutrin* is a mock heroic poem that later served as a model for Alexander Pope's *The Rape of the Lock*. 

*L'Art poetique*, written in imitation of Horace's *Ars Poetica*, established a code for future French verse. The work is divided into four books:
* **First and Last Books:** These focus on general precepts, primarily emphasizing the rule of *bon sens*.
* **Second Book:** This section addresses the ode, the epigram, the elegy, the satire, and the pastoral.
* **Third Book:** This section focuses on epic and tragic poetry.

Beyond providing rules for poetic language, Boileau analyzed different types of verse composition and the principles specific to each. He was particularly concerned with the dangers of purely descriptive poetry, which he believed could become frigid, sterile, or dead if it lacked intensity. To avoid this, he advised poets to be "riches et pompeux" in their descriptions and to use numerous figures to create a "riante image," while warning against "stérile" abundance and unnecessary detail.

## Influence on English Literature
Boileau's maxims exerted a significant influence on English literature. This occurred through a translation revised by Dryden and through Alexander Pope's *Essay on Criticism*, which is described as a magnificent imitation of Boileau's work. While Boileau's influence on English letters was strong, it was less durable than his impact on French literature.

Furthermore, the critical rules derived by Boileau, along with those of Pope, Dryden, and others, were upheld by Dr. Johnson, who regarded the decisions of such "estimable critics" and the ancients as paramount.

## Critical Reception and Legacy
Boileau is recognized for his role in destroying the exaggerated reputations of mediocre writers of his era, though it is noted that his own judgment was occasionally at fault. While he was a great critic in verse, he is not considered a great poet, and some argue that his rules tended to make poetic efforts too mechanical.

Despite periods of undue depreciation, recent writers have rehabilitated Boileau's critical work, noting that most of his criticisms were substantially adopted by those who followed him. He is remembered as an honest, benevolent, and outspoken man.

His works were published in numerous editions during his life, including the 1701 *Oeuvres diverses*, known as the "favourite" edition. Later critical texts were established by Berriat Saint-Prix (1830–1837), who utilized approximately 350 editions, and were subsequently edited by Paul Cheron and reprinted by Garnier *freres* in 1860.
