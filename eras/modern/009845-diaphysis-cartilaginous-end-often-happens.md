# Diaphysis and Cartilaginous Bone Structures

## Anatomy of Long Bones in Growing Persons
In children and growing individuals, the long bones are composed of a shaft, known as the diaphysis, and cartilaginous ends. Bone develops within these cartilaginous ends. In cases of injury, the end of the bone may become detached, a specific type of fracture referred to as diastasis. Regardless of the quality of treatment, such a fracture may result in the stiffness of the neighboring joint or an arrest in the growth of the bone.

## Septic Osteomyelitis
Septic osteomyelitis is an infective disease. Its starting point is the delicate growing tissue that has been recently deposited between the cartilaginous end and the diaphysis (the main part of the shaft of the bone). The earliest complaints of pain associated with this condition often occur just above or below the wrist, the elbow, the ankle, or the knee.

The disease is characterized by micro-organisms that germinate in the damaged bone and enter the blood-stream, where they can develop and cause blood-poisoning. This poses a significant risk to surgeons; if a surgeon pricks his finger while operating on a case of septic osteomyelitis, his blood may be poisoned, potentially leading to the loss of his hand, finger, or life. In infants, these germs frequently move into the neighboring joint, resulting in a serious condition known as acute arthritis of infants.

## Surgical Treatment and Complications
Once septic osteomyelitis is recognized, the affected area must be disinfected with a solution of corrosive sublimate, and an incision should be made down to the bone to scrape out the diseased tissue. A sub-periosteal abscess may strip a considerable area of the bone bare, and necrosis is likely to follow. 

Depending on the severity, the surgeon may need to open the shaft of the bone for much of its length to clear it of pus and germs. In some instances, the entire diaphysis—the section of bone located between the joint-ends—may be found loose within a large abscess cavity. In such cases, or in instances of grave constitutional disturbance and long-continued suppuration, immediate amputation of the limb may be necessary to save the patient's life. If several bones are affected simultaneously, inflammatory engorgement and devastating abscess can cause large pieces of bone to be killed outright, a condition termed multiple necrosis. Septic ostitis can be distinguished from rheumatism or erysipelas by the presence of tenderness and central thickening.

## Other Cartilaginous Structures in Vertebrates
Cartilaginous tissues appear in various other anatomical structures across different species:

*   **Fish:** In cartilaginous fishes, the branchiae lack an operculum. Aristotle noted that these fishes are viviparous, with the exception of the sea-devil. In the chondrocranium (the cartilaginous trough protecting the brain), there is a parachordal portion and a praechordal portion developed from lateral cartilaginous rods called trabeculae cranii. These connect to form a primitive cranium that is open dorsally but nearly complete ventrally and laterally. Additionally, cartilaginous capsules develop around the auditory and olfactory organs. In Teleostomes, expanded bases of arcualia fuse to form cartilaginous centra.
*   **Birds:** The trachea (windpipe) is strengthened by rings that are often osseous or cartilaginous. In petrels and humming-birds, a longitudinal cartilaginous septum partly divides the trachea. The keel, or carina sterni, is formed as a direct cartilaginous outgrowth of the sternum's body. While absent in Ratitae, traces of a cartilaginous keel are present in ostrich embryos.
*   **Humans:** The parotid salivary gland's upper extremity embraces the lower surface of the cartilaginous ear-conch.
