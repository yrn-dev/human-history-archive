# Baptist Church Government and Organization

## Principles of Governance
In the matter of church government, Baptists align with the Congregationalists in the belief that every separate church is complete in itself. Consequently, each individual church possesses the power to establish its own regulations and choose its own ministers. These actions are taken to best serve the purpose of the church's existence, which is the advancement of the kingdom of Christ.

While this autonomy is the general rule, a small section of the denomination maintains that a complete organization for every separate church requires a "plurality of elders" or pastors. This specific organizational trait is a distinctive peculiarity of the churches in the north of England and Scotland known as Scotch Baptists.

## Associations and Unions
The majority of Baptist churches are united in associations that are formed voluntarily and determined by geographical limits. In Great Britain and Ireland, these associations, along with churches that are not connected to them, are united in the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland. Formed in 1813 by the Particular Baptists, this union does not exert authoritative action over the separate churches. Instead, one of its primary functions is the collection of information for the benefit of all member churches.

The Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland eventually became the sole body for the denomination in 1891, following the union of the General and Particular Baptist denominations under the influence of Dr. John Clifford. Despite this, the Union remains purely voluntary; some powerful and prosperous churches choose to remain aloof from the organization.

## Other Organizational Bodies
Outside of the Baptist Union, other bodies exist, including "Strict" Baptist churches which function as a separate denomination. These are often linked by geographical associations, such as the Suffolk and Norfolk Association of Particular Baptist Churches or the Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches. The Strict Baptists are further divided into the "Vessel" and "Standard" parties, named after their respective organs, the *Earthen Vessel* and the *Gospel Standard*.

On a global scale, the Baptist World Alliance was established in 1905 during the first Baptist World Congress in London. The alliance's constitution emphasizes the "essential oneness" of Baptist churches worldwide in the Lord Jesus Christ as their God and Saviour. The alliance promotes cooperation, service, and fellowship while explicitly recognizing the independence of each particular church and avoiding the assumption of functions belonging to existing organizations.

## Historical Developments in Governance
The history of Baptist organization includes various experiments and shifts in leadership. In the 18th century, "Separate" Baptists in Virginia and the Carolinas experienced rapid growth. For a brief period starting in 1774, some members of the Virginia association believed the apostolic office should be perpetuated, leading to the ordination of Samuel Harris as an apostle, followed by Elijah Craig and John Waller. This arrangement was eventually abandoned.

To preserve harmony and align with "Regular" Baptists of the Philadelphia type, the "Separate" Baptists adopted the Calvinistic Philadelphia Confession of Faith, though they included provisions against a construction that was too rigid. In 1783, when the General Association was subdivided, a General Committee consisting of delegates from each district association was created to manage matters for the good of the society, specifically focusing on the separation of church and state and religious equality.

## Denominational Statistics and Reach
By 1909, the Baptist presence in the United Kingdom included 3,046 churches, 4,124 chapels, and 424,008 members. The infrastructure supported 2,078 pastors in charge, 5,615 local preachers, and 58,687 Sunday school teachers for 578,344 scholars.

The movement expanded globally throughout the 19th century. In Europe, churches were formed via missionaries in Roman Catholic countries (France in 1832, Italy in 1866 and 1884, and Spain in 1888). In Protestant countries and Russia, the movement grew without such intervention, beginning with J. G. Oncken's church in Hamburg in 1834. This led to the formation of churches in Denmark (1839), Holland and Sweden (1848), Switzerland (1849), Norway (1860), Austria and Rumania (1869), Hungary (1871), and Bulgaria (1884), as well as Finland and Russia in the 1850s and 1860s.

In the British colonies, unions of Baptist churches were established in New Zealand, Canada (four unions), South Africa, and several Australian states, including New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Tasmania, Western Australia, and South Australia.
