# Czech Bohemia

Bohemia is a region of the Austrian dominions characterized by significant industrial development, rich natural resources, and a complex demographic struggle between Czech and German populations. As of 1900, the population of Bohemia was 6,318,280, with a density of 315 inhabitants per square mile. This population was composed of approximately 65% Czechs and 35% Germans. Geographically, the Czech population is concentrated in the south, south-east, and the middle of the country, while Germans are primarily located in the large towns and near the borders, particularly in the north and west.

## Political Struggles and National Identity
Bohemia is described as the richest part of the Austrian dominions. A strong sense of national identity exists among the Czechs, who maintain a lively recollection of the period before they were Austrian. This sentiment was encapsulated by the statement from Palacky: "We existed before Austria; we shall continue to exist after it is gone."

A significant political party, led by Rieger and Palacky, sought the full restoration of the Bohemian monarchy. Their demands included the inclusion of Silesia and Moravia, the coronation of the emperor as the king of Bohemia in Prague, and a status within the monarchy similar to that of Hungary. This movement was supported not only by Czechs but also by several high-ranking nobles of German descent, such as Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg and Count Leo Thun. In 1868, a "declaration" signed by eighty-one members, including bishops and feudal nobles, officially stated these claims and contended that no foreign assembly was qualified to impose taxes in Bohemia.

## Ethnic and Linguistic Conflict
The relationship between the Czech and German populations has been marked by vehemence and mutual suspicion. Germans in Bohemia, fearing Czech control, became ardent advocates of centralization. Conversely, the Czechs sought to increase their influence, leading to a "constant encroachment" on German positions over a twenty-year period.

Linguistic disputes were central to this conflict. While educated Czechs were generally bilingual, German parents in Bohemia ceased teaching their children Czech after 1867. This created a professional divide, as bilingual Czechs could more easily obtain appointments in districts requiring the Czech language, leading Germans to view mandates for the use of Czech as a means of excluding them from certain posts.

Government ordinances eventually shifted the linguistic balance. An 1880 ordinance required government offices and law courts to deal with business in the language in which it was introduced. In 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Prague and Bruenn. Furthermore, Czech elementary and middle schools were founded, and in 1882, Czech classes were introduced at the university of Prague, an event viewed by Germans as a desecration of the oldest German university.

## Governance and Parliamentary Obstruction
The political deadlock in Bohemia frequently paralyzed the Austrian parliament. Between 1903 and 1905, Dr. von Koerber was unable to secure budget votes and had to rely on imperial ordinances under paragraph 14 of the constitution. Koerber attempted a compromise in 1902 and 1903 by proposing the division of Bohemia into ten districts (five Czech, three German, and two mixed), but this was rejected. The Czechs demanded that both languages be placed on an equal footing for all official purposes throughout Bohemia.

Czech political tactics included the use of "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction; one parliamentary sitting lasted 57 hours without interruption. In response, various German parties—including the Christian Socialists, German Populists, German Progressists, and Constitutional Landed Proprietors—formed a joint executive committee to protect German racial interests.

Internal Czech politics were also divided. The "Old Czechs" were eventually superseded by the "Young Czechs," who were more impatient for results and opposed a 1890 agreement that would have separated German and Czech districts, fearing it would threaten the unity of the Bohemian country.

## Regional Centers and Administration
Budweis (Czech *Bud[ve]jovice*) serves as the principal commercial center of South Bohemia. Located at the junction of the Maltsch and the Moldau, it is a river port and an important railway junction. Its industry is varied, producing chemicals, paper, machinery, bricks, tiles, matches, and black-lead pencils. It is also known for its beer, much of which is exported to America. Architecturally, the town features a Renaissance-style town hall built in 1730, a cathedral from 1500, and the Marien-Kirche. While Budweis was a center for a German enclave, the Czech element increased steadily, reaching 60% of the population by 1908. Nearby stands the castle of Frauenberg, built in the mid-19th century by Prince Schwarzenberg based on the model of Windsor Castle.

Administratively, Bohemia is divided into the archbishopric of Prague and three bishoprics: Leitmeritz, Koniggratz, and Budweis. The population is overwhelmingly Roman Catholic, comprising 96% of the total.
