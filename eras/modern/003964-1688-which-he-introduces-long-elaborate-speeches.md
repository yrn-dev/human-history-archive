The use of elaborate speeches is a recurring theme in the analysis of historical narratives. In the field of English scholarship, Joshua Barnes (1654–1712), a regius professor of Greek born in London, authored a *History of that Most Victorious Monarch Edward III* in 1688. In this specific work, Barnes is noted for introducing long and elaborate speeches into his narrative.

Similarly, in ancient Greek history, the congress of Gela was described at considerable length by Timæus in his twenty-first book. Timæus composed an elaborate speech for Hermokratês, though this particular piece of writing was later condemned by Polybius as empty declamation. 

The nature of elaborate rhetoric is also discussed regarding the works of Thucydidês. Dionysius Halikarnassus criticized Thucydidês for including an elaborate funeral harangue for Periklês, arguing that the number of dead buried on that occasion was too small and the actions too insignificant to warrant such a speech. Dionysius suggested that other events, such as the loss of the Sicilian army, were more worthy of such a harangue. However, it is noted that Periklês' speech was a real speech that was heard and reproduced, although it was likely dressed up by Thucydidês.
