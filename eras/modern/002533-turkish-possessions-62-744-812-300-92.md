# Turkish Possessions

## Geographic and Demographic Overview
As of 1909, the Turkish possessions in the Balkan Peninsula included Albania, Macedonia, and other territories. These possessions covered an area of 62,744 square miles and had a population of 5,812,300. The population density for these regions was 92.6 persons per square mile. Additionally, the Sanjak of Novibazar was a Turkish possession, encompassing 2,840 square miles with a population of 153,000 and a density of 53.5 persons per square mile.

## Religious Composition and Conversions
The religious landscape of the Peninsula was significantly altered following the Turkish conquest due to numerous conversions to Islam. Consequently, the Mahommedan population in the region largely exceeded the purely Turkish element. The total Moslem population of the Peninsula was approximately 3,300,000.

Conversion rates varied by nationality:
* **Albanians:** More than half of the Albanian nation adopted the creed of the conquering race.
* **Bosnians:** 35% of the inhabitants of Bosnia and Herzegovina converted to Islam.
* **Bulgarians:** Conversions were less numerous among Bulgars. The Bulgarian Mahommedans, known as Pomaks, inhabit certain districts in northern Bulgaria and the valleys of Rhodope; they are considered numerically insignificant.
* **Greeks:** Followers of Islam among the Greeks were almost entirely confined to Crete.

## Christian Communities and Administration
The majority of the Christian population belonged to the Orthodox Church. While the oecumenical patriarch at Constantinople served as the nominal head and held precedence over other ecclesiastical dignitaries, the churches of Greece, Montenegro, Servia, and Bulgaria were autocephalous in reality. 

The Bulgarian church held a unique position; its spiritual chief, the exarch based in Constantinople, maintained control over Bulgarian prelates within both the kingdom of Bulgaria and European Turkey. Conversely, Greek prelates located in Bulgaria remained subject to the patriarch.

The Turkish government recognized only the heads of the various religious communities as representatives of the Christian population. These leaders supervised Christian schools and held seats on local administrative councils. While various branches of the Orthodox Church sought separate organizations within Turkish dominions, these efforts were driven by political motives rather than differences in dogma.

Other Christian denominations included:
* **Roman Catholic Church:** This included some Gheg tribes in Albania, approximately 21% of Bosnians, a small number of Bulgarians in Macedonia and the kingdom, and a few Greeks in the islands.
* **Uniate Church:** A certain number of Bulgars at Kukush in Macedonia and other locations formed a "uniate" church, which preserved its traditions while accepting the dogma and authority of Rome.

## Military and Strategic Presence
Turkish administrative and military presence extended beyond the Balkans. In Jerusalem, the citadel—also known as the Tower of David or Castellum Pisanum—served as the residence of the Turkish aga. This position commanded a prospect of Judea, Arabia, and the Dead Sea. Additionally, the Turkish fleet maintained a presence in the Black Sea.
