# Indian Surveying and Geographical Mapping

The progress of scientific geography on the northern and north-western frontiers of India has been marked by a wide extension of scientific geographical mapping rather than simple exploration. This professional labor has resulted in a series of geographical maps spanning from the Oxus to the Indus, and from the Indus to the Euphrates.

## Frontier Mapping and Professional Assistance
The development of these maps was facilitated by several key geopolitical events and systematic efforts, including:
* The Afghan war of 1878-80.
* The Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1885.
* The occupation of Chitral and Gilgit.
* The extension of boundaries between Baluchistan and Persia, as well as those east and north of Afghanistan.
* A systematic survey of Baluchistan that progressed steadily beginning in 1880.

In these professional labors, Indian surveyors received assistance from several scientific geographers. In Persia, they were assisted by Major Percy M. Sykes, Captain H.B. Vaughan, and General Sir A. Houtum Schindler. In the Hindu Kush and Kafiristan, assistance was provided by Cockerill and Sir George Robertson.

## Trigonometrical and Topographical Surveys
A trigonometrical survey of British India commenced in 1800, creating a survey system that is in most respects equal to those of most European states. Since 1862, these surveys have been extended beyond the boundaries of India proper. The scales used for these surveys vary based on the nature of the country or the necessity of the case:
* **Revenue surveys** for land settlement are published at a scale of 1:4000.
* **Topographical maps** typically utilize a scale of 1:63,360.
* **The Indian Atlas**, which includes the Malay Peninsula and Ceylon, was begun in 1827 on a scale of 1:255,660, though many of its sheets remain unpublished.
* **The official map of India**, first published in 1903, uses a scale of 1:1,000,000.
* **Provincial maps**, including those of Burma, are produced on a scale of 1:2,827,520.

Additionally, the Federal Malay States, the Straits Settlements, and Ceylon maintain their own surveyors-general. Former official of the Indian survey, Mr. J. McCarthy, organized a regular survey in Siam between 1881 and 1883, which contributed to the determination of the Franco-Siamese frontier in 1906.

## Exploration in Asia and Tibet
Beyond the limits of actual surveys, scientific geographical exploration in Asia has focused heavily on the central depression and the elevated table-land of Tibet. Native surveyors attached to the Indian survey, known as Pundits, significantly expanded knowledge of the great plateau.

Pundit Nain Singh explored the Upper Brahmaputra and the sources of the Indus between 1865 and 1867. Later, from 1874 to 1875, he traveled from the eastern frontiers of Kashmir to Lhasa, spending several months in the city, and visiting the Tengri Nor lake. Pundit Krishna undertook a journey from 1879 to 1882 that extended from Lhasa northwards through Tsaidam to Sachu (or Saitu) in Mongolia. Krishna also traveled through eastern Tibet to Darchendo (or Tachienlu) on the borders of China and the high road to Peking. Although he failed to reach India via Upper Assam, he returned to the vicinity of Lhasa and crossed the Himalayas using a more westerly route.

## Historical Figures in Indian Surveying
James Rennell, who served as the surveyor-general of India between 1742 and 1830, published the *Bengal Atlas* in 1781. Rennell is noted for his sagacious arrangement of vast amounts of information gathered by others and British travellers within Africa and India.
