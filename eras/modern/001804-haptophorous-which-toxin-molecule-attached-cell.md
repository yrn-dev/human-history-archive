# Haptophorous

The haptophorous group is a specific atom group within a toxin molecule responsible for the attachment of the toxin to the protoplasm of a living cell. This concept was developed through the work of Ehrlich, specifically during his studies on diphtheria toxin, to explain the mechanism by which toxins interact with living tissues.

## Dual Composition of Toxin Molecules
According to the theory established by Ehrlich, a toxin molecule is composed of at least two primary atom groups that serve distinct functions:

*   **The Haptophorous Group:** This is the combining group. Its role is to attach the toxin molecule to the cell protoplasm.
*   **The Toxophorous Group:** This group possesses a ferment-like action on the living molecule. Once the toxin is attached, the toxophorous group produces a disturbance that results in toxic symptoms.

The interaction between these groups and the living cell determines susceptibility to a toxin. For a toxin to be effective, there must be both a chemical affinity of certain tissues for the toxin molecule (facilitated by the haptophorous group) and a sensitiveness to the actions of the toxophorous group. Conversely, non-susceptibility occurs if either of these two properties is absent. For example, the nervous system of the fowl exhibits immunity against tetanus toxin because it possesses little combining affinity for it.

## Interaction with Antitoxins
The haptophorous group is central to the process of neutralization by antitoxins. Antitoxins act directly on the toxin chemically or physically. One established view is that the antitoxin combines with the toxin to create a body whose combining affinities are satisfied.

If the haptophorous (combining) group of a toxin molecule has its affinity satisfied through union with an antitoxin, the toxin can no longer combine with living cells. This renders the toxin harmless. Evidence for the distinct nature of these two groups is found in the fact that a toxin's toxic action can be diminished while it still requires the same amount of antitoxin for neutralization; this suggests that the toxophorous group is more labile than the haptophorous group.

## Relation to Side-Chain Theory
The function of the haptophorous group is further explained by Ehrlich's "side-chain" (*Seitenkette*) theory. In this model, living protoplasm (a biogen molecule) consists of a central atom group (*Leistungskern*) with numerous secondary atom groups, or side-chains, which have unsatisfied chemical affinities. 

These side-chains are the means by which other molecules, such as those involved in nutrition, are added to the living molecule. Toxin molecules use these side-chains to attach to the protoplasm, which subsequently brings the living molecules under the influence of the toxin's toxophorous groups.

In the production of antitoxin, this combination occurs in amounts insufficient to produce serious symptoms. Because the combination is somewhat firm, the side-chains that have combined with the toxin are lost to the cell and are thrown off. When fresh toxin is introduced, the process repeats, and the regeneration of side-chains increases.
