# Army

## Reorganization of the Indian Army
In 1904, Lord Kitchener undertook a reconstitution of the Indian army, which involved the abolition of old commands to make way for new army corps. The old Bombay command was replaced by the Western army corps, led by a lieutenant-general and divided into three divisions under major-generals:

*   **The 4th Division:** Headquartered at Quetta, this division comprises troops in the Sind and Quetta districts.
*   **The 5th Division:** Headquartered at Mhow, this division includes the previous districts of Bundelkhand, Nerbudda, Nagpur, Deesa, and Mhow, as well as the Bombay district north of the Tapti. It consists of three brigades located at Jhansi, Jubbulpore, and Nasirabad.
*   **The 6th Division:** Headquartered at Poona, this division consists of three brigades located at Aden, Ahmednagar, and Bombay. It comprises the previous Poona district, the Bombay district south of the Tapti, the Dharwar and Aurungabad districts, and the Belgaum district north of the Tungabhadra.

Similarly, the old Bengal command was abolished and replaced by the Eastern army corps, which encompasses all troops from Assam to Meerut. The 8th division's boundaries include the former Presidency, Assam, Allahabad, and Oudh districts. In Bengal, the troops now consist only of the Presidency brigade, headquartered at Fort William.

## Lord Kitchener's Military Career and Roles
Horatio Herbert Kitchener, Viscount Kitchener (born June 24, 1850), was a British field marshal and the son of Lieut.-Colonel H. H. Kitchener. After entering the Royal Military Academy, Woolwich, in 1868, he was commissioned as a second lieutenant in the Royal Engineers in 1871. His early career included survey work in Palestine and Cyprus. In 1883, as a captain, he was attached to the Egyptian army during its reorganization.

Kitchener served on the staff of the British expeditionary force on the Nile in 1884 and later served as commandant at Suakin from 1886 to 1888, where he was severely wounded during the action of Handub. He commanded a brigade in the actions of Toski and Gamaizieh in 1888 and served as the army's adjutant-general from 1889 to 1892. 

In 1892, Kitchener became the sirdar of the Egyptian army. After completing the reorganization of the khedive's forces, he formed an expeditionary force at the military frontier of Wady Halfa. His administrative work in preparing the advance into the Sudan was widely admired. He won the action of Ferket on June 7, 1896, and advanced the railway and frontier to Dongola. In 1898, as a British major-general, he commanded a united force that stormed the Mahdist zareba on the river Atbara on April 8.

## British Army Administration and Strategy (1914)
During the onset of the Great War, Lord Kitchener served as the Secretary of State for War. Under his direction, recruiting efforts were launched, resulting in nearly two million men being under arms before Christmas. At the start of the conflict, Britain's military consisted of a small regular army—primarily a garrison for Oversea Dominions—and a Territorial Force intended for home defence. An Expeditionary Force of approximately 100,000 expert soldiers was placed on the left of the French line.

Kitchener's strategic planning included the following:
*   **Anglo-Indian Army:** Kitchener stated that in cases of grave need, six of the nine field divisions could be withdrawn from India. This would involve moving two native regiments out of India for every British regiment. Such a force of 100,000 could be brought to France via Marseilles by the fortieth day.
*   **Home Defence:** To secure Great Britain, plans included embodying the whole Territorial force, calling for volunteers with military experience, and raising a compulsory levy of 500,000 men.
*   **International Coordination:** In September 1914, Kitchener arranged for officers from the War Office and the Admiralty to plan the seizure of the Gallipoli Peninsula by a Greek army to allow the British Fleet access to the Sea of Marmora.

## Operational Challenges and Logistics
In October 1914, Lord Kitchener's plans were central to the defense of Antwerp. Churchill communicated Kitchener's plans to Belgian authorities to emphasize the importance of delaying German forces. During this period, differences arose between Kitchener and the Commander-in-Chief, Sir John French, specifically regarding the exclusion of General Rawlinson's forces from the main British army.

Logistical complexities also affected the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps. Due to changes requested by the New Zealand Government, the Australian convoy's sailing was delayed by three weeks. Furthermore, a rebellion in South Africa led the Cabinet to arrange for the corps to travel via the Cape instead of the Suez Canal. Ultimately, the convoy proceeded to Colombo, where it was determined that the corps was more urgently needed in Egypt than at the Cape. The convoy departed on November 1 under the escort of the *Sydney*, *Melbourne*, *Ibuki*, and *Minotaur*.
