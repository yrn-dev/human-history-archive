# The Bonaparte Family

The Bonaparte family has been the subject of extensive historical documentation, focusing on its origins, its imperial branch, and the individual lives of its members, including Napoleon I and his siblings.

## Historical Documentation of the Family
Various scholarly works have traced the genealogy and history of the family. D. Morali's *Storia genealogica della famiglia Bonaparte* (Florence, 1846) examines the family's origins and the extinction of the branch that existed in the city of S. Miniato. Other foundational texts include *Le antichita dei Bonaparte*, which features an introduction by L. Beretta (Venice, 1857) and was written by F. de Stefani. 

The imperial history of the family is detailed in *La Famille imperiale. Hist. de la famille Bonaparte depuis son origine jusqu'en 1860*, authored by L. Ambrosini and A. Huard and published in Paris in 1860. Further chronological accounts include C. Leynadier's *Histoire de la famille Bonaparte de l'an 1050 a l'an 1848*, which was continued by de Brugere until 1866.

## Napoleon I and His Siblings
Napoleon I had several brothers and sisters. Listed in order of age, the first of these siblings was Joseph Bonaparte, followed by Lucien Bonaparte and Elisa. 

Detailed studies on the parents and siblings of Napoleon I were published by A. Kleinschmidt in 1876. The family's maternal history is explored in *Madame Mere* (Paris, 1892) by Baron H. Larrey and *Napoleons Mutter* (Leipzig, 1901) by Clara Tschudi. Additionally, T. Nasica wrote memoirs concerning the childhood and youth of Napoleon up to the age of twenty-three, which included a historical notice regarding Napoleon's father.

## Joseph Bonaparte
Joseph Bonaparte is the subject of numerous specialized works. Baron A. du Casse authored the ten-volume *Memoires et correspondance politique et militaire du roi Joseph* (Paris, 1854), as well as *Les Rois freres de Napoleon* (1883). Other accounts of his life include J.S.C. Abbott's *History of Joseph Bonaparte* (New York, 1869) and G. Bertin's *Joseph Bonaparte in America*. 

The relationship between the brothers is documented in the two-volume *Correspondence of Napoleon with Joseph Bonaparte* (New York, 1856). Further context on the Napoleonic Empire in Southern Italy was provided by R.M. Johnston in a two-volume set published in London in 1904.

## Lucien Bonaparte
Lucien Bonaparte spent several years living in Italy, primarily in Rome, where he displayed marked hostility toward the emperor. In December 1807, the emperor attempted to reach an arrangement where Lucien would become a French prince on the condition that he annul his marriage; Lucien refused. 

Lucien resided for a time at his estate of Canino, taking the papal title of prince of Canino, before departing for America. During this journey, he was captured by a British ship and taken to Malta and then to England, where he remained under surveillance until the peace of 1814. 

Upon returning to Rome, Lucien offered his assistance to Napoleon during the Hundred Days in 1815. He stood by Napoleon at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris and was the final person to defend Napoleon's prerogatives during his second abdication. Lucien spent the remainder of his life in Italy and died in Rome on June 29, 1840. His family included six daughters and four sons. 

As a writer, Lucien authored the two-volume epic *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree* (1814), as well as *La Verite sur les Cent Jours* and a set of *Memoirs*, though the latter remained unfinished.
