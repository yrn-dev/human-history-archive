# Bucharest

Bucharest (also written as Bucarest, Bukarest, Bukharest, Bukorest, and Bukhorest) is the capital of Rumania and serves as the chief town of the department of Ilfov. While Bucharest is the conventional English spelling, the forms Bukarest and Bucarest more closely represent the correct pronunciation.

## Geography and Layout
The city covers more than 20 square miles, including its outlying parts. It is situated in a hollow and is built primarily on the left bank of the river Dimbovitza (also spelled Dâmbovita or Dîmbovita), which traverses the city from the north-west to the south-east. The city is sheltered by a range of low hills to the south-west and west. On all other sides, the landscape consists of drained marshes that are still considered unhealthy, extending toward the central Walachian plains.

## History and Origins
The foundation of the city is subject to various theories. One tradition attributes its founding to the half-mythical first Walachian prince, Radu Negru (c. 1290-1314). Another account suggests the name derives from the Albanian word *Bukur*, meaning joy, commemorating a victory won by Prince Mircea of Walachia (c. 1383-1419) over the Turks, leading to the city being called "The City of Joy." Modern historians propose that the city began as a fortress built on the site of the Daco-Roman Thyanus to control the approaches to Tîrgovishtea, the former Walachian capital.

The city served as a summer residence for the court before Prince Constantine Brancovan designated it as his capital in 1698. Throughout the 18th century, possession of the city was frequently contested by the Russians, Austrians, and Turks. In 1812, the city gave its name to a treaty that ceded a third of Moldavia and Bessarabia to Russia. 

The 19th century saw significant political transitions. The city was occupied by Russian troops in 1828 and again from 1853 to 1854, followed by an Austrian garrison that remained until March 1857. In 1858, Bucharest hosted the international congress for the organization of the Danubian principalities. Following the proclamation of the union of Moldavia and Walachia in 1861, Bucharest became the capital of Rumania. In 1866, an insurrection in the city drove Prince Cuza, the first ruler of the united provinces, from his throne.

## Urban Development and Infrastructure
Until approximately 1860, Bucharest was characterized by dimly lit lanes paved with rough stone blocks embedded in clay soil, which often subsided and created an undulating surface. Drains were rare and epidemics were common. Due to frequent earthquakes, many structures were built of wood. This vulnerability to fire was evidenced in 1847, when a quarter of the city was destroyed. The city also suffered from several outbreaks of plague in 1718, 1738, 1793, and 1813; the latter epidemic resulted in 70,000 deaths within six weeks.

A period of gradual reform began following the accession of Prince Charles in 1866. These improvements included the installation of electric and gas lighting, the supply of pure water, and the creation of a sewerage system. The river was enclosed within stone embankments, and electric or horse tramways were established on the principal thoroughfares.

## Population and Culture
As of 1900, the population of Bucharest was 282,071. This total included 53,056 aliens, the majority of whom were subjects of Austria-Hungary, and 43,274 Jews.

The city presents a mixture of modern and antique elements. Its skyline is noted for its picturesqueness, characterized by numerous gardens and the gilded or metal-plated turrets and cupolas of its churches. In older districts where land value is lower, one can find antique one-storeyed houses surrounded by acacias and poplars. The city's atmosphere is further distinguished from western capitals by the presence of pedlars, Russian coachmen or sleigh-drivers of the banished Lipovan sect, and Rumans and gipsies dressed in brightly coloured native costumes.
