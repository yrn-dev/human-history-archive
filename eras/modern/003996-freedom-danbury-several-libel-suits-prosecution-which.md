# The Herald of Freedom and Phineas Taylor Barnum

## Early Life and Business Ventures
Phineas Taylor Barnum was born on July 5, 1810, in Bethel, Connecticut. The son of a store-keeper who also operated an inn, Barnum began his professional life as a store-keeper. During this period, he became involved in the lottery mania that was prevalent across the United States. Following a failure in his business endeavors, Barnum transitioned into publishing.

## The Herald of Freedom
In 1829, Barnum established a weekly newspaper in Danbury, Connecticut, titled *The Herald of Freedom*. His tenure as a publisher in Danbury was marked by legal conflict; he faced several libel suits and a prosecution that resulted in his imprisonment. These legal challenges preceded his departure from the city; in 1834, Barnum moved to New York.

## Career as a Showman
Barnum began his career as a showman in 1835 through the purchase and exploitation of Joyce Heth, a coloured woman who was reputed to be over 160 years old and the nurse of George Washington. Barnum conducted successful, well-advertised tours across America with Heth and a small company until 1839. Although Heth died in 1836, it was proven at that time that her age was no more than seventy.

After a period of failure, Barnum purchased Scudder's American Museum in New York in 1841. He expanded the museum, turning it into one of the most popular shows in the United States. A significant success occurred in 1842 with the exhibition of Charles Stratton, known as "General Tom Thumb," with whom Barnum toured England in 1844. Barnum also demonstrated his enterprise in 1850 by engaging the singer Jenny Lind for a tour in America, paying her $1,000 a night for 150 nights and covering all expenses.

## Later Career and Legacy
Barnum retired from the show business in 1855, but he was forced to return to his career as a museum proprietor and showman after having to settle with creditors in 1857. In 1871, he established the "Greatest Show on Earth," which was a travelling amalgamation of a museum of "freaks," a menagerie, and a circus. This venture was incorporated as "Barnum, Bailey & Hutchinson" and later as "Barnum & Bailey's," touring globally. The business was eventually sold to Ringling Brothers in 1907.

In addition to his exhibitions, Barnum was an author. His written works include *Autobiography* (first published in 1854 with later editions), *The Humbugs of the World* (1865), and *Struggles and Triumphs* (1869). Phineas Taylor Barnum died on April 7, 1891.

## Geography of Danbury
The city of Danbury, where Barnum published his newspaper, is located in the south-west part of Connecticut on the Still river, a tributary of the Housatonic. It serves as one of the county-seats of Fairfield county. Settled in 1684 by emigrants from Norwalk, the township received its name in 1687. 

By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Danbury was a center for the manufacture of felt hats, an industry begun in 1780. By 1905, the city had approximately thirty factories producing felt hats, valued at $5,798,107 for the year. This industry represented 71.9% of the city's total factory product value and 15.8% of all felt hats produced in the United States. Other notable aspects of the city include the manufacture of silver-plated ware (primarily by Rogers Bros.), the annual Danbury Fair, and the discovery of the rare mineral danburite by C.U. Shepard in 1839.
