# Austria-Hungary

Austria-Hungary, also known as the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy (German: *Oesterreichisch-ungarische Monarchie* or *Oesterreichisch-ungarisches Reich*), was a country located in central Europe. Its borders were defined by Russia and Rumania to the east, and Rumania, Servia, Turkey, and Montenegro to the south. To the west, it was bounded by the Adriatic Sea, Italy, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, and the German Empire, while the German Empire and Russia formed its northern boundaries.

## State Structure and Geography
The monarchy occupied approximately one-sixteenth of the total area of Europe, totaling 239,977 square miles as of 1905. It was composed of two independent states:
*   **Cisleithania:** Unofficially called Austria, consisting of the lands and kingdoms represented in the council of the empire (*Reichsrat*).
*   **Transleithania:** Unofficially called Hungary, consisting of the "lands of St Stephen's Crown."

The entity is frequently referred to unofficially as the Dual Monarchy. The official name was established by a diploma issued by Emperor Francis Joseph I on November 14, 1868, which replaced the previous designation of the Austrian Empire.

## Population and Territorial Changes
In 1901, the population of the monarchy was 45,405,267, representing about one-eighth of the total population of Europe. 

The monarchy's territory expanded through the Berlin Treaty of 1878, which placed the principalities of Bosnia and Herzegovina under Austro-Hungarian administration. These principalities, which recognized Turkey as suzerain, covered an area of 19,702 square miles and had a population of 1,591,036 in 1895. Their annexation in 1908 was subsequently recognized by the Powers in 1909, fully integrating them into the monarchy's dominions.

## Internal Governance and Political History
The political landscape of the monarchy was marked by periods of restriction. Between 1811 and 1825, the diet in Hungary was not summoned, and in Transylvania, the diet remained unsummoned between 1811 and 1834. During certain periods, forbidden newspapers were the only ones believed, and forbidden books were the only ones read.

Administrative control was centralized in specific matters; for example, while the war department could settle the number of recruits annually, any claim for exemption by a single conscript based on grounds not recognized by precedent required an imperial decree.

## Historiography and Academic Sources
A vast body of literature exists regarding the history of the monarchy, categorized by focus:

**General Histories**
Archdeacon William Coxe wrote the *History of the House of Austria, 1218-1792* (1817), which was continued by W. Kelly. Other general works include J. Majlath's *Geschichte des oesterreichischen Kaiserstaates* (1834-1850) and Franz X. Krones' *Handbuch der Gesch. Oesterreichs* (1876-1879).

**Constitutional and Diplomatic Works**
Constitutional history has been explored by E. Werunsky, A. Bechmann, A. Huber (with a 1901 edition by A. Dopsch), and A. Luschin von Ebengreuth, whose *Oesterreichische Reichsgesch* is noted as a work of first-class importance. G. Kolmer documented the parliament and constitution from 1848 to 1885. 

Diplomatic relations have been analyzed by A. Beer, A. Fournier, and F. von Demelitsch. H. Uebersberger focused on relations between Austria and Russia from 1488 to 1605. Regarding the "Austrian question" at the turn of the 20th century, works by Andre Cheradame, Rene Henry, and "Scotus Viator" provide analysis.

**Specific Political and Economic Studies**
The 1867 compromise with Hungary is detailed in works by J. Andrassy and L. Eisenmann. Economic perspectives are provided by Matlekovits, who presents the Hungarian point of view on customs policy (*Zollpolitik*), and Bazant, who wrote on trade policy (*Handelspolitik*).
