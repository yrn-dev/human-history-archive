# Fauna of Asia

The distribution of animal life across the Asian continent is closely linked to climatic conditions and corresponds very closely with the continent's botanical provinces. According to the primary divisions of the earth established by Dr. Sclater, which have received general approval from naturalists, Asia is divided into several distinct zoological regions.

## Zoological Regions of Asia

The northern portion of Asia, extending as far south as the Himalaya, is not zoologically distinct from Europe. Together with the strip of Africa located north of the Atlas, this area constitutes the Palaearctic region. In contrast, the south-eastern portion of Asia, including the Philippines and the islands of Borneo, Java, and Sumatra, forms the Indian region.

A separate zoological district exists in the extreme south-west of the continent. This district includes southern Persia, Palestine, and Arabia, and extends to Sind and Baluchistan. This area belongs to the Ethiopian region, which also covers Africa south of the Atlas. Further east, the islands beyond Wallace's line—including Papua, Celebes, and other islands east of Java—fall within the Australian region.

A significant biological barrier exists between the Indian and Australian regions. A strait approximately 15 miles wide separates the islands of Bali and Lombok; Bali belongs to the Indian Region, while Lombok belongs to the Australian Region. There is no true transition between the two, as no species are common to both.

## Northern Asia: Mammals and Birds

The fauna of northern Asia is closely allied to that of Europe, with the ornithology showing an even stronger connection than the mammal fauna. Nearly three-fourths of well-known European bird species extend through Siberia into the islands of the Japanese empire. This region is characterized by a development of groups suited to temperate and cold regions and a total absence of tropical forms.

Among the most distinctive birds of northern Asia are those of the genus *Phasianus*, which are restricted to this region in their wild state. Additionally, eared pheasants (*Crossoptilon*) and gold pheasants (*Thaumalea*) are confined to specific districts within the mountains of north eastern Asia. The *Passeres* group is well developed, featuring many species of grosbeaks, linnets, finches, stone chats, and larks.

## The Fauna of Lake Baikal

The fauna of Lake Baikal, explored by Godlewski, Dybowski, and later by Korotnev (1900-2), possesses an original character and is richer than previously assumed. While hypotheses regarding a direct communication between the Arctic Ocean and the lake during the Tertiary or Post-Tertiary ages remain unproven, the lake contains a seal (*Phoca baikalensis* or *Phoca vitulina*) closely akin to the seals found in Spitsbergen. Other marine-like inhabitants include polychaetes, marine sponges, a marine mollusc (*ancilodoris*), and certain marine gammarids.

The lake's waters are swarming with fish, specifically *salmonidae* and sturgeons. The herring (*Salmo omul*) serves as the primary product of the fisheries, though catches have notably decreased over the last forty or fifty years. Plankton is abundant in the lake. Furthermore, the small Lake Frolikha, which communicates with Lake Baikal via the Frolikha river, is home to a peculiar species of trout, *Salmo erythreas*, which is not found anywhere else.

Generally, the region exhibits a relative poverty of zoological groups but a great wealth of species within those groups. For example, there are as many as 300 species of gammarids. Those living at depths of 380 to 330 fathoms tend to develop abyssal characters similar to the deep-sea fauna of the ocean.
