# The Germ Theory of Disease and Bacteriology

The modern "germ theory of disease" posits that all contagious and infectious diseases are caused by the action of bacteria or, in fewer instances, protozoa or fungi. This scientific framework grew from a nucleus of facts that replaced earlier, vague notions regarding the relationship between fermentation and disease. While thinkers once suggested that a "contagium" or "virus" might be a minute organism capable of reproduction, the theory gained definite shape through the work of Cagniard de la Tour, Schwann, and especially Pasteur.

## Development of the Germ Theory
The acceptance of the germ theory accelerated from approximately 1870 onwards. Early observations by P. F. O. Rayer and Davaine identified bacilli in the blood of animals dead of anthrax (splenic fever). In 1863, Davaine proposed that these bacilli caused the disease, a claim later proven through inoculation. In 1876, Koch published observations on these bacilli, confirming their causal relation to splenic fever and discovering their saprophytic phase and spores. Other milestones include Pasteur's 1870 proof that a silkworm disease was bacterial, and Oertel's 1871 demonstration that a *Micrococcus* is intimately involved in producing diphtheria. By 1872, Cohn grouped several "pathogenous" Schizomycetes together. Consequently, diseases such as tuberculosis, glanders, relapsing fever, fowl-cholera, and septicaemia have been brought within the range of biology.

## Methods of Establishing Causal Relationships
Establishing the relationship between a bacterium and a disease involves three primary steps:
1. The discovery of a bacterium in affected tissues using a microscope.
2. The obtaining of that bacterium in a pure culture.
3. The production of the disease via inoculation with that pure culture.

Microscopic examination utilizes fine lenses and staining methods, primarily basic aniline dyes, to color bacteria deeply while leaving tissues faint. Some methods, such as Gram's method, involve treating the sample with an iodine solution before decolorizing. To fully describe a bacterium, scientists examine its microscopical characters, biological properties, and growth in various culture media. For example, sugars (glucose, lactose, saccharose) are used to test fermentative action, and sterilized milk is used to see if the growth curdles the medium.

## Factors Influencing Infection and Virulence
The entrance of a virulent bacterium into an animal's tissues does not produce a disease with fixed characters; rather, the result varies based on circumstances. The two chief factors are virulence (of the bacterium) and susceptibility (of the host).

Susceptibility varies by species, race, individual, and period of life; for instance, adults are less susceptible to diphtheria than young subjects. Acquired susceptibility can be increased by fatigue, starvation, exposure to cold, or local injury and depressed vitality. In a healthy state, internal tissues and blood are bacterium-free, though organisms may occasionally enter the blood and be rapidly killed.

Virulence is generally increased when an organism sojourns in the tissues of an animal. This is especially marked when the organism is inoculated from animal to animal in a series, a process known as "passage," which is viewed as an adaptation to surroundings. Conversely, virulence tends to diminish when bacteria are cultivated on artificial media outside the body.

## Bacterial Toxins and Pathology
Bacteriology also focuses on toxins or poisons formed by bacteria. These may be free in culture fluid—which can be isolated by filtering through unglazed porcelain using negative pressure—or retained within the bacteria. To release intracellular toxins, Koch ground tubercle bacilli in an agate mortar with distilled water, while Rowland and Macfadyen used liquid air to make the bacterial bodies brittle for breaking.

These toxins are often mixtures of organic substances; "dry toxins" are produced through precipitation, re-solution, and desiccation. In pathology, the excretion of microbic poisons can cause renal disturbance. For example, "active congestion" of the kidneys can be produced by the toxins of various diseases, leading to engorgement and inflammation.

## Immunity and Prevention
Immunity is defined as non-susceptibility to a specific disease, bacterium, or toxin. There are two primary types of artificial immunity:

*   **Active Immunity:** This is produced by a reaction in the body, often through the injection of bacteria or their products. One method is inoculation with an "attenuated virus" (bacteria with diminished virulence). Attenuation is achieved by growing organisms under unsuitable conditions, such as higher temperatures or in the presence of weak antiseptics. Immunity can be increased by using vaccines of increasing virulence, ending with "supervirulent" cultures created via the method of passage. Another method involves injecting dead bacteria.
*   **Passive Immunity:** This is produced by transferring the serum of an actively immunized animal to a fresh animal, requiring no active change in the second animal's tissues. This forms the basis of serum therapeutics.

The process of immunization involves the formation of bodies with specific combining affinity for the introduced substance (toxin or bacterium). This includes agglutinins, which combine with agglutinogens on the bacteria in the presence of salts.
