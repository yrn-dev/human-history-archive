# BOLLANDISTS

The Bollandists are Belgian Jesuits responsible for the publication of the *Acta Sanctorum*, a comprehensive collection of the legends and biographies of saints. The work is organized according to the calendar, with entries arranged by days. Considered the first biographical dictionary compiled in Europe, its initial publication caused a great sensation.

## Origins and Early Development
The original concept for the *Acta Sanctorum* was conceived by a Jesuit father, Heribert Rosweyde. In 1607, Rosweyde issued a prospectus titled *Fasti sanctorum quorum vitae in Belgicis Bibliothecis manuscriptae*, in which he outlined his intention to publish the lives of saints compiled from manuscripts across eighteen volumes, accompanied by sober notes. Although Rosweyde collected a vast amount of material, he died in 1629 before the work could begin.

Following his death, Father John Bolland was appointed to continue the project and was sent to Antwerp. Bolland expanded the scope of the work and continued to amass material. The first two volumes, covering January, were published in 1643. Subsequent volumes followed: February in 1658, March in 1668, and April in 1675. During this period, Godfried Henschen (Henschenius) collaborated with Bolland from 1635 until 1681. From 1659 to 1714, Daniel van Papenbroeck (Papebroch) also collaborated. The era of Papebroch's involvement is regarded as the most brilliant period of the *Acta Sanctorum*, though his critical approach earned him many enemies and forced him to defend his work against their attacks.

## Suppression and Resumption
The work continued in the same spirit, despite some inequalities, until the Society of Jesus was suppressed in 1773. The final volume of this initial phase, volume iii of October, appeared in 1770. Following the dispersion of the Jesuits, the Bollandists were authorized to continue their activities in Antwerp until 1778, after which they moved to the monastery of canons regular of Coudenberg in Brussels. There, they published volume iv of October in 1780 and volume v of October in 1886, the year the Coudenberg monastery was suppressed.

The work of the Bollandists ceased in 1788. Their remaining library was acquired by the Premonstratensians of Tongerloo, who attempted to maintain the project, publishing volume vi of October in 1794. The Society of Jesus was later re-established in Belgium, and in 1837, at the support of the Belgian government and the suggestion of the Academie Royale of Belgium, the Bollandists were installed at the college of St Michael in Brussels. A new series began in 1845 with volume vii of October, eventually reaching volume xiii of October in 1883. Collaborators in this series included Jesuit fathers Joseph van der Moere, Joseph van Hecke, Benjamin Bossue, Victor and Remi de Buck, Ant. Tinnebroeck, Edu. Carpentier, and Henr. Matagne. Additionally, Father John Martinov of Theazan edited the *Annus Graeco-Slavicus*, which appeared in volume xi of October in 1864.

## Modern Methods and Scientific Expansion
In 1882, the Bollandists shifted their direction to align the work with progress in historical methods. Jesuit fathers J. de Backer, G. van Hooff, and C. de Smedt established a quarterly review called *Analecta Bollandiana*. By 1906, this review had reached its 25th volume and was edited by P. Peeters, A. Porcelet, H. Delehaye, F. van Ontroy, and de Smedt. The *Analecta Bollandiana* served as a venue for dissertations, inedited texts, and studies intended to remould and continue the *Acta Sanctorum*. Since 1892, it has also included the *Bulletin des publications hagiographiques*, which provides criticisms of recent hagiographic works.

The Bollandists also undertook the analysis of hagiographic manuscripts in major libraries, publishing numerous catalogues in the *Analecta* for cities including Venice, Messina, Ghent, Namur, and Chartres. Separate volumes were dedicated to:
* Latin manuscripts in the Bibliotheque Royale at Brussels (1886-1889).
* Latin and Greek manuscripts in the Bibliotheque Nationale at Paris (1889-1896).
* Greek manuscripts in the Vatican (1899).
* Latin manuscripts in the libraries of Rome (1905 onwards).

Furthermore, they prepared inventories of published hagiographic texts, resulting in the *Bibliotheca hagiographica graeca* (1895), the *Bibliotheca hagiographica latina* (1899), and the *Bibliotheca hagiographica Orientalis*. While these scientific endeavors delayed the publication of the main collection, they provided a more solid foundation for the work. Consequently, volume i for November appeared in 1887, volume ii in 1894 (preceded by the *Martyrologium Hieronymianum*), and the *Propylaeum ad Acta Sanctorum Novembris* (containing the *Synaxarium ecclesiae Constantinopolitanae*) in 1902.

## Editions and Scope
There are three primary editions of the *Acta Sanctorum*:
1. The original edition (Antwerp, Tongerloo, and Brussels), consisting of 63 volumes published between 1643 and 1902.
2. The Venice edition, which concludes at volume v of September (1734-1770).
3. The Paris edition, which concludes at volume xiii of October (61 volumes, 1863-1883).

A volume of tables edited by the abbe Rigollot also exists. The *Acta Sanctorum* serves as a primary source for various hagiographic studies, such as the life of St Dominic (Aug. 4) and the five lives of St Brigid (Feb. 1).
