# Asiatic Russia, Eastern Siberia, and Mongolia

Asiatic Russia, with a particular emphasis on Mongolia and eastern Siberia, has been integrated into the sphere of Russian exploration. This process has resulted in a development of Old World resources that parallels the best records of the New World, marking a significant epoch in the history of Asia.

## Geography and Topography
Siberia is defined as the portion of Asia situated between the Arctic Ocean and the mountainous belt that bounds Turkestan, Mongolia, and Manchuria to the north. The region is characterized by an immense, high, and broken plateau that extends from the south-west to the north-east, decreasing in both altitude and width as it advances. This plateau is bordered by high ridges that eventually subside into high plains (1,500 to 2,000 feet high) and further into lowlands a few hundred feet above sea level.

The region's hydrology is dominated by some of the world's largest rivers, specifically the Ob, Yenisei, and Lena. The Lena flows through Siberia into the Arctic Sea. To the east, the Stanovor mountain range, reaching heights of 4,000 to 5,000 feet, follows the southern coast from Kamchatka to the borders of Manchuria. The Yablonoi range continues this direction, acting as a divide between the waters of the Lena and the Amur river, the latter of which falls into the North Pacific and constitutes the basin of Manchuria.

Further south, the Khingan range stretches toward the Chinese frontier near Peking. East of this range, drainage flows into the Yellow Sea and the Amur; to the west lies Mongolia, a region characterized by an inclination toward the central area of the continent.

## The Central Asian Deserts and Mongolia
The Gobi, the great desert of central Asia, is bounded by the Tibetan plateau to the south, the Khingan range to the east, and the Yablonoi Mountains and Tian-shan to the north and north-west. The eastern portion of the Gobi is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia, while the western portion forms eastern or Chinese Turkestan.

The topography of this central area is consistently high; no part of the Gobi desert is believed to be less than 2,000 feet above sea level. In northern Mongolia, elevations range between 4,000 and 6,000 feet, and the route between Kiakhta and the Chinese frontier never drops below 3,000 feet. While the precise positions of the mountain ridges traversing this area are not fully known, many points are recorded to exceed 10,000 or 12,000 feet.

## Climate, Environment, and Population
Siberia experiences extreme variations in heat and cold. While rainfall is not heavy, it is sufficient to support vegetation compatible with the temperature, often resulting in peaty or swampy surfaces. Pine forests commonly cover the mountain-sides, while shrubs and grasses cover the plains.

The population of the region is very scanty, with cultivated tracts being small and restricted to settled districts. The towns are entirely Russian. The indigenous populations consist of nomadic Mongols, described as peaceful but in a backward state of civilization. Historically, the eastern steppes and deserts of Mongolia served as the region of origin for the Turkish, Tartar, Mongolian, or Hunnish peoples, who shared akin languages, races, and ways of life. These tribes frequently migrated as raiders, conquerers, and wanderers into China, driven by population increases during periods of plenty or by cattle disease and grass shortages.

## Political and Social History
In the context of early 20th-century conflict, Siberia has been viewed as a home for freedom-seeking exiles, separated from Russia by the Volga. During this period, Siberia was seen as a region ready to welcome the Czechs, who held the confidence of the Russian people. There was a noted distinction between the attitudes toward Slavic forces and non-Slavic Allied forces, particularly the Japanese, who were despised by the Russians.

Economic conditions in the region have faced severe instability, with reports of whole sections of Siberia and Russia facing starvation. Efforts were proposed to provide economic aid to Siberia by repairing railroad lines and installing modern distribution methods to counter the disorganization attributed to Prussianism.

## Infrastructure and Trade
The town of Kungur, located in the government of Perm on the highway to Siberia, serves as an example of regional industry. As of 1897, it had a population of 14,324. Its economy is based on the manufacture of machinery, iron castings, leather, gloves, boots, and overcoats. Kungur exports cereals, tallow, linseed, iron wares, and boots, while importing tea directly from China.

Regarding historical administrative practices, a trace of Mongol influence survives in the form of travel permissions. The Mongol *Paizah* (a tablet) was a government authorization for the use of post-horses. This practice is mirrored in the requirements for travel by government post-horses in Russia. In Mongolia, the *horse-Bai*—an ornament on the horse caparison—continues to grant the rider the right to provisions and horses during a journey.
