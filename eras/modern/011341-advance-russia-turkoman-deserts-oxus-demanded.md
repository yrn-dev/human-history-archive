# Russian Expansion to the Turkoman Deserts and the Oxus

The advance of Russia into the Turkoman deserts and the Oxus basin was part of a broader eastward and southward expansion of the Russian state. Beginning in the seventeenth century, Cossacks spread eastward from European Russia, establishing settlements where agricultural conditions permitted. This movement was supported by cordons of forts and stations that formed a moving frontier, particularly to the south where the Turkomans remained strong and active.

## Relations with and Conquest of Khiva

Russia first established relations with Khiva in the 17th century after Yaik Cossacks, during raids across the Caspian, discovered the territory and conducted plundering expeditions to the town of Urgenj. In 1717, Peter the Great dispatched a military force to Khiva with the goals of releasing Russian subjects from slavery, opening mercantile relations with India through Turan, and seeking auriferous sand in the bed of the Oxus. Although the Russian arms were initially victorious in a three-day battle, the army was later treacherously annihilated after being divided into small detachments.

Interest in the khanate resumed in the third decade of the 19th century. In 1839, General Perovsky led a force from Orenburg across the Ust-Urt plateau to occupy the khanate and liberate captives, but this expedition also ended in disaster. A strategic shift occurred in 1847 when Russia founded a fort at the mouth of the Jaxartes (Syr-darya), which provided a base for further operations and deprived the Khivans of territory and tax-paying Kirghiz. Following the capture of Tashkent in 1865, Russia turned its attention back to Khiva. After thorough surveys of the region between 1871 and 1872, a large-scale expedition of 10,000 men under General Kaufmann was launched in 1873 from the bases of Tashkent, Orenburg, and Krasnovodsk. Khiva was occupied with almost no opposition, and Russia annexed 110,000 souls and 35,700 square miles of territory on the right bank of the Oxus. Following this occupation, the slave trade was abolished and 29,300 Persian slaves—stolen by Turkoman raiders—were set free.

## Boundary Demarcation and Geopolitics

The Russian advance necessitated the establishment of definite boundaries between trans-Caspian conquests and the kingdom of Afghanistan. The Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1886 fixed a largely artificial boundary between the Oxus and the Hari Rud (the river of Herat). This line leaves the Hari Rud at Zulfikar (70 miles south of Sarakhs) and reaches its southernmost point 60 miles north of Herat at the Kushk. From Khamiab, the main channel of the Oxus river serves as the northern boundary of Afghanistan, extending to its source in Victoria Lake of the Great Pamir.

Further demarcation occurred via the Anglo-Russian Commission of 1895, which established a line to the glaciers and snowfields overlooking the Chinese border. The Sarikol range separates the Russian Pamirs from Chinese Turkestan, though the exact dividing line remained indefinite. To the north-east, the boundary of Siberia follows an irregular course defined partly by the Alatau and Tian-shan mountains, reaching 53 deg. N. lat. at the Sayan range. The boundary then follows the Kerulen affluent to the Amur river, then the Amur to the Usuri, and finally reaches the Pacific coast at the mouth of the Tumen river, 100 miles south of Amur bay and the port of Vladivostok.

## Geography and Environment

The region between the Caspian and Aral Seas, extending to the Tibeto-Himalayan mountains and the valleys of the Jaxartes and Oxus, is primarily a low-lying desert plain. Agriculture in this tract is only possible through irrigation. The climate is extreme; in the lowlands, hot summers are followed by cold winters where the Oxus may freeze for a fortnight and temperatures may drop to -20 deg. Fahr. The area is prone to malignant malaria after wet springs and severe sand-storms from the west.

Russian scientists have investigated the depression westward of the Caspian and Aral basins. They have proposed that the Caspian Sea extended eastwards into Trans-Caspian territory during the post-Pleiocene age, displacing the older theory of an ancient connection between the Oxus and the Caspian. This hypothesis is supported by the discovery of Caspian shells 100 miles inland at altitudes of 140 to 280 feet, and the nature of the Kara-kum sands, which appear to be marine formations lacking fluviatile delta deposits from the Oxus.

## Population and Economy

The region includes Turkestan proper, Khiva, and Bokhara. The population is diverse, featuring both settled and nomadic elements:
*   **Uzbegs:** The dominating race among settled inhabitants and the source of official recruitment. In Bokhara, they are described as fanatical Moslem Sunnites.
*   **Sarts and Tajiks:** Agriculturists and tradespeople. Sarts are a mixture of Turks and Iranians, while Tajiks are primarily Iranian.
*   **Turkomans:** Nomads of Turkish origin living in the steppes.
*   **Kara-kalpaks (Black Bonnets):** A Turki tribe of about 50,000 living south of Lake Aral in towns such as Kipchak, Khodsheili, and Kungrad.
*   **Others:** The population also includes Kirghiz, Persians, Arabs, Armenians, and a colony of Hindus from Shikarpur who handle much of the trade.

The economy of Khiva involves the export of silk cocoons, wool, cotton, and rough woollen cloth to Russia, and animal products to Bokhara. Imports from Russia include hardware, pepper, velveteen, and cottons. Khivan merchants frequently attend the Nizhniy-Novgorod and Orenburg fairs. Domestic industries include the embroidery of leather, silks, and cloth.
