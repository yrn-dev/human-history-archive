# Projectiles with Ogival Heads and Ballistic Coefficients

In the study of ballistics, the physical design of a projectile significantly influences its resistance to the air. Modern projectiles are designed to reduce this resistance by increasing their length and incorporating a sharper point. To account for these design variations, a factor known as the coefficient of shape, denoted as $\kappa$, is utilized.

## The Coefficient of Shape ($\kappa$)
The coefficient of shape is a variable used to calculate the resistance of a projectile based on its head design. According to Bashforth, the value of $\kappa$ varies depending on the geometry of the projectile's head:

*   **Ogival Heads:** For a projectile in which the ogival head is struck with a radius of 2 diameters, Bashforth assigns a $\kappa$ value of 0.975.
*   **Flat-Headed Projectiles:** These are typically required at proof-butts and have a $\kappa$ value of approximately 1.8, or an average of 2.
*   **Spherical Shot:** For spherical projectiles, $\kappa$ is not constant, requiring the construction of a separate ballistic table, though it may be taken as 1.7 on average.

## Related Ballistic Factors
The coefficient of shape is one of several factors used to determine the overall coefficient of reduction ($n$). Other critical factors include:

*   **The Factor $\tau$:** This becomes important during high-angle fire over long ranges when a shot reaches the higher, attenuated strata of the atmosphere. Conversely, in calculations for shooting under water, $\tau$ is approximately 800.
*   **The Coefficient of Steadiness ($\sigma$):** Introduced by Bashforth to account for the superior centering provided by breech-loading systems. This factor can vary during flight; a shot may be unsteady immediately after leaving the muzzle before steadying like a spinning-top. Additionally, $\sigma$ may increase as a gun wears out after firing numerous rounds.

These coefficients are collected into a single formula for the coefficient of reduction: $n = \kappa \sigma \tau$. This is used in the equation $R = nd^2p = nd^2f(v)$.

## Effects of Heavy Projectiles
The impact of large-caliber projectiles, such as the 42-cm mortars known as "fat Bertha" and "black Maria," demonstrates the destructive potential of heavy artillery against fortifications. These projectiles can penetrate half a metre of metal armour and concrete beds several metres thick. 

The effectiveness of such shells is often enhanced by a *Zünder mit Verzögerung* (fuse timed for retarded ignition). This fuse ensures that detonation occurs a couple of seconds after impact, allowing the projectile to penetrate deep into armoured concrete before exploding. This process creates a sequence of destruction that operates first from above downward and then from below upward.

The results of such impacts include:
*   **Structural Destruction:** The displacement of 40-ton cupolas and the rending of metal cupolas.
*   **Personnel Casualties:** High mortality rates within forts, with some instances of 70% of a complement being killed. Deaths may result from direct impact, air pressure flinging soldiers against walls, or suffocation caused by the gases of the projectiles and whirling concrete dust.

## Projectile Behavior in Combat
Projectiles exhibit various behaviors depending on the target and environment. In naval engagements, shells may rain over a vessel, causing fires in the stern or fore-part and perforating bridge plating from beneath. In coastal or maritime settings, projectiles may ricochet from the surface of the water, "dancing like flat stones," before crashing into quay walls or burying themselves in stone promenades without bursting. On land, shell explosions can create immense craters in roads or twist electric tramway rails upward.
