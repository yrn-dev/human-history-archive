# Decoction System and Bottom Fermentation

The decoction system is a specific method of mashing used in brewing. The process begins by mashing grist with cold water until a homogeneous mixture is achieved. Hot water is then added to the mash-tun to raise the temperature to between 85-100° F., depending on the circumstances.

## The Mashing Process
The process involves a series of withdrawals and heating stages. Approximately one-third of the mash, including the "goods," is transferred to a mash copper known as the *Maisch Kessel*. This portion, referred to as the *Dickmaische*, is gradually heated to about 165° F. and maintained at this temperature until the mash becomes transparent. Following this, the *Dickmaische* is brought to a boil, with the ebullition sustained for a period between a quarter and three-quarters of an hour.

A sufficient amount of the *Dickmaische* is then returned to the main mash-tun to raise the overall temperature to 111-125° F. After a few minutes, another third of the mash is withdrawn and treated in the same manner as the first to create a second "thick mash." Once this second portion is returned to the mash-tun, the mixture is thoroughly worked up and allowed to stand so that solids may deposit.

A final third, called the *Läutermaische* (or "clear mash"), is then withdrawn and boiled until coagulable albuminoids are precipitated. This is reconveyed to the mash-tun, where mashing continues for some time, reaching a final heat slightly over 160° F.

## Bottom Fermentation
After the mashing process, the wort is cooled and boiled with hops, similar to the English system. It then undergoes a specific process called bottom fermentation. This system is characterized by "pitching" and fermentation occurring at very low temperatures and within very small vessels compared to the English system.

The fermenting cellars are kept at a temperature of approximately 37-38° F., and the temperature of the fermenting wort does not exceed 50° F. The name "bottom fermentation" is derived from the fact that the yeast, which is a different type than that used in the English system, remains at the bottom of the fermenting tun. Primary fermentation in this system lasts about eleven to twelve days, which is longer than the three days required by the English system. Following this, the beer is moved into store (lager) casks, where it is kept at a temperature approaching the freezing-point of water.
