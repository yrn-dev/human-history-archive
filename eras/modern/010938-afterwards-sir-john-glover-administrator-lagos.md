# The Ashanti War of 1873-1874

The Ashanti War of 1873-1874 was a military conflict triggered by territorial disputes and the captivity of European nationals. The conflict culminated in a British expedition to Kumasi and the strategic deployment of forces across the region.

## Causes of the Conflict
Two primary events served as the immediate causes of the war. The principal cause was the transference of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti regarded the Elmina as their subjects and the king of Ashanti held the "custom-note," through which he received an annual payment from the Dutch. While the Dutch viewed this payment as a present for trade purposes, it was originally a ground rent for the fort. Consequently, the Ashanti viewed the British occupation of the fort as an occupation of Ashanti territory.

A secondary, minor cause involved the captivity of four Europeans held by the Ashanti. In June 1869, an Ashanti force invaded Krepi, a territory located beyond the Volta. During this invasion, they captured Mr. J. Kuhne and Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, along with Ramseyer's wife and infant son; the child died shortly thereafter due to privation. These individuals were members of the Basel mission. Additionally, a French trader named Monsieur M.J. Bonnat was captured at a different location. All captives were taken to Kumasi. Although negotiations for their release had begun, they remained prisoners at the time of the Elmina fort sale.

## Outbreak of Hostilities
The Ashanti delayed the start of the war until their preparations were complete. During this period, Gold Coast officials appeared to believe that the risk of hostilities was remote. However, on January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force crossed the Prah and invaded the British protectorate. The Ashanti forces defeated the Fanti and stirred up disputes at Elmina. The situation escalated when the Ashanti encamped at Mampon, near Cape Coast, causing great alarm among the local inhabitants.

## The British Expedition
In response to the invasion, the British parliament voted £800,000 to fund an expedition for the defense of the territory and the punishment of the assailants. Sir Garnet (later Viscount) Wolseley was dispatched as the British administrator. 

Upon landing at Cape Coast on October 2, Wolseley discovered that the Ashanti forces had been decimated by fever and smallpox and were preparing to return to their homes. Despite this, Wolseley determined to march to Kumasi. To support this advance, Captain (later Sir) John Glover, R.N., who served as the administrator of Lagos, was assigned to co-operate from the east with a force of native levies to take the Ashanti in the rear. During these maneuvers, the enemy broke up their camp, though they continued to be harassed by native levies.
