# Sir Charles M'Carthy

Sir Charles M'Carthy was the first governor appointed by the British crown to oversee British settlements following the government in London's decision in 1821 to assume direct control of these territories.

## Conflict with the Ashanti
Sir Charles M'Carthy's administration was marked by the tensions between the Ashanti and the coast natives, specifically the Fanti, who were supported by the British. Prior to M'Carthy's appointment, diplomatic efforts had been volatile. In 1817, a mission to Kumasi involving T.E. Bowdich, W. Hutchinson, and Frederick James had ended in anger when the Ashanti king accused the emissaries of being liars and cheats regarding the payment of quit rents for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort. Later, in 1820, consul Joseph Dupuis negotiated a treaty in Kumasi that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies. This treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, who viewed it as a betrayal of the natives under British protection.

Upon taking office, Sir Charles M'Carthy espoused the cause of the Fanti. This stance led to a military confrontation with the Ashanti.

## The Battle of Essamako
On January 21, 1824, Sir Charles M'Carthy faced the Ashanti army at a location beyond the Prah known as Essamako. The engagement was characterized by a significant disparity in force, as the Ashanti commanded 10,000 men against Sir Charles's 500. 

The battle resulted in a defeat for the British forces. Sir Charles M'Carthy and eight other Europeans were killed during the conflict. Following the battle, the skull of the governor was taken to Kumasi and used as a royal drinking-cup. 

There were assertions that the defeat was caused by a logistical error in which the ordnance-keeper provided kegs filled with vermicelli instead of ammunition. However, records indicate that while such a mistake may have occurred, it only hastened a catastrophe that was otherwise inevitable.

## Aftermath and Regional Impact
The day of Sir Charles's defeat coincided with the death of Osai Tutu Quamina, who was succeeded by Osai Okoto. This transition period was followed by a state of chronic warfare. The tide shifted on August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at Dodowah. 

Following the battle of Dodowah, the power of the Ashanti over the coast tribes began to wane. The payment of ground rent for British-held forts ceased. By 1831, the Ashanti king was forced to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, at the cost of 600 oz. of gold and the delivery of his son as a hostage to Cape Coast.
