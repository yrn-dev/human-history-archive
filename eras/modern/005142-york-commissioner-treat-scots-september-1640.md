# York and the Scottish Negotiations of 1640

During the early 1640s, the city of York served as a critical center for political and military activity as King Charles I attempted to manage a rebellion in Scotland and escalating tensions with the English Parliament.

## The Scottish Rebellion and the Great Council
The conflict originated in 1638 when Charles I attempted to extend the characteristics of the Church of England—described as half-Catholic and half-Protestant—to Scotland. This was met with revolt from the Scots, who had established Presbyterianism as their national church, a form of Christianity that was non-sacramental and non-sacerdotal. The rebellion was further complicated by the mutiny of English levies raised by the King to fight the Scots.

Facing insolvency and a lack of trustworthy troops, Charles was forced to summon a Parliament in 1640, known as the Short Parliament, which he subsequently dismissed. In the same year, he convened a Council of Peers at York. Digby, who had previously been restored to royal favor, served as the leader in this Great Council. During the outbreak of the Scottish rebellion, Digby had warned Charles against attacking with forces that were inadequate.

## Negotiations at Ripon
In September 1640, diplomatic efforts to resolve the conflict led to meetings at Ripon. Digby served as a commissioner to treat with the Scots during these September discussions. Later, in October, other commissioners were present at Ripon, including John Campbell, the 1st Earl of Loudoun. Loudoun had previously been a leading figure in the promulgation of the Covenant and a supplicant against the introduction of the English liturgy in 1637. After a period of imprisonment in the Tower of London for a letter addressed to Louis XIII, Loudoun had re-entered England with the Scottish invading army.

## Political Aftermath and the Civil War
Following the negotiations, Digby strongly advised the summoning of parliament. By February 1641, he was among the peers advocating for administrative reforms and was granted a seat in the council. However, Digby later became a target of the House of Commons; on December 27, he was declared an "evil counsellor." This charge was based on the plea that he had advocated bringing the northern army up to overawe parliament during the preceding spring, though sources indicate there is no evidence to support this charge.

As the situation devolved into the English Civil War, York remained a focal point. John Ashburnham, a chief adviser to the King and member of the Long Parliament of 1640, attended Charles at York during the outbreak of the war with Scotland. Digby also eventually joined Charles at York, believing that any armed rebellion against the king constituted a wicked violation of solemn oaths. Similarly, in 1642, the Scottish council sent the Earl of Loudoun to York to offer mediation in the dispute between Charles and the parliament.
