# British Colonies

The British colonies across the globe were characterized by a diverse array of local conditions, religious developments, and evolving political and economic relationships with the British government.

## Religious Developments and the Baptist Movement
Baptists have maintained a considerable presence in every colony. In several regions, unions of Baptist churches have been established, including New Zealand, Tasmania, Canada (which has four Unions), South Africa, and the Australian colonies of New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, Western Australia, and Queensland. In South Africa, the Baptist South African Missionary and Colonial Aid Society, based in London, provides assistance to the work. In British Honduras, the Baptist church is one of several represented, alongside the Presbyterian, Wesleyan, Anglican, and Roman Catholic churches; none of these receive public funding.

In the early North American colonies, the Quaker colonies attracted many Baptists from England, Wales, and New England due to their high degree of religious liberty. Thomas Dungan, an Irish Baptist minister, helped found a church at Cold Spring in Bucks county, Pennsylvania, around 1684. In 1688, Elias Keach, son of the English evangelist Benjamin Keach, formed the Pennepek church. While services were held in Philadelphia under the Pennepek church from 1687, an independent organization was not established there until 1698. Some Keithian Quakers eventually joined this church.

The Philadelphia Association was formed in 1707 as a delegated body to maintain sound doctrine, supply ministers, and consult on church needs. This body was highly influential in educational and missionary cooperation. Seventh-day Baptist churches also formed within the association from 1705 onward.

In the southern colonies, the spread of Baptist principles was largely due to "New Light" evangelists Shubael Stearns and Daniel Marshall. Stearns and Marshall, along with about twelve believers, settled at Sandy Creek, North Carolina, where Stearns built a church with over six hundred members. Marshall later organized a church at Abbott's Creek and eventually formed a church at Kiokee, Georgia. From North Carolina, "Separate" Baptist influence spread into Virginia, Tennessee, and Kentucky. The Sandy Creek Association eventually included churches across several colonies. 

In Virginia and North Carolina, the Baptist cause was further strengthened by missionaries from the Philadelphia Association, including John Gano, Benjamin Griffith, John Thomas, Samuel Eaton, Benjamin Miller, John Garrard, and David Thomas. While the Virginia colonial government provided limited toleration to some, "Separate" Baptists—who opposed state control in religious matters—refused to take licenses for their meetings, leading to sharp conflicts with authorities. Additionally, Isaac Backus (1724-1806), a leader who came from Congregationalism, became a historian for the denomination and a champion of equality and religious liberty in New England.

## Economic and Trade Policies
For nearly two hundred years, British trade was governed by a mercantile system designed to restrict the commercial intercourse of the empire with the rest of the world. While trade in the early North American colonies was practically free, the Navigation Act of 1660 restricted the import and export of goods to British ships, requiring the master and three-fourths of the mariners to be English. This was intended to benefit British merchants and encourage British shipping.

Such restrictions continued until the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 and the introduction of free trade in Great Britain. The Navigation Acts were repealed in 1849. Monopolies were common under this system, most notably that of the East India Company, though trade in India was opened to competition in 1813. By 1846, principal British colonies that had not yet received responsible government were empowered to abolish differential duties on foreign trade. This commercial emancipation led to the rise of the "Manchester school" in manufacturing centers, which questioned the value of retaining colonies that no longer provided a monopoly of their commercial markets.

## Political Conflict and the American War
Tensions developed between Britain and the American colonies based on three main grievances: systematic restrictions on trade to keep colonial exports and goods within British hands, attempts by the British government or adventurers to secure profits from new lands, and attempts at taxation by the British Parliament.

These "annoyances" led colonial figures like James Otis and Patrick Henry to discuss the nature of political association. In 1762, James Otis asserted that God made all men naturally equal and that ideas of superiority were educational rather than innate. Colonists began to deny the supremacy of the British Parliament and the divine origin of kingship.

Specific conflicts arose over revenue; for example, the Stamp Acts, which required stamps on documents and newspapers, were stiffly resisted and repealed in 1766. Further tension occurred regarding land defence. While early American colonies provided their own defence, the costs of the French wars eventually exceeded local resources. In 1755, Great Britain intervened with troops under General Braddock. To cover these costs, the imperial parliament claimed the right to tax the colonies and passed a Quartering Act in 1765 to station 10,000 imperial troops in the colonies.

The resulting war was characterized as a conflict between the British government and the colonists, rather than Britain as a whole, with some English public feeling and Whig noblemen supporting the colonists. During the conflict, British forces marched on Concord to arrest Adams and Hancock. Following a clash at the bridge, the British suffered a disastrous retreat to Boston, facing sharpshooters firing from behind buildings, fences, and trees. The colonial levies subsequently blockaded Boston from their quarters in Cambridge.
