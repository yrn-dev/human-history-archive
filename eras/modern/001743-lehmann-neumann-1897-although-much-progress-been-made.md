# Lehmann and Neumann (1897)

The names Lehmann and Neumann appear together in the historical record of the late 19th century specifically in the context of the scientific classification of bacteria.

## Bacterial Classification
In the field of bacteriology, the classification of bacteria has been a difficult subject since 1872, when Cohn published a system that was later extended in 1875 and dominated the study of the field. Various other schemes were proposed over the following decades, including a provisional and impracticable system by Zopf in 1885 based on extreme views of pleomorphism. Other attempts at classification focused on the formation of endospores and arthrospores, though this was considered unsatisfactory because arthrospores are not true spores and both types of reproductive bodies can be found in a single form.

Medical bacteriologists further attempted to construct classification schemes based on physiological properties, such as:
* The ability of growing colonies to secrete coloured pigments.
* The power to liquefy gelatine.
* The induction of pathological conditions in animals.
* The fermentation of certain media resulting in the evolution of other gases or carbon dioxide.

These physiological systems failed to maintain their positions because such properties are liable to great fluctuations in culture. Factors such as the age of the organism, its mode of nutrition, its degree of vitality at the time, and the influence of external factors on growth make these characters too variable to aid in the discrimination of genera and species, even when used alongside morphological characters.

Among the more characteristic schemes adopted during this period were those proposed by Eisenberg (1891), Miquel (1891), and Lehmann and Neumann (1897). Despite these efforts and progress in determining the constancy and value of morphological characters, the need for a comprehensive and easily applied classification scheme persisted. This was due in part to the existence of microscopic characters that had not been examined with sufficient accuracy and forms in the literature that were imperfectly described or whose life-history remained unknown.
