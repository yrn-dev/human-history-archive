# We left our country for our country's good

The phrase "We left our country for our country's good" reflects a sentiment of patriotism associated with individuals who depart their homeland to serve a perceived national interest or higher cause. This concept appears in historical records ranging from colonial voyages to diplomatic and military conflicts.

## Colonialism and Patriotism
In the context of early colonial ventures, the sentiment of leaving one's homeland for the benefit of the nation was expressed by those traveling to distant lands. One such individual was Barrington, the author of *A Voyage to Botany Bay* (1801), *The History of New South Wales* (1802), and *The History of New Holland* (1808). Barrington and his companions described themselves as true patriots who crossed widespread seas and distant climes, noting that they left their country for the good of that country, even though they did so without "much éclat or beat of drum." Barrington eventually died at Paramatta at a ripe old age, though the exact date of his death is not recorded.

## National Defense and Loyalty
The tension between leaving one's home and staying to defend it is a recurring theme in historical chronicles. In the *Heimskringla*, during the reign of King Olaf, the king attempted to levy people and shipping to defend Norway against King Canute of Denmark, who had assembled an immense armament of 1,200 vessels. As the threat grew, many people became unwilling to join King Olaf. This prompted a reflection by Sigvat, who argued that it was "evil counsel" to deprive a king of countrymen who would strive to save their country "sword in hand," suggesting that the betrayal of the land was driven by money.

Conversely, some individuals left their country as a matter of strategic survival or negotiation. In another account from the *Heimskringla*, a man named Sveinke agreed to leave his country for the duration of a winter after being persuaded by Kolbjorn. This decision was made based on promises that his estates would be faithfully protected and that he would not be forced to pay scat for his land against his will until his return.

## Imperial Interests and Foreign Relations
The relationship between a ruling power and the territories it occupies often involves claims of mutual benefit. During the Peloponnesian War, Athenians argued to the Melians that their presence was in the interest of the Athenian empire and was intended for the "preservation" of the Melians' country. The Athenians claimed they wished to exercise empire without trouble and see the Melians preserved for the "good of us both," asserting that submission was preferable to suffering the worst.

In other instances, foreign entities were forced to leave a country for the perceived good of the state. In Japan, foreign priests were ordered to quit the estates within twenty days under pain of death. This order was issued because the priests preached a law contrary to that of Japan and had destroyed temples dedicated to Hotoke and Kami. While the priests were expelled, Portuguese merchants were permitted to remain and continue their trade as long as their presence was needed for Japanese affairs, provided they did not bring foreign priests into the country.

## Honor and National Sacrifice
The concept of serving one's country extends to the pursuit of honor and the ultimate sacrifice. In the records of ancient history, Solon recounted the life of Tellus, a citizen of Athens. Tellus was described as an honest and good man who lived without indigence and saw his country flourish. His life concluded with the satisfaction of seeing his grandchildren and dying "gloriously in fighting for his country."

Similarly, in the Balkans, M. Venizelos spoke of his efforts to save Greece, noting the assistance provided by the entry of America and the Russian revolution. Venizelos expressed a desire to "make our honor white" before the Allies and the world by providing substantial assistance in the field to settle the score of the Allies against his country.
