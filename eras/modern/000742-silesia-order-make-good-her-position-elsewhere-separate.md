# Silesia

Silesia is a region that served as a central point of contention in 18th-century European power struggles, specifically between the Kingdom of Prussia and the Habsburg Monarchy of Austria. Its transfer of power from Austria to Prussia was the primary driver of the war that began in 1740.

## Territorial Disputes and the War of Succession
The conflict over Silesia originated from a legal dispute regarding the duchies of Liegnitz, Brieg, and Wohlau. In 1537, Frederick II, the duke of these territories, entered into a treaty with Joachim II, the elector of Brandenburg, stipulating that the duchy would pass to the house of Brandenburg if Frederick's line became extinct. When the last duke, George William, died in 1675, Austria refused to recognize the validity of this treaty and annexed the duchies.

The determination of Frederick II of Prussia to assert his claim to these lands led to the outbreak of war in 1740. This conflict ended two years later with the cession of Silesia to Prussia.

## The Campaign of 1742
Following the initial conflict, Frederick of Prussia sought to secure Silesia through a truce. However, Maria Theresa, the archduchess of Austria and queen of Hungary and Bohemia, maintained a firm opposition. Her resolve was bolstered by the successes of Khevenhueller and an "insurrection" of Hungary—a *levee en masse* of light troops moved by Maria Theresa's personal appeals.

In December 1741, the Prussian general Schwerin crossed the border into Moravia and captured Olmuetz. By January 1742, the Prussian army was concentrated around Olmuetz, and Glatz was invested. While a combined plan involving the French, Saxons, and Prussians was formed to rescue Linz, the city fell. Frederick subsequently marched toward Iglau and invested Bruenn in February. He then pushed southwards through Nikolsburg and Znaim, with Prussian outposts appearing before Vienna.

This advance proved to be a foray. Prince Charles of Lorraine marched to cut the Prussians off from Silesia, while Hungarian levies entered Upper Silesia via the Jablunka Pass. Demoralized Saxon forces departed for their own country, and Frederick fell back through Leutomischl and Zwittau to Kuttenberg in Bohemia. At this point, he remained in contact with Broglie and the now-surrendered Glatz, maintaining a link to Silesia.

## Geopolitical Consequences
The loss of Silesia had significant long-term effects on Austrian and Prussian foreign policy:

*   **Austrian Compensation:** Deprived of Silesia, Austria sought "compensation" elsewhere, specifically in Bavaria. Austrian statesmen viewed the annexation of Bavaria—ruled by the house of Wittelsbach—as a way to remove a perpetual menace and gain overwhelming strength in South Germany. This ambition culminated in the War of Bavarian Succession in 1777 following the death of elector Maximilian III. However, fearing further struggle with Prussia, Maria Theresa overruled her son, Joseph II, and settled for the cession of some districts and the Quarter of the Inn (Innviertel) via the treaty of Teschen.
*   **Prussian Expansion:** Frederick the Great later used his position to incite Catherine the Second of Russia and Maria Theresa of Austria to launch a joint attack on Poland. Frederick found the existence of Poland provocative because a Polish arm reaching the Baltic at Dantzig separated his territories in the empire from his ancestral dominions in East Prussia.

## Brieg
Brieg is a town located in the Prussian province of Silesia on the left bank of the Oder. Historically known as *Civitas Altae Ripae*, its name is derived from the Polish word *Brzeg* (shore). The town obtained municipal rights in 1250 from Duke Henry III of Breslau and was fortified in 1297.

From 1311 to 1675, Brieg served as the capital for an independent line of dukes who were a cadet branch of the Polish dukes of Lower Silesia. The town suffered greatly during the Thirty Years' War and was heavily bombarded by Prussian forces during the war of the Austrian succession. In 1807, it was captured by the French and Bavarians, and its fortifications were destroyed by the French that same year. By 1900, Brieg had a population of 24,090 and was a center for trade and manufacture, producing leather, machinery, linen, cotton, woollen goods, ribbons, hats, cigars, and pasteboard.
