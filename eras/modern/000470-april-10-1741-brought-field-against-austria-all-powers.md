# The Battle of Mollwitz and the War of the Austrian Succession

The Battle of Mollwitz, fought on April 10, 1741, served as a pivotal military engagement that expanded a regional conflict into a broader European struggle known as the War of the Austrian Succession. The battle was the first pitched engagement fought by Frederick II of Prussia and his army.

## Origins of the Conflict
The conflict began in 1740 when Frederick II of Prussia invaded Silesia. This invasion occurred during a period of instability for the Habsburg monarchy following the accession of Maria Theresa to the throne. The monarchy faced potential dissolution due to the fragility of the Pragmatic Sanction; Charles VI had failed to follow the advice of Prince Eugene to maintain an effective army of 200,000 men as a guarantee of the sanction. Consequently, the Austrian forces, which had been disorganized by the Turkish wars, were unable to withstand the Prussian army bequeathed to Frederick by his father.

## The Battle of Mollwitz
In February 1741, the Austrians organized a field army under Count Neipperg to reconquer Silesia. While the Austrians maintained holds in Brieg and Neisse, the Prussians, led by Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Dessau, stormed Glogau on March 9 with mathematical precision. As Neipperg marched from Neisse to Brieg, the Prussians were cut off from their own country, leading to the engagement at Mollwitz on April 10.

The battle was characterized by a stark contrast in military performance. The Prussian right wing of cavalry was routed quickly, and Frederick himself fought in the cavalry melee. When the battle appeared lost, Field Marshal Schwerin persuaded the king to ride away, and Frederick narrowly avoided capture by Austrian hussars. However, the Prussian infantry's tenacity and discipline retrieved the day. The Austrian infantry gradually melted away under Prussian volleys, and the Austrian cavalry was shattered in repeated attempts to break the Prussian lines. Field Marshal Schwerin remained behind to secure the victory.

Among those who distinguished themselves at Mollwitz was Ewald Christian von Kleist, a German poet and Prussian officer who had been recalled to Prussia by Frederick II in 1740.

## Geopolitical Consequences
The Prussian victory at Mollwitz signaled the rise of a new military power in Europe. This realization prompted France to send Marshal Belleisle to Frederick's camp to negotiate an alliance. The victory also encouraged other powers ambitious for Austrian expansion to enter the field, including Spain, Bavaria, Saxony, and Sardinia.

The war subsequently evolved into a wider struggle. France and Prussia were consistently allied with Bavaria and Spain, while England and Holland—traditional enemies of France—supported Austria. Sardinia and Saxony also served as important Austrian allies at various intervals. The primary battle-grounds for France and Austria became Italy, the Low Countries, and Southwest Germany.

## Internal Austrian Struggles and Recovery
The external threats were compounded by internal instability. The Habsburg dynasty was unpopular in its German states and in Vienna, where many favored the claims of Charles of Bavaria. The monarchy suffered from financial ruin, administrative corruption, and the perennial discontent of Slavs and Magyars.

Despite these weaknesses, Maria Theresa provided an unexpected source of strength through her judgment and resolution. Her personal appeals and timely concessions rallied the Hungarians, who provided a *levee en masse* (insurrection) of light troops. This marked a historical shift in which the Hungarians recognized that their interests were bound to those of Austria.

## Resolution of the War
The conflict saw various shifts in territory and power. In Bohemia, the French stormed Prague on November 26, 1741, while the grand-duke Francis, consort of Maria Theresa, moved too slowly to save the fortress. Charles of Bavaria was crowned king of Bohemia on December 19, 1741, and elected imperial throne as Charles VII on January 24, 1742. However, Austrian forces under Field Marshal Khevenhueller later drove the allies back on the Danube and captured Munich.

The War of the Austrian Succession ended with the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen) in 1748. As a result of the war and the treaty of Dresden, Silesia was transferred from Austria to Prussia. Additionally, cessions were made in Italy at the expense of the house of Habsburg.
