# England

## Early Conquests and Nordic Influence
The history of England is characterized by successive waves of Nordic peoples. In the fifth and sixth centuries, the Jutes, Saxons, and Angles conquered most of the region. They were followed in the eighth and ninth centuries by the Danes. By 1013, a Danish King named Canute reigned over England, as well as Norway and Denmark, suggesting the possibility of a great Northmen confederation. 

In 1066, a third wave of Nordic stock arrived from the "Norman" state in France. These settlers had been established there since the days of Rolf the Ganger (912) and had adopted the French language. William, Duke of Normandy, became known as William the Conqueror upon his ascension in 1066. Another account of a King William notes that he was proclaimed king of England and ruled for twenty-one years, after which his descendants continued to rule. This William is noted for the execution of Earl Valthiof, whom the English consider a saint.

## The English Monarchy and Governance
Following the era of the early conquests, various monarchs shaped the English state. Henry VII was served by ministers such as Edmund Dudley (c. 1462-1510), a member of the baronial family of Sutton or Dudley. Dudley served as a privy councillor and speaker of the House of Commons in 1504, assisting the king in replenishing the exchequer and checking the lawlessness of the barons. After the death of Henry VII in April 1509, Dudley was imprisoned by Henry VIII on charges of constructive treason and executed in August 1510.

Later, the throne was held by William III and Mary. Following the period where William reigned alone as a widower, the throne passed to Mary's sister, Anne (1702-14). Upon Anne's death, the Elector of Hanover became George I (1714-27). George I was entirely German and could not speak English. He was succeeded by George II (1727-60). It was not until the reign of George III, the grandson of George II, that England again had a king who was born in England and possessed a fair command of the English language.

## Political and Legal Structures
During the eighteenth century, England entered a phase described as the "Venetian oligarchy" stage. Supreme power resided in Parliament, which was dominated by the Lords. The House of Commons lost much of its original vigour due to the methods of working elections and bribery, a process advanced by Sir Robert Walpole. Parliamentary representation became skewed; populous new centers lacked representation, while "old towns" with little to no population, such as old Sarum, continued to return members.

The legal profession in England is divided into several specialized branches. Barristers handle the advocacy of causes in open court and serve as the chief conveyancers. The highest rank among them is king's or queen's counsel. A significant distinction exists between barristers practicing in the courts of common law and those in chancery. While the Judicature Acts of 1873 and 1875 sought to fuse law and equity, the separation between these branches largely remains. Additionally, common law counsel attach themselves to specific circuits into which England is divided.

## External Suzerainty and the Countship of Auvergne
The kings of England once held suzerainty over the countship of Auvergne, a province of France containing volcanic mountains and fertile valleys. This occurred through the marriage of Henry Plantagenet to Eleanor of Aquitaine. During this period, the countship was divided. William VII, known as "the Young" (1145-1168), was despoiled of a portion of his domain by his uncle, William VIII, known as "the Old." This action was supported by Henry II of England. As a result, William VII retained only the region bounded by the Coux and the Allier, a district that became known from the end of the 13th century as the *Dauphine d'Auvergne*.
