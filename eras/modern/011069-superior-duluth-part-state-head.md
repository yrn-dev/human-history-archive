# Duluth and Superior

Duluth, Minnesota, and Superior, Wisconsin, are two cities located at the western end of Lake Superior. Together, they form one of the most significant commercial ports in the United States, serving as a primary hub for lake transportation and a major gateway for the northwestern tributary region.

## Geography and Infrastructure
Duluth is the county seat of St. Louis County, Minnesota, situated at the mouth of the St. Louis river, approximately 150 miles northeast of St. Paul and Minneapolis. The city is built along the base and side of a high bluff that rises 600 feet above the lake level. 

The area is characterized by a unique natural harbor formed by the combination of St. Louis Bay and Superior Bay (referred to locally as Duluth Harbour). A seven-mile strip of land called Minnesota Point extends toward Wisconsin Point, which projects from the Wisconsin shore; these two points separate the bay from the open lake. While the natural entrance to the harbor is the narrow channel between these points, a ship-canal also exists across Minnesota Point. This canal is spanned by an aerial bridge 186 feet above the water and 400 feet in length.

Additionally, a swing bridge connects Duluth and Superior over the St. Louis river. This turning bridge features a 500-foot center opening and is 58 feet wide. Its 2,000-ton girders support a double track for trains, while cantilevers on each side provide a roadway, footway, and trolley track. The bridge is operated by two large electric motors and can be opened in two minutes.

## History and Population
The site of Duluth was likely first visited by French *coureurs-des-bois*, possibly Groseilliers and Radisson. The first known visitor was Daniel Greysolon, Sieur Du Lhut, a French explorer and trader who built a stockaded trading post on the north shore at the mouth of the Pigeon river around 1678; the city is named after him. In approximately 1752, a trading post was established at Fond du Lac, which later served as a depot for Astor's American Fur Company.

Permanent settlement in Duluth proper did not begin until 1853. By 1860, the population was only 80. The city was incorporated in 1870, the same year railway connections to the south were established. Growth remained slow until 1880, when the population was 3,483. However, the development of the Mesabi and Vermilion iron ranges, the opening of Lake Superior to commerce, and the extension of railways into the northwestern wheat region triggered rapid expansion. The population grew by more than 850% between 1880 and 1890, reaching 33,115. By 1900, the population was 52,969, and the 1910 census recorded 78,466 residents.

Of the 52,969 residents in 1900, 20,983 were foreign-born. This group included 5,099 English-Canadians, 5,047 Swedes, 2,655 Norwegians, 1,685 Germans, and 1,285 French-Canadians. The 1900 census also recorded 357 negroes.

## Commerce and Industry
Duluth-Superior is a premier commercial port and the largest coal-distributing center in the northwest, possessing some of the world's largest coal docks. The port handles enormous quantities of grain from the Dakotas, Manitoba, and the Red River Valley via more than twenty grain elevators with a total net capacity of nearly 35,000,000 bushels.

The region is critical for the shipment of iron ore from the Mesabi and Vermilion ranges. In 1907, more than 29,000,000 tons of iron ore were shipped from the port. Other industrial activities include:
* **Manufacturing:** Flour and lumber mills, foundries, machine shops, and factories producing wooden ware, cooperage, lath, shingles, sashes, doors, and blinds.
* **Steel:** The Minnesota Steel Co. began constructing great mills in 1909.
* **Other:** The city serves as an important hay market and utilizes the St. Louis river for water power.

In 1905, the value of Duluth's factory products was $10,139,009, representing a 29.8% increase over 1900.

## Transportation and Urban Features
Duluth is served by several major railways, including the Northern Pacific, the Great Northern, the Chicago & North-Western (the North-Western line), the Duluth, South Shore & Atlantic, the Duluth, Missabe & Northern, and the Duluth and Iron Range. It is also connected to Buffalo via the Great Northern Steamship Company.

The city's residential sections are located on commanding heights, which are considered healthful and attractive. The city features a system of boulevards—including the popular Boulevard drive—and parks such as Chester, Lincoln, Cascade, Portland, Fairmount, and Lester.

Notable buildings and institutions include:
* **Education:** A state normal school (erected 1902) and a technical high school. The central high school, costing approximately $500,000, features an auditorium for 2,000 people and a 230-foot square clock tower.
* **Public Services:** A Carnegie library (containing 50,000 volumes in 1908), the Masonic temple, the court house, the board of trade, and the chamber of commerce.
* **Healthcare:** St. Luke's and St. Mary's hospitals.
* **Government:** A federal fish hatchery and a life-saving station located on Minnesota Point.
