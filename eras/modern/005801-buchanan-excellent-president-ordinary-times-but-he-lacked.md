# James Buchanan

James Buchanan (1791–1868) served as the fifteenth president of the United States. Born near Foltz in Franklin County, Pennsylvania, to parents of Scottish-Irish Presbyterian descent, he graduated from Dickinson College in 1809. After studying law from 1809 to 1812, he was admitted to the bar in 1812. His early political career included serving in the lower house of the Pennsylvania state legislature from 1814 to 1816 and as a representative in Congress from 1821 to 1831.

## Congressional and Diplomatic Career
During his tenure in Congress, Buchanan chaired the judiciary committee, where he led the 1830 impeachment trial of Judge James H. Peck. He also attempted to increase the number of Supreme Court judges and relieve them of circuit duties, and successfully defeated an effort to repeal the twenty-fifth section of the Judiciary Act of 1789. Originally a member of the Federalist party, he later associated with the Democrats and supported the Jackson-Van Buren faction.

Buchanan held several high-level diplomatic and executive roles before the presidency. He represented the United States at the court of St Petersburg (1832–1833), where he negotiated a commercial treaty. He served in the United States Senate from December 1834 until March 1845 and was Secretary of State under President Polk from 1845 to 1849. This period was characterized by the Mexican War, the annexation of Texas, and negotiations with Great Britain regarding the Oregon question. After a period of private legal practice, he was appointed minister to Great Britain by President Pierce in 1853.

## Presidential Election and Early Administration
Buchanan's views on slavery aligned with the conservative Northern Democrats; while he felt the institution was morally wrong, he believed Congress could not interfere with it in existing states nor hinder territorial expansion. He approved of the Compromise of 1850, the annexation of Texas, and the war with Mexico, while opposing the Wilmot Proviso. In 1854, he joined Pierre Soule and J.Y. Mason in issuing the Ostend Manifesto.

Because he was abroad during the Kansas-Nebraska debates, he avoided the unpopularity that affected President Pierce and Stephen A. Douglas. Consequently, he was nominated as a compromise candidate and elected president, receiving 174 electoral votes against 114 for Republican John C. Frémont and 8 for Millard Fillmore.

At the start of his term, Buchanan appointed Frederick P. Stanton and Robert J. Walker (territorial governor of Kansas) and assured them of his commitment to the principle of popular sovereignty. However, he later used his influence to push for the admission of Kansas under the pro-slavery Lecompton Constitution, ignoring the wishes of the majority of settlers. This shift in policy led Walker to resign and Stanton to be removed from office. This change was attributed to timidity rather than a desire for Southern Democratic favor. Influenced by Jacob Thompson and Howell Cobb, Buchanan believed this path was the only way to prevent civil war. Federal patronage was utilized to support the Lecompton measure and the compromise English Bill, and to block Douglas's 1858 Senate election. These actions were later explored in the 1860 Covode Investigation by the House of Representatives, though the investigation was considered partisan.

## The Secession Crisis
Following the election of Abraham Lincoln, the South Carolina legislature called for a convention to consider secession. Buchanan's response, based on a report by Attorney-General Jeremiah S. Black, argued that while states had no legal right to secede, the federal government lacked the power to prevent it forcibly. He maintained that the president's duty was limited to protecting federal laws and property.

As the secession movement grew, Southern cabinet members resigned, and Buchanan fell under the influence of Northern leaders, including Stanton, Black, and Dix. He continued seeking a peaceful resolution, supporting the Peace Congress and the Crittenden Compromise. Regarding the removal of troops from Fort Moultrie to Fort Sumter in December 1860, Buchanan disapproved of Major Anderson's actions. The archive notes that charges claiming this removal violated a presidential pledge to preserve the status quo in Charleston harbor are unfounded, as is the claim that Buchanan was only prevented from ordering Anderson back by threats of cabinet resignations.

## Later Life and Legacy
Buchanan's term expired on March 4, 1861. He retired to his home, Wheatland, near Lancaster, Pennsylvania, where he died on June 1, 1868. He never married. While his presidential mistakes are often emphasized, he is described as a man of considerable ability, highest patriotism, and unimpeachable honesty.
