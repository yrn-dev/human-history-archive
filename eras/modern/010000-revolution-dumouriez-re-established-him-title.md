# Charles François Dumouriez

Charles François Dumouriez (1739–1823) was a French general and political figure who played a significant role during the French Revolution. Born in Cambray, he was educated at the college of Louis-le-Grand and began his military career in 1757 as a volunteer during the campaign of Rossbach. After serving with distinction in the later German campaigns of the Seven Years' War, he was retired as a captain.

## Early Career and Diplomatic Service
Following his initial military service, Dumouriez traveled through Spain, Portugal, Italy, and Corsica. His reports on Corsican affairs led to his appointment as a lieutenant-colonel on the staff of the French expeditionary corps sent to that island. He subsequently joined the *Secret du roi*, the secret service of Louis XV. In 1770, he was sent on a mission to Poland, where he organized a Polish militia. During this period, the court of Versailles appointed him commander-in-chief of the Confederation of Bar. While he reorganized the confederates' army, he was described as an adroit adventurer who failed to distinguish himself as a politician; notably, he called King Stanislaus a tyrant and traitor just as the king was preparing to join the Confederation.

Following the fall of Choiseul, Dumouriez was recalled and imprisoned for six months in the Bastille, then detained in Caen until the accession of Louis XVI. In 1778, after submitting memorials regarding the defense of Normandy's ports, he was appointed commandant of Cherbourg, a post he held for ten years. He reached the rank of *marechal de camp* in 1788.

## Role in the French Revolution
At the start of the Revolution, Dumouriez moved to Paris and joined the Jacobin Club. After the flight to Varennes, he gained prominence by offering to march to the assistance of the Assembly. He aligned himself with the Girondist party and was appointed minister of foreign affairs on March 15, 1792. In this capacity, he planned the invasion of the Low Countries and was primarily responsible for the declaration of war against Austria on April 20, 1792. 

Briefly, he served as minister of war in June 1792 but resigned two days later due to the king's refusal to reach terms with the Assembly. Following the events of August 10 and the flight of Lafayette, he was given command of the "Army of the Centre."

## Military Campaigns and the Republic
Dumouriez led French troops into the Belgian possessions of the Hapsburgs in 1792. His forces, including subordinate Kellermann, checked a Prussian advance at the battle of Valmy on September 20, 1792, a decisive encounter that saved the Revolution and preceded the proclamation of the republic on September 21. Dumouriez then achieved a severe defeat of the Austrians at Jemappes on November 6, 1792, allowing French forces to enter Belgium.

During this period, Dumouriez utilized his influence to appoint associates; for instance, he re-established Guillaume de Bonne-Carrere as director-general of the department of foreign affairs in March 1792 and appointed Jacques Étienne Joseph Alexandre Macdonald as his aide-de-camp.

## Treason and Exile
Dumouriez's standing declined as he clashed with the extremist Jacobins, who criticized his methodical approach to warfare. After suffering a defeat at Neerwinden in January 1793, he plotted to march on Paris and overthrow the republic. He was joined in this plot by the duc de Chartres (who had been serving under him in Holland). On April 5, 1793, Dumouriez and the duc de Chartres escaped into Austrian lines.

This betrayal had repercussions for his associates; Guillaume de Bonne-Carrere, who had been sent to Belgium as an agent of the republic, was arrested on April 2, 1793, for his involvement in Dumouriez's treason. Conversely, General Macdonald refused to desert to the Austrians with Dumouriez.

## Later Life and Death
Dumouriez spent his remaining years in exile, engaging in intrigues with Louis XVIII and attempting to establish an Orleanist monarchy. In 1795, while in Hamburg, he hoped to make the duke of Orleans king. In 1804, he settled in England, where the government provided him with an annual pension of £1,200. He served as a valuable adviser to the War Office during the struggle against Napoleon.

Between 1814 and 1815, Dumouriez unsuccessfully sought the baton of a marshal of France from Louis XVIII. He died at Turville Park, near Henley-on-Thames, on March 14, 1823. His memoirs were published in Hamburg in 1794, with an enlarged edition, *La Vie et les memoires du General Dumouriez*, appearing in Paris in 1823.
