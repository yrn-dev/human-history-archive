# Revolution

The term revolution appears in the archive's records in several distinct contexts, ranging from political upheavals in France and Great Britain to the systemic shifts of the industrial era.

## The French Revolution and the Directory
Following the French Revolution, a period of governance known as the Directory emerged. This five-year interlude was characterized by a leadership that was victorious in foreign affairs but uncreative domestically. Members of the Directory, such as the honest republican Carnot and the rogue Barras, were described as being too dishonest to manage the necessary economic and financial reconstruction of France and too anxious to maintain the glories of their offices to create a superseding constitution.

The zeal of the revolution drove French armies into north Italy, south Germany, Switzerland, Belgium, and Holland, resulting in the expulsion of kings and the establishment of republics. However, this propagandist zeal was accompanied by the looting of treasures from liberated peoples to address the French Government's financial embarrassments. Consequently, these conflicts shifted from being a "holy war of freedom" toward aggressive wars reminiscent of the ancient régime. The Directorate maintained a French-centered, restless, and grasping tradition of foreign policy that remained as vigorous as it had been before the revolution.

In the aftermath of the Revolution, the state confiscated various books. In 1794, Antoine Alexandre Barbier, a librarian and bibliographer, was appointed to a temporary commission of the arts. His task was to distribute these confiscated books among the libraries of Paris. During the execution of this duty, Barbier discovered the manuscripts of the works of Fenelon and the letters of Huet, the bishop of Avranches.

## The British Revolution and Constitutional Government
In Great Britain, the Revolution led to a gradual evolution of the executive government. It was several years after the Revolution, specifically by the reign of William III in 1693, that the king adopted two fundamental principles of constitutional executive government:
1. A ministry must consist of statesmen who are identified with one another and hold the same political principles.
2. The ministry must operate on a parliamentary basis, meaning it must retain and command the majority of votes in the legislature.

These principles eventually resulted in the formation of the Cabinet Council. The cabinet serves as the government of Great Britain, exercising nearly all powers vested in the sovereign. Members of the cabinet are collectively and jointly responsible for all measures; if differences of opinion are publicly manifested, the cabinet ends. The cabinet is responsible to the sovereign for executive business and collectively responsible to parliament for both legislative measures and executive conduct.

## The Mechanical and Industrial Revolution
The nineteenth century was marked by a mechanical and industrial revolution. Between 1814 and 1914, the fermentation of ideas can be divided into two main periods. The first period, spanning 1814 to 1848, saw liberal thinking and writing occur primarily within limited circles, without significant development of thought among the general mass of the people. 

During this time, world affairs operated on "old intellectual capital" based on the ideas of the Revolution and the counter-revolution. The dominant ideas of this era were split between:
* **Liberal ideas:** A certain vague equalitarianism and freedom.
* **Conservative ideas:** Social privilege, obedience, organized religion, and monarchy.
