# Grenoble and the Dauphine in the Revolutionary Era

## Historical Overview of Grenoble
Grenoble was established on the site of Cularo, a village of the Allobroges. It gained significance at the end of the 3rd century when it was fortified by Maximian and Diocletian. The city's name is derived from Gratianopolis, a title likely given in honor of Gratian in the 4th century, who elevated the settlement to the rank of a *civitas*. 

Following periods of control under the Franks (532) and the Burgundians (c. 440), the city became part of the kingdom of Provence between 879 and 1032. After the collapse of that kingdom, a struggle for power occurred between the counts of Albon and the city's bishops. The counts of Albon eventually prevailed in the 12th century, adopting the title of Dauphins of the Viennois in the 13th century. In 1349, Grenoble and the rest of the Dauphine were ceded to France, though the town maintained various municipal privileges granted by the dauphins, starting with a charter in 1242.

## Political Unrest and the "Day of the Tiles"
In the years leading up to the French Revolution, Grenoble became a center of political tension. The city's *parlement*, while strictly a judicial authority, had maintained traditions of independence following the 1628 suspension of the states-general of the Dauphine. In 1788, the king attempted to weaken the power of this *parlement*, an action that roused the local population to arms. This culminated in the "day of the tiles" on June 7, 1788, which is noted for the defeat of royal forces.

Further premonitory signs of the revolution appeared on July 21, 1788, when the "States" of Dauphine held an unauthorized meeting at the *Salle du Jeu de Paume* (tennis court) of the castle of Vizille, located near Grenoble.

## Antoine Barnave and the Dauphine
Antoine Pierre Joseph Marie Barnave (1761–1793) was a prominent figure born in Grenoble to a Protestant family. His father served as an advocate at the *parlement* of Grenoble. Due to his Protestant faith, Barnave was educated by his mother. At age twenty-two, he gained recognition for a discourse delivered before the local *parlement* regarding the division of political powers.

Barnave became a leading voice for the general sentiment in the province during the early revolutionary excitement, publishing a pamphlet titled *Esprit des edits enregistres militairement le 20 mai 1788*. He and his father were elected deputies to the states of Dauphine. Later, Barnave was chosen as a deputy of the *tiers etat* for his native province to the states-general convoked at Versailles on May 5, 1789. 

In the Assembly, Barnave became a friend to leaders of the popular party and formed "the triumvirate" with Alexandre Lameth and Adrien Duport. He was a principal member of the club that later became the Jacobins, drafting its first rules and manifesto. While he sought to preserve both the monarchy and the freedom of France, he participated in attacks on church property, the clergy, the monarchy, and provincial *parlements*.

## Administrative and Economic Development
In 1790, Grenoble was designated as the capital of the newly formed department of the Isère. The city's geography has historically made it susceptible to floods, specifically from the wild Drac; notable floods occurred in 1219 and in 1778 (the *deluge de la Saint Crepin*).

Economically, the city is known for the manufacture of kid gloves, specifically *gants Jouvin*, named after X. Jouvin (1800–1844). The industry comprises approximately 80 factories employing 18,500 people, the majority of whom are women, with an annual output of about 800,000 dozen pairs. Other local productions include carved furniture, straw hats, liqueurs, and artificial cements.

## Later History and Notable Figures
Grenoble played a role in the return of Napoleon from Elba, being the first important town to open its gates to him on March 7, 1815, though it surrendered to the Austrian army in July of that year.

The city has been associated with various notable figures and institutions. In 1084, St. Bruno and six companions met with the bishop of Grenoble, who directed them to a desolate, rocky spot called Chartreuse in the nearby mountains. There, they established a life of extreme austerity and prayer, founding the Carthusian order. In the field of science, Charles Lory (1823–1889), a geologist, held the chair of geology at the University of Grenoble starting in 1852, conducting extensive research on the geology of the French Alps and the departments of Hautes Alpes, Drôme, and Isère.
