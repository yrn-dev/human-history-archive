# National Defences (1860) and War in Bulgaria, a Narrative of Personal Experience

The titles *Our National Defences* (1860) and *War in Bulgaria, a Narrative of Personal Experience* (1879) are works authored by Baker Pasha. Baker was a military figure who served as the commander of the Egyptian police until his death in 1887.

## Military Career of Baker Pasha
Baker Pasha is noted for his involvement in the Sudan War. During this conflict, he led a force of 3,500 men in an attempt to relieve Tokar. At El Teb, Baker encountered enemy forces under the command of Osman Digna. The initial rush of the enemy caused Baker's men to become panic-stricken, leading to a slaughter of his troops and the annihilation of his force. Baker and a small number of officers managed to fight their way out of the engagement.

Following this defeat, British troops arrived at Suakin, and Sir Gerald Graham initiated an offensive. Baker Pasha accompanied the British force, serving as a guide during the march back to the site of his previous defeat. During the second battle of El Teb, which was desperately fought, Baker was wounded. In addition to the aforementioned titles, Baker authored a work titled *Clouds in the East*, published in London in 1876.

## Bulgarian Military Conflicts and Political Instability
The archive contains records of various conflicts involving Bulgaria across different eras. In the ninth and tenth centuries, Bulgarians reigned south of the Danube. During this period, they achieved a significant victory over the Roman Empire when they surprised the camp of Emperor Nicephorus. The emperor and his great officers were slaughtered in their tents; Nicephorus's head was displayed on a spear and his skull, decorated with gold, was used during victory feasts. Later in the same century, the Bulgarian royal line produced Simeon, who was educated in Constantinople in the logic of Aristotle and the rhetoric of Demosthenes before becoming a king and warrior.

In later history, Bulgaria faced an invasion by Servia, which declared war on November 14. This occurred while Turkish troops were massed at the frontier. The Bulgarian army faced a crisis when the tsar ordered the withdrawal of Russian officers who held all higher posts. Despite being imperfectly equipped and led by subaltern officers, the Bulgarian army resisted the invasion. Under the leadership of Prince Alexander, the army won victories at Tsaribrod and Slivnitza (November 19), and captured Pirot on November 27. Further progress was stopped by Austrian intervention.

The resulting treaty of Bucharest, signed March 3, 1886, restored peace. While Servia escaped a war indemnity, the union with Eastern Rumelia was practically secured. On April 5, the convention of Top-Khané recognized Prince Alexander as the governor-general of eastern Rumelia, administratively uniting the countries.

## Internal Unrest and the Abdication of Prince Alexander
The military successes of Prince Alexander made him a national hero, which prompted Russia to seek his removal. A conspiracy was formed involving prominent figures, including Archbishop Clement, Karaveloff (the prime minister), and Tzankoff, as well as officers who felt slighted regarding the distribution of rewards. On the night of August 21, officers seized the prince in his palace and forced him to sign his abdication under threat of death. He was subsequently transported to Reni on Russian soil via the Danube at Rakhovo. This action was met with instant disapproval by the majority of the nation, including Colonel Mutkuroff and Stamboloff, the president of the assembly.

## Bulgaria in the Great War
Bulgaria entered the European struggle in 1915, though it was already weakened by two previous bloody wars. The population was initially told the campaign would be short, but by 1918, the country was suffering severely. Bulgarian manhood remained mobilized in distant Macedonia, leaving fields fallow. Harvests, reaped by children and women, had to be shared with the Germans, leading to widespread want and semi-starvation.

By the opening months of 1918, Bulgarian political leaders were disillusioned. The people, wearied by fighting and recognizing that a German victory was hopeless, sought peace. This culminated in a complete collapse and the flight of King Ferdinand, leading to Bulgaria's surrender.

## Post-War Settlements
Following the conflict, Germany accepted all arrangements made by the Allied and Associated Powers regarding rights or interests claimed by Germany or its nationals in Bulgaria. Additionally, Germany agreed to transfer to the Allies all claims against Bulgaria related to wartime agreements. Germany also agreed to expropriate any rights or interests of its nationals in public utilities within Bulgaria and transfer them to the Reparations Commission.
