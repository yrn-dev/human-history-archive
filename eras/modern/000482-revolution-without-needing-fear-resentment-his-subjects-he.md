# Political Stability and the Management of Resentment

The maintenance of political order often depends on a ruler's ability to navigate the resentment of subjects or rivals, whether through the restoration of traditional structures, the use of diplomacy, or the strategic application of force. History provides various examples of how leaders and figures of influence have managed these tensions to prevent revolution or consolidate power.

## The Habsburg Policy of Stability
Under Leopold II, the Habsburg Empire navigated a period of significant external and internal pressure. While Leopold had previously earned a reputation as a liberal and enlightened ruler during his time as grand-duke of Tuscany, the progress of the Revolution in France prompted a shift toward reaction. To ensure stability, Leopold reverted to traditional Habsburg methods, restoring the supremacy of the Church as the primary effective bond of the empire. 

He resolved the *Einheitsstaat* back into its original elements, reinstating the old abuses and the machinery of estates and diets. This policy of "stability," which would later be associated with Metternich and last until 1848, was initially successful because the revolutionary spirit of France had not yet reached the heart of the empire. Instead, national rivalries were characterized by a "somnolent clinging to traditional privileges." Consequently, Leopold was able to act as a champion of order against the Revolution and execute the Empire's ban against the insurgent Liegeois without fearing the resentment of his own subjects. This position allowed him to skillfully negotiate the treaty of Reichenbach on August 15, 1790, ending a quarrel with Prussia, and the armistice of Giurgevo with Turkey on September 10. By August 4, 1791, a definitive peace was signed at Sistova with Turkey, establishing the *status quo*.

## Diplomacy and Neutrality in Flanders
In 14th-century Flanders, the management of conflicting loyalties was central to maintaining economic and political order. While Count Louis of Flanders sympathized with France and supported King Philip of Valois, the Flemish cities feared a rupture with England. Their cloth industry relied entirely on English wool, making friendly relations with England an economic necessity.

Jacques Van Artevelde, a respected and wealthy member of a patrician family, emerged as the leader of public opinion and captain of the municipal army in 1338. When delegates from Edward III arrived to win Flanders to the English cause, Artevelde initially pursued a policy of neutrality. His goal was to prevent Flanders from becoming a battlefield for hostile armies and to avoid the resentment of the French King, while simultaneously assuring England of his friendship to secure necessary commercial advantages.

## Power and Public Exposure in Athens
In ancient Athens, the relationship between power and public resentment was often played out in the theater. Aristophanes used comedy to attack the government and powerful figures, such as Cleon. Despite Cleon's reputation as the greatest captain of the age following his return from the expedition against Sphacteria, Aristophanes portrayed him as the son of a tanner whose rise was due only to impudence.

The fear of Cleon's resentment was so pervasive that no other comedians dared to represent him on stage. Consequently, Aristophanes was forced to play the role of Cleon himself. Because no workman would venture to create a mask resembling Cleon, Aristophanes smeared his face with wine-lees. Through this performance, he accused Cleon of seducing the people, a passion for bribes, and embezzling public treasures.

## Internal Threats and Imperial Control
The fear of internal revolution has historically driven the actions of states. The Lacedaemonians, facing incursions in Laconia and the desertion of Helots, became seriously uneasy as they feared a "march of revolution" in their own country. This fear drove them to send envoys to Athens to attempt to recover prisoners and Pylos, despite their unwillingness to reveal their vulnerability to the Athenians.

In the case of Athens' subject-allies, such as the Akanthians, the lack of resentment toward their rulers often determined their loyalty. Evidence suggests the Akanthians had little to complain about as subject-allies and would have remained in that capacity if not for the combined pressure of impending loss and the assurances offered by the Spartan representative, Brasidas. The absence of actual suffering or a sense of present grievance meant that the Akanthians did not hail Brasidas with the cordiality he expected; instead, they required a combination of threats and promises to be induced to revolt.
