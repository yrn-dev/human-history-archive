# The Austro-Hungarian Bank and the Agreement of 1907

## Overview of the Austro-Hungarian Bank
The Austro-Hungarian Bank is a joint-stock company founded in 1816, originally titled the Austrian National Bank until it was renamed in 1878. It holds the legal exclusive right to issue bank-notes for the two states of the monarchy. Under a bank statute from 1899, the bank operated with a stock of L8,780,000. Its notes of issue are required to be covered by legal specie—specifically current silver and gold—to the extent of two-fifths, with the remainder of the paper circulation handled according to bank usage.

The state is entitled to a portion of the bank's clear profits under certain conditions. Management and state supervision are conducted on a footing of equality, ensuring both states maintain the same influence. Financial records from the end of 1900 show a capital of L8,750,000, a reserve fund of L428,250, cash of L50,754,000, and a note circulation of L62,251,000. By 1907, the cash increased to L60,036,625, the note circulation rose to L84,501,000, and the reserve fund reached L548,041.

## The Agreement of 1907
The relations between the two halves of the Dual Monarchy became critical due to a conflict between Habsburg imperialism, which sought to preserve military and economic unity, and Magyar nationalism, which aimed to maintain ascendancy in an independent Hungary. This tension was eased when the introduction of a Universal Suffrage Bill by Joseph Kristoffy, the minister of the interior in the cabinet of Baron Fejervary, brought Hungarian opposition leaders to terms.

The resulting agreement of 1907 served as a truce. Its primary points included:
*   **Bank Charter:** The charter of the bank was renewed until 1910, with Hungarians receiving a larger share in its direction than they had previously.
*   **Customs Union:** The reciprocal and binding treaty of the Customs Union lapsed, though both sides promised to continue it in practice until December 31, 1907. 

The agreement stipulated that negotiations for a renewal of the alliance begin no later than 1901, with a possible renewal starting in 1903 to coincide with the expiration of commercial treaties. If renewed, tariffs would be revised before new commercial treaties were established. If not, no new treaties would extend beyond 1907, allowing each party to determine its own policy. While the Hungarian parliament sanctioned these arrangements, the Austrian parliament did not; consequently, they were proclaimed by imperial warrant. This included the Bank charter on September 23 and new duties on sugar, brandy, and beer on July 20. Additionally, in November, Quota-Deputations agreed that Hungary would pay 33-3/4, a small increase also proclaimed by imperial warrant in Austria.

## Subsequent Conflicts and the 1910 Expiration
The compromise of 1907 proved unstable. The Wekerle ministry, which took office on April 9, 1906, contained elements that made a lasting agreement impossible. Specifically, the "Magyar word of command" remained an unsettled issue, and the renewal of the Austro-Hungarian State Bank charter was not part of the 1907 agreement. Furthermore, while universal suffrage had been granted in the Austrian half of the monarchy, the Wekerle ministry failed to redeem its pledge for franchise reform in Hungary.

In March 1908, Mr. Hallo proposed to the Hungarian parliament that the bank charter, set to expire at the end of 1910, should not be renewed. He suggested that if a convention between the banks of Hungary and Austria could not be negotiated, a separate Hungarian Bank should be established. This issue became acute again in the spring of 1909 during a Hungarian ministerial crisis. Extreme members of the Independence party demanded the creation of separate banks for each state, suggesting that they might, at most, share common superintendence.
