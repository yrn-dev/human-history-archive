# Louis Gabriel Ambroise, Vicomte de Bonald

Louis Gabriel Ambroise, Vicomte de Bonald (1754–1840), was a French politician and philosopher. Born on October 2, 1754, at Le Monna near Millau in Aveyron, Bonald became a prominent figure in the theocratic or traditionalist school of thought. This school, which also included writers such as Lamennais, d'Eckstein, Ballanche, and de Maistre, stood in opposition to the rationalism of the previous age. The theocratic school emphasized the principle of authority, prioritizing order over progress and freedom, and placing revelation above individual reason.

## Political Career and Life
Due to his dislike of the principles of the Revolution, Bonald emigrated in 1791. During this period, he joined the army of the prince of Conde and eventually settled in Heidelberg. It was there that he wrote his first major work, *Theorie du pouvoir politique et religieux* (1796), a highly conservative text that was condemned by the Directory. 

Upon returning to France, Bonald lived in retirement as an object of suspicion. However, in 1806, he became involved in the conduct of the *Mercure de France* alongside Fievee and Chateaubriand. In 1808, he was appointed as a councillor of the Imperial University, an institution he had frequently attacked. Following the restoration, Bonald served as a member of the council of public instruction and sat as a deputy in the chamber from 1815 to 1822. His political speeches were characterized by extreme conservatism, and he advocated for literary censorship. This led to his appointment as minister of state in 1822, where he presided over the censorship commission. He was made a peer in 1823, though he lost this dignity in 1830 after refusing to take the oath. A member of the Academy since 1816, he retired to Le Monna after 1830 and died there on November 23, 1840.

## Philosophical System
Bonald's writings focused primarily on political and social philosophy. His system was based on the "divine origin of language," asserting that the first language contained the essence of all truth. He believed that "L'homme pense sa parole avant de parler sa pensee." From this central principle, Bonald deduced the existence of God, the infallibility of the church, and the supreme authority and divine origin of the Holy Scriptures.

A recurring formula in his speculations is the triad of cause, means, and effect, which he observed throughout nature:
*   **The Universe:** The first cause is the mover, movement is the means, and bodies are the result.
*   **The State:** Power is the cause, ministers are the means, and subjects are the effects.
*   **The Family:** This relation is exemplified by the father, mother, and children.
*   **The Religious World:** In the triad of God, the Mediator, and Man, God is to the God-Man as the God-Man is to Man.

Using these foundations, Bonald constructed a system of political absolutism. Critics have noted that this system relied on baseless hypotheses rather than well-grounded premises and lacked the acquiescence of those it sought to subject. While his style was considered pure, vigorous, and ornate, some assessments suggest he was a dilettante in abstract thought, relying more on the sincerity and vigour of his statements than on cogent reasoning.

## Published Works
Bonald authored several significant texts, including:
*   *Theorie du pouvoir politique et religieux* (1796)
*   *Essai analytique sur les lois naturelles de l'ordre social* (1800)
*   *Du divorce considere au XIXe siecle* (1801)
*   *Legislation primitive* (1802)
*   *Recherches philosophiques sur les premiers objets de connaissances morales* (1818, 2 vols.)
*   *Melanges litteraires et politiques, demonstration philosophique du principe constitutif de la societe* (1819, 1852)

His collected works first appeared in 12 volumes between 1817 and 1819, with a later three-volume edition published by the Abbe Migne in 1859.

## Family and Descendants
Bonald had four sons:
*   **Victor de Bonald (1780–1871):** He followed his father into exile and served as the rector of the academy of Montpellier. Though he lost his post during the Hundred Days, he regained it during the second restoration before resigning in 1830. He wrote a biography of his father, as well as *Moise et les geologues modernes* (1835) and *Des vrais principes opposes aux erreurs du XIXe siecle* (1833).
*   **Louis Jacques Maurice (1787–1870):** A cardinal (1841) who was condemned by the council of state for a pastoral letter attacking the *Manuel de droit ecclesiastique* by Dupin the elder. In 1851, he advocated in the senate for the use of force to maintain the temporal power of Rome.
*   **Henri (d. 1846):** A contributor to legitimist journals.
*   **Rene:** Served as the interim prefect of Aveyron in 1817.
