# Marriage

Marriage practices and legal frameworks have varied significantly across different cultures, time periods, and jurisdictions, ranging from customary arrangements and political alliances to formalized state and international laws.

## Early and Classical Customs
In early Roman tradition, some accounts suggest that the practice of parting a bride's hair with the point of a spear was performed to commemorate the first Roman marriage, which was said to have been effected through war and battle.

In ancient Greece, specifically among the Spartans, views on marriage differed from later norms. Some evidence suggests that only the eldest brother might have been able to afford to marry. Additionally, the laws of Solôn permitted a relative in his old age to claim an *ἐπίκληρος* in marriage. In other Greek contexts, marriage was used as a tool for political and territorial gain; for example, a city that accepted a marriage offer from a particular leader was immensely enriched through the annexation of lands and collective property from destroyed cities such as Kaulonia and Hipponium.

## Early English and Kentish Practices
Information regarding early marriage customs in England is limited, but it is evident that prohibited relationships differed from those later recognized by the Church. In Kent, it was customary for a man to marry his stepmother. The Kentish laws portray marriage as being little more than a matter of purchase. 

Customs included a payment made to the bride's guardian and a present given to the bride herself, known as *morgengifu*. For queens, this *morgengifu* often consisted of considerable estates and a residence. Queens also maintained their own fortified residences and retinues. Furthermore, Kentish law provided for widows to receive a proportionate share of their husbands' property.

## Legal Frameworks in the United States
In the United States, marriage and divorce laws are state laws and statutes, as the national power lacks legislative or judicial cognisance over these matters within those localities. While the absence of ecclesiastical courts created some difficulty, English decisions on marriage and divorce are generally referred to with deference.

Under the common law of the states, a complete marriage is constituted by a contract *per verba de presenti* or *per verba de futuro cum copula*. While state legislatures may impose conditions or extend the minimum age for marriage, American jurisprudence generally holds that a marriage valid under common law remains good despite the existence of a statute, unless that statute contains express words of nullity. 

Examples of this legal application include:
* **Pennsylvania:** Marriages were held to be good even if they were not solemnized before twelve witnesses, as required by statute.
* **New Hampshire:** Although only ministers of the gospel and justices are authorized to solemnize marriages, marriages by consent (as per common law) without such officials have been held valid.
* **Massachusetts:** In contrast, it was held that parties could not solemnize their own marriage, and mutual agreements not following the statute were considered void, though this is regarded by some as an isolated exception.

## British International Marriage Law
The Foreign Marriage Act of 1892 consolidated English law regarding marriages celebrated abroad. Under this act, a marriage between British subjects abroad is considered as valid as one solemnized in England if it is performed according to local law or in the presence of diplomatic or consular agents appointed as "marriage officers." 

The act removed the "fiction of assimilation," meaning a marriage at a British embassy solemnized by an ordained clergyman could no longer be upheld on the basis that the embassy was British soil. Additionally, an order in council dated October 28, 1892, requires marriage officers to be satisfied that a marriage between a British subject (woman) and an alien (man) will be recognized by the law of the alien's home country.
