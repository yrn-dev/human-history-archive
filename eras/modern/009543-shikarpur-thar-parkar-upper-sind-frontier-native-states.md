# Shikarpur, Thar and Parkar, and Upper Sind Frontier

The districts of Shikarpur, Thar and Parkar, and the Upper Sind Frontier are administrative divisions located within the Sind division of the Bombay Presidency in British India. The Bombay Presidency is a province governed by a governor, consisting of both British districts and native states.

## Geography and Administration
The Bombay Presidency extends from 13 deg. 53' to 28 deg. 45' N. and from 66 deg. 40' to 76 deg. 30' E. It is bounded by the Arabian Sea to the west, Madras and Mysore to the south, Indore, Hyderabad, and the Central Provinces to the east, and Rajputana, the Punjab, and Baluchistan to the north. 

The presidency is organized into four commissionerships: the southern (Carnatic), the central (Deccan), the northern (Gujarat), and Sind. Within the Sind division, the territory is further divided into districts, which include Karachi, Hyderabad, Shikarpur, Thar and Parkar, and the Upper Sind Frontier.

## Climate of Upper Sind
The climate of Upper Sind is characterized by extreme heat and dryness. Combined with the aridity of its sandy soil, the region resembles the sultry deserts of Africa. While the mean maximum temperature in the shade at Hyderabad in Lower Sind is 98 deg. F. during the six hottest months, temperatures in Upper Sind are higher, with the thermometer having registered 130 deg. in the shade. In this region, the waters of the Indus River can reach "blood heat." Unlike other coastal areas of the presidency, the monsoon rains exert little influence in Sind, making travel in the region less difficult between June and October than in other parts of the province.

## Native States of the Region
The Bombay Presidency contains a total of 353 separate native state units. These states are administered either by collectors of the districts in which smaller states are located or by political agents. All such native states became relatively dependent on Great Britain through treaties or conquest following the annexation of neighboring provinces.

In the Sind region, the primary native state is Khairpur (also spelled Khyrpoor). 

### Khairpur State
Khairpur is a native state in the Sind province of Bombay with an area of 6,050 sq. m. As of 1901, its population was 199,313, reflecting a 55% increase over the previous decade. The state's estimated revenue is £90,000.

The geography of Khairpur consists largely of a great alluvial plain that is very rich and fertile near the irrigation canals and the Indus. Other areas consist of continuous sand-hill ridges covered in stunted brushwood, rendering cultivation impossible. A limestone hill ridge passes through the northern part of the state, continuing a ridge known as the Ghar that runs south from Rohri. The state is watered by the Eastern Nara (an old bed of the Indus) and five other canals drawn from the Indus. Natron pits are found within the desert tracts.

Khairpur town is located 15 m. east of the Indus on a canal and 20 m. south of Sukkur. It is served by a railway station on the Kotri-Rohri branch of the North-Western railway. The town had a population of 14,014 in 1901 and maintains an export trade in oilseeds, grain, and indigo. Local manufactures include arms, goldsmiths' work, carpets, and cloth.

## Regional Context and Frontiers
The Upper Sind Frontier borders Baluchistan, a country named for its dominant race. Baluchistan is divided into British Baluchistan and foreign territories. The latter includes tribal areas and native states, such as Las Bela and Kalat (which includes Kharan and Makran). 

Historically, the British conquest of Sind occurred in 1843 under General Sir Charles Napier. Following this, diplomatic relations were established with the Baluch states. On May 14, 1854, General John Jacob, who served as the commandant and political superintendent on the Sind frontier, concluded a treaty with Nasir Khan, the chief of Kalat. Under this treaty, the chief of Kalat and his successors agreed to act in subordinate cooperation with the British government, oppose its enemies, and refrain from negotiating with other states without British consent. The treaty also allowed the British to station troops within Kalat territory if deemed necessary.
