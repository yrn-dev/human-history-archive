# Halbe and Ganze Instrumente

The classification of brass wind instruments as "half" and "whole" instruments (*Halbe* and *Ganze Instrumente*) is a distinction first recognized in Austria and Germany. This system of categorization is based on the relationship between the instrument's bore diameter, its length, and its ability to produce fundamental sounds.

## Theoretical Principles of Tube Length
Historically, it was a well-understood principle for brass wind instruments featuring a cup-shaped mouthpiece and conical bore that the tube must theoretically be twice as long as an organ pipe that produces the same note. For example, to sound the 8 ft. C of an 8 ft. organ pipe, a French horn requires a tube 16 ft. long. In this configuration, C serves as the second harmonic of the series for the 16 ft. tube, while the first or fundamental sound remains unobtainable.

## Development of "Whole" Instruments
A new era in brass construction began with the introduction of pistons and experiments with the bugle. The bugle is characterized by a conical bore with a very wide diameter in proportion to its length. Instrument-makers discovered that when baritone and bass instruments were constructed using this same principle—specifically with a sufficiently wide conical bore—they could produce fundamental sounds that were clear, full, and of immense power.

Because these instruments can produce the fundamental note, their tubes are made only half the length of the older instruments. Consequently, the tube length corresponds directly to the length of an organ pipe of the same pitch. For instance, a euphonium sounding 8 ft. C no longer requires a 16 ft. tube, but only one that is 8 ft. long. These are classified as "whole" instruments.

## "Half" Instruments and the Saxhorns
Instruments with a narrow bore, such as the saxhorns, are denominated "half instruments" because they require the longer tube length to produce their notes, lacking the ability to sound the fundamental. The valve-ophicleide in F of German make is another example of a "half instrument," measuring approximately 12 ft.

## Related Instruments and Evolution
The bombardon (or bass tuba) and the euphonium are outcomes of applying valves to the bugle family, a process that also produced the saxhorns. The bombardon serves as the bass to the euphonium. In military bands, the bombardon is used in two forms: an upright model for stationary music and a circular model called the helicon, which is worn around the body with the bell resting on the left shoulder, similar to the Roman *cornu*.

The bombardon was the first bass wind instrument to be fitted with valves. It was initially known as the *bass horn*, *clavicor*, or *corno basso*. The original *corno basso* featured a narrow bore and horizontal pistons. In the Prussian Jager military bands, the keyed bass horn (*corno basso*) was introduced as the bass in 1829, followed by the bombardon (or valve-ophicleide) in 1831. By 1835, these were superseded in the Guards by the bass tuba invented by J.G. Moritz and Wieprecht.
