# Burma, Africa, and the West Indian Colonies

## Burma
Burma is a province of British India that encompasses British Burma and the former independent kingdom of Burma. The British Indian government acquired these territories during two wars in 1826 and 1852. The province is divided into Lower Burma and Upper Burma, with the latter being annexed on January 1, 1886.

Geographically, Burma is situated east of the Bay of Bengal. Its boundaries extend from the Pakchan river in the south (9° 55' north latitude) to the Kachin (Chingpaw) and Naga hills in the north (between the 27th and 28th degrees of north latitude). From west to east, it spans from the Bay of Bengal to the Mekong river, which serves as the boundary for the dependent Shan States. The province's extreme north-to-south length is nearly 1,200 miles, and its broadest point, located at approximately 21° north latitude, measures 575 miles. The coastline from the estuary of the Pakchan at Maliwun to Taknaf at the mouth of the Naaf is about 1,200 miles.

The total estimated area of the province is 238,738 square miles. This is comprised of Burma proper (168,573 square miles), the Shan States in the eastern portion (59,915 square miles), and the Chin hills (10,250 square miles). Burma is bordered by the Bay of Bengal and Chittagong to the west; the Siamese Malay States and the Bay of Bengal to the south; the Siamese Lao (Shan) States and Siam to the east; and the Chinese Shan States, portions of Yunnan, the French province of Indo-China, the Mishmi hills, and the dependent state of Manipur to the north and east.

## Africa: East, West, and Central
The African territories of the British Empire are characterized as being chiefly black. Population estimates for these regions include 18,863,000 in West Africa, 7,425,000 in East Africa, and 2,000,000 in British Central Africa. In the early 19th century, British presence in West Africa consisted of a few trading stations on the coast. Later, chartered companies in the 19th century extended the British flag into the interior from the west, east, and south.

In tropical Africa, the development of the region has relied on opening communications via the Nile, the Congo, and various lakes. During the war, two railways were constructed in the Congo territory, extending communications from the center to the south and from east to west. These railways created a route allowing travel from Dar-es-Salaam on the Indian Ocean via the German Central Railway to Ujiji on Lake Tanganyika, then by steamer to Albertville, by train to Kabalo, by steamer to Kongolo, by train to Kindu, and finally by rail and steamer down the Congo to the Atlantic Ocean.

Germany also held possessions in East and West Africa. Unlike the British model, these colonies had no significant white population before the war, as Germany viewed tropical Africa as unsuitable for white settlement and preferred to concentrate its population in the fatherland to avoid labor shortages in German agriculture. Strategically, German interests in West Africa focused on the coast from the mouth of the Orange River to the mouth of the Cross River. It was argued that if the Suez Canal were closed, all British traffic to India, Australia, and South Africa would have to pass the coast of German Central Africa via the Cape of Good Hope, forcing Britain to station a fleet in South Africa and weakening its forces in European waters.

## The West Indian Colonies
The West Indian colonies were largely founded by trading companies. By 1815, British holdings included Jamaica and several minor possessions based on slave labour. The populations of these colonies are diverse, consisting largely of imported African negroes—who make up four-fifths of the population in Jamaica—as well as Chinese and imported East Indian coolies. The aboriginal populations include Caribs and American Indians from the mainland. These groups have intermixed with Portuguese and Spanish blood, resulting in many mixed types.

The total European population in this group of colonies is over 80,000, with an additional 15,000 in Bermuda. The total native population of the West Indies is recorded as 1,912,655.

## Imperial Context and Climate
Burma, the West Indian colonies, and East, West, and Central Africa are noted as notable exceptions to the general rule that the British Empire's land surface is suitable for white settlement, as these regions are tropical. The British Empire occupies nearly one-quarter of the earth's land surface (approximately 12,000,000 square miles). 

Trade has served as the primary bond of union and cause of expansion for the empire. The protection of this colonial commerce by the imperial navy remains one of the strongest material links between the crown and its outlying possessions.
