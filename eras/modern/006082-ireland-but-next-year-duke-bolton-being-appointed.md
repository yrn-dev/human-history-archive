# Ireland

The administration of Ireland has historically been managed through the appointment of various officials, including lieutenants and lord-lieutenants, often drawn from the British nobility and political class.

## Administrative Appointments and Governance

The role of lord-lieutenant has been held by various figures over several centuries. In the 14th century, William, the 3rd earl, was appointed lieutenant of Ireland in 1331, though he was murdered in his 21st year. Later in the century, Roger de Mortimer, the 4th earl of March and Ulster, was appointed lord-lieutenant of Ireland shortly after succeeding to his family titles as a child of seven. In 1394, Mortimer accompanied Richard II to Ireland; however, despite a royal commission as lieutenant over districts he nominally held by hereditary right, he made little progress against native Irish chieftains. During his time there, Mortimer illegally adopted native Irish costume before being killed in a fight with an Irish clan in August 1398.

In the 18th century, the position continued to be a significant political appointment. Lord Wharton served as lord-lieutenant of Ireland in 1710. During this tenure, he appointed Addison as his secretary, who in turn brought Eustace Budgell into the office as one of his clerks. Budgell subsequently rose through the administrative ranks to become the deputy-clerk of the council, a member of the Irish parliament, and the chief secretary to the lords justices of Ireland. By 1717, Budgell had been procured the position of comptroller-general and accountant of the revenue in Ireland.

## The Tenure of the Dukes of Bolton and Dorset

The 2nd duke of Bolton (1661-1722), Charles Powlett, served as lord-lieutenant of Ireland for two short periods. His appointment as lord-lieutenant occurred the year after 1717, an event that coincided with a shift in the Irish administration. During this transition, Eustace Budgell wrote a lampoon against his secretary, E. Webster, which resulted in Budgell's removal from his post as accountant-general and his subsequent return to England.

Later in the 18th century, Lionel Cranfield Sackville, the 1st duke of Dorset (1688-1765), held the office of lord-lieutenant of Ireland on two separate occasions. His first term lasted from 1730 to 1737. He was appointed to the position for a second time in 1750, though this second viceroyalty was described as stormy and ended with his dismissal from office in 1755.
