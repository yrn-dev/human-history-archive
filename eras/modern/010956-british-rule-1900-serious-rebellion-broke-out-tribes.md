# British Rule and the Rebellion of 1900

## Administration of Ashanti
Following the deportation of Prempeh, the British government did not appoint a successor to the Ashanti throne. Instead, Captain Donald W. Stewart, a British resident, was installed at Kumasi. While other states within the confederacy were permitted to retain their tribal systems and kings, the affairs of Kumasi were administered by chiefs under British guidance. To secure the area, the British built a fort in Kumasi, which was garrisoned by the Gold Coast constabulary. During this period, missionaries returned to the region, including Mr. and Mrs. Ramseyer, who had previously been imprisoned for four and a half years by King Kofi Karikari.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Despite an outward appearance of submission, the chiefs of Kumasi remained unreconciled to British rule, leading to a serious rebellion in 1900. The tribes involved in the uprising were the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi, although other tribes within the Ashanti confederation remained loyal to the British. The rebel forces were reported to number 40,000.

The tension escalated on March 28, during a public palaver in Kumasi. Sir F. Hodgson, the governor of the Gold Coast, announced that the Ashanti chiefs were required to pay the British government 4,000 oz. of gold annually. Additionally, he reproached the chiefs for hiding the golden stool, which the Kumasi had kept concealed since 1896.

## Military Conflict and Escalation
Hostilities began three days after the governor's announcement when Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa who had been sent specifically to discover the location of the golden stool. This followed a failed secret attempt to seize the stool in January. Driven by a desire to erase the dishonour of Prempeh's deportation, which had occurred without a fight, the Kumasi subsequently threatened the fort at Kumasi.

Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, along with the Basel missionaries and Mr. Ramseyer, took refuge inside the fort and requested urgent reinforcements. The British response occurred in stages:
* **April 18:** 100 Gold Coast constabulary arrived.
* **April 29:** The Kumasi launched a forceful attack but were repulsed. On this same day, 250 Lagos constabulary reached Kumasi, having fought their way up with limited ammunition.
* **May 15:** Major A. Morris arrived from the British territory north of Ashanti with 250 men.

By mid-May, the garrison total reached 700, which included 29 Europeans and four women.

## Other British Colonial Conflicts
British rule in other regions also faced tribal resistance and rebellion. In the Khasi Hills, which were conquered by the British in 1833, the inhabitants live in primitive communities under elective chiefs known as *Siems*. While the Khasis remained in political subordination, the Syntengs of the Jaintia Hills—a cognate tribe—rebelled in 1860 and 1862 against the introduction of a moderate income tax. This revolt was ended by the Jaintia and Khasi Expedition of 1862-63.

In Kalat, the British government provided a political agent to assist the Khan in maintaining control over turbulent tribes. In 1857, during the height of the Indian rebellion, Major Henry Green was deputed for this role. Later, during the absence of political agent Major Malcolm Green, Khodadad Khan was attacked and wounded by his cousin, Sher dil Khan, who was then proclaimed Khan. Although Khodadad was eventually re-elected after Sher dil Khan was murdered in the Gandava Pass in 1864, the country fell into anarchy due to Khodadad's inability to govern without British material assistance, which the government was not disposed to give. Consequently, diplomatic relations with the Kalat state ended after 1874.
