# Lhasa

Lhasa is a city located on the great elevated table-land of Tibet. It has served as a destination for various scientific geographical explorations directed toward the Tibetan plateau.

## Explorations and Visitors
During the modern period of scientific exploration in Asia, several expeditions have reached or approached Lhasa. In 1874-1875, the native surveyor Pundit Nain Singh traveled from the eastern frontiers of Kashmir to the Tengri Nor lake and then to Lhasa, where he remained for several months. 

Between 1879 and 1882, the explorer Krishna undertook a journey that extended from Lhasa northwards through Tsaidam to Sachu (also known as Saitu) in Mongolia. Krishna later traveled through eastern Tibet to the town of Darchendo (or Tachienlu), which is situated on the borders of China on the high road between Lhasa and Peking. After failing to reach India via Upper Assam, Krishna returned to the vicinity of Lhasa before crossing the Himalayas through a more westerly route.

Additionally, the Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky penetrated southwards during his travels between 1871 and 1873, reaching a point within a month's march of Lhasa.

## Regional Geography
Lhasa is situated within the Tibetan plateau, a region characterized by high elevations. To the north of the plateau lies a central depression that extends from the desert of Gobi through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. This depression, which may be below sea-level in central portions such as Lop Nor, is separated from the Tibetan highlands by a system of elevated valleys and mountain ranges.

The Kuen-lun mountains serve as a significant geographical marker in this region. While the Kuen-lun chain extends from the frontiers of Kashmir, north of Leh, east to the Chinese province of Kansu, it is identified as the northern limit of the Chang Tang, the elevated highland steppes of Tibet. Branching north-east from this chain is a minor system that culminates in the Altyn Tagh and extends to the Nan Shan mountains north of the Koko Nor basin. Between Tibet and the sands of the Gobi, the Tsaidam region forms an intermediate steppe with elevated valleys reaching 8,000 to 9,000 feet above sea-level.

## Surrounding Mountain Systems
The geography surrounding the Tibetan plateau is defined by a series of parallel ranges that rise in steps or terraces from the deserts of Takla-makan and Gobi. These ranges increase in altitude from north to south.

The most northerly ranges include the Lower Astin-tagh (also referred to as Altyn Tagh), the Upper Astin-tagh, and the Akato-tagh. The Altyn Tagh is etymologically derived from Astin-tagh or Astun-tagh, meaning the "Lower or Nearer Mountains." Further south, the system includes the Chimen-tagh, Ara-tagh, and Kalta-alaghan.

The most southerly and imposing of these is the Arka-tagh (known to Russian geographers as the Przhevalsky Range), which acts as the actual border-range of the Tibetan plateau. The Arka-tagh is described as the backbone of the Kuen-lun system, with general altitudes ranging from 15,750 to 16,250 feet. Its peaks are characterized as broad, rounded, flattened domes rather than jagged pinnacles. The difference in altitude between the most northerly range (Lower Astin-tagh) and the most southerly (Arka-tagh) is nearly 7,500 feet.
