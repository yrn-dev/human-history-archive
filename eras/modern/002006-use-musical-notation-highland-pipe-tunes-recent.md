# Musical Notation and the Highland Bag-pipe

## Transition to Musical Notation
The use of musical notation for Highland pipe tunes is a recent innovation. Historically, pipers did not utilize written musical characters but instead relied on verbal equivalents for the notes to teach and preserve their music. This method of verbal instruction was used to train apprentices; for example, the piobaireachd *Coghiegh nha Shie* ("War of peace") was taught to apprentices through a series of phonetic sounds. 

Capt. Niel MacLeod recorded these verbal instructions from the piper John McCrummen of Skye. The opening of the tune was taught as "Hodroho, hodroho, haninin, hiechin, Hodroha, hodroho, hodroho, hachin, Hiodroho, hodroho, haninin, hiechin," while the conclusion was expressed as "Hiundratatateriri, hiendatatateriri, hiundratatateriri, hiundratatateriri." While such verbal records appear to be an unintelligible jumble when written down, they possess specific rhythmical values when sounded by pipers. Alexander Campbell noted that when a melody was translated from the "syllabic jargon of illiterate pipers" into musical characters, the resulting notation coincided exactly with the music.

## Technical Characteristics and Ornamentation
The Highland bag-pipe possesses a technical peculiarity that necessitates the extensive use of ornamental notes. Because it is difficult to repeat the same note on the instrument, pipers interpolate "warblers" or "cuts," which are grace notes fingered with great rapidity. These warblers can consist of single notes or groups of three to seven notes played in leaps rather than consecutively. Their primary function is to relieve the constant discord created by the drone bass. Highly skilled pipers are capable of introducing up to eleven warblers between two beats in a single bar.

## Tuning and Scales
The natural scale of the bag-pipe's chaunter does not form any known diatonic scale. The notes are not strictly in tune with one another, and both the F and C are too sharp. According to the treatise by Donald MacDonald, pipe music does not use the flats and sharps marked on a clef. While the pipe can imitate several real keys, the music cannot be transposed into any key other than the one in which it was first marked or played.

The instrument has nine invariable notes. Mr. Glen observed that if the chaunter were made perfect in any one scale, it would not harmonize well with the drones. However, the instrument is capable of playing tunes that fall within the keys of A major, D major, B minor, G major, E minor, and A minor, although it does not possess all the intervals necessary to form scales in all those keys. Alex. J. Ellis suggested that the natural scale of the chaunter most closely corresponds to the Arab scale of Zalzal, a lutist who died circa A.D. 800.

The drones are typically tuned to A. The largest drone is tuned two octaves below the A of the chaunter, while the two smaller drones are tuned one octave below.

## Historical Instrument Construction
An example of a Highland bag-pipe from the 15th century (dated MCCCCIX) was exhibited at the Royal Military Exhibition in London in 1890. This instrument, held by Messrs. J. & R. Glen of Edinburgh, featured a chaunter with a thumb-hole at the back and seven finger-holes in front. It included two drones inserted into a single stock in the form of a wide-spread fork, both of which were tuned to A in unison with the lowest note of the chaunter.
