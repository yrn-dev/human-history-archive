# Italians

## Art and Illumination
Italian illumination, particularly in the style influenced by Byzantine elements, developed rapidly and reached a peak of splendor during the 15th century. While existing examples from earlier centuries are few, frescoes indicate that Italian artists were capable of great work as early as the 12th century. This is evidenced by two masterly paintings—the Virgin enthroned and the death of the Virgin—found in the *Winchester Psalter* from the middle of that century.

By the beginning of the 14th century, many Italian specimens remained ordinary and lacked the skill found in the Northern schools of France and England. However, Italian book ornamentation adopted the general system of decoration from these schools, including the use of the border, the initial, the miniature, and the miniature-initial. In the 14th century, illumination expanded with great energy, especially in Florence. Notable examples of the Florentine type include the legends of the saints (circa 1370) and a work containing an address to Robert of Anjou, king of Sicily (1334-1342). As the century progressed, the art became more prolific, extending to great folio choral books and large volumes of other law books, such as the Decretals. Due to political causes and geographical contiguity, Italy and France exerted mutual influence on this art.

## Naval Development and Administration
Benedetto Brin (1833-1898), born in Turin, is regarded as the practical creator of the Italian navy. Originally a naval engineer, Brin was appointed under-secretary of state in 1873 by Admiral Saint-Bon. Together, they collaborated on ship designs, with Saint-Bon conceiving the types and Brin directing the construction and planning. 

Brin served as minister of marine across several cabinets between 1876 and 1898 (under Depretis, Crispi, and Rudini). During his tenure, he established the first organic scheme for the development of the Italian fleet, creating the huge warships "Dandolo" and "Italia," and later developing the faster "Garibaldi" and "Varese" classes. Brin transformed the Italian naval industry, which was almost non-existent in 1873, by creating metallurgical works for the production of steel plates, guns, and armor, as well as engine works and large private shipyards. He also served as minister for foreign affairs in the Giolitti cabinet in 1892.

## Literature and Public Life
Antonio Giulio Barrili (1836- ) was an Italian novelist born in Savona. After being educated for the legal profession, he turned to journalism in Genoa. Barrili was active in military campaigns, serving as a volunteer in 1859 and serving with Garibaldi in 1866 and 1867. He published numerous popular works of fiction starting in 1865 with *Capitan Dodero*, followed by *Santa Cecilia* (1866), *Come un Sogno* (1875), and *L'Olmo e l' Edera* (1877). His work *Raggio di Dio* was published in 1899. Additionally, he wrote two plays and critical volumes, including *Il rinnovamento letterario italiano* (1890). Barrili was elected to the Italian chamber of deputies in 1876 and later became a professor of Italian literature at Genoa in 1889.

## Regional and Linguistic Shifts in Dalmatia
In Dalmatia, the law of 1867 led to regulations between 1872 and 1876 that determined the recognition of Slavonic idioms. Previously, Italian—the language of a small minority in seaport towns—had been used for all business. These laws raised Croatian to equality with Italian, and Croatian was introduced into schools, becoming the primary language of education. Italian maintained its presence only in a few towns, such as the ancient capital, Zara. By 1882, a Slav majority existed in the diet, and Italian ceased to be used in its proceedings. These concessions were made by the Liberal ministry to secure the parliamentary support of Dalmatian representatives and to cultivate the loyalty of Slav races as a counterweight to the Russian party in the Balkan Peninsula.

## Military History and the World War
During the early stages of the conflict in 1914, the Italian Government declared neutrality. This was strictly respected by the British Admiralty, which ordered ships to remain at least six miles from the Italian coast.

In later campaigns, the Italian Army faced significant challenges. The Italians had seized the town of Caporetto on the Upper Isonzo upon the declaration of war. However, on October 24, German forces captured Caporetto, which served as a strategic gateway to the mountains where the Italian main line lay. The Germans pushed northward, occupying Idersko and Caporetto, and taking Luico after a frontal attack on the ridge of Zagradan. While a brigade of bersaglieri successfully retook the big-gun position at Mount Globocak, and the line at Zaga resisted, the Italians were forced to withdraw from Kal on the Bainsizza plateau.

The subsequent retreat was marked by the rain-swollen Tagliamento river. While the retreat was generally gloomy, the Italian rear-guard was noted for gallant and skilful conduct, specifically when they killed or captured a German detachment that had forced its way across a flimsy bridge near Gemona.

By the time the Italian supreme command announced the evacuation of the Bainsizza plateau on the 26th, the Italian Government fell. The ministry was attacked by a combination of pacifist socialists, who cited infringements of constitutional rights, and the pro-war party, who believed the government lacked firmness. This created a psychological malaise within the Italian Army.
