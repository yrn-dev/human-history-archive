# Naples: Military and Political History

The history of Naples is characterized by frequent foreign interventions, shifting alliances, and its role as a strategic center of conflict involving the major powers of Europe, particularly France, Spain, and Austria.

## Early Conflicts and Dynastic Struggles
The region experienced early instability involving various claimants to the throne. Louis, a son of Philip, prince of Taranto, became the husband of Queen Joanna I and was crowned king in 1352, though he died in 1362 after attempting to conquer Sicily. Later, Louis II, duke of Anjou, took up the struggle for Naples following his father's death and was crowned king in 1389. Despite several years of contest, he was eventually forced to take refuge in France. He later gained a victory at Roccoserra in May 1411 but was again driven back to France. His son, Louis III, duke of Anjou, attempted to conquer Naples in 1420 and achieved considerable success before his death in Cosenza in 1434.

In an earlier period, the city was taken by surprise by Pandulf IV, prince of Capua. In response, Sergius, the head of the republic and master of the soldiers, retired to Aversa. With financial support from the Greeks and faithful citizens, Sergius employed Norman adventurers to attack the garrison of the prince of Capua. After defeating the garrison and reentering Naples, Sergius confirmed the Normans in the possession of Aversa, raising it into a count's fief for Rainulf.

## Spanish Influence and the "Great Captain"
The late 15th and early 16th centuries saw Naples become a focal point of rivalry between Spain and France. In 1495, the Catholic sovereigns decided to support the Aragonese house of Naples against Charles VIII of France. Gonzalo, known as the "Great Captain," was chosen to command the Spanish expedition. He led a force of over five thousand men to assist Ferdinand of Naples in recovering his kingdom, achieving success by 1498.

By 1501, Ferdinand of Spain and Louis XII of France had entered into a compact for the division and spoliation of the kingdom of Naples. Gonzalo returned to Italy to command the Spanish portion of this coalition, serving as both general and viceroy of Naples until 1507. During this period, Gonzalo utilized veterans of the Granadine war—light cavalry and foot soldiers armed with arquebuses, crossbows, and sword and buckler—to conduct guerrilla warfare in Calabria. 

Following the expulsion of the French by 1498, the Great Captain later aided in robbing King Frederick of Naples, the brother of his ally Ferdinand. After the French and Spaniards quarreled over the booty, the Spaniards held an entrenched camp at Barletta from late 1502 to early 1503. Gonzalo eventually took the offensive at Cerignola, using field works and wire entanglements to route a French front attack. Subsequent operations on the Garigliano led to the total expulsion of the French from Naples.

## 18th Century Warfare and Alliances
In the mid-18th century, Naples was again embroiled in European power struggles. In 1744, during a serious phase of the Italian war, the Spanish army under Count de Gages was driven back toward the Neapolitan frontier by Lobkowitz. At this juncture, the king of Naples was compelled to assist the Spaniards at all hazards. A combined army was formed at Velletri, which defeated Lobkowitz on August 11. Following this victory, the king of Naples returned home while de Gages pursued the Austrians.

By March 1745, a secret treaty allied Naples with France, Spain, and the Genoese republic. This alliance saw the concentration of 80,000 troops between the Scrivia and the Tanaro in July 1745. These allied forces defeated the Sardinians at Bassignano on September 27 and captured Casale, Valenza, and Alessandria. However, the concentration was later destroyed by Austrian interventions and the cutting of communications on the lower Po.

In subsequent operations, Traun successfully held his own against the combined forces of the Neapolitans and Spaniards. Naples was eventually forced to withdraw its troops for home defense after the intervention of a British squadron.

## Cultural Figures
Beyond its military history, Naples was the birthplace of the poet Giambattista (or Marino) Marini, born there on October 18, 1569. Marini, the chief of the "Secentisti" school, was known for his long poem *Adone* and his collection *La Lira*. After spending years in Paris under the recognition of Marie de' Medici, he returned to Naples, where he died on March 25, 1625.
