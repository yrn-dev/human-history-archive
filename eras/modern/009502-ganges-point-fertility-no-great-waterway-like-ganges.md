# The Ganges River and its Valley

The Ganges (also referred to as the B’hagiratha) is one of the primary waterways of India, originating in Thibet from the ridges of the hills. It issues from under a low arch at the foot of a grand snow bed in a location described as the "Cow’s Mouth." From its source, the river follows a winding course of 2,000 miles before meeting the Burrampooter—which also rises in Thibet—near the Gulf of Bengal.

## Geography and the Great Plain
The Ganges is a central feature of the great plain of northern India, a region extending between the Tibetan table-land and the Indian Ocean. This plain, which includes the valley of Assam, covers approximately 500,000 square miles and is characterized by deposits so finely comminuted that it is possible to travel from the Bay of Bengal up the Ganges, through the Punjab, and down the Indus to the sea—a distance exceeding 2,000 miles—without encountering a single pebble.

The river's course defines several administrative and geographic regions. In the lieutenant-governorship of Bengal, the sub-province of Bengal proper stretches to the banks of the Ganges, with its northern portion consisting of the Ganges valley and its southern portion formed by the Ganges delta. To the northwest lies Behar, which comprises the higher valley of the Ganges from the point where it issues from the United Provinces. In Behar, the river enters near Buxar and flows eastward through towns such as Patna, Monghyr, and Colgong before leaving the province at Rajmahal.

## Fertility and Agricultural Production
The valley of the Ganges is recognized as one of the most fertile and densely populated tracts of country in the world. This fertility is so pronounced that other regions, such as the area served by Bombay, cannot vie with the Ganges valley in this regard. The region teems with a vast array of natural products, including:

*   **Crops and Spices:** Wheat, innumerable grains and pulses, rice, barley, sugar-cane, pepper, ginger, betel-nut, turmeric, and various costly drugs and spices.
*   **Industrial Plants:** Indigo, cotton, the silk mulberry, and inexhaustible crops of jute and other fibres.
*   **Specialized Produce:** Quinine, oil-seeds, and the opium-poppy. In Behar, the cultivation of opium is a government monopoly managed by the Behar Opium Agency in Patna.
*   **Forestry:** Timber ranging from the coroneted palm and feathery bamboo to the "iron-hearted" *sal* tree.

In specific districts, such as Benares, the soil is typically a sandy or clayey loam that remains very fertile, except in the Usar tracts where saline efflorescence occurs. Similarly, the district of Darbhanga consists of an alluvial plain that is especially fertile in the elevated areas southwest of the Buri Gandak.

## Waterways, Navigation, and Irrigation
The Ganges and its tributaries—including the Ghagra, Gandak, Kusi, Mahananda, and Sone—serve as vital arteries for traffic. In Behar, the Ganges and its northern tributaries are navigable by large country boats throughout the year. In the district of Bulandshahr, the Ganges is navigable year-round, whereas the nearby Jumna is only navigable during the rains.

In the delta region, rivers and canals carry a large portion of Bengal's traffic. The government maintains the Eastern and Calcutta canals through the Sundarbans and works to keep the Nadiya rivers open to maintain communication between the Hugli and the main stream of the Ganges. 

Irrigation is managed through various systems, such as the Ganges canal and the Lower Ganges canal (with headworks at Narora) in Bulandshahr, and canals on the Sone river in southern Behar. However, in Bengal, expenditure on irrigation is generally not considered remunerative—except as a protection against famine—due to the general dampness of the climate and the abundance of existing rivers.

## Regional Impact and Commerce
The presence of the Ganges contributes to the commercial prosperity of the eastern regions of India. While Bombay serves as a chief seaport and railway centre with a monopoly on the cotton industry, it lacks a great waterway comparable to the Ganges or the Brahmaputra. Consequently, the Ganges valley supports some of the richest and most civilized districts of India. In areas like Bulandshahr, the river supports a highly cultivated and thickly populated landscape featuring cotton pressing mills and indigo factories.
