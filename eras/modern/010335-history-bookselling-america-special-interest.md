# The History of Bookselling in America

The development of the bookselling trade in America is characterized by a gradual evolution from colonial imports and early printing centers to a sophisticated publishing industry. While the Spanish settlements initially drew significant talent and enterprise away from their home country—establishing presses in Mexico and other cities that produced religious, linguistic, and historical publications—bookselling in the United States grew at a later pace.

## Early Printing and Colonial Trade
Printing was introduced to the American colonies in the late 17th century, appearing in Boston in 1676, Philadelphia in 1685, and New York in 1693. During this early period, the trade was made illustrious by the efforts of Franklin. However, at the start of the 19th century, few individuals were engaged in the profession. The market was primarily served by imports from Europe, which consisted chiefly of books intended for libraries and scholars.

The region also saw occasional visits from foreign booksellers. For example, John Dunton, an English bookseller and author born in 1659, visited New England in 1686. He remained there for eight months, selling books and observing the inhabitants of the new country. His letters regarding this visit were later published in America in 1867.

## Expansion and the Role of British Literature
Following the second war, there was a rapid multiplication of printing presses. This growth was fueled by the spread of education and the rise of newspapers, which created a heightened demand for books. American publishers began to exploit the vast field of English literature, as they were able to reprint works without paying the authors or proprietors. 

Between 1820 and 1852, half of the authors listed in the "American" catalogue of printed books were British. Notable authors reprinted during this era included Wordsworth, Moore, Byron, Scott, and Southey. By producing these works in the cheapest possible forms, publishers brought the writings of the best authors to all social classes.

## Impact of the Civil War and Market Shifts
The American Civil War brought about significant changes in the trade due to the high cost of labor and the implementation of restrictive duties designed to protect native industry. Despite the lack of international copyright laws, American publishers and booksellers began making liberal offers for early sheets of new publications, aided by frequent intercourse with England. 

Throughout these transitions, Philadelphia, New York, and Boston maintained their supremacy as the primary centers of bookselling. Over time, the distinct business of publishing grew, and its operational conditions eventually became assimilated to those found in Europe.

## Individual Contributions and Regional Growth
The trade also extended to the Western United States. Hubert Howe Bancroft, born in 1832, operated as a bookseller in San Francisco from 1852 to 1868. During his time in the trade, he accumulated a massive library of historical material. He eventually left bookselling to publish extensive works, including the 21-volume *History of the Pacific States of North America* (1882-1890) and the 5-volume *Native Races of the Pacific States* (1874-1876). His vast collection was acquired by the University of California in 1905.

## Professional Distinctions in the Trade
As the industry matured, a fundamental distinction emerged between the publisher and the bookseller. The publisher acts as the intermediary between the author and the bookseller, managing the production of the book from the manuscript. The bookseller, meanwhile, serves as the intermediary between the publisher and the public.

Booksellers are further categorized into several roles:
*   **Retail Booksellers:** Those who sell books directly to the public.
*   **Wholesale Booksellers:** Distributing agencies that supply retail and country booksellers. These agencies are critical because retail shops cannot stock every published title.
*   **Second-hand Dealers:** A specialized class of business requiring bibliographic knowledge, dealing in individual books or "remainders" (books that have ceased to sell with the publisher).
*   **Periodical Trade:** A sector that grew during the 19th century, aided by the introduction of attractive illustrations, the abolition of the duty on paper and the newspaper stamp, and the availability of installment purchasing.
