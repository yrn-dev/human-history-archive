# Literature and Imperialism in British History

The subject of British imperialism has inspired a growing body of literature in recent years, encompassing various perspectives on colonial policy, geography, and the political evolution of the empire. This literature reflects a period of significant ideological conflict between different visions of British governance and national identity.

## Literature on British Imperialism
A diverse range of works has been produced to address different topics within the study of the empire. Key texts include Sir C.P. Lucas's *Historical Geography of the British Colonies* (1888, et seq.), H.E. Egerton's *Short History of British Colonial Policy* (1897), and H.J. Mackinder's *Britain and the British Seas* (1902). The expansion and growth of British policy were explored by Sir J.R. Seeley in *Expansion of England* (1883) and *Growth of British Policy* (1895).

Sir Charles Dilke contributed several works, including *Greater Britain* (1869), *Problems of Greater Britain* (1890), and *The British Empire* (1899). Other authors focused on the structure and defense of the empire, such as G.R. Parkin (*Imperial Federation*, 1892), Sir John Colomb (*Imperial Federation, Naval and Military*, 1886), and Sir G.S. Clarke (*Imperial Defence*, 1897). Further perspectives are found in *The Empire and the Century* (1905) by Sidney Goldmann and others, J.L. Garvin's *Imperial Reciprocity* (1903), and J.W. Welsford's *The Strength of a Nation* (1907). Additionally, the *Compatriots Club Essays* (1906) and Sir H. Jenkyns' *British Rule and Jurisdiction beyond the Seas* (1902) provide further analysis. Bernard Holland wrote *Imperium et libertas* (1901), while J.A. Hobson provided an anti-imperialist view in his 1902 work, *Imperialism*.

Researchers may also consult the Reports of various colonial conferences, specifically the Imperial Conference of 1907, and J. Holt Schooling's *British Trade Book* for trade statistics.

## Political Conflict and "New Imperialism"
The rise of "infectious imperialism" in Europe coincided with a shift in British political history. A Unionist Government, characterized by a predominantly Tory element, adopted an "imperialist" spirit more pronounced than any previous government. This era was marked by a conflict between a "British" nationalism, which sought to override the rest of the empire, and an English liberalism that favored developing the empire into a confederation of willing and free allies.

This ideological divide was evident in the approach to Ireland. While "British" imperialists desired a subjugated Ireland, English Liberals advocated for a free, participating Ireland. Although Gladstone's second Home Rule Bill passed the Commons in 1893, it was rejected by the Lords. An imperialist government, sustained by the "Unionist" party, took office in 1895 and remained in power for ten years. This government was responsible for the conquest of South Africa but was defeated in 1905 during an attempt to establish a tariff wall based on the Teutonic model. The subsequent Liberal Government created the self-governing Dominion of South Africa to turn the conquered South African Dutch into contented fellow-subjects.

## Imperial Defence and the Navy
The continued existence of the empire relied heavily on the question of defence, specifically the maintenance of a strong navy since the oceans served as the empire's main thoroughfares. The "two-power standard" became a fundamental axiom of defence, requiring the British navy to maintain a 10% margin of strength over the joint strength of the two next most powerful navies.

Until the decade of 1890-1900, the colossal expense of this armament was borne solely by United Kingdom taxpayers. Over time, self-governing colonies began contributing to a "truly imperial navy." In 1895, the Australasian colonies provided an annual subsidy of £126,000, which increased to £240,000 by 1902. The Cape Colony offered £30,000 a year in 1897, later increased to £50,000. Other contributions included £35,000 annually from Natal and £3,000 from Newfoundland. Despite these contributions and the 1908-1909 establishment of an Australian torpedo-boat flotilla, the primary cost of the imperial navy remained with taxpayers in the British islands. The urgency of this burden was highlighted in 1909 following revelations regarding the increase of the Austrian and German fleets, leading to offers of "Dreadnoughts" from New Zealand and other colonies.
