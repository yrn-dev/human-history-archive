# Independence Party and the Austro-Hungarian Bank

## The Austro-Hungarian Bank
The Austro-Hungarian Bank served as the common financial institution for the two states of the monarchy, holding the legal exclusive right to issue bank-notes. Founded in 1816, the institution was originally titled the Austrian National Bank until it was renamed in 1878. Under a bank statute enacted in 1899, it operated as a joint-stock company with a stock of £8,780,000. 

The bank's financial structure required that two-fifths of its notes of issue be covered by legal specie in reserve, consisting of current silver and gold, with the remainder of the paper circulation handled according to bank usage. The state received a portion of the bank's clear profits under specific conditions. Management and state supervision were conducted on a footing of equality, ensuring that both states maintained the same level of influence.

As of the end of 1900, the bank's accounts showed a capital of £8,750,000, a reserve fund of £428,250, cash of £50,754,000, and a note circulation of £62,251,000. By 1907, these figures had shifted to a reserve fund of £548,041, cash of £60,036,625, and a note circulation of £84,501,000.

## Political Conflict and the Independence Party
The charter of the Austro-Hungarian Bank, which had expired in 1897, was renewed until the end of 1910. The question of this renewal became a central issue during the Hungarian ministerial crisis of 1909. During this period, the more extreme members of the Independence party demanded the establishment of separate banks for Hungary and Austria, suggesting that the two separate entities have, at most, common superintendence.

This demand was part of a broader conflict between two irreconcilable principles: Habsburg imperialism, which sought to maintain the military and economic unity of the Dual Monarchy, and Magyar nationalism, which was determined to maintain its ascendancy within an independent Hungary.

## Legislative Proposals and Negotiations
The tension regarding the bank's charter was a significant point of contention that remained unsettled following the agreement of 1907. In March 1908, Mr. Hallo presented a formal proposal to the Hungarian parliament. He proposed that the charter of the Austro-Hungarian Bank should not be renewed upon its expiration at the end of 1910. Furthermore, he suggested that if negotiations to create a convention between the banks of Hungary and Austria failed, a separate Hungarian Bank should be established.

This issue became acute again in the spring of 1909. While opinion was sharply divided within the Coalition cabinet, the views of the Independence party played a conspicuous role in the crisis.

## Economic Context of the Dual Monarchy
The push for separation was tempered by economic realities. A severe economic crisis eventually sobered Hungarian opinion, highlighting that separation from Austria could lead to a period of distress or commercial ruin for Hungary. Simultaneously, Austria recognized that separation would deprive Austrian manufacturers of their primary market and increase the cost of living in Cisleithania.

These factors contributed to the "customs and commercial treaty" of October 1907. While this treaty addressed various issues—such as the establishment of separate but identical customs tariffs, railway communications between Vienna, Dalmatia, and Prussia, and the redemption of the old Austrian debt—the specific question of the renewal of the bank charter had not been part of that 1907 agreement.
