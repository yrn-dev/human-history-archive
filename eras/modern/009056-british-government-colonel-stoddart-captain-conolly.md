# British Government and Military Personnel

The British government and its military apparatus have historically managed a wide array of global operations, ranging from the administration of colonial territories and the securing of strategic mountain passes to the coordination of naval squadrons during international crises.

## Administration of the Khyber Pass
The British government adopted the Khyber as the primary road to Kabul, though this route had not been regarded as such by previous rulers of India due to its inherent difficulties. These challenges were eventually addressed by British engineers. During the first Afghan War, the pass was the site of several disasters for British troops and numerous skirmishes with the Afridis. Notable actions included the capture of the fortress of Ali Masjid by Colonel Wade in July 1839. In 1842, Colonel Moseley occupied the same fort during the blockade of Jalalabad but was forced to evacuate due to a lack of provisions; the fort was later reoccupied by General Pollock.

The second Afghan War was triggered in 1878 when a friendly mission led by Sir Neville Chamberlain to the amir Shere Ali was halted at Ali Masjid. The fortress was subsequently captured by Sir Samuel Browne. Following the treaty that ended the war in May 1879, the Khyber tribes were placed under British control. To protect the pass, the British government paid a subsidy to jezailchis from the Afridi tribe. Colonel R. Warburton controlled the Khyber for 18 years starting in 1879, maintaining its safety for most of that tenure. However, in 1897, a wave of fanaticism led the Afridis to attack the pass. Although the British government received warnings of this movement, they only withdrew British officers of the Khyber Rifles, leaving the pass to fall into Afridi hands for several months. This event led to the Tirah Expedition of 1897.

## Naval Operations and Command
In the early 20th century, the British Admiralty coordinated complex naval strategies, including cooperation with the Japanese Admiralty. In November 1914, the Japanese agreed to a strategical scheme, withdrawing a previous proposal from November 6 and agreeing to take measures near the Dutch East Indies and Sumatra. The Japanese First Southern Squadron was dispatched to Fiji, with the possibility of extending operations to the Marquesas Islands. The British Admiralty also coordinated the movement of ships such as the *Hizen*, *Asama*, and *Idzumo*, for which the Japanese requested coal supplies.

During this period, the Admiralty managed the safety of reinforcing ships and the survivors of Admiral Cradock’s squadron. On November 4, 1914, the Admiralty issued urgent orders to H.M.S. *Kent* to proceed to the Abrolhos Rocks to join Rear-Admiral Stoddart’s squadron.

## Notable Military Figures
The British army has been shaped by various officers across different eras and theaters:

*   **Sir Benjamin D'Urban (1777-1849):** A general and colonial administrator who entered the army in 1793. He served in Holland, Westphalia, and San Domingo. During the Peninsular War, he served as quartermaster-general in the reorganization of the Portuguese army, participating in nine sieges and pitched battles, including Toulouse, Salamanca, and Badajoz. He was promoted to colonel in the British army in 1813 and eventually became a lieutenant-general in 1837.
*   **Sir William Francis Butler (1838- ):** A soldier who served in the Red River expedition, the Ashanti operations, the Zulu War, and the Sudan (1884-85). He served as colonel on the staff in Egypt from 1890 to 1892. In 1898, he became commander-in-chief in South Africa. While acting as high commissioner from December 1898 to February 1899, he expressed views on the probability of war that were not approved by the home government, resulting in his order to return home to command the western district.
*   **Viscount Horatio Herbert Kitchener (1850- ):** A field marshal who began his career in the Royal Engineers in 1871. He performed survey work in Palestine and Cyprus before joining the Egyptian army. He served as commandant at Suakin and later as adjutant-general of the army. In 1892, he became sirdar of the Egyptian army. He is noted for his administrative work in preparing the advance into the Sudan and commanding the united force that stormed the Mahdist zareba on the river Atbara on April 8, 1898.

## Professional Development and Doctrine
The British army has also integrated foreign military theory into its official doctrine. The work of Paul Bronsart von Schellendorf, a Prussian general and former war minister, was of significant importance. His treatise, *Der Dienst des Generalstabes* (The Duties of the General Staff), was translated into English and issued officially to the British army. A new edition of this work, updated by Major Bronsart in 1904, was reissued in English by the General Staff in 1905.
