# Baptist Development in Colonial America

## Early Establishments and Migrations
Baptist congregations began appearing in various regions of North America during the 17th century. In 1682, Humphrey Churchwood and William Screven, members of the Boston church, organized a small congregation at Kittery, Maine. Due to persecution, Screven and other members migrated to South Carolina, where they collaborated with wealthy English Baptists to organize the First Baptist church in Charleston around 1684. This center became pivotal for the dissemination of Baptist principles throughout the region, although the church nearly vanished around 1746 due to the intrusion of Socinianism and the withdrawal of members to form neighboring churches.

In the New Netherlands (New York), antipaedobaptists began settling from 1643 onward. Lady Deborah Moody moved from Massachusetts to the New Netherlands in 1643 because of her antipaedobaptist views, eventually settling at Gravesend after receiving a guarantee of religious liberty from Dutch authorities. Other early efforts in the region included those of Francis Doughty in 1656 and William Wickenden of Providence, both of whom were banished following their evangelical work.

## Expansion in the 18th Century
The early 1700s saw a spread of Baptist faith across several colonies. In New York, Valentine Wightman of Connecticut, a General Baptist, began making missionary visits in 1711. In 1714, he baptized Nicolas Eyres and several others, assisting them in the organization of a church. By 1724, a Baptist church, likely Arminian, was formed at Oyster Bay.

In Virginia, Baptists of the general (Arminian) type appeared from 1714 onward; these individuals were fostered and organized by missionaries from the English General Baptists. This movement expanded into North Carolina by 1727, where a church was constituted.

In Pennsylvania, the Quaker colonies' religious liberty attracted Baptists from England, Wales, and New England. Thomas Dungan, an Irish Baptist minister, helped found a church at Cold Spring in Bucks County around 1684. In 1688, Elias Keach, son of the English evangelist Benjamin Keach, helped form the Pennepek church. While services were held in Philadelphia under the Pennepek church from 1687, an independent organization was not established there until 1698.

## Organizational Structures and Associations
To maintain sound doctrine and coordinate efforts, Baptists formed delegated bodies. The Philadelphia Association was established in 1707 to set churches in order and consult on missing needs. This body was influential in educational work, missionary efforts, and the supply of ministers. Within this association, Seventh-day Baptist churches began forming from 1705 onward due to the appearance of Sabbatarianism.

In South Carolina, the First Church in Charleston experienced a period of prosperity starting in 1749 under the leadership of Oliver Hart. Following the model of the Philadelphia Association, the Charleston Association was formed in 1751. By 1755, this association began raising funds for domestic missionary work, and in 1756, it established a fund for the education of ministers. Beneficiaries of this fund included Samuel Stillman, who later became the pastor of the Boston church.

## Growth and Diversification
Between 1787 and 1812, the Baptist population in South Carolina grew remarkably from 1,620 to 11,325 members. This growth was primarily driven by the "Separate" Baptist movement led by Marshall and Stearns, rather than the Charleston Association. Both the "Separate" and the more cultivated Charleston types of Baptist life spread into Georgia, with the former focusing on evangelizing the masses.

During this period, many negro slaves in Georgia, the Carolinas, and Virginia became Baptists. While most joined white Baptist churches, some were encouraged to establish their own churches in towns such as Savannah and Richmond.

## National Missions and Institutionalization
At the start of the 19th century, Baptists in Philadelphia, Charleston, and Boston supported the work of English missionary William Carey in India. A significant turning point occurred in 1812 when Luther Rice and Adoniram Judson converted to Baptist views. Rice returned to America to organize missionary societies, leading to the formation of "The Baptist Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in India and other Foreign Parts" in Boston in January 1813.

In May 1814, thirty-three delegates from eleven states met in Philadelphia to organize the "General Missionary Convention of the Baptist Denomination in the United States of America for Foreign Missions." Known as the "Triennial Convention" because it met every three years, it established a Board of Commissioners in Philadelphia (moved to Boston in 1826). To address the need for educated ministers, Columbian College (now George Washington University) was established in Washington in 1821. The Triennial Convention also engaged in home mission work from 1817 onward, eventually constituting the American Baptist Home Mission Society in 1832.

## Sectional Division
Tensions regarding slavery between Northern and Southern members created friction during Triennial Convention sessions before 1844. A controversy between Richard Fuller and Francis Wayland regarding slavery led Southern Baptists to seek a separate organization. In 1845, the Southern Baptist Convention was formed, including its own Foreign and Home Missionary Boards and a later Sunday-school Board. While Northern and Southern Baptists remained in fellowship, they maintained separate annual meetings and boards. In 1905, a General Baptist Convention for America was formed to promote comity and fellowship, though it was designed not to interfere with administrative or sectional organizations.
