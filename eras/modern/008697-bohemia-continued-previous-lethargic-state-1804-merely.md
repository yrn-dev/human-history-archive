# Bohemia

## Early History and Origins
The region of Bohemia derives its name from the Boii, a Celtic tribe that inhabited the land during the earliest historical period. The Boii, with their capital at Boiohemum, were eventually subdued around 12 B.C. by the Teutonic tribe of the Marcomanni. Following the expulsion of the Marcomanni by other Teutonic tribes, the land was conquered by Slavic tribes, most notably the Cechs. Archaeological evidence indicates Slavic inhabitants were present as early as the beginning of the Christian era, though the Cechs became the masters of the country in the 5th century.

Among the early rulers, Samo is recorded as having defeated the Franks at the battle of Wogatisburg in 630 and defeating the Avars, a Turanian tribe that had previously held overlordship over Bohemia. After a period of obscurity, chroniclers such as Cosmas describe the foundation of a principality by Krok and his daughter Libussa, who married a peasant named Premysl. This established a list of Premyslide princes, though their specific dates and deeds are regarded as semi-mythic rather than historical.

## Christianization and Early Principality
Christianity first attempted to enter Bohemia from Germany, but these efforts saw little success due to the Bohemians' distrust of their western neighbors. The faith was more successfully introduced via Moravia through the Greek monks Cyrillus and Methodius. Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius around 873, leading to a rapid adoption of Christianity.

A prominent ruler of this era was Wenceslas, known as the Holy, who was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized by the Church of Rome. This murder sparked a war with Germany, in which Boleslav was generally successful. Under Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its frontiers to include Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and the area of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. This empire was short-lived; Boleslav III lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland. Following a period of civil war between Boleslav III and his brothers Jaromir and Ulrick, the Bohemians dethroned Boleslav III due to his cruelty and chose the Polish prince Vladivoj as their ruler.

## The Thirty Years' War and Habsburg Rule
Bohemia was a central theater of the Thirty Years' War, which began in its capital. The conflict was precipitated by the efforts of Ferdinand II, who had been elected king of Bohemia in 1618 and sought to establish the rule of the Jesuits and win back the region to the Catholic faith. In response, the Bohemian estates formally deposed Ferdinand on July 31, 1619, and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as king. Frederick and his queen, Elizabeth of England, were crowned at St Vitus's cathedral in Prague.

The tide turned in 1620 when a large imperialist army, supported by Spain, Poland, several Italian states, and the Lutheran elector of Saxony, entered Bohemia. On November 8, 1620, the imperial forces defeated the Bohemian army at the "Bila Hora" (White Hill), leading to the occupation of Prague and the flight of King Frederick. Throughout the remainder of the war, Bohemia suffered frequent pillaging by Swedish troops. In 1648, the Swedish general Konigsmark took part of Prague, marking the final event of the war. Efforts by Swedish envoys to secure toleration for Bohemian Protestants were rejected by inflexible imperial representatives.

## Habsburg Succession and Administrative Changes
In the early 18th century, the possibility of the extinction of the Habsburg male line led the estates of Bohemia to sanction female succession to the throne on October 16, 1720, recognizing the Pragmatic Sanction. However, upon the death of Emperor Charles VI, Maria Theresa faced opposition from Charles, elector of Bavaria, who claimed the Bohemian throne. Charles invaded with Bavarian, French, and Saxon troops, occupying Prague and receiving oaths of allegiance from many Bohemian knights and nobles on December 19, 1741.

Maria Theresa eventually recovered Bohemia. During her reign and that of her son, Joseph II, internal administration was altered to limit Bohemian autonomy. A 1749 decree abolished separate Bohemian law-courts, and an Austro-Bohemian chancellor was later appointed to oversee the administration of Bohemia and the German domains of the house of Habsburg.

## National Rights and the Language Question
In the 19th century, Bohemians sought the re-establishment of their ancient constitution. In 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reach an agreement to recognize the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia, but this failed. By 1879, under Count Eduard Taaffe, Bohemian representatives returned to the parliament of Vienna, maintaining that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. Taaffe's government removed some electoral anomalies that favored the German minority and permitted the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.

Tensions resurfaced regarding language. In 1897, Count Badeni issued a decree requiring government officials in Bohemia to know both the Bohemian and German languages. This caused violent opposition from German inhabitants of Austria and led to the fall of Badeni's cabinet. Subsequent governments under Count Thun and Count Clary repealed these equal-rights decrees, resulting in troubles in Prague that were severely repressed by Austrian authorities.
