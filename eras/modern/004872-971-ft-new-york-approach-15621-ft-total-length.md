## Bridges of the East River
The East River in New York is spanned by several remarkable bridges of great length. The Brooklyn Bridge, construction of which began in 1872, has a total length of 5,989 ft. This total includes a center span of 1,595½ ft., side spans of 930 ft., a Brooklyn approach of 971 ft., and a New York approach of 1,562½ ft. The bridge features four cables, each 15¾ in. in diameter, which support a roadway, an electric railway, and a promenade. These cables are composed of 19 strands, with each strand containing 278 parallel steel wires (7 B.W.G.). The use of parallel wires was preferred by Roebling as they are 10% stronger than twisted wires. The saddles for the chains are positioned 329 ft. above high water, and the main span's stiffening girders are 67 ft. apart and 40 ft. deep.

The Williamsburg Bridge, which began construction in 1897 and opened for traffic in 1903, has a span of 1,600 ft., a width of 118 ft., and a versed sine of 176 ft. It is designed with two decks that carry two footways, two bicycle paths, two carriageways, four electric tramcar lines, and two elevated railway tracks. The bridge utilizes four cables, each 18¾ in. in diameter and weighing approximately 1,116 tons. These cables, which have a nominal breaking strength of 22,320 tons, are composed of 37 strands of 208 wires (7,696 parallel steel wires, No. 8 B.W.G.). The cables are sheathed in sheet iron and wrapped in varnish and oxidized oil-soaked cotton duck. The saddles are 332 ft. above the water. The cables support a live load of 4,017 tons and a dead load of 7,140 tons.

Additionally, the Manhattan bridge was designed to accommodate four tramway and four railway tracks.

## Financial Systems and the Panic of 1907
The United States possesses a highly developed system of clearing cheques, with clearing-houses in approximately 112 leading cities. As of the year ending September 30, 1907, aggregate clearings reached $154,662,515,258. The New York Clearing-House handles a significant portion of this volume; in 1906, its clearings accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities.

In October 1907, a financial panic occurred, precipitated by over-speculation by national banks. This led to the suspension of the Knickerbocker Trust Company on October 22, which held deposits of $48,000,000. Consequently, runs occurred on other companies, and New York banks faced a deficit in required reserves of $38,838,825 during the week of November 2. Arrangements were made to import foreign gold approaching $100,000,000. Between the autumn and the peak of the panic, national bank circulation increased by about $77,000,000, and $72,000,000 was transferred from the treasury to the banks. The total currency added to or disappeared from circulation during these weeks exceeded $275,000,000, nearly one-tenth of the country's usual circulation volume. Payment of cheques in currency was suspended in New York from October 28, 1907, until approximately the beginning of 1908. By December 28, 1907, bank-note circulation had risen to an abnormal $687,340,835, which was reduced during the spring of 1908.

## Metropolitan Correctional Center (MCC) New York
The Metropolitan Correctional Center (MCC) New York utilizes a Special Housing Unit (SHU) to securely separate certain inmates from the general population. Inmates in the SHU are kept locked in their cells for approximately 23 hours a day. This unit is used for inmates on disciplinary segregation or administrative detention—a non-punitive designation used to protect the public or ensure the safety and orderly operation of the facility.

### Inmate Counting Procedures
Institutional counts are conducted on weekdays at 12 a.m., 3 a.m., 5 a.m., 4 p.m., and 10 p.m. The 10 p.m. count is a "stand-up count," requiring inmates to stand. According to BOP policy and SHU Post Orders, two Correctional Officers (COs) must perform the count for each housing unit. In the SHU, these officers must walk from tier to tier to observe each inmate. One officer counts while the other observes, after which they switch roles and compare totals. If the numbers do not match, the count is repeated.

The results are documented on an official "Metropolitan Correctional Center; New York, New York; Official Count Slip." Both COs must sign the slip and record the date, time, and total number of inmates physically present. These slips are delivered to the Control Center, where officers compare them against the institution's overall inmate count sheet. A count is only "cleared" once all slips are collected and matched.
