# Particular Baptists

The Particular Baptists were a denomination of English Baptists characterized by their adherence to Calvinistic theology. They emerged as a distinct group from the Independents, differing significantly from the General Baptists, who were Arminian due to the influence of Mennonite Anabaptists. While both General and Particular Baptists rejected infant baptism, they were initially unpledged to immersion and rarely practiced it.

## Origins and Early Development
The rise of the first Calvinistic or Particular Baptist Church is associated with the Jacob church in Southwark. Originally an Independent church, it later became Baptist. Notable members of this congregation included William Kiffin (1616-1701), Hanserd Knollys (ca. 1599-1691), Henry Jessey (1601-1663), Praise-God Barbon (ca. 1596-1679), and John Lothropp or Lathrop (d. 1653). 

The first Particular Baptist Church was actually formed in 1633 by a group of members dismissed from the Jacob church after the congregation had grown beyond what was considered proper limits. From the Jacob church, six other churches eventually sprang, five of which were Baptist. By 1644, the Particular Baptist churches of London had grown to seven and published a Confession of Faith. Although they were commonly called Anabaptists, the sources note that this label was false.

## Theological Distinctions
The primary distinction between the two main Baptist sections was their view of redemption. General Baptists held to "General Redemption" based on Arminian views, while Particular Baptists agreed with the Calvinistic theory of "Particular Redemption."

Over time, the theology of the Particular Baptists evolved. In the 18th century, the Calvinism in many churches became more rigid, in some cases approaching or becoming Antinomianism. This period also saw the prevalence of "hyper-Calvinism," which held that nothing spiritually good was the duty of the unregenerate and that they should not be addressed with exhortations, except regarding external obedience.

Toward the end of the 18th century, the writings of Andrew Fuller (1754-1815) led many Particular Baptist churches to become more moderate in their Calvinism. Fuller sought to counteract hyper-Calvinism through his work, *The Gospel worthy of all Acceptation*. He also wrote *The Calvinistic and Socinian systems examined and compared as to their moral tendency* (1793) to rebut accusations of antinomianism.

## Communion and Membership
Historically, a great majority of Baptists required baptism as a prerequisite for membership or communion. A notable exception existed in the churches of Hertfordshire and Bedfordshire influenced by Bunyan, who argued that differences of opinion regarding water baptism should not bar communion. 

By the beginning of the 19th century, the question of "mixed communion" became a subject of extensive discussion, with Robert Hall (1764-1831) playing a principal role. This practice gradually spread, and some churches eventually allowed full membership to those professing faith in Christ even if they disagreed on the ordinance of baptism, citing the right of private judgment.

## Strict Baptists and Later Unions
A separate group known as the Strict Baptists maintained a rigorous adherence to a type of Calvinistic theology that later became generally obsolete. They insisted that baptism was a mandatory condition for Christian communion. The Strict Baptists are organized loosely into geographical associations, such as the Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches and the Suffolk and Norfolk Association of Particular Baptist Churches (the latter preferring the name "Particular" but remaining "strict"). The Strict Baptists are further divided into the "Standard" and "Vessel" parties, named after their respective organs, the *Gospel Standard* and *Earthen Vessel*.

In 1891, under the influence of General Baptist leader Dr. John Clifford, the General and Particular denominations united to form the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland. This union is voluntary; some prosperous churches remain aloof, and the Strict Baptist churches continue to exist as a separate denomination.

## Missionary Work and Organizations
The Strict Baptists established a Strict Baptist Missionary Society in 1860 (refounded in 1897) to conduct work in South India. As of 1905, this society had 72 pastors and workers, 730 church members, and an income of £1146. Additionally, Andrew Fuller was heavily involved with the Baptist Missionary Society formed at Kettering.
