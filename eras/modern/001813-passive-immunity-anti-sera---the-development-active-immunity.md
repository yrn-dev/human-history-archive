# Passive Immunity and Anti-sera

Immunity is defined as a non-susceptibility to a specific disease or to experimental inoculation with a particular toxin or bacterium. This state is relative and depends on various conditions, including the general or local vitality of the animal. While some animals possess natural immunity, others acquire it either by surviving a disease attack or through artificial inoculation. Artificial immunity is divided into two primary types: active and passive.

## Active and Passive Immunity
Active immunity is produced by a series of reactions within the animal's body, typically triggered by injections of bacteria or their products. This process is used in preventive inoculation. In contrast, passive immunity is achieved by transferring a quantity of serum from an actively immunized animal into a fresh animal. It is termed "passive" because it does not involve an active change in the tissues of the recipient animal. This method forms the basis of serum therapeutics.

## Anti-substances and Antigens
The development of active immunity is associated with the appearance of "anti-substances" in the blood serum. These substances allow for the transfer of passive immunity to another animal. Anti-substances are not produced solely in response to bacteria; they also occur when proteins, ferments, or various alien cells are injected. Organic molecules are categorized by whether they trigger these anti-substances; those that do are known as antigens. Each antigen possesses a specific combining affinity for its corresponding anti-substance, functioning like a lock and key. Conversely, vegetable poisons such as glucosides and alkaloids do not produce anti-substances.

## Classification of Anti-substances
Ehrlich arranged anti-substances into three main groups based on their interaction with antigens:
1. **Simple Combination:** The anti-substance combines with the antigen without producing a known change in it; antitoxins are examples of this group.
2. **Physical Change:** The anti-substance combines with the antigen and produces a recognizable physical change, as seen with agglutinins and precipitins.
3. **Complement Activation:** After combining with the antigen, the anti-substance leads to the union of a third body called complement (also known as cytase or alexine), which is present in normal serum. This three-way union often results in a digestive or dissolving action, which is the mechanism for bacteriolytic or haemolytic sera.

## Anti-bacterial Sera and Lysogenic Action
Anti-bacterial sera are prepared by injecting living (virulent or supervirulent) or dead bacteria, or their maceration products, into an animal. The resulting serum provides powerful protection to other animals, though it is generally not antitoxic. These sera can demonstrate three main activities outside the body: agglutinative, opsonic, and bacteriolytic (lysogenic) action.

Lysogenic action involves a change in the bacterium that causes it to swell, become granular, and eventually dissolve. This process requires two substances: a stable "immune body" (referred to by Ehrlich as *Immunkoerper* and by Bordet as *substance sensibilisatrice*) that resists temperatures of 60°C, and a labile, ferment-like substance present in normal serum that is destroyed at 60°C. This disintegrative effect is not limited to bacteria but can also affect spermatozoa, leucocytes, and red corpuscles.

## Phagocytosis and Opsonic Action
Phagocytosis is the process by which cells, particularly leucocytes, ingest and digest bacteria. Leucocytes are guided toward the focus of production via positive chemiotaxis (sensitivity to chemical substances). Metchnikoff observed that phagocytosis is present in immune animals but deficient or absent in susceptible ones.

Opsonic action refers to the effect of serum in making bacteria more susceptible to phagocytosis. This is measured by the "phagocytic index," which compares the average number of bacteria within leucocytes in a test serum against a normal serum control. There are two classes of substances involved in this process:
* **Normal Opsonins:** Labile substances present in normal serum that are destroyed at 55°C.
* **Immune Opsonins:** Thermostable anti-substances developed through active immunity.

The increased ingestion of bacteria during active immunity depends on these immune opsonins, which act on the bacteria before the leucocytes intervene.

## Agglutination
Agglutination occurs when agglutinins combine with molecules in the bacteria known as agglutinogens. This process requires the presence of salts in the fluid; if agglutinated bacterial masses are washed salt-free, the clumps resolve. Agglutinins are used up during this process as they combine with elements of the bacterial structure.
