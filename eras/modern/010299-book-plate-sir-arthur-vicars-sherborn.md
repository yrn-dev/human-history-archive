# BOOK-PLATE OF SIR ARTHUR VICARS

## Overview
The book-plate of Sir Arthur Vicars is a work created by the artist C.W. Sherborn. It exists within the broader tradition of the book-plate, or *ex-libris*, which is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes.

## The Art of the Book-Plate
The book-plate is nearly as old as the printed book itself, serving as a printed equivalent to the hand-painted armorial or symbolical personal devices found in medieval manuscripts. Historically, the creation of these plates was almost exclusively the domain of the heraldic stationer. However, in more recent years, the composition of personal book-tokens has been recognized as a minor branch of a higher art.

This evolution has led to a new class of designs characterized by great variety. In these modern styles, the purely heraldic elements tend to become subsidiary, while symbolic or allegorical elements assert themselves more strongly.

## C.W. Sherborn and Modern Design
C.W. Sherborn is identified as one of the modern English artists who have specifically focused on the devising of book-plates and produced admirable designs. While the popularization of book-plates has been aided by the development of process work—which has made the reproduction of elaborate designs cheaper and easier—Sherborn is noted as one of the few remaining artists who utilize the skill of the graver. His work is cited as being among the best-known in England and is considered to challenge comparison with the finest productions of engravers from previous eras.

## Historical Context of Book-Plates
The practice of using *ex-libris* began with German examples; the oldest movable examples are woodcuts from approximately 1480, created by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach for the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim. In England, the earliest known example is a gift-plate created by Sir Nicholas Bacon for books presented to the university of Cambridge.

English book-plates from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian periods reflect the prevailing decorative arts of their respective epochs. Early English plates were remarkably simple and primarily armorial, lacking the elaborateness found in German examples. Following the Restoration, book-plates became a standard accessory for well-ordered libraries, eventually adopting tincture lines and dots (a method devised by Petra Sancta) and more elaborate mantling that mirrored the periwigs of the period.

## Collecting and Study
The study of book-plates is a subject of interest to "ex-librists." While some bibliophiles have criticized the removal of plates from old books as vandalism, these plates are often viewed as high-interest personal relics of well-known personages or specimens of bygone decorative fashions. In some instances, the value of the book-plate exceeds that of the volume to which it is affixed.

Authorities and resources for the study of book-plate composition include the *Ex-libris Series* (London), as well as works by authors such as Hon. J. Leicester Warren, Sir A.W. Franks, Egerton Castle, and H.W. Fincham.
