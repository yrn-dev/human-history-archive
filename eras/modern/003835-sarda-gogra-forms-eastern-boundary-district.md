# The River Gogra (Sarda) and Associated Districts

The Gogra, also referred to as the Sarda, is a principal river system in British India that serves as a critical geographical boundary and drainage artery for several districts. It is noted for being navigable in certain regions and is central to the alluvial plains of the United Provinces.

## Geographical Influence on Districts

The Gogra defines the borders and landscapes of multiple administrative areas:

*   **Bareilly:** The river Sarda or Gogra forms the eastern boundary of the Bareilly district, where it serves as the principal stream. The district itself is a level country of 1580 sq. m. with fertile soil and a general slope toward the south.
*   **Bara Banki:** In the Bara Banki district, the Gogra forms the northern boundary. This district is characterized as a level plain with numerous marshes (*jhils*). The Gogra, along with the Gumti river, is navigable, facilitating the active trade of agricultural produce.
*   **Bahraich:** The Gogra skirts the Bahraich district for a distance of 114 m. The district contains a "great plain of the Gogra" on its western side, which sits approximately 40 ft. below the level of the district's central elevated triangular plateau.
*   **Ballia:** This district, constituted in 1879, occupies an angle at the junction where the Gogra meets the Ganges. Trade in the region is conducted largely via these two bordering rivers.
*   **Azamgarh:** The Gogra bounds this district to the north, separating it from the Gorakhpur district. The land along the banks of the Gogra is a low-lying tract.

## Regional Hydrology and Terrain

The Gogra is part of a broader network of rivers and streams. In the Bareilly district, it is the primary stream, followed in importance by the Ramganga—which collects hill torrents from the Kumaon mountains—the Deoha, and the Gomati (or Gumti). In the Fyzabad district, the land lies entirely between the two great rivers, the Gogra and the Gumti.

The terrain surrounding the Gogra is predominantly alluvial. In the Bahraich district, the river is situated near the *tarai* (forest and marshy tracts) along the southern slopes of the Himalayas. In the Azamgarh district, the land slopes from the north-west to the south-east, though general drainage is described as very inadequate.

## Economic and Agricultural Impact

The presence of the Gogra and its associated plains supports intensive agriculture. In the Bara Banki district, the river supports the production of rice, wheat, pulse, sugar-cane, and opium. Similarly, the fertile lands of the Azamgarh district, located near the Gogra, produce magnificent crops of indigo, sugar-cane, and rice, with several indigo factories operating in the area. In Bahraich, the district is purely agricultural, though it lacks canals and relies on rivers, tanks, and wells for irrigation.

## Administrative and Political Context

The districts influenced by the Gogra have seen various shifts in governance. The Fyzabad division, which includes the districts of Bahraich and Bara Banki, encompasses an area of 12,113 sq. m. Bara Banki came under British rule in 1856 along with the rest of Oudh. The Azamgarh district was ceded to the Company in 1801 by the wazirs of Lucknow. In Bareilly, the territory of the Rohillas (descendants of Yusafzai Afghans) was annexed to Oudh in 1774 after they were subjugated by a confederacy of British troops and the army of the nawab of Oudh; this territory was later ceded to the Company in 1801.
