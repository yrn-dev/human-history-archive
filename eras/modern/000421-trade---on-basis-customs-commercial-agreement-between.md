# Trade

Trade has historically served as a primary driver for imperial expansion and a unifying force in the development of empires. In the case of the British Empire, trade with temperate and tropical regions formed its foundation. Trading companies were responsible for the founding of most American and West Indian colonies, the colonization of northwestern Canada, and the acquisition of India. Furthermore, chartered companies in the 19th century extended British influence into the interior of Africa from the east, west, and south, while other trading companies developed Fiji and Borneo. The British command of the sea, established through commercial wars during much of the 18th century, enabled the settlement of Australasia. The imperial navy's protection of colonial commerce remains one of the strongest material links between the crown and its outlying possessions.

## British Imperial Commercial Policy
During the late 19th century, the British Empire explored preferential systems for trade. At an 1887 Colonial Conference in London, a South African delegate proposed a 2% duty on all foreign goods to fund the imperial navy, which required denouncing certain treaties with foreign nations that restricted trade between different parts of the empire. By 1897, treaties with Belgium and Germany were denounced to remove foreign engagements that hindered domestic trade arrangements between the mother-country and its colonies.

A shift in perception occurred as Great Britain recognized that its foreign trade in manufactured goods enlarged home markets for colonial raw materials. This was expressed at the 1894 Ottawa Colonial Conference, leading to the relinquishment of demands for Great Britain to impose differential duties on foreign goods in exchange for colonial concessions. Canada led this movement in 1897 by introducing an imperial tariff that granted a 25% deduction on customs duties for British goods and other British colonies trading on equal terms. Similar preferential treatment was later granted by New Zealand, Australia, and the South African states.

## Austro-Hungarian Customs and Commercial Agreements
The economic relationship between Austria and Hungary was governed by a customs and commercial agreement established in 1867, renewable every ten years. Under this arrangement, both states followed the same principles regarding commercial affairs, customs legislation, and legislation on duties connected to industrial production, specifically for mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer. The agreement also covered coinage, legal tender, the principles of the Austro-Hungarian Bank, and railways affecting both states. By the 1899 renewal, the monarchy functioned as a single customs and commercial territory, including the principality of Liechtenstein and Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Foreign trade data for the monarchy shows a steady increase in both imports and exports between 1900 and 1905. In 1900, imports were L70,666,000 and exports were L80,916,000; by 1905, these figures rose to L89,430,000 and L93,500,000, respectively.

## Evolution of the Austro-Hungarian Tariff
Initially, the customs tariff of the dual state relied on commercial treaties with nations such as Italy, France, Germany, and Great Britain. These treaties included "most favoured nation" clauses and maximum duties, resulting in a system that nearly lacked customs duties. However, after 1873, Austrian manufacturers—facing competition from Belgian, English, and German goods—pushed for protective duties. While Hungary initially favored free trade to secure markets for its agricultural produce, a new treaty in 1877 introduced low duties on corn and textile goods and raised excises on brandy and sugar. All duties were required to be paid in gold. Following the expiration of treaties with France and Great Britain and the failure of agreements with Germany under Bismarck, Austria-Hungary transitioned to a system where tariffs were determined by legislation.

## Economic Strife and the 1907 Agreement
The early 20th century saw tension between the two states. In 1905, the Hungarian government sought an autonomous tariff. Although a "Szell-Koerber Compact" was concluded in 1902 to establish a joint tariff for negotiating with other states, it was never fully ratified and was eventually discarded. By 1907, a severe economic crisis in Hungary and the realization that separation would increase the cost of living in Cisleithania and deprive Austrian manufacturers of their best market led to a new "customs and commercial treaty."

Key features of the 1907 agreement included:
* The establishment of separate but identical customs tariffs for each state.
* Mutual facilitation of railway communications: Hungary for the line between Dalmatia and Vienna (to be completed by 1911), and Austria for communication between Prussia and Hungary.
* The abolition of practical impediments to free trade in sugar.
* Reform of Hungarian Stock Exchange and produce laws to prevent agrarian speculation.
* The creation of a court of arbitration with four judges from each state and a presidency decided by lot.
* Financial arrangements allowing Hungary to redeem its share of the old Austrian debt at a rate of 4.325% within ten years.
* The ranking of government securities as investments for insurance companies and savings banks in both countries.

## International Commercial Treaties
Other global commercial arrangements include the 1887 treaty between China and Portugal. This agreement ended a long diplomatic difficulty over Macao, where Portugal had paid an annual rent of £71 to China until 1849. Under the treaty, China confirmed Portugal's perpetual government of Macao, and Portugal agreed not to alienate the territory without Chinese consent and to cooperate in opium revenue work. This allowed Chinese imperial customs to collect duties on vessels trading with Macao.

Additionally, following a war, Japan and Russia agreed to a system of reciprocal treatment on the "most favoured nation" footing for commercial relations. This included customs formalities, import and export duties, tonnage dues, transit, and the treatment of vessels and subjects.

In 1917, Germany recognized that all agreements with Siam, including the right of extra-territoriality, had ceased. Germany waived claims regarding the liquidation of property or seizure of ships and transferred public property (excluding diplomatic premises) to Siam without compensation. Germany also renounced rights under 1911 and 1912 arrangements regarding Liberia, abrogating all commercial treaties with that nation.
