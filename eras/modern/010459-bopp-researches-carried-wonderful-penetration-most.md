# Franz Bopp and the Science of Comparative Grammar

Franz Bopp (1791–1867) was a German philologist whose meticulous research into linguistic phenomena is credited with the inception of the science of comparative grammar. Born in Mainz on September 14, 1791, Bopp was raised in Aschaffenburg, Bavaria, due to political instability. He received a liberal education at the Lyceum, where he was introduced to the literature and languages of the East through the lectures of Karl J. Windischmann. Bopp's interest in the sacred language of the Hindus was further stimulated by Fr. Schlegel's 1808 work, *Uber die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier*.

## Education and Early Research
In 1812, the Bavarian government funded Bopp's studies in Paris, where he focused on Sanskrit. During this period, he associated with eminent scholars including S. de Sacy, A.L. Chezy, L.M. Langles, and Alexander Hamilton. Hamilton, who had acquired knowledge of Sanskrit while in India, had co-authored a descriptive catalogue of Sanskrit manuscripts for the Imperial library. Bopp utilized this library's extensive collection, which included manuscripts brought from India by Father Pons in the early 18th century, as well as books from the Serampore and Calcutta presses.

The first result of his four years of study was published in 1816 in Frankfort-On-Main: *Uber das Conjugationssystem der Sanskritsprache in Vergleichung mit jenem der griechischen, lateinischen, persischen und germanischen Sprache*. This work, which featured a preface by Windischmann, established the trajectory of Bopp's lifelong philological research. Rather than attempting to prove the common parentage of Sanskrit, Persian, Greek, Latin, and German—a fact already established—Bopp sought to trace the common origin of their grammatical structures.

## Academic Career and Major Works
Following a brief stay in Germany and a sojourn in London—where he befriended Wilhelm von Humboldt and provided him with Sanskrit instruction—Bopp was appointed to the chair of comparative grammar and Sanskrit at Berlin in 1821 upon Humboldt's recommendation. He was elected to the Royal Prussian Academy the following year.

Bopp's bibliography includes several significant linguistic and literary contributions:
*   **Sanskrit Texts:** In London (1819), he published a critical edition of the story of *Nala and Damayanti* from the Mahabharata. He later published further episodes of the Mahabharata in Berlin (1824, 1829, and a new edition of *Nala* in 1832).
*   **Grammars and Glossaries:** He produced the *Ausfuhrliches Lehrgebaude der Sanskrita-Sprache* (1827), a Latin edition (1828–1832), and a shorter grammar (1834). He also compiled a Sanskrit and Latin glossary (1830), with later editions in 1847 and 1867 incorporating cognate languages.
*   **Comparative Grammar:** His primary achievement was the *Vergleichende Grammatik des Sanskrit, Zend, Griechischen, Lateinischen, Litthauischen, Altslavischen, Gothischen, und Deutschen*, published in six parts between 1833 and 1852. This work was preceded by a series of monographs in the *Transactions of the Berlin Academy* (1824–1831).

## Methodology and Influence
The objective of Bopp's *Comparative Grammar* was threefold: to investigate the origin of grammatical forms, to trace phonetic laws, and to describe the original grammatical structure of languages through intercomparison. The first two goals served the third. His work evolved over time; for instance, Old-Slavonian was included from the second part onwards, and a revised German edition (1856–1861) incorporated Old-Armenian.

Bopp's influence extended internationally. His *Comparative Grammar* was translated into English by E.B. Eastwick in 1845 and into French by Professor Michel Breal in 1866. In England, the philologist John William Donaldson utilized Bopp's principles of comparative philology as the foundation for *The New Cratylus* (1839), though that work was published before Bopp's grammar was completed.

## Legacy and Death
Bopp's research provided a wide view into the domestic usages, tenets, practices, and affinities of ancient Indo-European nations. His work was universally accepted during his lifetime. In recognition of his contributions, *Die Bopp-Stiftung*—a fund for the promotion of comparative grammar and Sanskrit studies—was established in Berlin on May 16, 1866, marking the fiftieth anniversary of Windischmann's preface to Bopp's first work.

Franz Bopp died on October 23, 1867. Despite his professional fame, he died a poor man. He was remembered by his peers and family for his modesty, unselfishness, and kindliness.
