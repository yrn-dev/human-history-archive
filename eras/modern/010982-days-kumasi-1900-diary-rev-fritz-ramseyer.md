# Days at Kumasi, 1900, from the Diary of the Rev. Fritz Ramseyer

*Dark and Stormy Days at Kumasi, 1900, from the diary of the Rev. Fritz Ramseyer* is a historical account published in London in 1901. It was written by a besieged resident of Kumasi during the rebellion of 1900.

## Background of the Author
The Rev. Fritz Ramseyer was a member of the Basel mission. His history with the Ashanti people began in June 1869, when an Ashanti force invaded Krepi, a territory beyond the Volta. During this invasion, Ramseyer, his wife, and their infant son were captured. The child died shortly thereafter due to privation. Ramseyer and his wife were among four Europeans—including Mr. J. Kuhne and a French trader named Monsieur M.J. Bonnat—who were taken as captives to Kumasi. Ramseyer and his wife were imprisoned by King Kofi Karikari for a period of four and a half years.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Following the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was appointed to the Ashanti throne. Instead, the affairs of Kumasi were administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart. During this period, Mr. and Mrs. Ramseyer returned to Kumasi, joined by other missionaries. A fort was established in the city and garrisoned by the Gold Coast constabulary.

Although the Kumasi chiefs appeared submissive, they remained unreconciled to British rule. Tensions escalated on March 28, 1900, during a public palaver where the governor of the Gold Coast, Sir F. Hodgson, announced that the Ashanti chiefs were required to pay the British government 4,000 oz. of gold annually. Sir F. Hodgson further reproached the chiefs for hiding the golden stool, which had been concealed since 1896.

Three days after this announcement, Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa who had been sent to discover the location of the golden stool. Driven by a desire to erase the dishonour of Prempeh's deportation, the Kumasi warriors subsequently threatened the fort.

## The Siege of the Fort
As the rebellion broke out, involving the Kumasi, Adansi, and Kokofu tribes, the rebels commanded a force reported to be 40,000 strong. Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, along with the Basel missionaries including Mr. Ramseyer, took refuge inside the Kumasi fort and requested urgent reinforcements.

The defense of the fort progressed through several stages of reinforcement:
* **April 18:** 100 Gold Coast constabulary arrived.
* **April 29:** The Kumasi attacked in force but were repulsed. On this same day, 250 Lagos constabulary reached the city, though they arrived with little ammunition after fighting their way up.
* **May 15:** Major A. Morris arrived from the British territory north of Ashanti with 250 men, bringing the total garrison to 700.

The European population within the fort consisted of 29 individuals, including four women.

## Relief of Kumasi
A relief expedition was organized at Cape Coast by Colonel James Willcocks, who arrived from Nigeria on May 26. The mission faced severe obstacles, including a lack of carriers and local food supplies, an impenetrable bush, and the peak of the rainy season, which turned roads into deep mire. Additionally, the enemy utilized concealed stockades.

Colonel Willcocks reached Bekwai on July 9. To divert the enemy, he spread a report that he would attack Kokofu on July 13. This maneuver drew several thousand rebels away from Kumasi. After a feint toward Kokofu, Willcocks marched west. Following smart fighting on July 14, the main rebel stockade was carried at the point of the bayonet by Yoruba troops on the afternoon of July 15.

The relieving force, consisting of 1,000 West African fighting men, 60 white officers and non-commissioned officers, two 75-millimetre guns, four seven-pounder guns, and six Maxims, entered Kumasi that evening. They found a war-worn garrison too weak to stand and a city consisting of putrid bodies and burnt-down houses outside the fort.

## Impact on Kumasi
The 1900 revolt caused further injury to the city of Kumasi, which had already suffered in 1874 (when Sir Garnet Wolseley's forces blew up the red sandstone royal palace) and in 1896 (when fire destroyed houses in the Bantama suburb). Following the suppression of the 1900 revolt, measures were taken to improve trade between Kumasi and Cape Coast. The city eventually saw a revival in trade and expanded rapidly after the railway from the coast reached Kumasi in September 1903.
