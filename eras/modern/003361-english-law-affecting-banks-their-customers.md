# ENGLISH LAW AFFECTING BANKS AND THEIR CUSTOMERS

## The Legal Relationship Between Banker and Customer
The legal nature of the relationship between a banker and a customer is defined as that of debtor and creditor. This was established by the House of Lords in the 1848 case of *Foley v. Hill*, which rejected previous contentions that the relationship was one of depositee and depositor, or trustee and *cestui que trust*. Under this framework, when a person pays money into a bank account, they are legally lending that money to the banker. Consequently, the banker is not bound by a fiduciary relation and is not responsible to the customer for how the funds are utilized.

Because this relationship is a mere debt, the Statute of Limitations bars a customer's right to recover paid-in money after six years, provided there has been no acknowledgment or payment of interest or principal in the interim (*Pott v. Clegg*). In practice, this is rarely an issue in current accounts because, absent specific appropriation, earlier withdrawals are attributed to earlier payments, causing credit and debit items to cancel each other. An exception occurs when a person wrongfully pays money into their own account that they hold in a fiduciary capacity; in such instances, it is presumed they drew out their own funds first, and any remaining balance is attributed to the trust money.

## Obligations and Rights Regarding Accounts
Bankers are bound to maintain secrecy regarding a customer's account, an obligation that continues even if the account becomes overdrawn (*Hardy v. Veasey*), unless there is good cause for disclosure. 

Regarding account records, entries in a pass-book serve as *prima facie* evidence against the banker. If a customer returns a pass-book without comment, it is also *prima facie* evidence against the customer, though the idea that this constitutes a final settlement of account is disputed. For example, if a cheque is repudiated on the grounds of forgery, a customer's omissions or dealings with the pass-book do not preclude them from objecting to the debit and holding the banker responsible for the loss (*Kepitigalla Rubber Co. v. National Bank of India*). However, a banker cannot dispute credit entries in a pass-book if the customer has altered their position based on those entries, and cheques drawn against an apparent balance must be honored (*Holland v. Manchester & Liverpool District Bank*).

## The Law of Cheques
Under section 53 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, a cheque in England is not an assignment of funds. Therefore, if payment is refused, the holder of the cheque has no claim against the banker, but must seek remedy from the endorser or drawer. This legal standing supports the English banking custom of refusing to pay a portion of a cheque's amount if funds are available but insufficient to cover the total sum. This differs from Scotland, where banks appear bound to pay whatever funds are available toward the cheque.

Bankers may hold paid cheques as vouchers until a settlement of account is reached with the customer. Additionally, while the Bills of Exchange Act does not list the lapse of time as a reason to revoke the authority to pay a cheque, a common banking custom exists regarding "stale" cheques—those presented an unreasonable time after their date. While some banks consider a cheque stale after six months and others after twelve, this practice finds indirect support in section 74 (2) of the Bills of Exchange Act.

## Collection of Crossed Cheques and Immunity
Under section 82, a banker is granted qualified immunity when collecting crossed cheques for a customer. As clarified by the House of Lords in *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903), this protection requires the banker to meet specific conditions:
* The banker must receive the cheque and the money from a customer with whom they have existing and definite business relations.
* The cheque must already be specially or generally crossed to the banker; a crossing made by the banker under section 77 is inefficacious.
* The banker must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care for the true owner of the funds. To avoid this, the banker must ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct and verify authority for endorsements *per procuration*. The banker must not take a cheque for a private account if the holder is an agent, an official, or holding it for partnership purposes, nor take an "account payee" cheque for any account other than the one indicated. Furthermore, the banker must act strictly as an agent or "conduit pipe"; if the banker treats the cheque as their own—such as by crediting cheques as cash in bank books before the money is actually received—they are viewed as the owner or transferee and lose the protection of section 82.

## Trust Accounts and Fiduciary Responsibilities
While bankers are theoretically supposed to refuse accounts known to be trust accounts, many are opened with headings indicating fiduciary capacity. In these cases, the banker's primary obligation is to honor the customer's cheque. The banker is not entitled or bound to question the object of the cheque unless there is clear evidence of impending fraud (*Gray v. Johnston*). A banker's personal benefit from a transaction does not impeach it unless the banker was privy to the fraud, such as when a banker presses for the reduction of a private overdraft using a cheque from a trust account (*Coleman v. Bucks & Oxon Union Bank*).

When dealing with known trust moneys, the safest course for a banker is to insist on the authority of the entire body of trustees. Additionally, under the Public Trustee Act 1906, Joint Stock Banks may be appointed as custodian trustees, a role that involves receiving and making payments and the custody of securities on behalf of an estate, while managing trustees provide active direction.

## Banking Customs and Business Restrictions
Bankers may end restrictions arising from their course of business, provided they give reasonable notice to the customer and provide for outstanding liabilities the customer undertook based on the previous state of affairs (*Buckingham v. London & Midland Bank*).

Bankers may also rely on general and reasonable banking customs. Anyone entering business relations with a banker is presumed to agree to conduct business according to such customs. This includes the right of a banker to summarily debit a customer's account with a returned cheque, even if the customer did not indorse it and the banker was the transferee.
