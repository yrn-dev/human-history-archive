# The Imports and Exports Recorded in Statistical Returns

Statistical returns of imports and exports are the primary records used by nations to track foreign trade. However, these records often vary from the actual facts of international purchases and sales, making the deduction of conclusions regarding the excess of imports or exports a task of enormous statistical difficulty.

## Discrepancies in Statistical Records
The published returns of imports and exports are estimated values rather than actual values of goods paid for. Several factors contribute to the divergence between these records and actual trade:
*   **Payment Failures:** A recorded sale may prove abortive if the debtor fails to pay.
*   **Timing:** Purchases made in a single year may not be paid for by the sales of that same year, meaning the "squaring" of the account can take a significant amount of time.
*   **Non-Trade Movements:** Statistical returns may include movements of goods that are not international sales or purchases. For example, the United Kingdom exports ships, stores, and ammunition, or railway materials for foreign construction, which may be set off by loans rather than purchases. Similarly, goods may be remitted to pay interest or repay capital on previous loans.
*   **Financial Offsets:** Sales may be set off by loans, while imports may include the repayment of capital or remittances of interest.

## Valuation Methods and International Comparison
Comparisons between the trade statistics of different countries are difficult due to a lack of uniformity in valuation methods. A primary example is the difference between the United Kingdom and the United States:
*   **United Kingdom:** Imports are valued at the port of arrival, and exports are valued at the port of departure.
*   **United States:** Imports are valued at the port of shipment rather than the port of arrival, excluding freight and other charges.

Because these two great countries use different points of valuation, their balance of trade results differ, making them difficult to compare.

## The Balance of Trade and "Invisible" Exports
In popular speech and politics, the "balance of trade" is often identified as the excess of exports over imports (favourable) or imports over exports (unfavourable). Under the older "mercantile theory," this balance was identified with the sum of precious metals received. While Adam Smith demonstrated that gold and silver are merely commodities, the popular notion persists that a nation with an excess of imports is "living on its capital."

However, statistical returns of imports and exports are incomplete because they omit "invisible" transactions. These include:
*   The earning of commissions and freights.
*   Services such as repairs performed for others.

The United Kingdom serves as a conspicuous example of this discrepancy. Between the middle and end of the 19th century, its annual excess of imports over exports rose to 180 million sterling. In 1906, the recorded excess was 147 million sterling. This is attributed to the country's large lending abroad in former times and its magnitude of services as a commission merchant and shipowner. Furthermore, British foreign investments are estimated to be no less than 2,000 million sterling, providing an enormous annual income that helps balance the recorded trade deficit.

## National Trade Examples
### The United States and New Countries
In the first decade of the 20th century, the United States was characterized as a borrower with a large excess of exports, though signs of change were appearing. New countries, including Australia, Canada, and South American nations, generally resemble the United States in this regard.

### Bolivia
The foreign trade of Bolivia is considered comparatively unimportant, and its statistical returns are unsatisfactory and incomplete. In 1904, imports aggregated L1,734,551 (consisting of clothing, wines, spirits, machinery, hardware, provisions, live-stock, woollens, and cottons), while exports were L1,851,758. 

In 1903, the principal exports included:
*   Tin and its ores: L1,039,298
*   Silver and its ores: L636,743
*   Rubber: L260,559
*   Copper ores: L157,609
*   Coca: L28,907
*   Other minerals: L20,948
*   Bismuth: L16,354
*   Cinchona: L9,197

These figures do not accurately represent total trade because Bolivian imports and exports passing through Mollendo, Arica, and Antofagasta are largely credited to Peru and Chile. Bolivian exports are limited to pastoral, forest, and mineral products due to tariffs from neighboring countries and transportation costs.

### Specific Commodity Records (Great Britain)
Records for specific goods provide further insight into trade patterns. In 1901, the United Kingdom imported 3,702,810 cwt. of butter, valued at L19,297,005. Regarding hops, approximately 30% of the total available for consumption is imported, with the largest proportion coming from the United States. Between 1877 and 1906, annual average imports of hops ranged from 186,362 to 215,219 cwt., while annual average exports of home-grown hops ranged from 9,437 to 14,808 cwt.
