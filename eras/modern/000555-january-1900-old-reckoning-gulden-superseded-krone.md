# Transition from the Gulden to the Krone

The transition from the gulden to the krone was a process of monetary reform in Austria-Hungary, characterized by a shift toward a new coinage system and the regulation of gold and silver standards.

## Early Monetary Developments
In 1867, Austria-Hungary participated in a monetary conference that resulted in the formation of the Latin Union. While the empire intended to join the Union, it did not ultimately do so. However, it took an initial step toward this goal by issuing gold coins valued at four and eight gulden. During this period, there was no regulation regarding the relationship between these gold coins and the "Austrian" silver coinage; consequently, the two issues remained unconnected. Unless it was specifically agreed that a payment be made in gold, all payments were conducted in silver.

By 1879, the free coinage of silver was suspended due to the continued depreciation of the metal.

## Introduction of the Krone
In 1892, both parliaments passed laws introducing a completely new coinage based on agreements made by ministers. The new unit of currency was the krone, which was divided into 100 heller. The krone was designed to be nearly equal in value to the franc (specifically 24-25ths). In terms of weight, a twenty-krone gold piece weighed 6.775 grams, compared to 6.453 grams for a twenty-franc piece.

The value of the gold krone was set at .42 of the gold gulden. To account for the depreciation of silver, the gold krone was declared equal to .5 of the silver gulden.

## Implementation and Circulation
The practical implementation of the new coinage began with the issuance of nickel coins and silver one-krone pieces, the latter of which circulated as half a gulden. During this transition, the silver gulden was the only silver coin left in circulation, while all other silver and copper coins were recalled. The issuance of the eight- and four-gulden gold coins was suspended.

Further progress was delayed until the supply of gold increased. The bank was mandated to buy gold—purchasing over forty million gulden in 1892—and was required to coin any gold brought to it into ten- or twenty-krone pieces.

## Funding and Redemption of State Notes
To facilitate the new system, a loan of 150 million gulden was secured at 4% interest. Rothschild undertook this loan and paid in gold, consisting primarily of sovereigns and bar gold. From this gold, coins of the new issue were struck with a total value exceeding 34 million kronen. 

These new coins were not immediately put into general circulation; instead, they were first utilized to pay off *Staatsnoten*. By 1894, the state successfully redeemed 200 million gulden of these notes, including all those valued at one gulden.
