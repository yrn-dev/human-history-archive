# Spanish Influence and Populations in the Americas

## Demographic Composition in Bolivia
The white population of Bolivia, numbering 231,088, is largely descended from early Spanish adventurers who entered the country in pursuit of mineral wealth. This group has been supplemented by a small number of Spanish Americans arriving from neighboring republics, as well as some Portuguese Americans from Brazil. While Europeans of various nationalities have settled in the country as traders or miners, there has been no direct immigration from Europe. Consequently, the white population—which comprises only one-eighth of the total—does not increase at the rate seen in Brazil or Argentina.

The *mestizos* in Bolivia number 486,018. While they are less numerous than the Indians, they outnumber the white population by more than two to one. Although they have been characterized as ignorant, apathetic, cruel, intemperate, and intensely superstitious, some individual strong characters have emerged from this group. Given a white population that is wanting in energy and a decreasing Indian population, the future of Bolivia is considered to depend mainly on the *mestizos*.

## Population and Language in Costa Rica
In 1904, official returns recorded the total population of Costa Rica at 331,340, reflecting an increase of more than one-fourth over the preceding decade. The principal language is Spanish, though it includes many Indian words and various dialect modifications. The majority of inhabitants claim descent from Spanish colonists—primarily Galicians—who arrived during the 16th century and subsequent centuries.

Costa Rica possesses a higher percentage of Spanish blood than other Central American republics, though intermarriage with native Indians has created a large population of half-castes, known as *mestizos* or *ladinos*. Resident foreigners in 1904 numbered fewer than 8,000 and consisted mostly of British subjects, Germans, Italians, and Spaniards. The native Indian population is estimated at 4,000; while some were civilized or exterminated, others, such as the Guatusos or Prazos in the Talamanca country and along the Nicaraguan border, remain in a state of complete savagery. Additionally, the Mosquito Indians visit the Atlantic coast every summer for turtle fishing. The black population is remarkably small, totaling some 1,200, as only 200 negroes were settled there when slavery was abolished in 1824.

## Colonial and Regional Intermixture
In the West Indies, the populations are diverse and largely composed of imported African negroes—who make up four-fifths of the population in Jamaica—along with Chinese and imported East Indian coolies. The aboriginal races, including Caribs and American Indians of the mainland, experienced intermixture with Portuguese and Spanish blood, resulting in many mixed types.

Historically, the Papacy divided the American continent between Portugal and Spain. This division provoked hostility from other nations, including the Hollanders, the French, and the seamen of England, who disregarded Spanish and Portuguese claims to establish their own settlements in the West Indies and North America.

## Spanish Influence on Language and Currency
The influence of Spanish extends to the English language through the voyages of English navigators in the late 16th century. This period introduced American words in Spanish forms and direct Spanish words, including *cargo*, *alligator*, *tobacco*, *potato*, *negro*, and *galleon*.

In terms of currency, the Spanish piece-of-eight (*reals*) was commonly referred to as a dollar. These coins were current in the Spanish-American colonies and were used extensively in the British North American colonies. During a period of coin scarcity in 1797, the Bank of England put several millions of these Spanish coins into circulation after countermarking them with the initials G.R. in a shield or a small oval bust of George III.

## Notable Individuals and Administration
Spanish influence is evident in the lives of various figures. Andres Bello (1781–1865), a South American scholar and poet born in Caracas, Venezuela, wrote the *Gramatica castellana* (1847), which became a leading authority on the subject. James Gordon Bennett, founder of the *New York Herald*, worked briefly as a translator of Spanish for the *Courier* of Charleston, South Carolina, in 1823. In the arts, the American composer Edward Alexander MacDowell received part of his early musical education under Spanish-American teachers.

Administratively, Spanish domination existed in certain regions during specific periods, such as from 1762 (or 1769) to 1803. Governors during this era included Antonio de Ulloa, Alejandro O'Reilly, Luis de Unzaga, Bernardo de Galvez, Estevan Miró, the Baron de Carondelet, and others such as M. Gayoso de Lemos, Francisco Bouligny, José M. Vidal, the Marquis de Casa Calvo, and Juan M. de Salcedo.
