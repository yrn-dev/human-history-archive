# The Sack of Panama (1671)

The capture of Panama in 1671 was a significant military operation carried out by buccaneers, who were officially titled "privateers." This expedition marked a turning point in the history of these marauders, serving as the conclusion of the first epoch of their history, which began with their rise and was characterized by a lack of government restriction or aid.

## Planning and Execution
The operation was facilitated by Thomas Modyford, the governor of Jamaica (1620–1679), who maintained a close alliance with the buccaneers. Modyford granted commissions to Henry Morgan and other leaders to launch a major attack on the Isthmus of Panama. This location was strategically vital as it served as the route for transporting bullion from South American mines to Porto Bello, where it was subsequently shipped to Spain.

The buccaneering force numbered 2,000 men. Their campaign began with the seizure of Chagres, followed by a march toward Panama in 1671. The journey was described as difficult, requiring the men to travel on foot and in canoes. After this arduous trek, the force reached the shores of the South Sea and came into view of the city.

## The Fall of the City
On the morning of the tenth day of their approach, the buccaneers commenced an engagement with the city's defenders. The conflict ended in a rout of the defenders, and the town was taken. During the capture, the city was burnt, whether accidentally or intentionally. The subsequent sack of Panama was characterized by great barbarities.

Despite the victory, the primary objective of the raid—the treasure—was not secured, as the Spaniards had removed it before the city fell.

## Aftermath and Consequences
The failure to secure significant treasure led to disputes among the victors. Henry Morgan was accused of defrauding his followers during the division of the booty. The share received per man was small, and many of the buccaneers died of starvation during their attempt to return to Jamaica.

The political fallout was severe for the leadership. Governor Modyford was recalled, and in 1672, Morgan was called back to England and imprisoned in the Tower. Although Morgan was permitted to return to Jamaica in 1674 as lieutenant-governor alongside Lord Vaughan, he remained highly unpopular. He was threatened in the streets by the relatives of those who had perished during the 1671 expedition. In his later years, Morgan shifted his activities toward suppressing the buccaneers, who had developed inconvenient claims against him.

## Subsequent Expeditions in the Region
The 1671 event preceded a second epoch of buccaneering (1671–1685), during which their operations expanded from the Caribbean to the entire Pacific range from Chile to California. 

In 1680, another group of over 300 well-armed marauders landed at Darien. Unlike Morgan's earlier expedition, these adventurers received eager assistance from the native Indians, who sought revenge for the cruelty and mismanagement of Spanish policy. The natives served as guides during a nine-day difficult journey, provided food and canoes, and assisted the invaders until they reached the fort of Santa Maria and embarked on a river leading to the South Sea. Under the command of John Coxon, this group entered the Bay of Panama and captured four Spanish ships. While Coxon and seventy men returned home, others under the leadership of Sharp, Watling, and Sawkins continued to ravage the coast of Peru.
