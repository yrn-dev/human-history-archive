# The Slav Races in Central and Southeastern Europe

The growth and political assertion of the Slav races in the 19th century were driven by a combination of demographic, economic, and political factors. While some government assistance existed, the expansion of these populations began long before the administration of Count Taaffe. This growth was evident in election results and census data; for example, Prague shifted from being a German city to one where, by 1880, Czechs numbered 120,000 compared to 36,000 Germans. Similarly, in Pilsen, Germans held a majority in 1861 but constituted less than a quarter of the population by 1880.

## Factors of Growth and National Identity
The increase in Slav influence was not due to German laxity, as many in the generation demanding national rights had been educated in German schools. Instead, several factors contributed to this shift:
* **Economic and Demographic Causes:** There was a greater population increase among the Czechs and a significant migration from rural areas to towns.
* **Intellectual Movements:** A romantic and nationalist movement emerged around 1830.
* **Political and Educational Access:** The simultaneous establishment of parliamentary government and popular education allowed these races to reassert their national individuality once they received political liberty.

## Political Status and Conflict in Austria
Within the Austrian empire, the Slav races—including the Czechs, Poles, Slovenes, Serbs, and Croatians—often found themselves in positions of political inferiority. Under the principle of dualism, the empire was effectively divided between the rule of the Magyars and the Germans. While the constitution of 1867 recognized the equal rights of all races and the "inviolable right" to preserve their own language and nationality, the practical application depended on the government's definition of "customary" (*landesueblich*) languages.

The Liberal German party, which later became the National German party, advocated for centralization. They viewed the German language as the only means of preserving the monarchy's unity and the sole medium for the army, civil service, and parliament. They regarded the German schoolmaster as an "apostle of German culture" and hoped that a common Austrian nationality would eventually reduce Slavonic idioms to mere local dialects of the peasantry.

## Regional Variations and National Aspirations
The Slav populations exhibited different levels of organization and distinct political goals:
* **Czechs and Poles:** These groups possessed strong historical traditions and organization.
* **Southern Slavs:** The Slovenes, who formed a majority in Carniola and minorities in Carinthia and southern Styria, sought the use of their language in education and government. Their political ideal was an "Illyrian" kingdom encompassing Croatia and other southern Slavs.
* **Serbs:** Though sharing a race and language with Croats, Serbs were separated by their adherence to the Orthodox Church. This religious tie attracted them to Russia and fueled hopes for a great Servian kingdom, which often conflicted with loyalty to Austria.

## Migration and Settlement in Macedonia and Dalmatia
In Macedonia, the rural population is predominantly Slavonic. Slavs first crossed the Danube in the 3rd century, with major immigrations occurring in the 6th and 7th centuries. During these migrations, they drove Greeks to the Aegean shores, Albanians into the Mirdite country, and the latinized population into highland districts like Olympus, Agrapha, and Pindus. While they often failed to capture fortified towns, which remained centers of Hellenism, they assimilated many latinized races in central regions. By the end of the 7th century, the eastern and central Slavs were conquered by the Bulgarians, an Ugro-Finnish horde; however, the Bulgarians were eventually absorbed by the more numerous Slavs.

In Dalmatia, the Morlachs (or Morlaks) constitute 96% of the population and belong to the Serbo-Croatian branch of the Slavonic race. The term "Morlachs" is an abbreviation of *Mavrovlachi* ("Black Vlachs" or "Sea Vlachs"), originally referring to Latinized inhabitants of central Illyria who were nomadic shepherds. By the 14th century, they migrated into maritime Croatia and northern Dalmatia, eventually adopting the language and manners of the Slavs.

## Education and the Role of the Church
Education became a primary point of contention. The law of 1867 required elementary education in Slav districts to be in Slovenian or Czech. Slavs further demanded their own schools even in towns where they were a minority, a request granted by Minister Prazak. The movement of Slav populations into manufacturing districts and cities like Vienna further spread their languages, challenging the German view of German as the sole *Kultur-sprache*.

The Roman Catholic Church also influenced these dynamics. Because the Slav races provided more candidates for ordination than the Germans, the Church's influence tended to favor them. This led to a "Los von Rom" movement among some Germans in Styria and other territories, who left the Church for Protestantism or Old Catholicism in protest against the alliance between the Clerical party and the Slav parties.
