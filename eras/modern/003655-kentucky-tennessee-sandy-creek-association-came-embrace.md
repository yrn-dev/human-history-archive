# Kentucky and Tennessee

## Geography and Boundaries of Kentucky
Kentucky is a South Central state of the United States of America, situated between 36° 30´ and 39° 6´ N. and 82° and 89° 38´ W. The state covers an area of 40,598 square miles, of which 417 square miles are water surface, including the entire breadth of the Ohio river. Kentucky is bounded to the north, northwest, and northeast by Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois. Its eastern border is defined by Virginia and the Big Sandy river and its eastern fork, the Tug, which separates the state from West Virginia. To the southeast and south, it is bounded by Virginia and Tennessee, while the Mississippi river separates it from Missouri to the west.

The physiography of the state is characterized by a northwestern slope from the eastern mountain heights across two dissected plateaus. The eastern quarter of the state is known as the region of the "mountains," which is part of the Appalachian Mountain Province. This area includes the Cumberland and Pine ridges, with crests between 2,000 and 3,000 feet, and the Big Black Mountain, which reaches 4,000 feet. The highest point in the state is The Double on the Virginia state line in eastern Harlan county, exceeding 4,100 feet. Much of this eastern region properly belongs to the Alleghany Plateau Province, featuring rugged terrain with steep-sided narrow valleys.

The remainder of the state, lying east of the Tennessee river, consists of a lowland basin known as the Blue Grass Region and the Highland Rim Plateau. The Highland Rim Plateau encompasses about half of the state and is noted for cavernous regions and circular depressions called sink holes. Kentucky is well known for its caves, specifically Colossal Cavern and Mammoth Cave, which were cut into limestone beds.

## Early Settlement and History of Kentucky
The settlement of Kentucky was the first movement in the development of the United States west of the Alleghany Mountains following the close of the Seven Years' War in 1763. At that time, the region was claimed by Virginia (via a 1609 charter), the Six Nations (Iroquois), and the Cherokees. The area was sparsely inhabited, primarily by a small tribe of Shawnees in the north and a few Chickasaws near the Mississippi river.

Early exploration included a 1750 mission by Dr. Thomas Walker through the Cumberland Gap and a 1751 exploration by Christopher Gist from the mouth of the Scioto river. In 1752, an Indian trader named John Finley descended the Ohio river to the site of Louisville; his descriptions later attracted Daniel Boone, who first visited in 1767. James Harrod established the first permanent English settlement at Harrodsburg in 1774. In October of that year, Ohio Indians signed a treaty surrendering claims south of the Ohio river following their defeat by Virginia troops at the battle of Point Pleasant.

## Boundary Disputes and State Lines
The boundary between Kentucky and Tennessee was the subject of several surveys and treaties. In 1779-1780, commissioners from North Carolina and Virginia surveyed the southern boundary to the Tennessee river. Although intended to follow the parallel of latitude 36° 30´, the line was mistakenly run north of that parallel. Following a treaty in 1819 that extinguished Indian title to the territory west of the Tennessee, a new line was run along the 36° 30´ parallel from the Mississippi to the Tennessee. In 1820, commissioners from both Kentucky and Tennessee formally adopted the 1779-1780 line and the 1819 line as the official boundary.

## Civil War and Military Activity
During the American Civil War, Kentucky experienced internal division. In January 1861, Governor Magoffin proposed a convention to determine the future of inter-state and Federal relations and refused to provide troops to subdue other Southern states. While a bare majority (48 to 47) initially called for neutrality, the state formally declared allegiance to the Union in September 1861 after Confederate invasions. However, a "sovereignty convention" of Kentucky men who had joined the Confederate army met at Russellville on November 18, 1861, passing an ordinance of secession and electing G. W. Johnson as governor.

Military operations in the region included the investment of Fort Donelson in Tennessee. This entrenched Confederate camp, designed to guard the lower Cumberland river, was taken by Federal forces on February 16, 1862. Brigadier-General U. S. Grant led the Union army overland to the fort, while Commodore A. H. Foote's gunboat flotilla ascended the Cumberland. The Confederate commander in Kentucky, Albert Sidney Johnston, had placed a large garrison under General Floyd at Donelson.

Other military figures active in the region included Alexander McDowell McCook, who commanded a brigade in Kentucky in the winter of 1861 and the 1st Corps in Kentucky in October 1862.

## Religious Expansion and the Sandy Creek Association
The "Separate" Baptist movement saw phenomenal growth in Virginia and the Carolinas between 1760 and 1770, led by evangelists such as John Waller and Samuel Harris. Shubael Stearns and his brother-in-law Daniel Marshall settled at Sandy Creek, North Carolina, where Stearns established a church with over six hundred members. From this center, "Separate" Baptist influence spread through North and South Carolina, crossed into Georgia (where Marshall formed a church at Kiokee), and permeated Virginia, eventually extending into Tennessee and Kentucky.

The Sandy Creek Association, under Stearns' leadership, grew to embrace churches across several colonies. Stearns strongly resisted the formation of other associations to maintain harmony. However, the association's policy of "unfellowshipping" ministers and churches that acted independently led to a division, resulting in the creation of the Congaree Association of South Carolina and the General Association of Virginia.
