# British Control and Imperial Administration

British control during the era of the British Empire was characterized by a diverse array of political arrangements, ranging from direct official rule and military administration to alliances with self-governing states. This system was centered on the "crowned republic" of the United British Kingdoms—comprising England, Wales, Scotland, and Ireland (the latter included against the will of a considerable part of its people). The supreme government of the entire empire was the ministry, whose policy and headship were determined by the British Parliament. This ministry held the powers of peace and war over all imperial territories.

## Administrative Structures of the Empire

The British Empire utilized several distinct tiers of political organization. Below the central British States were the "crowned republics" of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and Newfoundland. Newfoundland is noted as the oldest British possession, dating to 1583. These nations were practically independent and self-governing states in alliance with Great Britain, though each maintained a representative of the Crown appointed by the government in office.

In contrast, the Indian Empire functioned as an extension of the empire of the Great Mogul. This territory reached from Burmah to Baluchistan and included Aden. In this region, the India Office (under Parliamentary control) and the British Crown assumed the role previously held by the original Turkoman dynasty. Other territories, such as Egypt, existed under a different arrangement; while nominally part of the Turkish Empire and retaining its own monarch (the Khedive), Egypt was subject to almost despotic British official rule.

## Control in Baluchistan and Afghanistan

British control in the frontier regions of Asia was often established through treaties and military campaigns. In Baluchistan, the region is divided between "British Baluchistan" and other areas that, while possessing various degrees of independence, remain subject to British control. British Baluchistan officially consists of the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts were originally Afghan but were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879. Within the Baluchistan Agency, which extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea, supreme control is practically exercised by the agent to the governor-general of India and his staff of political assistants.

Strategic passes were central to maintaining this control:

*   **The Bolan Pass:** This important pass connects Sibi and Jacobabad with Quetta. Following the 1879 treaty of Gandamak, the Bolan route was brought directly under British control. It was chosen for the first alignment of the Sind-Pishin railway. While the railway originally followed the Bolan stream, floods forced a realignment through the Mashkaf valley to a junction at Mach. To secure the pass from Baluch marauders (primarily Marris), the Quetta agency was founded in 1877.
*   **The Khyber Pass:** Adopted as the main road to Kabul, the Khyber was the site of various British military actions, including the capture of the Ali Masjid fortress by Colonel Wade in 1839 and General Pollock in 1842. The treaty of May 1879 placed the Khyber tribes under British control. For 18 years starting in 1879, Colonel R. Warburton controlled the pass, employing paid subsidies for jezailchis from the Afridi tribe to ensure safety. However, in 1897, a wave of fanaticism led the Afridis to attack the pass, resulting in its temporary loss and the subsequent Tirah Expedition.

## Naval Control and International Cooperation

In the context of global conflict, British naval predominance led to a system where allied activities were often controlled by British forces. During cooperation with the United States, a major part of American naval activities were controlled by the British, who carried a significant portion of the "naval load" of the war. This coordination often resulted in practical consolidation, though American forces sought to preserve their own administration and organization.

Specifically, cooperation with the British was typically effected by operating as units under British control. This differed from cooperation with the French, which allowed United States naval forces a larger measure of initiative. British-controlled convoys provided information regarding submarine activities and routes to the Brest headquarters to help adjust coastal routes.

Furthermore, British naval strategy emphasized the control of "confluences"—narrow channels or defiles where trade necessarily converges—rather than attempting to protect vast ocean spaces. By employing older cruisers in adequate force to create sanctuaries or catchments, the British sought to make these terminals too dangerous for enemy commerce destroyers to approach.
