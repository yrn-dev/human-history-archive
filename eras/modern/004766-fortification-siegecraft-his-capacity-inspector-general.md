# FORTIFICATION AND SIEGECRAFT

## Definitions and Types of Fortifications
Fortification encompasses a variety of structures designed for defense, ranging from prehistoric earthworks to modern military installations. The castle, derived from the Latin *castellum* (a fort) and *castra* (a camp), is defined as a small self-contained fortress. While most commonly associated with the Middle Ages, the term is also applied to prehistoric sites such as Maiden Castle and Hollingbury Castle, as well as citadels like those found in Burgos and Badajoz. In modern contexts, the term may refer to small detached *forts d'arret*. Beyond its military function, the castle often served as a residence for noblemen or princes; in France, the term *chateau* persists as a relic of the feudal age to describe country seats.

## Evolution of Engineering Principles
The development of fortification has been marked by shifts in school of thought and the influence of specific engineers. Early modern plans often followed the ideas of Vauban. For example, the French military engineer Louis de Cormontaingne (c. 1697–1752) modeled his ideas closely on Vauban. Cormontaingne developed his own system of fortification, which he used to build Forts Bellecroix and Moselle at Metz. His work, including the *Architecture militaire* (1714) and *Memorial de l'attaque des places* (published 1803), remained largely private or secret for years before being published for the general public between 1806 and 1809.

In the 19th century, Henri Alexis Brialmont (1821–1903), a Belgian general and military engineer, initially followed Vauban's ideas, utilizing a bastioned trace for the enceinte and forts in his original plans for Antwerp. However, by 1859, Brialmont transitioned to the school of polygonal fortification and the ideas of Montalembert.

## Modern Fortification and the Impact of Artillery
The introduction of long-range guns and the events of 1870-71 led to a period of confusion regarding fortification ideas. Brialmont addressed these challenges by developing final principles that were applied to the fortifications on the Meuse at Namur and Liége, as well as the extreme detached forts of the Antwerp region. His principles emphasized:
* The multiplication of intermediate batteries.
* The suppression of all artillery positions that were open to overhead fire.
* The lavish use of armour to protect artillery located inside the forts.

Brialmont documented these advancements in several works, including *La Fortification du temps présent* (1885) and *Influence du tir plongeant et des obus-torpilles sur la fortification* (1888).

## Siege Warfare and Field Operations
Siege warfare is distinguished from general field operations. In the field, fire direction for rifles and machine-guns is based on the "effective" beaten zone, which contains 75% of the bullets. For example, the Maxim Gun has an effective zone depth of 150 yards at 500 yards, decreasing to 50 yards at 2,000 yards. The S.L.E. Rifle has an effective zone of 220 yards at 500 yards, decreasing to 100 yards at 1,500 yards.

Historical siege operations often required specialized engineering duties. Sir John Fox Burgoyne (1782-1871) performed such duties during the Peninsular War, including the construction of the lines of Torres Vedras in 1810 and the blowing up of Fort Concepcion on the river Turones. He was involved in the unsuccessful siege of Badajoz in 1811, though he later performed "skilful" engineer duties at the sieges of Badajoz and Ciudad Rodrigo in 1812. Later in his career, during the Crimean War, Burgoyne served as the principal engineer adviser to the English commander during the initial phase of the siege of Sevastopol.

## Administrative Oversight of Defences
The management of fortifications and military lines often falls under the purview of an inspector-general. In Belgium, Henri Alexis Brialmont served as the inspector-general of fortifications and of the corps of engineers, drafting and executing the entire scheme for the nation's defences. Similarly, Sir John Fox Burgoyne was appointed inspector-general of fortifications in 1845.

In other military contexts, the "Inspectorate" may oversee lines of communication. This role involves managing the transport of reserve troops and matériel to the army, while simultaneously removing wounded soldiers, prisoners, and worn-out material. This administrative burden includes supervising railway traffic, telegraphs, ammunition supply, and road and hydraulic construction.
