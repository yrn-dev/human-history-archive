# Support and Participation in the Coup d'état of Brumaire

The *coup d'état* of 18-19 Brumaire (November 9-10, 1799) was a pivotal event that resulted in the overthrow of the French Directory and the establishment of the consulship. Various political and military figures played distinct roles in facilitating Napoleon Bonaparte's rise to power, ranging from active diplomatic overtures and strategic complicity to open military support.

## Political Facilitation and Overtures
In the period leading up to the *coup d'état*, certain individuals provided essential political groundwork. One figure assisted Napoleon by making overtures to Moreau and Sieyes, though his contributions beyond these efforts were limited. Following the event, this individual declined a position in the ministry, opting instead to serve in the *Corps Législatif* and the council of state, where he provided useful advice regarding public opinion.

Other political figures were involved through their positions within the existing government. Barras, one of the five Directors controlling the executive of the French republic, had a long history with Bonaparte, having previously nominated him as a subaltern during the events of 13 Vendemiaire in 1795 and facilitating his marriage to Josephine de Beauharnais. By the time of the Brumaire coup, Barras recognized the necessity for a change in government and acted as an accomplice to Bonaparte's designs, although he did not fully grasp the extent of Bonaparte's ambition. Following the coup, Barras was sidelined by the three Consuls who replaced the five Directors.

## Military and Administrative Support
The success of the *coup d'état* was supported by various military officers. General Pierre Antoine Dupont de l'Etang warmly supported Napoleon during the events of 18 Brumaire, a gesture that later provided him with further professional opportunities, including serving as chief of staff to Berthier during the 1800 campaign. Similarly, Auguste Frédéric Louis Viesse de Marmont, the Duke of Ragusa—who had been an intimate of Bonaparte since his time in Dijon and served as his aide-de-camp—was present during the *coup d'état*. Marmont subsequently organized the artillery for the expedition to Italy.

## Reactions and Aftermath
The transition of power was not universally accepted. Within the Council of Five Hundred, Joseph Marie Dessaix, Count and general, was among the few members who opposed the *coup d'état* of 18 Brumaire.

Conversely, the new regime provided liberty or advancement to those who had previously been marginalized or exiled. François, Marquis de Barbe-Marbois, who had been arrested and transported to French Guiana during the *coup d'état* of 18 Fructidor in 1797, owed his eventual liberty to Napoleon after 18 Brumaire. Similarly, the Marquis de François Barthelemy, who had also been deported to French Guiana following the 18 Fructidor coup, returned to France after 18 Brumaire and entered the senate in February 1800. Pierre Roger Ducos, a former judge of the peace and member of the executive Directory, accepted the *coup d'état* and served as one of the three provisional consuls before becoming vice-president of the senate.

Other figures returned to public life following the event. Carnot, who had been forced into exile during the 18 Fructidor coup of 1797, returned to France after 18 Brumaire and was re-elected to the Institute in 1800, later serving as minister of war.
