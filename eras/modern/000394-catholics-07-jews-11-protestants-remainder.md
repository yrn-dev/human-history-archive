# Catholics

The term Catholics refers to adherents of the Catholic faith, appearing in historical records across various geographic regions and time periods. The archive's sources document their presence in Europe, the Middle East, and their interactions with other religious groups.

## Demographic Distribution in Europe

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Roman Catholics constituted significant portions of the populations in several European regions:

*   **Budapest:** In 1900, the total population of Budapest (civil and military) included 445,023 Roman Catholics and 5,806 Greek Catholics.
*   **Baden:** As of 1905, approximately 60% of the population of Baden were Roman Catholics.
*   **Belgium:** The population of Belgium is described as being almost entirely Roman Catholic. While the state provides absolute liberty of conscience and has no state religion, the government voted nearly 7,000,000 francs in 1904 to support the benevolent institutions and religious establishments of the Roman Church. The hierarchy in Belgium includes the archbishop of Malines and the bishops of Namur, Tournai, Bruges, Ghent, and Liege.
*   **Bukovina:** In this Austrian duchy and crownland, 11% of the population belonged to the Roman Catholic Church.
*   **Basel:** In 1900, the half-canton of Basel Stadt had 37,101 Romanists (a figure that includes Old Catholics), while the half-canton of Basel Landschaft had 15,564 Romanists.

## Historical and Social Interactions

### Conflict and Coexistence
Historical records indicate periods of both intense conflict and unity involving Catholics. In Ireland, the Reformation created a religious incompatibility between the Catholic remainder of the island and the Protestant community formed by Presbyterian Scotch colonists in Ulster. 

Conversely, during a period of national crisis in Germany, barriers of creed were reported to have broken down. In one instance, a joint service for all creeds was conducted by a Catholic priest, bringing together Catholics, Protestants, and Jews under a shared identity as Germans.

In the Roman Empire, historical accounts describe a period of "tumult" where the church was pillaged and the sanctuary polluted by a crowd of Barbarians and Jews, leading the Monothelite Pyrrhus to flee from the "zeal of the Catholics."

### Chronological Perspectives
Catholics, along with Protestants, have historically preferred a chronological period of approximately 4,000 years from the creation of the world to the birth of Christ. This preference is attributed to the authority of the Hebrew text and the vulgate, contrasting with earlier calculations by the Greek church and the primitive church of Antioch, which estimated the period at 5,500 to 6,000 years based on the Septuagint.
