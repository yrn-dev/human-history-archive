# Virginia

Virginia is a state in the U.S.A. containing several significant industrial and educational centers, including the cities of Bristol, Lynchburg, and Danville.

## Bristol
Bristol is a city situated across the boundary of Washington county, Virginia, and Sullivan county, Tennessee. The Tennessee-Virginia boundary line runs through the principal street, dividing the city into two separate corporations. The Virginia portion was known as Goodson before it was chartered as a city in 1890; it remains administratively independent of its county. Settled around 1835, the city is located 130 miles northeast of Knoxville, Tennessee, at an altitude of approximately 1700 feet.

The population of Bristol has grown steadily, from 3,209 in 1880 to 13,395 in 1910. Of the 1910 population, 6,247 residents lived in Virginia and 7,148 lived in Tennessee. Bristol serves as an important railway center, served by the Southern, the Holston Valley, the Norfolk & Western, and the Virginia & South-Western railways. 

The city is positioned near great mineral deposits in Tennessee, Virginia, Kentucky, North Carolina, and West Virginia, making it a key distributing point for coke, coal, and iron. Its industrial base includes iron furnaces, packing houses, furniture factories, tobacco factories, lumber mills, and tanneries. Educational institutions in the Virginia section include the Baptist Virginia Institute for Women (est. 1884) and the Methodist Episcopal, South Sullins College for women (est. 1870).

## Lynchburg
Located on the James river approximately 125 miles west by south of Richmond, Lynchburg is a city in Campbell county. Its population grew from 18,891 in 1900 (including 8,254 negroes) to 29,494 in 1910. The city is served by the Norfolk & Western, the Chesapeake & Ohio, and the Southern railways.

Lynchburg is one of the state's principal manufacturing cities, utilizing the water power provided by the James river. While the neighborhood produces granite, iron ore, coal, and superior quality tobacco, the boot and shoe industry—established in 1900—is the most important. By 1905, Lynchburg was one of the largest distributors of these articles in the country and the largest southern manufacturer. The value of factory products rose from $2,993,551 in 1900 to $4,905,435 in 1905.

The city features the 140-foot high and 1100-foot long Rivermont Viaduct, public parks, well-equipped hospitals, and a public library. It is the see of a Protestant Episcopal bishop. Educational institutions include the Virginia collegiate and industrial school for negroes, the co-educational Virginia Christian college (est. 1903), and Randolph-Macon Woman's college (opened 1893), which is conducted under the auspices of the Methodist Episcopal Church, South.

## Danville
Danville is a city in Pittsylvania county located on the Dan river, about 140 miles southwest of Richmond by rail. Settled around 1770 and incorporated as a town in 1792, it became a city in 1833 and is politically independent of Pittsylvania county. Its population was 10,305 in 1880, 16,520 in 1900 (including 6,515 negroes), and 19,020 in 1910.

The city is served by the Southern railway's main line, with branches to Norfolk and Richmond, and the Danville & Western railway, which connects to Stuart, Virginia. Danville is a major wholesale market and jobbing center for northern North Carolina and southern Virginia; it is likely the largest loose-leaf tobacco market in the country, selling roughly 40,000,000 lb. annually.

Industrial activity is supported by the water power of the Dan river. In 1905, Danville ranked first among Virginia cities in the value of cotton goods produced. Other industries include flour and knitting mills, as well as overall, furniture, and tobacco factories. The industrial suburb of Schoolfield housed a large textile mill in 1908. The city operates its own electric and gas lighting plants and a water-supply system with a filtration plant installed in 1904.

Historically, Danville was the site where the archives of the Confederacy were carried after the evacuation of Richmond on April 2, 1865, and where President Jefferson Davis paused during his flight southward.
