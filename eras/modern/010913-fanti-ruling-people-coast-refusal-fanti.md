# Fanti

The Fanti are a people of the Gold Coast who historically served as the ruling population on the coast. They are believed to be of the same race as the Ashanti and are thought to speak the same language. According to tradition, the separation of the Fanti and the Ashanti was caused by a famine; the Fanti moved south and lived on herbs (*fan*), while the Ashanti remained and subsisted on Indian corn (*san*), which gave rise to their respective names.

## Conflict with the Ashanti Empire

The relationship between the Fanti and the Ashanti was characterized by repeated invasions and struggles for sovereignty. In 1807, two chiefs of the Assin, who had been defeated by the Ashanti king Osai Tutu Quamina, sought refuge among the Fanti. When the Fanti refused to deliver these fugitives to the king, Osai Tutu Quamina invaded their country, defeated them, and drove them toward the sea.

This campaign reached the coast near Anamabo, where a British fort was located. Despite the governor's exhortations to come to terms and offers of mediation, the townsmen resolved to continue the contest. The result was the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants and the destruction of the town. While the Ashanti lost over 2,000 men and failed to storm the English fort, the conflict ended in a truce. Colonel G. Torrane, the governor of Cape Coast, eventually surrendered one of the fugitive Assin chiefs (who was executed) and concluded an agreement acknowledging the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland. Under this agreement, Torrane delivered half of the fugitives in Anamabo fort to the Ashanti, while most of the remaining fugitives were sold as slaves by Torrane and his council. Additionally, the governor agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

## British Intervention and Sovereignty

Throughout the early 19th century, the British were intermittently involved in the disputes between the Fanti and the Ashanti. In 1817, a British mission to Kumasi was sent following repeated Ashanti invasions of Fantiland. During these negotiations, the Ashanti king expressed rage over the payment of quit rents for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort, as the Fanti had induced the British to pay these rents to them, leaving only a nominal sum for the Ashanti. A treaty was eventually concluded that satisfied the king's demands and recognized the British right to control the natives in the coast towns.

However, tensions persisted. In 1820, a treaty was drawn up by consul Joseph Dupuis that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of the Ashanti. This treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and supported by the government in London. Sir Charles M'Carthy, the first crown-appointed governor, espoused the Fanti cause but was defeated by the Ashanti on January 21, 1824, at Essamako. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; his skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi.

## Decline of Ashanti Influence and Later Wars

The power of the Ashanti over the coast tribes began to wane after they suffered a significant defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826. By 1831, the Ashanti king was forced to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, paying 600 oz. of gold and sending his son as a hostage to Cape Coast. Following this, the payment of ground rent for British forts ceased.

Despite this decline, the Ashanti attempted to reassert authority over the Fanti in the Gold Coast protectorate in 1852. In 1863, further conflict arose when the British refused the Ashanti king Kwaka Dua I's demand for the surrender of a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief. Although the Ashanti were victorious in two battles, no further fighting followed immediately.

Following the death of Kwaka Dua I in 1867 and a period of civil war, Kofi Karikari succeeded him. Upon being enstooled, Kofi Karikari declared that his business would be war, leading to preparations to attack the Fanti tribes. This culminated in the war of 1873-74. On January 22, 1873, Ashanti forces crossed the Prah and invaded the British protectorate, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon near Cape Coast. This prompted the British government to dispatch Sir Garnet Wolseley as administrator to defend the territory.
