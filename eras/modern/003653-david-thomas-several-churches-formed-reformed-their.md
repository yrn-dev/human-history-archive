# David Thomas and Associated Baptist Churches

## Missionary Work in the Southern Colonies
David Thomas was among a group of missionaries from the churches of the Philadelphia Association who worked to strengthen the Baptist cause in North Carolina and Virginia. He served alongside other missionaries, including Samuel Eaton, John Gano, Benjamin Miller, John Garrard, Benjamin Griffith, and John Thomas. Through the influence of these individuals, several churches were either formed or reformed.

## The Ketockton Association
The churches that were formed or reformed under the influence of David Thomas and his fellow missionaries eventually united with the Philadelphia Association. In 1776, this specific group of churches formed the Ketockton Association.

## Religious Climate and the "Separate" Baptists
During this period, the colonial government of Virginia had previously been cruelly intolerant, though it eventually provided a limited degree of toleration to Baptists of the type associated with the Ketockton group. However, a different group known as the "Separate" Baptists remained unwilling to obtain licenses for their meetings. This resistance stemmed from their enthusiasm and their perspective on the evils of state control regarding religious matters, which led to sharp conflicts with the authorities.

## Expansion of Baptist Influence
While David Thomas and the Philadelphia Association missionaries focused on their specific regions, other "New Light" evangelists who became Baptists, such as Shubael Stearns and Daniel Marshall, contributed significantly to the multiplication of Baptist churches across the southern colonies. 

Stearns, along with his brother-in-law Marshall and approximately twelve other believers, established a settlement at Sandy Creek, North Carolina. Within a few years, they developed a church with a membership exceeding six hundred people. Marshall further expanded this influence by organizing and ministering to a church at Abbott's Creek, located about 30 miles away, and eventually forming a church at Kiokee, Georgia.

From the center in North Carolina, the influence of the "Separate" Baptists permeated Virginia and extended into Tennessee and Kentucky. The Sandy Creek Association grew to include churches across several colonies. Stearns, seeking to maintain the harmonious operation of the churches under his leadership, strongly resisted any proposals to form additional associations.
