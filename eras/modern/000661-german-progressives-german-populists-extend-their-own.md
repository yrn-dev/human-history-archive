# German Progressives and German Populists

In the political landscape of the Austrian empire, the German Progressives and German Populists were distinct factions categorized under the broader umbrella of German Liberals. These groups played significant roles in parliamentary dynamics, particularly regarding the extension of popular rights and the protection of racial interests.

## Political Affiliations and Composition
The German Liberals were divided into several subgroups. The German Populists held a more substantial presence in parliament with 29 seats, while the Progressives held 15. Other related groups included Pan-German radicals (the Wolf group) with 13 seats and unattached Pan-Germans with 3 seats. Additionally, there were 2 representatives listed simply as "Progressives."

These groups operated alongside other German factions, such as the Christian Socialists (the Lueger party), who held 96 seats, and the German Agrarians, who held 19.

## Stance on Popular Rights and Reform
The German Progressives and German Populists differed sharply in their approach to the extension of popular rights. Most German Liberals were antagonistic toward such measures, with some expressing bitter opposition. This antagonism was particularly strong among the Constitutional Landed Proprietors, who had led the German element in the country and parliament since the 1860s. These proprietors, specifically the German nominees of the *curia* of large landed proprietors, fought tenaciously to maintain their privileges, fearing that reform would doom their influence and transfer German leadership to the Christian Socialists.

In contrast, the German Populists did not oppose the extension of popular rights. Their position was based on the belief that a German "Liberal" party could not logically oppose such an expansion.

## Cooperation and Racial Interests
Despite internal differences, the German parties were capable of unification when faced with external political pressure. Due to the aggressiveness of the Czechs, the German Populists and German Progressives joined forces with the Christian Socialists and the Constitutional Landed Proprietors. Together, these parties established a supreme committee consisting of four members and a joint executive committee. The primary purpose of this coalition was to monitor and safeguard German racial interests.

## Parliamentary Context
The activities of these parties occurred during a period of significant parliamentary instability. Under Dr. von Koerber, the parliament struggled to vote on budgets for 1903, 1904, and 1905, leading the government to sanction estimates by imperial ordinance under paragraph 14 of the constitution. 

Tensions were further exacerbated by disputes in Bohemia. While the Czechs demanded that their language be placed on an equal footing with German for all official purposes, the Germans refused to recognize Czech as a language of internal service. This deadlock, combined with Czech "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction—including one sitting that lasted 57 hours—contributed to the environment in which the German parties sought collective action.
