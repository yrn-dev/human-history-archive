# The Treaty of 1841 and Subsequent British-Kalat Relations

## The Treaty of 1841 and its Annulment
In 1841, Major Outram concluded an offensive and defensive treaty between the British government and Nasir Khan II, who served as the chief of Kalat. This agreement remained the primary diplomatic framework until the mid-19th century. Following the 1843 conquest of Sind by British troops under General Sir Charles Napier, there was a period of no notable diplomatic intercourse between the British and the Baluch states that lasted until 1854.

In 1854, under the governor-generalship of the Marquess of Dalhousie, General John Jacob—the commandant and political superintendent on the Sind frontier—was tasked with arranging a new treaty. This new agreement, executed on May 14, 1854, formally annulled the former offensive and defensive treaty of 1841.

## Terms of the 1854 Agreement
The 1854 treaty established a relationship of subordinate co-operation. Under its terms, Nasir Khan II and his successors were bound to oppose all enemies of the British government to the utmost. Furthermore, the chief of Kalat was prohibited from entering into negotiations with any other states without the consent of the British government.

The treaty also addressed security and territorial control. It stipulated that British troops could be stationed in any part of the Kalat territory in positions deemed advisable by British authorities. Additionally, the Baluch chief was required to prevent his subjects from engaging in plundering within or near British territory.

## Renewal and Policy Shifts in 1876
The 1854 treaty was later renewed in 1876 by Lord Lytton, acting on the advice of Sandeman. While this renewal maintained the offensive and defensive alliance terms, it fundamentally altered the previous policy of non-intervention. The new arrangement recognized the sirdars in addition to the khan and appointed the British government as the referee for disputes between them.

As part of this renewed relationship, the khan received an annual subsidy of a lakh of rupees, an increase from the previously assigned Rs. 50,000. The British government also expanded its physical presence in the khan's country by locating troops there, founding Quetta, constructing roads, and projecting the development of railways and telegraphs to establish law and order.

## Final Consolidation and Incorporation
The administrative and territorial consolidation of the region continued through the late 19th century. In the spring of 1879, the treaty of Gandamak resolved the nebulous claims made by Afghanistan regarding Pishin and Sibi. In 1883, Sandeman led an expedition to Kharan, which, along with the reconciliation of the great Naushirwani chief, Azad Khan, with the khan of Kalat, finalized the existing form of Kalat administration.

The process concluded on November 1, 1887, when a resolution incorporated British Baluchistan into British India. The region was divided into two districts: Thai Chotiali and Quetta-Pishin, both of which were administered by a regular staff and a deputy-commissioner.
