# Filaments in Schizomycetes

In the study of Schizomycetes (bacteria), filaments are specialized aggregates of cells. They consist of elongated cylindrical cells that remain united end to end following the process of division. These structures are not always uniform in diameter throughout their length, and their segmentation can vary considerably. Depending on their orientation and attachment, filaments may be free or attached at a "basal" end.

## Classification and Morphology
Filaments are categorized based on their structural characteristics. A distinction is made between simple filaments, such as *Leptothrix*, and those that exhibit false branching, such as *Cladothrix*. In the order Trichobacterinae, the vegetative body consists of cell-filaments that may be branched or unbranched; the segments of these filaments separate as swarm-cells, known as gonidia. Within the family Trichobacteriaceae, some filaments are rigid, non-motile, and sheathed (e.g., *Crenothrix* and *Thiothrix*), while others, such as *Beggiatoa*, lack a distinct sheath and exhibit creeping and slow pendulous movements.

The physical dimensions of these forms can be extreme. While some unicellular Schizomycetes are less than 1 micron in diameter, the length of the rows of cylindrical cells in filamentous forms may be many hundred times greater than their breadth. Additionally, elongated forms may be straight, or they may be curved, sinuous, or twisted into a corkscrew-like spiral. When the sinuosity is slight, it is termed a *Vibrio* form; when the spiral winding is well marked, they are known as *Spirochaete* or *Spirillum*.

## Growth and Division
The formation of a filament occurs when the immediate results of active nutrition—the elongation of a cell and its division into two equal halves via a median septum—do not result in immediate separation. If the daughter cells remain connected, the resulting filament continues these processes of uniform elongation and subsequent division as long as conditions are maintained.

In an ordinary bacterium, growth consists of the uniform elongation of the rodlet until its length doubles, followed by division by a median septum. In filamentous forms, such as *B. ramosus*, this process repeats. If the cells divide in only one direction (transverse to the long axis), they produce aggregates of a long cylindrical shape.

## Factors Affecting Filamentous Growth
The growth of filaments is highly sensitive to environmental conditions. Observations of rodlets in nutrient gelatine at constant temperatures have revealed several facts regarding their growth curves:
* **Doubling Periods:** As long as conditions remain constant, the time taken for any portion of the filament to double its length remains constant, as each cell is equally active.
* **Temperature:** There are specific minimum, maximum, and optimum temperatures at which growth begins, runs at its best, or is exhausted. While a slow rise in temperature may accelerate growth, any sudden alteration typically causes a check.
* **Growth Inhibitors:** Growth can be checked or brought to a standstill by the exhaustion or dilution of food-media, exposure to light, or the presence of metabolic products and traces of poisons.
* **Cellular Injury:** The death or injury of a single cell within the filamentous series lengthens the doubling period because the potential progeny of that cell are removed. Such destruction can be caused by the attacks of other organisms.

## Fragmentation and Life Cycle
Filaments may not remain intact indefinitely. They may break up into elements such as rodlets or bacilli. In some filamentous forms, "fragmentation" into multicellular pieces of nearly equal length is a normal phenomenon. Each resulting partial filament then repeats the cycle of growth, division, and fragmentation.

Rapid division can produce hundreds of thousands of cells within a few hours. These may form deposits, cloud the cultivation fluid, or form films on the surface. When dense "swarms" of these vegetative cells become fixed in a matrix of their own swollen contiguous cell-walls, they enter a resting state known as zoogloea.
