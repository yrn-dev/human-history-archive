# Franz Xaver von Baader

Franz Xaver von Baader (1765–1841) was a German theologian and philosopher recognized as one of the most significant speculative theologians of modern Catholicism. Born on March 27, 1765, in Munich, he was the third son of F. P. Baader, who served as the court physician to the elector of Bavaria. Baader's early education included the study of medicine at Vienna and Ingolstadt, and he briefly worked in his father's medical practice.

## Professional Career and Education
Finding medicine uncongenial, Baader transitioned to the field of mining engineering. He studied under Abraham Gottlob Werner at Freiberg and traveled through various mining districts in northern Germany. Between 1792 and 1796, he resided in England, where he encountered the ideas of Godwin, Hartley, and Hume—concepts he found extremely distasteful. During this period, he became acquainted with the works of Jakob Boehme, whose mystical speculations, along with those of Saint Martin and Meister Eckhart, aligned with Baader's own mode of thought.

Upon returning to Germany in 1796, Baader established a friendship with F. H. Jacobi in Hamburg. He also became acquainted with the works of Schelling, and while his early publications show the influence of that philosopher, Baader was not a disciple of Schelling. Their friendship ended around 1822 after Baader wrote a letter to Emperor Alexander I of Russia denouncing modern philosophy, which alienated Schelling.

Baader continued his engineering profession for many years, earning a prize of 12,000 gulden (approximately L1000) for developing a method to use Glauber's salts instead of potash in glassmaking. He served as the superintendent of mines from 1817 to 1820 and was raised to the nobility for his services. After retiring in 1820, he turned toward academia and published *Fermenta Cognitionis* (1822–1825), a work that recommended the study of Boehme and combated modern philosophy. In 1826, he was appointed professor of speculative theology and philosophy at the newly opened university in Munich.

## Philosophical and Theological Thought
Baader's philosophy is described as essentially a theosophy. He maintained that human reason alone cannot reach its intended end and argued that the presuppositions of tradition, church, and faith cannot be discarded. His approach is characterized as Scholasticism, as he believed that philosophy and theology are not opposing sciences; rather, reason should be used to clarify truths provided by revelation and authority. He also drew close to the mysticism of Boehme, Paracelsus, and Eckhart.

Central to Baader's thought is the concept that human existence depends on being cognized by God (*cogitor ergo cogito et sum*). He posited that all self-consciousness is simultaneously God-consciousness, and that knowledge is not mere *scientia* but *con-scientia*—a participation in or consciousness of God.

Baader conceived of God not as an abstract Being (*substantia*), but as an everlasting activity or process (*actus*). This self-generation of God consists of two aspects: the exoteric (emanent) and the esoteric (immanent). He argued that God has reality only as absolute spirit, and spirit is only possible when the primitive will becomes conscious of itself. This cognition of self involves a distinction between the knower and the known, which provides the power to become spirit. This immanent process of self-consciousness is mirrored through the impersonal wisdom or idea of God.

## Later Life and Influence
In 1838, Baader opposed the Roman Catholic Church's interference in civil matters. As a result, he was interdicted from lecturing on the philosophy of religion during the final three years of his life. He died on May 23, 1841.

Baader's influence extended beyond the Catholic Church to figures such as Hans L. Markensen, Julius Mueller, and R. Rothe. He also played a role in the career of Karl Christian Friedrich Krause, who obtained a position in Munich through Baader's influence.

## Works and Documentation
Baader did not produce systematic works; instead, his doctrines are found in journals, extensive correspondence, short detached essays, and comments on the writings of Saint Martin and Boehme. He often expressed his deepest thoughts through mystical symbols, analogies, or obscure aphorisms. 

His collected works, *Baader's saemmtliche Werke*, were published in 16 volumes between 1851 and 1860 by adherents including Schlueter, von Osten-Sacken, Lutterbeck, E. v. Schaden, J. Hamberger, and F. Hoffman. Notable individual works include *Spekulative Dogmatik* (1827–1836) and *Fermenta Cognitionis*.
