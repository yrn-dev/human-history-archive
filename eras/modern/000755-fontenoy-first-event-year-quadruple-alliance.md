# Fontenoy

The name Fontenoy is associated with several distinct historical events and contexts within the archive, ranging from early medieval conflicts to 18th-century warfare and academic lectures.

## The Battle of Fontenoy (841)
In the 9th century, Fontenoy was the site of a significant conflict involving the sons of Emperor Louis I. Louis, known as the "German" and king of the East Franks, entered into an alliance with his half-brother, King Charles the Bald. Together, they faced their brother Lothair, who had claimed the entirety of the Empire. On June 25, 841, Louis and Charles defeated Lothair at Fontenoy. 

This victory contributed to a series of negotiations that began in June 842 on an island in the Sâone, where the three brothers and their representatives arranged the boundaries of their respective kingdoms. These efforts culminated in the treaty of Verdun in August 843. Under this treaty, Louis received the majority of the Carolingian empire's lands east of the Rhine, including Saxony, Franconia, Thuringia, and Bavaria, with Regensburg serving as the center of his government.

## The Battle of Fontenoy (1745)
During the war of the Austrian Succession, Fontenoy was the site of a major engagement in the Flanders campaign. On May 10, 1745, the forces of Louis XV and Saxe, who had been besieging Tournay, inflicted a great defeat upon the relieving army led by the duke of Cumberland.

This victory was part of a broader series of successes for the French in the Netherlands, driven by the genius of Marshal Saxe. Following Fontenoy, the French continued to advance, eventually driving the Austrians and Dutch back toward the Meuse in 1746 and winning the battle of Roucoux on October 11 of that year. Further French victories occurred at Lauffeld on July 2, 1747, and the storming of Bergen-op-Zoom on September 18, 1747, followed by the surrender of Maestricht on May 7, 1748.

Despite the victory at Fontenoy and other successes such as Raucoux, the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748 brought no gains to France. This diplomatic failure led to criticism of the king and preceded a "Diplomatic Revolution" before the Seven Years' War, during which France ended its alliance with Frederick the Great to join its former enemy, Austria.
