# The Principle of Maintaining an Intervening Width of Neutral Territory

The principle of maintaining an intervening width of neutral territory is a geopolitical strategy used to create a physical buffer between two sovereign powers. This approach is designed to prevent direct contact between boundaries and establish a zone of independence or neutrality to mitigate conflict.

## Application in Afghanistan and British India
A primary example of this principle is found in the demarcation of the eastern borders of Afghanistan and the boundaries of British India. To separate these two entities, a broad width of independent territory was established. This independent belt includes Bajour, Swat, and a portion of the Mohmand territory located north of the Kabul river. 

The boundary in this region is defined by several geographical markers. In the north-east, the Pamir extension of Afghan territory reaches nearly 75 deg. E., following the water-divide to the head of the Taghdumbash Pamir and the water-parting of the Hindu Kush. The line leaves the Hindu Kush near the Dorah Pass at the head of a minor Chitral affluent, subsequently dividing Chitral and Bajour from Kafiristan. It further separates sections of the Mohmands who fall under the respective sovereignties of the British and the Afghans, eventually crossing the Peshawar-Kabul route at Lundi-Khana.

This principle of maintaining neutral intervening width is consistently applied throughout the eastern borders of Afghanistan, extending to the point where it meets the northern limits of Baluchistan on the Gomal river. Beyond the Gomal, Baluchistan itself serves as the intervening state between Afghanistan and British India, with the dividing line between Baluchistan and Afghanistan marked with the same precision used in the northern sections.

## Neutrality in European Diplomacy
In Europe, the concept of neutrality has been applied to the state of Belgium to ensure regional stability and the interests of larger powers. The first King of the Belgians pursued a policy of permanent neutrality as mandated by the Treaty of London. This status was viewed by some, including the English government, as being in the general interest of Europe. 

Disraeli expressed a fundamental English policy that the region from the coasts of Dunkirk to the North Sea islands should be held by free, prosperous states practicing the arts of peace, specifically to ensure these areas did not belong to a great military power. To protect this neutrality, the English government proposed cooperation between English forces and any belligerent party against another should Belgian neutrality be violated. This was formalized in treaties dated August 9 and 11, 1870, during the eve of the Franco-German War, though the Treaty of London (1839) remained the primary protective framework until the violation of Belgian neutrality by Germany on August 3, 1914.

## Legal and Diplomatic Implications of Neutrality
The maintenance of neutral territory carries specific implications under international law. There is a division of opinion among authorities regarding the duties of contracting powers when a belligerent violates a neutral territory. Some maintain that these powers have not only the right but the duty to interfere *ex officio* to protect the neutral state using military power, even if the neutral state has not provided its consent.

In other historical contexts, neutral states have served as mediators. In ancient Greece, the various republics—which were often separated by diverse interests and distance—used the mediation of neutral states in alliance with them to resolve their differences. This occurred during the solemnization of games and festivals, which promoted harmony and friendship among the Greek states, helping them unite as a formidable nation against common enemies and Barbarians.
