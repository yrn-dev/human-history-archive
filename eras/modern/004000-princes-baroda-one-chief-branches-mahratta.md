# The Princes of Baroda

The princes of Baroda constituted one of the chief branches of the Mahratta confederacy, a power that spread terror and devastation across India during the 18th century. Baroda is recognized as one of the three most important Mahratta principalities, alongside Gwalior and Indore.

## Origins and Early History
The state was established around 1721 when Pilaji Gaekwar carved a fertile portion of territory out of Gujarat. Following this acquisition, the peshwa granted Pilaji Gaekwar the title of "Leader of the Royal Troops." While the Mahrattas generally rose in the southwest against Islam to restore Brahminism as the ruling religion, the Mahratta power eventually fragmented into several principalities, including Baroda, Indore, and Gwalior.

## British Relations and Administration
During the final thirty-two years of the 18th century, the house of Baroda was weakened by bitter family feuds. By 1800, the inheritance passed to a prince described as almost idiotic in mind and feeble in body. To protect this hereditary ruler from other claimants, British troops were deployed. A treaty signed in 1802 secured the prince's dependence on the British government and his independence from the peshwa.

By 1805, these arrangements were consolidated into a systematic administrative plan. Under this system, the prince maintained a revenue of three-quarters of a million sterling and remained perfectly independent in internal matters, though he was practically kept on the throne by subsidiary British troops.

## Governance and Internal Conflict
The history of the Gaekwars was characterized by a pattern of occasional able ministers or princes interspersed with long periods of incompetent leadership, venal advisers, and oppressive taskmasters. These tensions culminated in a fierce family feud. Malhar Rao succeeded to the throne after being imprisoned for a long period by his brother, the former gaekwar, Khande Rao.

In 1873, an English committee of inquiry was appointed to investigate complaints of oppression against Malhar Rao, though no real reform followed. In 1874, the gaekwar was formally accused and tried by a mixed commission for attempting to poison the British resident. Although the trial in 1875 failed to produce a unanimous verdict on the poisoning charge, the viceroy, Lord Northbrook, deposed Malhar Rao due to gross misgovernment. Subsequently, the widow of Khande Rao was permitted to adopt an heir from the descendants of the founder.

## Geography and Infrastructure
The native state of Baroda maintains direct relations with the government of India and is situated within the limits of the Bombay Presidency. The city of Baroda is located on the river Viswamitri and is served by the Bombay & Baroda railway, positioned 245 miles north of Bombay by rail. As of 1901, the city had a population of 103,790.

The city has undergone significant transformation through the widening of streets, the creation of parks, and the construction of handsome public buildings. Its water supply is provided by the Ajwa lake. The city's infrastructure includes a college, numerous schools, and several hospitals, including the Sayaji Rao, Jamnabai (the widow of Khande Rao), and Countess of Dufferin hospitals. Additionally, the city contains cantonments garrisoned by a native infantry regiment, which remain under British jurisdiction and house 4,000 people.
