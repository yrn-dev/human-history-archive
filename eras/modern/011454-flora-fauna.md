# FLORA AND FAUNA

The distribution and characteristics of plant and animal life across the globe are heavily influenced by geological formations, climatic conditions, and geographical boundaries. From the prehistoric eras of the Mesozoic to the diverse ecosystems of the modern world, flora and fauna vary significantly by region and altitude.

## Prehistoric and Reptilian Life
In the Mesozoic era, fauna and flora existed in a "hothouse" environment that endured no frosts. This era was characterized by a real dry land fauna and flora, distinguishing it from the mud and swamp environments of the previous heyday of life on earth. Reptiles of this period were limited by a requirement for warmth year-round and could not withstand exposure to cold. In the modern world, the genera of reptiles are comparatively few and limited in distribution, consisting of lizards, snakes, alligators, crocodiles, and the Chelonia (turtles and tortoises). These are more various than the surviving members of the amphibia order, which ruled the world during the Carboniferous period.

## Regional Distributions in Asia and Europe
The distribution of life forms in Asia is closely dependent on climatic conditions. The flora of northern Asia is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, though they differ in species rather than genera; notably, heaths and oak are absent east of the Ural. In the mountains of this region, the principal trees are birch, larch, and pine, while poplars, alders, and willow are found on lower ground. The pine reaches its northern limit in Siberia at approximately 70 deg. N. 

A flora essentially European in character extends along the warm temperate zone from the Himalaya to the Mediterranean. While many European species reach the central Himalaya, they are few in the eastern parts. Conversely, Chinese and Japanese forms are most numerous in the eastern Himalaya and less frequent in the west.

In the Balearic Isles, the fauna and flora resemble those of the Mediterranean coasts of France or Spain, supported by a generally healthy and mild climate.

## Mountainous and High-Altitude Ecosystems
The Carpathians and the Alps act as protective walls, creating milder climates to their south. The vegetation in the Carpathians is divided into four distinct zones based on altitude:
* **Beech:** Extends to 4000 ft.
* **Scottish fir:** Extends to 5000 ft.
* **Pine:** Grows above the fir but becomes dwarfed and disappears at approximately 6000 ft.
* **Lichen and Moss:** Found above 6000 ft. on almost bare rock.

The highest parts of the Transylvanian Mountains and the High Tatra possess a flora similar to the middle region of the Alps. Transylvania also features a remarkable sea-shore flora found in marshes, ponds, and salt-impregnated lakes. The fauna of the Carpathians includes birds of prey, lynxes, wolves, and numerous bears. Its mollusc fauna is particularly characteristic, containing many species found only in the Alpine region and not in neighboring areas. Additionally, the slopes are used for pasturing sheep and cattle in great numbers.

## Arctic and North American Flora and Fauna
In Greenland, the land mammals are decidedly more American than European. Species that arrived from America include the reindeer, the dog, the Eskimo, the white polar wolf (which recently invaded the east coast via the northern part of the country), the banded lemming (*Cuniculus torquatus*), and the musk-ox. Other mammals, such as the Arctic hare, the polar fox, the stoat (*Mustela erminea*), and the polar bear, are circumpolar forms.

Marine life in Greenland also shows American tendencies. The bladder-nose seal (*Cystophora cristata*) is considered a Greenland-American species, whereas the Scandinavian grey seal (*Halichoerus grypus*) is very rare in both America and Greenland. Among the sixty-one breeding bird species, four are American, eight are European-Asiatic, and the remainder are North Pacific, North Atlantic, or circumpolar. 

The flora of Greenland consists of about 310 species of vascular plants. These include fifteen endemic species, forty-four European-Asiatic species, and about forty American species, with the remainder common to Asia, Europe, and America.

## Central American and African Distributions
In Central America, countries such as Honduras and Costa Rica do not possess marked peculiarities in plant and animal life that differentiate them from their neighbors. Honduras's fauna and flora are influenced by two seasons: a dry season from November to May and a wet season from May to November.

In Africa, the Zambezi river serves as a definite boundary line for the distribution of many species of flora and fauna. Because of this, Barotseland is essentially categorized as belonging to Central Africa rather than South Africa. This geographical barrier has also prevented the spread of lung sickness and tick fever—disastrous cattle diseases—from South Africa into Barotseland.
