# Bacteria: Research and Authorities

The study of bacteria encompasses a wide range of scientific inquiries, including their physical structure, biological functions, classification, and their interactions with other organisms. Due to the extensive nature of bacteriological literature, research is often categorized by specific anatomical or environmental focuses.

## General Authorities and Literature
A significant body of work exists regarding the general structure and functions of bacteria. Key texts include Fischer's *The Structure and Functions of Bacteria* (published in Oxford in 1900, with a German edition in 1903) and Migula's *System der Bakterien* (1897). Other foundational works include Lafar's *Technical Mycology* (1898) and Mace's *Traite pratique de bakteriologie* (1904). 

In the field of pathogenic bacteria, the most important work is cited as *Handbuch der pathogenen Mikroorganismen* by Kolle and Wassermann (1904), which continues to be updated with supplements. Other notable texts on the subject include Abbott's *Principles of Bacteriology* (1905), Crookshank's *Bacteriology and Infective Diseases* (1896), and McFarland's *Text-book upon the Pathogenic Bacteria* (1906). For those studying the history of the field, Loeffler's *Vorlesungen ueber die geschichtliche Entwickelung der Lehre von der Bacterien* (1887) provides a historical overview.

## Cellular Structure and Morphology
Research into the physical makeup of bacteria is divided into several specialized areas:

*   **Cell Membrane and Cilia:** Studies on the construction of bacteria and Cyanophyceen have been conducted by Butschli (1896) and Fischer (1897). Rowland provided observations on bacterial structure in 1899. Research regarding cilia is primarily attributed to the works of Fischer and Migula.
*   **The Nucleus:** The nucleus of bacteria and its division have been examined by Vejdovsky, who published findings in *Cent. f. Bakt.* (1904, 1905). Other contributors to the study of the bacterial nucleus include Wager, Migula, Fischer, and Mencl.
*   **Spores:** The biology of *B. ramosus* has been documented by Marshall Ward (1895), while Sturgis researched a soil bacillus of the type of de Bary's *B. megatherium*. Additional research on spores was conducted by L. Klein in 1889.

## Environmental and Fossil Bacteria
Bacteriological research extends to various environments and time periods. Renault conducted research on fossil bacteria (*Bacteriacees fossiles*) in 1896. In aquatic environments, Frankland and Marshall Ward produced reports on the bacteriology of water, including specific studies on the bacteria found in the river Thames.

## Interactions with Plants
Bacteria exhibit specific behaviors when interacting with plant life, particularly leguminous roots. Observations show bacteria invading the epidermis of pea roots via an "infection thread" (zoogloea) that pushes through cell walls. Bacteria have also been observed in a thin stream pushing up into the root-hairs of peas.

In the case of the lupin, bacteria form root nodules. Longitudinal sections of these nodules reveal bacterial tissue (w) and fibro-vascular bundles (g). Within these bacterial tissues, cells contain a nucleus and protoplasm filled with bacteria. Research identifies both normal, undegenerate forms of bacteria in lupin nodules and "bacteroids" found in *Lupinus albus* and *Vicia villosa*.

## Classification and Methodology
The classification of Schizomycetes has been addressed by Marshall Ward, who examined the characters or marks employed for such classification in 1892. For those seeking further references, the *Centralbl. fuer Bakter. u. Parasitenk.* and the *Index Medicus* serve as primary consultation sources. Additionally, the New Sydenham Society published *Microparasites in Disease: Selected Essays* in 1886, which contains translations of classical researches from German and French.
