# Parliamentary and Political Dynamics in Austria and Britain

## The Party of Centralization in Austria
In the Austrian political landscape, the Liberal German party—later becoming the National German party—championed the cause of centralization. This party was supported by a small number of Ruthenes and Italians. Their primary objective was the creation of a common Austrian nationality through the establishment of a common parliament and the proliferation of German schools to spread the German language.

The National German party viewed any grant of self-government to various territories as a threat that would diminish German influence and restrict the use of the German language. In regions such as Bohemia, they believed full self-government would likely result in Germans becoming the subject race. This was considered intolerable, particularly as the national power of non-Austrian Germans was increasing and Germans were becoming the first race in Europe. Consequently, they refused to resign the ruling position they had held for three hundred years.

## Ideology and Cultural Goals
The proponents of centralization argued that German ascendancy was the only method to preserve the monarchy's unity. They maintained that German was the sole language capable of facilitating communication between different races, and therefore it had to be the language of the parliament, the civil service, and the army. 

They emphasized Austria's historic mission to bring German culture to the "half-civilized races of the east." To achieve this, they demanded that universities and higher schools remain German and that elementary schools be Germanized wherever possible. The German schoolmaster was viewed as an "apostle of German culture." The ultimate goal was for a common Austrian nationality to obscure the national feelings of the Slavs, reducing Slavonic idioms to local peasant dialects and transforming territories into provinces of a centralized state. Despite these ambitions, the total German population constituted less than one-third of the whole, requiring great political ability to maintain rule, especially since the party was divided by opposition from the Feudal party and the clergy.

## The Ausgleich and Parliamentary Challenges
Financial and economic reforms between Austria and Hungary were managed through a general settlement every ten years known as the "Ausgleich" (compact or compromise). This process included the determination of the Quota and involved a balance of advantages; for instance, a gain in a financial agreement might be offset by a concession in commercial policy. In 1877, Austria granted Hungary a share in bank management, while Austria benefited from arrangements regarding bonuses on exported sugar. Conversely, disputes arose over whether Hungary was responsible for a debt of 80 million gulden to the bank and the fact that increased duties on petroleum and coffee fell more heavily on Austria.

The legislative process for these acts was fraught with difficulty, as they had to be passed by two parliaments characterized by extreme race hatred and national jealousy. The first settlement took two years, from 1876 to June 1878, involving negotiations between two ministries, committees in both parliaments, and subsequent debates.

## Parliamentary Instability and Government Collapse
Parliamentary government in Austria was deemed impossible unless the party supported by the government in internal matters also supported it in foreign affairs and commercial policy regarding Hungary. The constitutional parties failed to provide this unity. During 1877 discussions on the economic arrangement with Hungary, many members voted against essential duties on petroleum and coffee. Additionally, 112 members led by Herbst voted against certain provisions of the treaty of Berlin and refused necessary supplies for the occupation of Bosnia in the Delegation.

This lack of unity and the failure to support a ministry drawn from their own ranks ruined the government. As a result, Austrian ministers and Andrassy sought assistance from the Poles, who subsequently became a government party. In early 1870, Auersperg resigned, and while constitutionalists remained in power, Count Taaffe became the most important member of the reconstructed cabinet as minister of the interior, with Stremayr serving as president.

## British Parliamentary Developments
In Britain, a new prime minister took office coinciding with the end of the South African War and the coronation of Edward VII. He enjoyed a large parliamentary majority and cordial relations with Mr. Chamberlain. Initial legislative priorities included the Irish Land Purchase Act (via Mr. Wyndham), which used the British exchequer to help Irish tenants buy out landlords, and the extension of the new Education Act to London.

While the budget initially showed a surplus, it became a source of political dissension. Mr. Ritchie's remission of the shilling import-duty on corn prompted Mr. Chamberlain to lead a crusade for colonial preference and tariff reform, creating a rift within the Unionist ranks.
