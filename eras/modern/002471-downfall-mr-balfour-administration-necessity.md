# The Downfall of Mr. Balfour's Administration

The administration of Mr. Balfour ended in late November 1905, when he tendered his resignation as Prime Minister to the King. This collapse was the culmination of internal party strife and a loss of public confidence, leading to the formation of a government under Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman.

## Causes of the Administration's Collapse
The downfall of the Unionist regime was driven by several intersecting factors. Internally, the administration was wrecked by dissensions over the "fiscal question." A significant struggle emerged within the Unionist party between those advocating for Tariff Reform and those adhering to free trade. Mr. Chamberlain led the campaign for a reorganized budget that included taxes on imports, including food, intended to stimulate employment and improve the position of the working classes. This was opposed by the "strait free-traders," including the Duke of Devonshire, Lord Goschen, Lord James, and Lord Hugh Cecil, who formed a Free Food league.

Mr. Balfour's position was complicated by his "dialectical dexterity" in avoiding a definite expression of his own fiscal opinions. While he did not fully support Mr. Chamberlain's scheme—specifically regarding the taxation of food—he did not discourage Chamberlain's campaign. This ambiguity led to contemptuous criticism in the Liberal press. The tension peaked in September, when the resignation of strait free-traders Lord George Hamilton, Mr. Ritchie, Lord Balfour of Burleigh, and Mr. Arthur Elliot was followed by the resignation of Mr. Chamberlain.

Beyond fiscal disputes, the Unionist ministers were perceived as having become "stale." The government faced public disgust over the retention of heavy taxation from the war years, particularly among income-tax payers. Additionally, the administration struggled with new issues regarding labour questions, the Education Act, and the 1904 introduction of Chinese labour into South Africa, all of which were used effectively against the government in various constituencies.

## Achievements and Leadership
Despite the eventual collapse of his regime, Mr. Balfour's tenure was marked by significant successes in foreign affairs, leaving the country in a position of international strength not seen since the Crimean War. His leadership was characterized by a statesmanlike grasp of imperial problems, oratorical ability, and intellectual finesse.

Among his most important domestic reforms were the 1904 institution of the new Army Council and the creation of the permanent Committee of Imperial Defence. These reforms resulted from the report of a "triumvirate" appointed in November 1903, consisting of Sir George Clarke, Sir John Fisher, and Lord Esher.

## Transition to Opposition
The electoral defeat that followed the administration's fall indicated a weakening of public confidence in Mr. Balfour. He was initially viewed with contempt by the immense Radical majority, who remembered his rejection at Manchester. However, this loss of confidence was based largely on temporary reasons. Within a year of entering opposition, Mr. Balfour's power in the House of Commons was once again realized, even with a small party supporting him.

The subsequent Radical government, led by Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, sought to bridge the divisions within the Liberal Party caused by the Boer War. This new Cabinet included Liberal Imperialists such as Mr. Asquith (Exchequer), Mr. Haldane (Secretary of State for War), and Sir Edward Grey (Foreign Office), alongside those who had condemned the war, such as Mr. John Morley and Sir Robert Reid.

## Necessity of Reorganization
The fall of the administration necessitated a reorganization of the Unionist forces. This process required the adoption of a common platform to rebuild the party's strength. The conditions for this fresh departure under Mr. Balfour's leadership depended, in part, on the opportunities provided by the proceedings of the Radical government.
