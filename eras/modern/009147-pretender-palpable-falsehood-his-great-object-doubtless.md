# Pretenders and Falsehoods in History

The concept of the "pretender"—whether referring to a claimant to a throne, a person falsely claiming wisdom, or the fabrication of evidence—appears across various historical contexts. These instances often involve political maneuvers, the pursuit of power, or the creation of mendacious narratives to influence public opinion.

## Political Pretenders and Power
In the context of British political history, the term "Pretender" is associated with Jacobite ambitions. Bolingbroke, who attained a position of supremacy, was linked to a projected ministry of Jacobites. This faction included Sir William Windham, who served as chancellor of the exchequer, and other individuals in important military posts. While Bolingbroke claimed in his work *The Patriot King* that there were no active designs to place the crown on the head of the Pretender, this statement is described as a palpable falsehood. Evidence suggests Bolingbroke's primary objective was to secure and maintain supreme power through any necessary means or betrayals. His shifting allegiances are highlighted by the fact that he ordered overtures to the elector and hosted a dinner for Whig leaders on the same day the earl of Oxford was dismissed. His ambitions were abruptly halted by the death of the queen on August 1, which ruined his projects.

## Intellectual and Lineage Pretensions
In ancient Greece, the term "pretender" was applied to those claiming intellectual authority or noble descent. Dr. Thirlwall characterized the Sophists as "pretenders to wisdom," depicting them as a new school that taught there was no real difference between right and wrong, or truth and falsehood. However, this characterization is contested by other historians who dissent from the view that figures like Alkibiadês were simply ensnared by these "corruptions."

Pretensions also extended to royal lineages. The kings of the Epirotic Molossi claimed descent from the heroic Æakid race of Greece. Similarly, the kings of the Macedonian Lynkêstæ professed descent from the Bacchiadæ of Corinth. Because of the presence of the Bacchiadæ gens in Epidamnus and Apollonia, such claims were viewed as plausible. In many of these cases, it remains difficult to distinguish between families that were originally Greek and Hellenized natives who pretended to have Grecian blood.

## Fabrications and Mendacious Plots
History records several instances where "pretended" events were used as political weapons. In 1678 and 1679, England experienced the "pretended Popish Plot," described by the Houses of Lords and Commons as a "hellish and damnable plot of the Popish Recusants." This event is characterized as a tissue of fabrications and falsehoods created by informers such as Bedloe and Oates, starting from no real fact.

In Athens, similar tactics were employed. The Lacedæmonians attempted a political maneuver against Periklês by pretending that religion required the "abomination of the goddess" to be driven out. This was based on a hereditary taint of sacrilege attributed to Periklês' ancestor, Megaklês. Additionally, in the accounts of Athenian generals, some historians have used the term "pretended" to describe the testimony of individuals; for example, one man claimed he was preserved by clinging to a meal-barrel. However, linguistic analysis of the Greek word *φάσκων* suggests it can mean simply "affirming" rather than "pretending."

## Forgeries and Literary Tales
Falsehoods have also appeared in the form of forged documents and legendary tales. In the records of the Moslems regarding the king of England, Richard, a "pretended letter" from the Sheich (the old man of the mountain) is described as an absurd and palpable forgery. This letter attempted to justify Richard by shifting the guilt of a murder to the prince of the assassins.

In literary history, tales existed claiming that Hesiod had vanquished Homer in recited composition. These stories, along with various other accounts of Homer's life, are found in the *Certamen Homeri et Hesiodi*.
