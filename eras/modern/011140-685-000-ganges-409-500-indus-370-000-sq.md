# Hydrographic Basins of Asia and the Geography of Northern India

## Asiatic Hydrographic Basins
The hydrographic basins of Asia are categorized into three primary divisions. The northern division, which encompasses drainage falling into the Mediterranean, the Aralo-Caspian depression, and the Arctic Sea, covers an area of approximately 6,394,500 sq. m. This total is comprised of the Arctic river basins (4,367,000 sq. m.), the Aralo-Caspian basin (1,759,000 sq. m.), and the Mediterranean (268,500 sq. m.).

The southern division is nearly equal in extent to the northern, totaling 6,514,000 sq. m. This is divided between the Indian Ocean drainage (2,873,000 sq. m.) and the Pacific drainage (3,641,000 sq. m.).

The interior or inland basins account for 3,141,500 sq. m., which is about half the size of the other two divisions. These inland basins include the Gobi depression, the Tibetan plateau, the Syro-Arabian inland basin, the Iranian (or Perso-Afghan) uplands, the basins of Asia Minor, and the lacustrine regions located south of the Arctic watershed.

## Major Asiatic River Basins
The Ob is the largest river basin in Asia, with an extent exceeding 1,000,000 sq. m. Other significant basins include:
*   The Amur: 776,000 sq. m.
*   The Yang-tsze-kiang: 685,000 sq. m.
*   The Ganges: 409,500 sq. m.
*   The Indus: 370,000 sq. m.

## Geography of British India
British India is situated approximately between the 70th and 95th meridians, extending from the Indian Ocean to the Tibetan table-land. The Indian peninsula, from 25 deg. N. southwards, is characterized as a table-land. Its general level ranges between 1,000 ft. and 3,000 ft. (maximum), though its highest points in the west rise to over 8,000 ft. The ordinary altitude of the higher hills in this region rarely exceeds 4,000 ft.

## The Great Plain of Northern India
The great plain of northern India is located between the foot of the Himalayan slope of the Tibetan plateau and the table-land of the peninsula. It extends from the Indus delta in the west to the Ganges and Brahmaputra delta in the east. Including its prolongation up the valley of Assam, the plain covers an area of about 500,000 sq. m. This region is noted as being the most civilized, most populous, and richest districts of India.

The plain is characterized by an almost unbroken surface consisting of deposits so finely comminuted that a traveler could journey over 2,000 m. from the Bay of Bengal up the Ganges, through the Punjab, and down the Indus to the sea without encountering a pebble. The highest point of this plain rises to approximately 1,000 ft.
