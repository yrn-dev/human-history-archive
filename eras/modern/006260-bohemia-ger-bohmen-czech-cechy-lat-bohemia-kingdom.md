# BOHEMIA

Bohemia (German: *Bohmen*; Czech: *Cechy*; Latin: *Bohemia*) is a kingdom and crownland of Austria. It serves as the principal province of the Austrian empire and is situated in the geographical center of the European continent. The region is enclosed by high mountains, yet it remains accessible via the valley of the Elbe to the German plain and through Moravia from the Danubian plain. Historically, its location has made it a junction for the migration of peoples and a borderland between the Slavonic and German worlds.

## Geography and Demographics
Bohemia covers an area of 20,060 square miles, which is approximately two-thirds the size of Scotland. It is bounded to the northeast by Prussian Silesia, to the southeast by Lower Austria and Moravia, to the south by Upper Austria, to the southwest by Bavaria, and to the northwest by Saxony.

As of 1900, the population of Bohemia was 6,318,280, with a density of 315 inhabitants per square mile. This population grew from just over 3,000,000 in 1800. The population is divided by nationality, consisting of approximately 65% Czechs and 35% Germans. Geographically, Czechs occupy the south, southeast, and middle of the country, while Germans are concentrated in the large towns and near the borders, particularly in the west and north. Despite significant industrial development and natural resources, there is a steady flow of emigration to Russia, Germany, other provinces of the monarchy, and America.

## Administration and Education
For administrative purposes, Bohemia is divided into ninety-four districts and two autonomous municipalities: the capital, Prague (population 204,478), and Reichenberg (population 34,204). Other significant towns include Pilsen, Budweis, Aussig, and Kuttenberg. 

The region's political representation includes 130 deputies to the Reichsrat in Vienna. The local diet consists of 242 members, including the two rectors of the universities, the archbishop, and three bishops. Education is highly advanced in Bohemia, which maintains the lowest proportion of illiterates among all Austrian provinces. The educational system is led by two universities in Prague—one Czech and one German.

## Religion and Urban Centers
The Roman Catholic Church is the faith of 96% of the population. The ecclesiastical division of the region comprises the archbishopric of Prague and three bishoprics: Leitmeritz, Koniggrätz, and Budweis.

Several cities serve as important industrial and historical centers:
*   **Budweis:** The principal commercial center of South Bohemia and a river port at the junction of the Maltsch and Moldau. It is a center for a German enclave, though as of 1908, the population was 60% Czech.
*   **Königgrätz:** An episcopal see and one of the oldest settlements in Bohemia. It is located in a fertile region known as the "Golden Road." The town was the first to declare for the national cause during the Hussite wars.
*   **Kuttenberg:** A town known for its Gothic architecture, including the five-naved church of St Barbara, and its industry in sugar-refining, brewing, and leather goods.

## Political and National Conflicts
Bohemia has been a site of intense racial and political tension between Czech and German populations. A powerful Czech party, led by Rieger and Palacky, sought the full restoration of the Bohemian monarchy to include Silesia and Moravia. They demanded that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia in Prague and that the kingdom achieve a status similar to that of Hungary. This movement was supported by some German-descent nobles, such as Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg and Count Leo Thun, who sought to preserve their power against German Liberals.

Conversely, Germans in Bohemia advocated for centralization to avoid falling under Czech control. In 1868, Czech leaders presented a "declaration" to the diet in Prague, asserting that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia and that the diet was not qualified to elect representatives for Vienna. This led to serious riots and the imposition of exceptional laws by the government.

Tensions continued into the early 20th century. Between 1902 and 1904, Dr. von Koerber attempted to find a compromise by proposing the division of Bohemia into ten districts (five Czech, three German, and two mixed). The Czechs rejected this, demanding that the Czech language be placed on an equal footing with German for all official purposes. This demand was refused by the Germans, leading to a period where Koerber governed largely without parliament, as the Czechs utilized "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction to block business. In response, various German parties formed a joint executive committee to protect German racial interests.
