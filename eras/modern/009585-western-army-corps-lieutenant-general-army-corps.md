# Western Army Corps

In the context of military organization, the term "army corps" refers to a large tactical unit composed of multiple divisions and supporting elements. The structure, command, and composition of these corps vary significantly across different national armies and historical periods.

## Indian Army Rearrangement (1904)
Under a 1904 re-arrangement of the Indian army led by Lord Kitchener, the former Bombay command was abolished. In its place, the Western army corps was established under the command of a lieutenant-general. This army corps was further subdivided into three divisions, each led by a major-general:

*   **4th Division:** Headquartered at Quetta, this division included troops from the Sind and Quetta districts.
*   **5th Division:** Headquartered at Mhow, this division consisted of three brigades located at Jhansi, Jubbulpore, and Nasirabad. It incorporated the Bombay district north of the Tapti, as well as the districts of Bundelkhand, Nerbudda, Nagpur, Deesa, and Mhow.
*   **6th Division:** Headquartered at Poona, this division consisted of three brigades located at Aden, Ahmednagar, and Bombay. It comprised the Bombay district south of the Tapti, the Poona district, the Dharwar and Aurungabad districts, and the Belgaum district north of the Tungabhadra.

## American Expeditionary Forces and Modern Warfare
In the context of early 20th-century warfare in France, army corps were utilized as elastic organizations. Assignments of divisions to specific corps were not permanent. For example, during operations against defenses near the Moselle River and St. Mihiel, the following corps structures were employed:

*   **First Corps:** Commanded by Major General Hunter Liggett, this corps included the Second, Fifth, Ninetieth, and Eighty-second Divisions, with the Seventy-eighth Division held in reserve.
*   **Third Corps:** Commanded by Major General Joseph T. Dickman, this corps consisted of the First, Forty-second, and Eighty-ninth Divisions.
*   **Fifth Corps:** Commanded by Major General George H. Cameron, this corps included a French division and the Twenty-sixth Division.

The preparation for large-scale operations involved the assembly of corps and army artillery, aircraft, tanks, ambulances, and transport. These efforts were supported by the Service of Supply and railheads. In some instances, French assistance was provided in the form of personnel and corps and army artillery, which included heavy guns capable of reaching Metz to disrupt German rail movements.

Command shifts occurred frequently during these campaigns. For instance, on October 9, Lieutenant General Robert L. Bullard—who had previously commanded the Third Corps and the First Division—was given command of the Second Army. Simultaneously, Major General Dickman was transferred to the First Corps, and Major General Charles P. Summerall was placed in command of the Fifth Corps. Major General John L. Hines was assigned to the Third Corps.

## American Civil War Corps
During the American Civil War, the Army of the Potomac utilized a corps system. Notable commands included the I. corps, which was led by Joseph Hooker during the Maryland campaign, where it forced the defile of South Mountain and opened the battle of the Antietam. At Fredericksburg, the V. corps was commanded by Daniel Butterfield. Later, in the battle of Fredericksburg, Joseph Hooker commanded a centre grand division consisting of the III. and V. corps. In Tennessee, the XI. and XII. corps were combined to form the XX. corps; Daniel Butterfield commanded a division within the XX. corps during the Atlanta campaign.

## European Corps and Field Commands
In the Napoleonic era, various corps were utilized across Europe. In 1805, Mikhail Larionovich Golenishchev-Kutuzov commanded the Russian corps that opposed the advance of Napoleon on Vienna, winning an action at Dürrenstein on November 18th-19th. 

In Prussia, Adolf Freiherr von Lützow organized a "free corps" during the "war of liberation," which included cavalry, infantry, and Tirolese marksmen. This corps operated in the French rear during the 1813 campaign. Although the corps was annihilated as a fighting force at Kitzen near Leipzig, it was reorganized and later served in regular warfare under Wallmoden, distinguishing itself at Göhrde and Gadebusch. Following the peace of 1814, the corps was dissolved, with its cavalry becoming the 6th Ulans and its infantry becoming the 25th Regiment.

## Other Army Corps Structures
During the Franco-British attacks of August 8, 1918, forces were grouped under Field-Marshal Haig. The British 4th Army (under Rawlinson) included the Canadian Corps (4 divisions), the Australian Corps (4 divisions), and the 3rd Corps (3 divisions). The French 1st Army (under Debeney) comprised the 35th Corps (4 divisions), the 31st Corps (4 divisions), the 10th Corps (3 divisions), and the 9th Corps (2 divisions).

In historical Japanese organization, army corps varied in size based on the province of their headquarters. Their staff for forces of 10,000 or more included a general (shogun), two lieutenant-generals (fuku-shogun), four secretaries (rokuji), two army-inspectors (gunkan), and four sergeants (gunso). The internal hierarchy for every thousand men included one colonel (taiki) and two lieutenant-colonels (shoki).
