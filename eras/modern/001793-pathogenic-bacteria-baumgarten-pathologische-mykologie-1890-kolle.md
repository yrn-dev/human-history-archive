# Pathogenic Bacteria

Pathogenic bacteria are organisms that act as agents of disease. While the vast majority of bacteria are not pathogenic—with many being harmless or indispensable to natural operations—those that cause disease do so largely through their general ability to produce chemical changes.

## Nature and Classification
There is no sharp line of distinction between pathogenic and non-pathogenic Schizomycetes. Research has demonstrated that the virulence or pathogenicity of these organisms can be modified, either increased or diminished, through specific treatments or alterations in the environment during the course of epidemics. Furthermore, some species are classified as facultative parasites, meaning they can function as either parasites or saprophytes depending on the circumstances.

From an evolutionary perspective, it is suggested that certain races of bacteria gradually acquired the ability to invade body tissues and produce disease. While some original characters may have changed during this acquisition, these changes are often slight and not always permanent. Consequently, pathogenic bacteria often possess characters closely allied to non-pathogenic members of the same group, making differentiation difficult.

## Identification and Study
A full description of a specific bacterium requires an account of its biological properties, its microscopical characters, its growth characteristics in various culture media, and the effects produced when animals are inoculated. To determine a species, researchers employ several tests:
* **Fermentative action:** Adding sugars such as glucose, lactose, or saccharose.
* **Reaction changes:** Using litmus to show changes in reaction.
* **Indol formation:** Utilizing peptone solution.
* **Curdling:** Using sterilized milk as a culture medium.

## Cultivation Methods
To study bacteria outside the body, media are prepared to mimic the natural habitat of the organism, which for pathogenic bacteria are the natural fluids of the body. These media are sterilized by heat and can be fluid or solid. Solid media are preferred because bacterial growth appears as a discrete mass, allowing accidental contamination to be easily recognized. Solidification is achieved through coagulation or the addition of agents like agar or gelatine. Meat extract and blood serum (solidified at a suitable temperature) are common bases; non-coagulable proteids, known as commercial "peptone," are added to replace proteids lost during coagulation.

Cultures are created by transferring material via a sterile platinum wire into tubes or flasks plugged with cotton-wool. These are kept in incubators at temperatures typically around 20°C (warm summer temperature) or 37°C (body temperature). "Pure cultures" are obtained when only one variety of bacterium is present, and growth can be maintained indefinitely through "subcultures."

## History of Pathogenic Research
The field was greatly stimulated by Pasteur's demonstration that bacteria could produce definite diseases. A significant advancement occurred in 1881 when Koch introduced the "plate-method" and solid culture media, simplifying the separation and cultivation of bacteria. Koch had previously obtained the anthrax bacillus in pure culture by 1876.

The decade between 1880 and 1890 saw the identification of organisms responsible for:
* Cholera, typhoid fever, and diphtheria.
* Tetanus, glanders, tubercle, and suppuration.

In the final decade of the 19th century, the bacilli for influenza (1892), plague (1894), and dysentery (1898) were discovered. Research into immunity began systematically around 1880. Pioneers in producing active immunity via attenuated virus included Pasteur, Toussaint, and Chauveau in France, as well as Duguid, J. S. Burdon-Sanderson, and W. S. Greenfield in Great Britain. Additionally, Metchnikoff's theory of phagocytosis, dating from approximately 1884, provided a major stimulus for further research.

## Environmental Resistance and Destruction
Certain bacteria exhibit extreme resistance to temperature. Some bacilli can survive seven days in liquid air (-192°C to -183°C) and even ten hours in liquid hydrogen (-252°C). This resistance is likely linked to the fact that completely dry albumin does not lose coagulability when heated to 110°C for several hours.

Conversely, radiant energy can be bactericidal. Experiments by Marshall Ward in 1892-1893 proved that rays of light—specifically blue-violet and ultra-violet rays—can kill organisms, including the spores of *B. anthracis* (which otherwise withstand temperatures of 100°C and above). This action occurs even in the absence of food materials. Pathogenic bacteria, such as those causing typhoid and anthrax, are especially susceptible to this light-action, which naturally destroys enormous quantities of germs in the atmosphere and rivers. This discovery led to the use of bactericidal rays in the cure of lupus and the hope that the electric arc could be used for hospital disinfection.
