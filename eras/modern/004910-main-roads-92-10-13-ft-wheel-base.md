# Main Roads

Main roads serve as critical infrastructure for the movement of military forces, the transport of goods, and the support of heavy vehicular loads. Their design, maintenance, and strategic use vary significantly depending on the region and the specific requirements of the traffic they carry.

## Engineering and Load Specifications
The structural integrity of road bridges is determined by the "live load" they must support. While a dense crowd of people is estimated to create a uniform load of 80 to 120 lb per sq. ft., modern traction engines and wagons often produce greater straining actions. In areas near large towns or manufacturing districts, authorities may require bridges to support loads of up to 30 tons.

In Switzerland, roads are categorized into three classes based on importance, with specific load prescriptions for designers:
*   **Main Roads:** These are designed for a crowd load of 92 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon load of 10 tons per axle with a 13 ft. wheel base.
*   **Secondary Roads:** These require a crowd load of 72 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon load of 6 tons per axle with a 10 ft. wheel base.
*   **Other Roads:** These are prescribed for a crowd load of 51 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon load of 3 tons per axle with an 8 ft. wheel base.

In England, provisions are made for even larger loads, including traction engines paired with boiler trolleys or trucks loaded with granite.

## Military and Strategic Use
Main roads are essential for the deployment of troops and the movement of artillery. During the Napoleonic era, the main road from Alessandria to Tortona was used to deploy Desaix's division as a unit, while the Alessandria-Genoa road served as a route for detachments searching for the enemy.

In more recent conflicts in north-eastern France, main roads have been described as slippery and unreliable during rainy weather, though they remain the primary arteries for movement. For example, the main road to Arras was utilized by military parties to avoid exposure to shell fire. In areas of intense conflict, main roads and the main streets they carry may be targeted; in one instance, the French blew up the western span of a three-arched stone bridge over the Meuse that carried both a main street and a high road. Such damage has been repaired by German engineers using wooden bridges.

## Logistics and Traffic Management
Main roads are vital for army supply chains. At Dun, supply columns consisting of requisitioned farmers' carts—drawn by two requisitioned horses each—moved provisions, tinned foods, meat, flour, oats, and wine from railway platforms to the troops. To manage this constant flow of vehicles and prevent dangerous congestion at cross-roads, several measures are employed:
*   **Special Roads:** Marked out by placards to guide columns to loading stations.
*   **Signage:** The use of finger-posts to direct traffic.
*   **Personnel:** The posting of pickets to guide traffic.

Vehicles on these roads are often strictly marked for identification; for example, wagons may be labeled to indicate the Foot Artillery Regiment, the specific Mortar Battalion, and the ammunition column number.

## Road Characteristics and Measurement
The nature of roads varies by location. In some regions, main roads are significantly wider than the lanes or by-roads that lead into the hills. In contrast, the streets within some villages and towns are described as horribly narrow, sometimes giving the impression they were designed for protection against foreign invasion.

To measure the length of roads as they appear on maps, specialized instruments called curvometers or kartometers are used. These devices employ wheels that are rolled along the curve of the road. While simple cyclometers count revolutions to calculate distance, more advanced versions use multiple wheels (three, five, or more) arranged symmetrically around a tracer to increase the accuracy of the measurement.

## Vehicle Technology for Road Travel
The evolution of road vehicles has focused on reducing noise and the physical impact of the road surface. The introduction of indiarubber for tires around 1852 was a significant development. Early methods by Mr. Reading were limited to slow vehicles on good roads, but Uriah Scott improved this by using sulphur to create a dense inner rim that could hold bolts. Later, Mr. Mulliner of Liverpool developed a system of iron flanges to hold indiarubber, though the cost of £36 per set limited its use. Other innovations included "rubber-cushioned axles" and indiarubber bearings placed between the axle-tree and the spring to insulate the carriage from road concussion.

Various types of carts have been used for road transport, ranging from two-wheeled, springless goods carts to specialized pleasure vehicles such as the dog-cart, the governess-cart, and the tax-cart (the latter named for its former exemption from taxation when valued under £21).
