# Bonds and Estate Administration in English Law

In English law, a bond is defined as an obligation created by deed. Its primary purpose is to ensure that the obligor—the person providing the bond—either performs a specific act, refrains from a specific act, or pays a sum of money. To secure this, the obligor binds himself to the obligee through a penalty.

## The Structure of a Money Bond
A common money bond follows a specific legal form. The obligor (identified as A.B. in standard forms) binds himself to the obligee (C.D.) for a specific sum, such as L[2000]. This sum is payable to the obligee, or to the obligee's assigns, attorneys, administrators, or executors.

The bond includes a specific condition known as the "defeasance," which serves to undo or defeat the bond if certain requirements are met. For example, if A.B., along with his administrators, executors, or heirs, pays the obligee (C.D. and his assigns, administrators, executors, or heirs) a smaller sum—usually half the penalty, such as L[1000]—plus interest at a specified annual rate without deduction by a certain date, the bond becomes void. If these conditions are not met, the bond remains in full force.

## Legal and Equitable Assets
Assets within an estate are categorized as either legal or equitable. This distinction is based on the remedy available to a creditor to recover a debt, rather than the nature of the property itself. If a creditor must sue the debtor's executor at law, the property is considered legal assets. If the only remedy is an action in chancery for the administration of the estate, the assets are equitable.

Equitable assets treat all unsecured creditors equally, ignoring the distinction between simple contract creditors and specialty creditors. While land was not historically liable at law for simple contract creditors, an act of 1833 provided that real estate should be assets administered by equity for the benefit of both simple contract and specialty creditors.

## Real and Personal Assets
Legal assets are further divided into personal and real assets. Personal assets devolve *virtute officii* on the administrator or executor and are available for both simple contract and specialty creditors since Hinde Palmer's Act. Real assets, which are devised to a devisee or descend to an heir, were historically only liable for specialty debts.

However, the Land Transfer Act 1897 largely removed this distinction by providing that the real estate of a deceased person devolves upon the executor and is administered with the same incidents as personal estate. The distinction persists primarily for property not included in the act, most notably land held in copyhold.

## Roles in Estate Administration
Several roles are involved in the handling of a deceased person's estate:
*   **Executor:** A person appointed to manage the estate. In some contexts, the eldest son usually acted as executor, equalizing shares among legitimate children.
*   **Administrator:** A person who manages the estate. In Scots law, an "executor-dative" is an executor appointed by a court rather than a testator, which corresponds to the English administrator.
*   **Heir:** A person who inherits property. In some legal systems, an adopted child could be a full heir or a residuary legatee.

Under the Supreme Court of Judicature, the costs associated with the administration of trusts and estates are generally at the discretion of the judge or court. However, executors, trustees, administrators, or mortgagees who have not unreasonably resisted or carried on proceedings retain the right to costs out of a particular fund or estate, as per rules acting in the chancery division before 1883.

## Security and Indentures
Beyond bonds, security for payment can be established through an indenture. In such an agreement, a party (A.B.) may assign specific chattels and things to another party (C.D.) and his administrators, assigns, and executors. This assignment serves as security for the payment of a principal sum and interest. Such agreements may include specific terms for the maintenance or defeasance of the security, such as rent payments or insurance. These chattels are protected from seizure except for causes specified in Sec. 7 of the Bills of Sale Act (1878) Amendment Act 1882.
