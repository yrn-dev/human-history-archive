# The Merchant of Venice and the Career of Edwin Booth

The production of *The Merchant of Venice* is closely associated with the career of the American actor Edwin Booth (1833–1893), who performed the role of Shylock and managed various productions of the play throughout his professional life.

## Early Career and Development
Edwin Booth, the second son of actor Junius Brutus Booth, began his regular stage appearances on September 10, 1849, at the Boston Museum. His initial roles included playing Tressel in Colley Cibber's version of *Richard III*. His first New York appearance occurred on September 27, 1850, at the National theatre in Chatham Street, where he played the character of Wilford in *The Iron Chest*. 

Following the death of his father, Booth established a secure position on the stage. Between 1852 and 1856, he performed in the Sandwich Islands, Australia, and California, where he captivated audiences with his acting. His performances in roles such as Richelieu, Richard, and Hamlet were eventually pronounced superior to those of Edwin Forrest.

## Management and Shakespearian Productions
In 1862, Booth became the manager of the Winter Garden theatre in New York. Between 1864 and 1867, he presented a series of Shakespearian productions noted for their unexampled magnificence, which included *The Merchant of Venice*, *Othello*, and *Hamlet*.

Later, in 1868–1869, Booth established his own venue, Booth's theatre, located at the corner of 6th Avenue and 23rd Street in New York. He organized a stock company that produced several plays, including *The Merchant of Venice*, *Much Ado about Nothing*, *Macbeth*, *Julius Caesar*, *The Winter's Tale*, and *Romeo and Juliet*. A notable characteristic of Booth's productions was his insistence on using the true text of Shakespeare, a reform that preceded similar movements in England by many years.

## Notable Roles and Artistic Legacy
Throughout his career, Booth performed a wide array of parts. His most famous role was Hamlet, a part he likely played more often than any other actor before or since, and for which he was particularly suited due to his eloquent sensibility, beauty, and grace. His final appearance as Hamlet took place in Brooklyn in 1891.

In addition to Hamlet, his repertoire included:
* **Shakespearian roles:** Shylock, Macbeth, Lear, Othello, Iago, Richard II, Richard III, and Benedick.
* **Other roles:** Richelieu, Wolsey, Petruccio, Sir Giles Overreach, Ruy Blas, Don Cesar de Bazan, and Bertuccio (in Tom Taylor's *The Fool's Revenge*). He also played Brutus in a production by Payne.

Booth achieved international success, performing at the Haymarket theatre in London during visits in 1851, 1880, and 1882. He also visited Germany in 1882, where his acting was received with high enthusiasm.

## Personal Life and Later Years
Booth's professional success was occasionally marred by personal and financial hardship. In 1865, his brother, John Wilkes Booth, assassinated President Lincoln. Prior to this event, in the autumn of 1864, the three Booth brothers—Junius Brutus, Edwin, and John Wilkes—had performed together in *Julius Caesar*.

Despite his success, Booth's generous and confiding nature led to uneconomical management. In 1874, he lost his theatre and his entire fortune. However, he eventually accumulated wealth again through arduous toil. He used this wealth to convert his Gramercy Park residence in New York into The Players', a club for the elect of the acting profession and other chosen members. He donated the house, his works of art, books, and stage mementos to the club, retaining only a single apartment for himself. Edwin Booth died in that apartment on June 7, 1893.

He was married twice: first to Mary Devlin in 1860 (who died in 1863) and later to Mary F. McVicker in 1869 (who died in 1881). His daughter from his first marriage, Edwina Booth Grossman, published *Edwin Booth: Recollections* in 1894.
