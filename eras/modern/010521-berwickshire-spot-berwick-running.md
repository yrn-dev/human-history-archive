# The Borders

The Borders is a term used to describe the territory situated on both sides of the boundary line between Scotland and England. While the term possesses geographical, historical, and literary meanings, it is most frequently applied to the Scottish side of the region.

## Geography and Boundary
The boundary line between the two nations begins on the coast of Berwickshire at a point 3 miles North by West of Berwick. From there, it runs a short distance South and West to reach the Tweed near the village of Paxton. The line follows the river to a point just beyond Carham, where it turns South-South-East toward the Cheviot Hills. The watershed of these hills forms the boundary for 35 miles.

Following the hills, the boundary is defined by a series of streams: the Liddel, the Kershope, the Bells Burn, and the Esk. After following the Esk for one mile, the line cuts due west across the country to the Sark, which it follows to the river's mouth at the head of the Solway Firth. The total length of this boundary is 108 miles, although the direct distance from the North Sea to the Solway is only 70 miles.

## Regional Divisions and Terrain
The region is divided into several counties. On the English side, the Border counties are Cumberland and Northumberland. On the Scottish side, they are Roxburgh, Dumfries, and Berwick. Additionally, the shires of Peebles and Selkirk are historically and by usage classified as Border shires.

The physical landscape differs markedly between the two sides. The southern region consists largely of rugged glens and bleak, lofty moorland used for the subsistence of cattle and sheep. The region is watered by various rivers:
*   **English side:** The North Tyne, Rede, Coquet, Bowmont, and Till.
*   **Scottish side:** The Sark, Esk, Liddel, Kershope, Jed, Kale, Leet, Whiteadder, and Tweed.

## Notable Districts and Territories
At the extreme eastern end of the boundary lies a small district of 8 square miles. This tract, located north of the Tweed but not included in Scotland, is known as the "liberties" or "bounds" of Berwick, serving as the country of the town and borough of Berwick-on-Tweed.

At the extreme west, between the Esk and the Sark (up to the Esk's junction with the Liddel), there existed a strip of country known as the Debatable Land. For generations, this area was a haunt for brigands and outlaws and was a constant source of contention between Scotland and England. The boundaries of the Debatable Land were finally adjusted in 1552.

## Historical Figures and Events
The region has been home to various notable figures and the site of significant military engagements.

**John Cairns (1818-1892):** A Scottish Presbyterian divine born at Ayton Hill, Berwickshire. The son of a shepherd, Cairns attended school at Oldcambus and Ayton. He later graduated from Edinburgh University in 1841 and served as the minister of Golden Square Church in Berwick-on-Tweed. He eventually became the principal of the United Presbyterian Theological College in 1879.

**The Earls of Marchmont:** Sir Patrick Hume (1641-1724), the 1st Earl of Marchmont, was the son of Sir Patrick Hume of Polwarth, Berwickshire. A member of the Scottish parliament, he was imprisoned for opposing the policy of the Earl of Lauderdale toward the Covenanters. He later served as the Lord High Chancellor of Scotland in 1696.

**The Battle of Dunbar:** Fought on September 3 (13), 1650, this battle took place between the Scots under David Leslie and the English army led by Oliver Cromwell. The engagement occurred on a plain about one mile wide, located approximately 3 miles South-East of the center of Dunbar, where the main road from Berwick to Dunbar runs. The battlefield was characterized by the Brocksburn (or Spott Burn) and Doon Hill, which rises to 650 feet.
