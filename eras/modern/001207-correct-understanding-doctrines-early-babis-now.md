# Doctrines of the Early Babis

The early Babis were followers of a religion founded in Persia between A.D. 1844 and 1845 by Mirza 'Ali Muhammad of Shiraz, a young Sayyid under the age of twenty-five. Before his "manifestation" (_zuhur_), which he dated to May 23, 1844, in the Persian *Bayan*, he had been a disciple of Sayyid Kazim of Rasht. Sayyid Kazim led the Shaykhis, a sect of extreme Shi'ites who held the doctrine of _Rukn-i-rabi_ ("the fourth support"), which posits that an intermediary—the "perfect Shi'ite"—must always exist between the faithful followers and the twelfth Imam.

## The Meaning of the Bab
The title *Bab* refers to the "gates" through which the twelfth Imam communicated with his partisans during the "minor occultation" (_Ghaybat-i-sughra_) from A.D. 874 to 940. Mirza 'Ali Muhammad assumed the title *Bab* in this specific sense, rather than as a "Gate of God" or "Gate of Religion." He eventually adopted the higher title of *Nuqta* ("Point"), and the title of *Bab* was subsequently given to his disciple, Mulla Husayn of Bushrawayh.

## Core Theological Tenets
A full understanding of early Babi doctrines requires familiarity with the theology of Islam, particularly the tenets of the Shi'a. The Babis recognize the Qur'an as a revelation and Muhammad as a true prophet, but they deny the finality of his revelation. They view revelation as progressive, asserting that as the human race progresses, ordinances more suitable to the age and a fuller measure of truth are vouchsafed.

The Babis teach that Divine Unity is incomprehensible and can only be known through its Manifestations. Consequently, the supreme duty of a human being is to recognize the Manifestation of the cycle in which they live. The doctrines of the early Babis most closely resembled those of the Hurufis and Isma'ilis.

## Metaphysics and the Afterlife
Due to the unsystematic nature and enormous volume of Babi scriptures, and because the movement lacked church councils, several important doctrinal points remain vague or undetermined. For example, the resurrection of the body is denied, although a form of personal immortality is generally, if not universally, accepted.

The early Babis placed great importance on the mystical values of numbers and letters. Specifically, they emphasized the number 19 (referred to as "the number of the unity"), the number 18, and the number 361 (19 squared), which they called "the number of all things."

## Evolution and Social Standing
Under the influence of Baha, the sect's goals shifted toward more ethical and practical aims. During this transition, the metaphysical "hair-splittings" and "wilder pantheistic tendencies" associated with the early Babis largely disappeared. 

While the Babis are noted for high levels of morality, integrity, and intelligence, the archive notes that claims regarding their efforts to improve the social position of women have been much exaggerated.

## Persecutions and Political Events
The Babi movement faced significant opposition and persecution. Following the death of Muhammad Shah in September 1848, several revolts occurred, including a seven-month defiance of the shah's troops at the ruined shrine of Shaykh Tabarsi in Mazandaran, led by Mulla Husayn of Bushrawayh and Mulla Muhammad 'Ali of Barfurush. Other struggles took place at Zanjan, led by Mulla Muhammad 'Ali Zanjani, and at Niriz in Fars, led by Aga Sayyid Yahya.

The Bab was publicly shot in Tabriz in front of the citadel (_arg_) after being held in captivity at Shiraz, Maku, and Chihriq. His body was eventually moved to Acre in Syria for burial. In August 1852, three Babis independently attempted to assassinate Nasiru'd-Din Shah; while the attempt failed, it triggered fresh persecutions. On August 31, 1852, approximately thirty Babis were executed in Tehran with "atrocious cruelty," including the poetess Qurratu'l-'Ayn and Hajji Mirza Jani of Kashan. The sect was not involved in the actual assassination of Nasiru'd-Din Shah in May 1896. Later notable persecutions occurred at Yazd in May 1891 and June 1903.
