# The Bass Clarinet

The bass clarinet (known in French as *clarinette basse*, German as *Bass-Klarinette*, and Italian as *clarinetto basso* or *darone*) is a wood-wind instrument of the clarinet family. It is essentially an A, Bb, or C clarinet that speaks an octave lower than its higher counterparts. Consequently, the general history, acoustic properties, transposition, and fingering of the standard clarinet apply to the bass clarinet.

## Acoustic Properties and Construction
A point of interest regarding the bass clarinet is the contrast between its low pitch (8 ft. tone) and its moderate length. The bore of the instrument measures only approximately 42 to 43 inches from the mouthpiece to the bell. In comparison, the bassoon, which shares the same pitch, possesses a bore twice that length. This difference is explained by the acoustic properties of the cylindrical tube and reed mouthpiece characteristic of the clarinet family; this configuration acts as a closed pipe, which speaks an octave lower than an open pipe of the same length and overblows a twelfth rather than an octave.

The construction of the instrument requires extreme mathematical accuracy. Theoretically, the bore must remain strictly cylindrical from the mouthpiece to the bell joint. Any deviation, such as an undue widening of the bell where it joins the body, can destroy correct intonation and muffle the lower notes.

Due to its length, the bass clarinet differs in form from higher clarinets. The mouthpiece is attached to a serpent-shaped crook made of silver or brass via screws and a strong ligature. Additionally, the bell joint is bent upward in front of the instrument and ends in a large bell shaped like a gloxinea.

## Tone and Range
The tone of the bass clarinet is less reedy than that of higher clarinets and resembles the bourdon stop of an organ. In its lowest register, the tone is somewhat hollow and lacks power, though it is mellower than the tone of the bassoon. Because the instrument speaks slowly in the lowest octave, it is primarily used for melody notes or sustained bass; rapid passages are impossible in this register.

The compass of the modern orchestral bass clarinet is generally the same as the higher clarinets in C, Bb, and A, but shifted one octave lower. For the instrument in C, the range is [Notation: E2 B5.]. For the bass clarinet in Bb, the real sounds are one tone lower, and for the bass clarinet in A, they are 1-1/2 tone lower, though the notation remains the same for all three. In orchestral works, the tenor clef is frequently used, though the treble clef is sometimes employed. When the treble clef is used, the instrument in C speaks an octave lower, the Bb instrument a major ninth lower, and the A instrument a minor tenth lower.

## Historical Development
The origin of the bass clarinet is found in Germany. In 1793, Heinrich Grenser of Dresden created the first bass clarinet, likely inspired by the popularity of the basset horn (the tenor of the clarinet family) and Grenser's own renown for making excellent fagottos. Early specimens, such as those preserved in the Snoeck Collection and the Brussels Conservatoire, featured tubes of great thickness and holes bored obliquely through the walls. In Italy, Nicolas Papalini of Pavia attempted to solve mechanical challenges by creating a serpentine bore pierced through two slabs of pear-wood.

Further developments occurred in the 19th century:
* **Joseph Uhlmann of Vienna** constructed a "bass basset horn" with twenty-three keys and a compass spanning four complete octaves from Bb, including all chromatic semitones. These instruments resembled saxophones in their bent bell joint and right-angle crook, though they maintained a cylindrical bore unlike the conical bore of the saxophone.
* **Georg Streitwolf of Goettingen** produced a bass clarinet in 1828 with nineteen keys and a compass from Ab to F. His instruments, which resembled the fagotto and used a beak mouthpiece, were adopted by the Prussian infantry in 1834 as the wood-wind bass.

The modern orchestral bass clarinet was produced through the improvements of Adolphe Sax (1814–1894), who worked from the models of Streitwolf and Grenser, as well as the work of Buffet and Halary. Sax secured the instrument's introduction into opera orchestras.

## Orchestral Use
The bass clarinet in C first appeared in opera in 1836 in Act V of Meyerbeer's *Huguenots*, and later in *Dinorah*. In 1838, P. Maino of Milan independently introduced a differently constructed bass clarinet at the theatre of Modena. Richard Wagner utilized the bass clarinet in C and Bb in *Tristan und Isolde*, specifically using the instrument at the end of Act II to characterize the reproachful utterance of King Mark.

Modern orchestral models may be equipped with various key-mechanisms, including the Boehm system. This level of ingenuity has resolved the difficulties faced by original inventors, who had previously attempted to bridge the distance between finger-holes by boring holes obliquely or making the bore serpentine.
