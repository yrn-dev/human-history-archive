# Tibet and the Asian Highlands

## Geography of Tibet
Tibet is a rugged table-land, known to its inhabitants as *Bodyul* or *Bod*. It is characterized by a narrow shape relative to its length and is broken up by a series of mountain ranges. These ranges generally follow the direction of the table-land's length and frequently rise into regions of perpetual snow. Between the flanks of these ranges are narrow, closely hemmed-in valleys with moderate inclinations. At various intervals, these valleys open into wide plains that are occupied by rivers or salt lakes that have no outflow.

The eastern termination of Tibet is marked by a line of snowy mountains that flank China on the west. This boundary is located between the 35th and 27th parallels of latitude, and at approximately 103 degrees east. To the west, the table-land continues beyond the political limits of Tibet, maintaining similar physical features until it terminates at about 70 degrees east. The ranges covered in perpetual snow extend as far west as Samarkand, after which they diminish rapidly in height and end in low hills located north of Bokhara.

## The Asian Plateaus and Mountain Systems
Tibet forms part of a great mass of mountain that serves as a natural line of division across a large portion of the continent. To the west of British India (excluding Syria and Arabia), there is another extensive plateau. While this region is as large as Tibet, it exists at a much lower altitude. Its northern boundary follows nearly 36 degrees N. to the Black Sea, the Sea of Marmora, and the southern shore of the Caspian. Its southern border runs along the Tigris, the Persian Gulf, and the Arabian Sea, extending westward to the north-east angle of the Levant.

This high region is occupied by Armenia, Asia Minor, Iran (or Persia), Baluchistan, and Afghanistan. The eastern flank of this table-land is defined by a line of hills near the 72nd meridian, situated a short distance from the Indus between the Himalaya and the mouth of the river. These hills typically do not exceed 5,000 or 4,000 feet, though some summits reach 10,000 feet or more.

## Regional Intersections and Ranges
The north eastern portion of the Afghan table-land and Tibet form a continuous mass of mountain between the 34th and 36th degrees N. and between the 72nd and 71st meridians. From the intersection of the 36th parallel and the 71st meridian, an unbroken mountain range extends in two directions: one toward the north east, reaching the crest of the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau, and the other nearly due west toward the Caspian.

The north eastern section of this range is of great altitude and serves as a divide between the headwaters of the Indus and its Kabul tributary (which flow south into the Arabian Sea) and the headwaters of the Oxus (which flow to the Aral Sea). The western part of this range, known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons, decreases in height west of the 65th meridian.

## Other Central Asian Ranges
A mountainous belt extends from the 115th meridian at the western end of the Yablonoi range to the extremity of Pamir. This belt is broken into several branches and encloses isolated drainage areas with numerous lakes and no outflow. Key ranges in this system include:

*   **Tian-shan (Celestial Mountains):** Located between 75 and 95 degrees E. and 40 and 43 degrees N., these mountains form the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. Some summits exceed 20,000 feet. The region's few towns and principal cultivated districts are situated along the foot of this range.
*   **Ala-tau:** A prolongation of the Tian-shan that flanks the Syr on the north, rising to 15,000 or 14,000 feet. It acts as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes.
*   **Altai:** Located near the 50th parallel and rising to 12,000 or 10,000 feet, this range separates the lakes of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria from the great rivers of western Siberia.
