# The Home Section of the British Isles in Relation to the Empire

The British Empire is described as a vast aggregate of states with widely differing characters, acquired through various methods. These territories are held together under the supreme headship of the crown. The structural basis of the empire's constitution is a generally acknowledged triple principle consisting of self-defence, self-support, and self-government. While this principle is applied more fully in some regions than others, it remains the recognized foundation of the imperial system.

## Distinction of the Home Section
Within the context of the empire, the home section of the British Isles is distinguished from all other parts by two primary factors: it is the residence of the crown and the place of origin of the British race. 

For the purposes of political administration, the empire is divided into three distinct sections:
1. The United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, which includes the Isle of Man and the dependencies of the Channel Islands.
2. The Indian empire, which comprises the feudatory native states and British India.
3. The colonial empire, which consists of all other dependencies and colonies.

## Territorial and Historical Development
The growth of the empire in the modern sense—defined as extension beyond the limits of the United Kingdom—is of a comparatively recent date. Most territorial acquisitions occurred during the 17th century and subsequent centuries. There are a few notable exceptions to this timeline:
* **The Channel Islands:** These became British as part of the Norman inheritance of William the Conqueror.
* **The Isle of Man:** This territory was held by conquest for a short time by Edward I. In 1393, its titular sovereign sold it to Sir William Scrope, earl of Wiltshire. Following Scrope's attainder for high treason and the confiscation of his estates, it became a fief of the English crown. In 1406, Henry IV granted it to Sir John Stanley. It remained with the ancestors of the earls of Derby until 1736, when it passed to James Murray, 2nd duke of Atholl. It was eventually inherited by his daughter Charlotte, who sold the island to the crown for an annuity of £2000 and £70,000.
* **Newfoundland:** Sir Humphrey Gilbert took nominal possession of this territory in 1583.

## Constitutional Relations and Citizenship
The relationship between the home section and the colonies has been shaped by the recognition of the value of independent initiative. A significant precedent was set by Queen Elizabeth's first patent to Sir Walter Raleigh, which guaranteed that British subjects accompanying him to America would continue to enjoy all the rights they enjoyed at home. This guarantee served as an unconscious charter of union, establishing citizenship rights in the colonies equivalent to those in England and preserving the status of the colonist as a British subject both abroad and at home.

Theoretically, the principal sections of the empire enjoy equal rights under the crown, and no section is subordinate to another. While the imperial parliament's intervention in colonial affairs is admitted theoretically when the crown's constitutional advisers require parliamentary support, the full theory of exclusive dependence of colonies on the crown has not been found practicable in the working of the British constitution.

## Regional Dynamics and the Case of Ireland
The relation of Ireland to Britain over the last half-century reflects a period of tension. Liberal England made several clumsy attempts to satisfy Irish complaints and establish a footing of fellowship, often facing opposition from the Ulster Irish and the Conservative Party. In 1886, Gladstone introduced the first Irish Home Rule Bill in an attempt to give Irish affairs to the Irish people for the first time in history. This proposal was viewed by some as dangerous and failed to provide satisfactory assurances to Protestant Irish, particularly those in Ulster, regarding protection from the "priest-ridden illiterates of the south." This led to the fracturing of the Liberal Party and the installation of a Unionist Government.

## Economic and Cultural Links
The British Isles maintain specific economic and cultural ties with the rest of the empire. In terms of production, the United Kingdom produces more coal than any other single country except the United States. However, the British Isles cannot grow certain fruits on an industrial scale, such as lemons, oranges, bananas, and almond-nuts; consequently, over £5,000,000 was spent on importing these items in 1908.

Culturally, the English language serves as a link. While the language has been supplemented by native words in colonial regions due to new conditions of life, the use of a common literature prevents the overgrowth of local peculiarities and keeps these adaptations familiar to those at home.
