# Buckingham

Buckingham refers to a town and county town, as well as a series of noble titles. It is distinct from the titles associated with Buckinghamshire.

## The Town and County of Buckingham
Buckingham serves as the county town of Buckinghamshire, with a population of 3,152. The surrounding shire of Buckingham was established during the reign of Edward the Elder following the division of Mercia. It was likely created by aggregating pre-existing hundreds around the county town, which resulted in irregular boundary lines. 

Historically, the region was likely part of the earldom of Leofwine, son of Godwin, during the Norman invasion. Following the battle of Hastings, the area suffered sweeping confiscations due to the support it provided to Leofwine. In later centuries, the county's proximity to London made it central to national events. During the conflict between King John and his barons, William Mauduit defended Hanslape Castle until its capture and demolition by Falkes de Bréauté in 1216. The region was also severely impacted by the Black Death, which almost entirely depopulated districts such as Winslow. During the civil war, Buckinghamshire joined a mutual defence association supporting parliament, maintaining garrisons at Brill and Aylesbury. However, royalist troops briefly garrisoned Newport Pagnell, and the king established his headquarters at Buckingham in 1644.

## The Earldom, Marquessate, and Dukedom of Buckingham
The origins of the earldom of Buckingham are obscure. While some charter evidence suggests it existed under William Rufus, the primary evidence identifies Walter Giffard, lord of Longueville in Normandy, as the first earl. Giffard held forty-eight lordships in the county and was described as "Comes Bucchingehamensis" in 1097 and 1102. Following the death of the 2nd earl, Walter Giffard, in 1164, the title was assumed by Richard de Clare, earl of Pembroke ("Strongbow"), via his wife Rohais; the title died with him in 1176.

The title was revived on July 15, 1377, when Thomas of "Woodstock" (duke of Gloucester) was created earl of Buckingham during the coronation of Richard II. After Thomas's death, his son Humphrey held only the title of earl of Buckingham. The title then passed to Humphrey's sister, Anne, who married Edmund Stafford, earl of Stafford. Their son, Humphrey Stafford, inherited the title and was created duke of Buckingham in 1444. This dukedom remained with the Stafford family until 1521, ending with the execution and attainder of Edward, the 3rd duke.

In later years, George Nugent Temple Grenville (1753–1813) was created marquess of Buckingham "in the county of Buckingham" in December 1784. His son, Richard Grenville (1776–1839), succeeded him as marquess in February 1813. Richard, who had served as a member of parliament for Buckinghamshire and as Earl Temple, married the only child of the 3rd duke of Chandos and was created duke of Buckingham and Chandos in 1822. Due to financial difficulties, he spent time living outside of England; his travels were later published in 1862 as *The Private Diary of Richard, Duke of Buckingham and Chandos*.

## Distinction from Buckinghamshire Titles
The titles of Buckingham are distinct from those of Buckinghamshire. The first earl of Buckinghamshire was John Hobart (c. 1694–1756), a descendant of Sir Henry Hobart. John Hobart was created Baron Hobart of Blickling in 1728 and earl of Buckinghamshire in 1746. He was succeeded by his son John (1723–1793), who served as ambassador to Russia and lord-lieutenant of Ireland. 

Confusion between the two titles occasionally arose because the 2nd earl of Buckinghamshire signed himself as "Buckingham" until 1784, the year George Grenville was created marquess of Buckingham.

## Land Ownership and Administration
Following the Norman Conquest, the primary landowners in the region were Odo, bishop of Bayeux, and Walter Giffard. While many estates were granted to the crown or religious institutions, the Hampden family claims to have held their estate in an unbroken line since Saxon times.

Administratively, the ancient county covers 475,682 acres, with the administrative county covering 479,358 acres. As of 1901, the population was 195,764. The county is divided into eight hundreds, including the "Chiltern Hundreds" (Stoke, Burnham, and Desborough). The region is primarily within the diocese of Oxford and is divided into three parliamentary divisions: Northern (Buckingham), Mid (Aylesbury), and Southern (Wycombe). A notable institution in the county is Eton College, founded by Henry VI.
