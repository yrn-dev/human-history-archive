# Government Rent for the Quetta District

## Overview of the Khan of Kalat's Revenues
The revenues of the khan of Kalat are derived from a combination of agricultural revenue and subsidies, with a total annual value of approximately Rs.500,000. A specific portion of this income is provided by the government in the form of rent for the Quetta district. Since 1882, the khan has received Rs.25,000 annually for this purpose.

In addition to the rent for Quetta, the khan receives Rs.30,000 in lieu of transit duties in the Boian. This specific sum has recently been increased by an additional Rs.9,000.

## British Administration and the Quetta Region
The establishment of Quetta and the surrounding administrative structures were part of a broader shift in British policy toward the region. While a treaty of alliance, both offensive and defensive, was established in 1854, it maintained a policy of non-intervention. This policy was altered by a renewed treaty in 1876, facilitated by Lord Lytton under the advice of Sandeman. 

The 1876 treaty changed the administrative landscape by recognizing the sirdars as well as the khan and appointing the British government as a referee for disputes between them. Under this new arrangement, the British government provided the khan with an annual subsidy of a lakh of rupees, replacing a previous assignment of Rs.50,000. This period saw the founding of Quetta, the construction of roads, the projection of railways and telegraphs, and the placement of British troops within the khan's country to establish law and order.

## Regional Integration and Infrastructure
The consolidation of the administration in Kalat was furthered by Sandeman's 1883 expedition to Kharan and the reconciliation of the khan of Kalat with Azad Khan, a prominent Naushirwani chief. By November 1, 1887, British Baluchistan was incorporated into British India. It was organized into two districts: Thai Chotiali and Quetta-Pishin, both managed by a regular staff and a deputy-commissioner.

Infrastructure development continued to link Quetta with other regions. The Bolan Pass, which connects Quetta with Sibi and Jacobabad, became a critical route for British campaigns in Afghanistan. Following the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, the Bolan route came under direct British control and was chosen for the initial alignment of the Sind-Pishin railway. Although floods eventually forced the railway to follow the Mashkaf valley to a junction at Mach, the region remained strategically vital. Additionally, the Quetta agency was founded in 1877, which helped secure the pass from the plundering of Baluch marauders, specifically the Marris.

## Later Administrative Changes
In the late 19th century, further military and political expeditions in 1890 and 1891 led to the occupation of the Zhob valley and the creation of the central cantonment of Fort Sandeman. This extended a line of outposts from Quetta to Wana, north of the Gomal, effectively bringing order to the independent Pathan tribes of the Suliman mountains.

Political instability within the court of the khan also occurred; in November 1893, Mir Khodadad Khan was deposed due to his conduct in managing his court and his treatment of officials, and was succeeded by his son, Mir Mahmud Khan. 

A significant administrative shift took place in July 1899 regarding the Nushki district and Niabat. Management and jurisdiction were transferred to the British agency through a perpetual lease. In exchange for this transfer, a perpetual rent of Rs.9,000 per annum was established. This move was intended to secure direct control over the great highway to Seistan, which served railway and khafila traffic.

## Economic Context of Kalat
The broader economic activity of the region was reflected in its trade figures. In 1899, the total imports of Kalat were valued at Rs.700,000, while its exports were valued at Rs.505,000.
