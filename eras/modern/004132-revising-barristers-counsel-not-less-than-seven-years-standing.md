# Revising Barristers

## Definition and Role
Revising Barristers are a specific category of legal counsel within the English legal system. To be appointed as a Revising Barrister, a counsel must have no less than seven years' standing. Their primary professional responsibility is the revision of the lists of parliamentary voters.

## The Profession of the Barrister
The rank of barrister serves as a necessary qualification for nearly all offices of a judicial character and is a common requirement for other significant appointments. This includes magistracies of minor rank, such as county court judgeships and recorderships, as well as judgeships in the superior courts of equity and law in England and its colonies. This structure creates a harmony of interest and opinion between the judicial bench and the legal profession.

Barristers hold the rank of esquires. While in active practice, they are privileged from serving on juries and are exempt from arrest while on circuit or in attendance on the superior courts.

## Qualifications and Admission
Entry into the profession begins with admission to the Inns of Court. Applicants must pay specific fees and either pass a general elementary examination or provide evidence of passing a university public examination. To be "called to the bar," a student must pass further examinations and "keep" twelve terms, with four terms occurring each year. A term is considered kept by dining in hall six times (reduced to three for university students).

Certain restrictions apply to admission:
* Individuals connected with the law in an inferior capacity or those who are professional or chartered accountants cannot enter an Inn of Court until they have entirely and bona fide ceased to practice in that capacity.
* Some Inns restrict membership to those not engaged in trade.
* Applicants must declare their nationality, age, condition in life, and occupation.
* Before being called, a student must declare they have not performed clerical functions during the preceding year, held clerical preferment, or been in holy orders.

Alternative paths to the bar exist for experienced practitioners. Practicing solicitors with at least five years' standing may be called to the bar upon passing necessary examinations without keeping terms. Similarly, barristers from colonies where the professions of solicitor and barrister remain distinct, as well as Irish barristers of three years' standing, may be called to the English bar after keeping three terms without further examination.

## Professional Hierarchy and Practice
The grade of barrister includes ordinary practitioners (sometimes called "utter barristers"), king's counsel, and the solicitor-general and attorney-general, who are the heads of the profession.

The highest rank is that of king's or queen's counsel. These individuals are appointed by the lord chancellor via letters patent. While there is no fixed time requirement between a barrister's "call" and their application for this rank, it is generally understood that ten years' standing is required. King's counsel lead in court and provide opinions on cases, but they do not admit pupils to their chambers or accept pleading and conveyancing work. Historically, the first king's counsel was Sir Francis Bacon, appointed by Queen Elizabeth as "queen's counsel extraordinary."

The primary business of barristers is the advocacy of causes in open court. They also serve as chief conveyancers and draft pleadings (counter statements of parties before joining issue). The profession is divided into those practicing in the courts of common law and those in chancery. Common law counsel typically attach themselves to specific circuits in England.

## Legal Obligations and Conduct
Counsel is not held answerable for statements made relative to a case that were suggested in the client's instructions, even if those statements are groundless or reflect poorly on another's character. However, counsel is liable for actions brought by injured parties if they mention an untruth of their own invention or an impertinent untruth provided via instructions.

Disciplinary actions can be taken by the benchers of the barrister's Inn on cause shown, or by a judge or court through summary power for contempt. The benchers of the Inns of Court possess the authority to reject applicants, refuse to call a student to the bar, or "dis-bar" (expel) barristers for misconduct they deem unworthy of the rank.

Regarding financial arrangements, barristers cannot enter into contracts for payment with clients for litigation, nor can they maintain actions for their fees, which are viewed as gratuities. Except in criminal cases—where a barrister may be engaged directly via a fee given in open court—they cannot undertake a case without a solicitor's intervention.
