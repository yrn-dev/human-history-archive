# Unfellowship Ordinations, Ministers, and Churches

In the history of ecclesiastical governance and religious organization, the processes of ordination and the regulation of ministers and churches have frequently been points of contention, leading to administrative divisions, schisms, and the implementation of disciplinary measures such as "unfellowshipping."

## The Sandy Creek Association and Unfellowshipping
Between 1760 and 1770, the "Separate" Baptist body experienced phenomenal growth in the Carolinas and Virginia, driven by evangelists such as John Waller (1741–1802) and Samuel Harris (1724–c.1794). During this period of expansion, the Sandy Creek Association, under the leadership of Stearns, sought to maintain organizational control by undertaking to "unfellowship ordinations, ministers and churches that acted independently." 

This policy of unfellowshipping provoked significant opposition, eventually necessitating a division of the association. As a result, the Congaree Association of South Carolina and the General Association of Virginia emerged as distinct bodies alongside the Sandy Creek Association.

## Apostolic Offices and Baptist Harmony
Within the Virginia "Separate" Baptists, who more than doubled their membership between May 1771 and May 1773, a belief emerged in 1774 that the apostolic office should be perpetuated. This led the association to appoint Samuel Harris as an apostle through solemn ordination. Shortly thereafter, Elijah Craig (1743–1800) and John Waller were appointed as apostles for the northern district. 

This apostolic arrangement was eventually abandoned. However, the group faced further internal contention in 1775 when Jeremiah Walker and two of the apostles argued for universal redemption. To preserve harmony and avoid schism, the body adopted the Philadelphia Confession of Faith—a Calvinistic document—which included provisions against overly rigid construction. This move helped harmonize the "Separate" Baptists with the "Regular" Baptists of the Philadelphia type. By 1780, the two groups cooperated in a struggle for religious equality and the total separation of church and state.

## Ordination in Other Ecclesiastical Systems
The practice of ordination and the role of ministers vary across different historical and regional church structures:

### The Roman Catholic and Lutheran Traditions
In the early Catholic church, the spiritual and legal jurisdiction was administered by eighteen hundred bishops. These bishops derived their powers and privileges from the laws, the people, and the apostles. A perpetual order of ecclesiastical ministers was established, characterized by popular election and the ordination of the clergy.

In the Lutheran state churches of Norway, Sweden, and Denmark, the episcopate has been retained. In these nations, the king is recognized as the *summus episcopus* (supreme authority) in ecclesiastical matters, though in Sweden and Norway, this power is somewhat limited by parliament. The rite of ordination remains in the hands of the bishops. In Denmark, bishops serve as inspectors of schools and clergy, while in Sweden, they preside over local consistories consisting of lay and clerical members.

### The Eastern Church and the Schism of the Greeks
In the ninth century, the ordination of Photius to the office of patriarch of Constantinople was described as "hasty" and his rise as "irregular." This contributed to a conflict with his predecessor, Ignatius, and led to an appeal to Nicholas I, a Roman pontiff. The resulting quarrel over jurisdiction and the status of the Bulgarians involved accusations of heresy and schism, with Photius eventually deposing the successor of St. Peter before his own eventual fall and subsequent restoration.

## The Role of Ministers in State and Law
Beyond spiritual functions, the term "minister" and the concept of "ordinances" appear in civil and state contexts:

*   **State Administration:** In the Japanese government, Ministers of State are responsible for giving advice to the Emperor. All imperial rescripts and public ordinances relating to state affairs require the counter-signature of a Minister of State.
*   **Civil Ordinances:** In 1897, Badeni published ordinances in Bohemia regarding the use of the Czech language in government correspondence and the requirement of Czech language examinations for government posts after 1903.
*   **Burial Regulations:** In the Church of England, the burial of excommunicated persons (for notorious crimes) or unbaptized persons was previously refused. However, the Burial Laws Amendment Act 1880 allowed for burials in parochial grounds without the Church of England service, provided proper notice was given to the minister.
