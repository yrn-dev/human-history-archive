# The American Gold Dollar and International Monetary Standards

The American gold dollar has served as a central unit of currency and a standard of value in the United States and various international territories, reflecting broader global shifts from bimetallic systems to the gold standard during the 19th and early 20th centuries.

## Origins and Development in the United States
In the early 19th century, the United States operated under a legally established double standard (1792). However, the role of gold as the principal currency metal was reinforced by the discovery of gold in California in 1848. By the law of 1834, gold had been somewhat over-rated (1:16) compared to the ratio used in France (1:15½), a factor that resulted in the expulsion of most silver previously in circulation.

Following the introduction of an inconvertible paper currency during the Civil War, the United States formally established the gold dollar as the standard coin in 1873. Arrangements for a return to specie payments were subsequently made in 1878. Despite this shift toward gold, the United States provided for the coinage of a specific amount of silver per month via the Bland-Allison Act in February 1878.

## Adoption in British Honduras
The American gold dollar also became a standard of value outside of the United States. On October 14, 1894, British Honduras adopted the American gold dollar as its standard coin. This adoption replaced the Guatemalan dollar and resulted in the cessation of the legal tender status of silver from North, South, and Central America. 

Under this system, the government issued notes in denominations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 50, and 100 dollars. The local currency included silver pieces of 5, 10, 25, and 50 cents, as well as bronze pieces of one cent. While the American gold dollar was the standard, the British half sovereign and sovereign remained legal tender.

## Global Monetary Context and the Gold Standard
The transition to the gold dollar occurred amidst a wider international trend of demonetization—the process of divesting a metal of its standard monetary value. During the latter half of the 19th century, increasing production led to a tremendous depreciation of silver, making it impossible to maintain a stable ratio between silver and gold. This led many nations to abandon silver as a standard, using it instead merely as token money.

Other nations followed similar paths toward gold:
* **Germany:** Following the Franco-German War (1870-71), Germany established a gold currency and demonetized its previous silver currencies. In 1873, the mark was adopted as the money of account and standard of value.
* **England:** England had accepted the gold standard by law in 1816, having previously seen silver demonetized as a standard of value between 1663 and 1717.
* **Japan:** In 1897, Japan adopted gold monometallism, utilizing a gold reserve obtained from an indemnity following a war with China (1894-1895).

## Related Currency Terms and Systems
The term "dollar" itself is a modified form of *thaler* (with variants such as *daler* or *tallero*), which was a shortened form of *Joachimsthaler*. The *Joachimsthaler* was a silver coin struck in Bohemia starting in 1518, intended to be the silver equivalent of the gold gulden. 

In other regions, such as Honduras, the monetary unit remained the silver *peso* or dollar of 100 cents (weighing 25 grammes, .900 fine), though gold dollars were also in circulation in denominations of 1, 5, 10, and 20 dollars.
