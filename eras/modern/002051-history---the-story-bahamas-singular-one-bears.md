# Bahamas

The Bahamas, also known as the Lucayos, is an archipelago located in the British West Indies. It extends approximately 630 miles from Florida in the northwest to Haiti in the southeast, situated between Cuba and the open Atlantic Ocean. The archipelago is estimated to consist of 2,387 rocks, 661 cays, and 29 islands, with a total land area of approximately 5,450 square miles.

## Geography and Geology
The main islands of the archipelago occupy 4,424 square miles. These include Great and Little Abaco, Great Bahama, Eleuthera, Cat, Rum Cay, Watling (or Guanahani), New Providence, Long Island, the Exuma chain, Andros, the Crooked Islands, Mayaguana, and Inagua. While the Turks and Caicos islands are geographically part of the archipelago, they are not politically so. 

The islands are characterized by shallow surrounding seas, though three distinct channels exist: the Old Bahama channel (between the archipelago and Cuba), the Florida or New Bahama channel, and the Providence channels. A depression called the Tongue of Ocean extends south along the east side of Andros. 

Geologically, the Bahamas are composed almost entirely of coral reefs and aeolian deposits. These deposits often form ridges and rounded hills reaching 100 to 200 feet, and nearly 400 feet on Cat Island. The material is largely calcareous, derived from shells and the disintegration of reefs. The rock is soft when first exposed but develops a hard crust due to the sea and rain, often weathering into arches, pillars, and pinnacles. On Andros, a fine white marl resembling chalky ooze is present.

## Climate and Natural History
The climate is characterized by mean temperatures of 88°F during the hottest months (June to September) and 66°F during the coldest (January to March). Rainy months occur from May to October, with hurricanes typically appearing between July and October. 

The flora is diverse, though soil is generally thin. New Providence contains "pine barrens," while other areas feature black soil (under forest), reddish clay, and white shore soil. The Abaco and Andros islands are noted for large timber, including bullet, iron, mastic, mahogany, and lignum vitae woods. The islands produce a wide array of fruits and spices, including pine-apples, oranges, lemons, limes, tamarinds, pomegranates, bananas, and coffee. Cotton is indigenous and was woven by aborigines, though it has suffered from insect pests. Sisal is also grown in increasing quantities. The fauna is less diverse than the flora; indigenous species include the guana and a small coney, and aborigines were said to have a breed of non-barking dogs.

## Early History and Colonial Governance
San Salvador is claimed as the landfall of Christopher Columbus on October 12, 1492. While Watling Island is generally accepted as the landfall, other claims have been made for Cat Island, Great Turk, and Mariguana. In 1880, G. V. Fox identified San Salvador as Samana (Atwood Cay), though this suggests the island was larger in the 15th century than it is now.

New Providence has historically been the seat of government due to its safe harbor for vessels drawing more than 9 feet, though it has also served as a headquarters for lawlessness. In 1718, Captain Woodes Rogers arrived as the first crown governor after merchants from Bristol and London petitioned the crown to restore order. Following this, the colony saw the introduction of pine-apple cultivation at Eleuthera. During the American war of independence, many colonists arrived bringing wealth and slave labour, leading to large-scale cotton attempts. By 1783, Long Island had 4,000 acres under cultivation and 800 slaves. However, cotton cultivation was largely abandoned by 1800 after red bugs destroyed crops in 1788 and 1794.

Political control of New Providence shifted several times in the late 18th century: it was taken by Commodore Hopkins of the American navy in 1776, captured by the Spanish governor of Cuba in 1781, and recaptured by Lieutenant-Colonel Deveaux of the South Carolina militia in June 1783. The islands were formally reconveyed to the crown in 1784 and 1786.

## Economic Shifts and Modern History
The finances of the Bahamas were unfavourably affected by the 1848 separation of the Turks and Caicos Islands, which had been the most productive salt-producing islands. The abolition of the slave-trade in 1834 also likely impacted landed proprietors.

A significant economic event occurred in 1861 when the Federals closed the southern ports of America, leading to the rise of the blockade-running trade. This trade peaked in early 1865; in January and February of that year, 20 steamers arrived at Nassau with 14,182 bales of cotton valued at £554,675. The economic impact was stark: imports rose from £234,029 in 1860 to £5,346,112 in 1864, while exports rose from £157,350 to £4,672,398. Despite the extravagance in Nassau and the government's ability to pay a debt of £43,786, the period saw an increase in sickness and crime.

Following the cessation of this trade, New Providence returned to quietude until October 1866, when a violent hurricane destroyed fruit-trees, orchards, and profitable sponges. Subsequent droughts left inhabitants of the out-islands in a state of want and indigence.
