# Fractures

Fractures are breaks in the bone that occur through various mechanisms of injury. A bone may be broken at the specific point where it is struck, which is termed a fracture from direct violence. Alternatively, a bone may break due to a strain applied to it, known as a fracture from indirect violence, or as a result of muscular action, such as when a violent cough causes a rib to break. Generally, fractures from direct violence are transverse, while those from indirect violence are more or less oblique.

## Types and Classifications
The nature of a fracture often depends on the age and development of the bone. Fully developed bones typically break fairly across. However, the soft bones found in young people may simply bend, a condition referred to as a willow fracture or green stick fracture.

Fractures are further categorized as either simple or compound:
*   **Simple Fractures:** These are analogous to subcutaneous lacerations in the soft parts.
*   **Compound Fractures:** These are analogous to open wounds in the soft parts. A compound fracture occurs when the force causing the break also wounds the soft parts (such as a cart-wheel passing over a limb) or when a sharp point of the fractured bone pierces through the skin. 

Compound fractures carry significantly higher risks because the communication between the injured bone and external air allows germs of suppuration to enter. This can lead to blood-poisoning, osteomyelitis, necrosis (the death of large pieces of bone), and delayed union.

## Diagnosis and Special Conditions
In most instances, a fracture is identified by crepitus, which is the feeling produced when two rough osseous surfaces rub together. Crepitus is absent in cases where the bone is merely bent or in impacted fractures, where the broken extremities are driven into one another.

Certain fractures are specific to children and growing persons. Because their long bones consist of a shaft with cartilaginous ends, an injury may cause the end of the bone to become detached; this variety of fracture is known as diastasis. This condition may result in stiffness of the neighboring joint or an arrest of the bone's growth, regardless of how well it is treated.

## Healing and Complications
For a firm bony union to occur, the fragments must be in accurate apposition. The process of putting these broken ends together is called "setting the fracture."

Complications regarding the union of the bone include:
*   **Delayed Union:** This occurs when consolidation happens very slowly or not at all. If the bones are positioned correctly, treatment involves patience, general health measures, and massage.
*   **Ununited Fracture:** This is a state where nature makes no attempt to consolidate the parts after many weeks or months. Causes may include constitutional defects in the patient, the interposition of a tendon or muscle between the ends, constant disturbance of the fracture seat, or the ends not being brought close enough together. Treatment typically involves cutting down to the ends, sawing off a thin slice to freshen them, and fixing them with a screw or wire. This condition is particularly rebellious and unsatisfactory when it affects the leg-bones of children.

Additionally, splintering of the bone into a joint—especially when tendons are injured—can cause effusion into the tendon sheaths and joint. This may organize into fibrous tissue and lead to permanent stiffness, a occurrence particularly common in old people.

## Treatment Methods
The primary goal of treatment is to secure the fragments through "setting" and providing rest via splints. It is generally advisable to fix the joint above or below the fracture with the splint. In cases of fractures near the wrist, splints are arranged to allow the movement of the thumb and fingers, and are removed daily to allow for gentle exercise and bending.

Modern improvements in treatment emphasize the use of X-rays to ensure fractured surfaces are in close apposition. Simple fractures are no longer kept in splints for extended periods; they are frequently "taken down" for gentle movement and massage to reduce pain and swelling and ensure a more rapid recovery.

In cases where ordinary methods cannot achieve close adjustment, surgeons may cut down to the broken ends and fix them with a permanent strong wire suture. This immediate wiring is also considered necessary for compound fractures where the ends cannot be easily adjusted.

## Fracture of the Patella
Treatment of the knee-cap (patella) has seen various approaches. Traditional methods involve fixing the limb in plaster of Paris or a back-splint. While many recover useful limbs this way, some are left with enfeebled limbs if the fragments remain wide apart. 

An alternative method introduced by Lister involves opening the knee-joint and securing fragments in close apposition with a stout wire suture, which allows the fragments to unite solidly by bone. To avoid the risk of septic micro-organisms invading the joint, Professor A.E.J. Barker introduced a compromise method of passing a strong silver wire vertically around the fragments with a large needle without opening the joint. Other surgeons utilize silk or metal sutures passed circumferentially.
