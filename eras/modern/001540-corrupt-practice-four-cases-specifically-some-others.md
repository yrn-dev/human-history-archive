# Corrupt Practice

In historical and legal contexts, "corrupt practice" refers to the use of illegal influence, such as bribes or gratuities, to sway official decisions or the administration of justice. The nature of such practices is often analyzed through the distinction between the receipt of funds and the actual perversion of justice.

## The Case of Francis Bacon
The records of Francis Bacon provide a detailed examination of the nuances of corruption. Bacon himself distinguished between three different ways in which bribes could be given. He admitted that his own actions amounted to corruption and were worthy of condemnation, though he explicitly denied the "deliberate sale of justice." Bacon affirmed that he never had a reward or bribe in mind when pronouncing any order or sentence.

An analysis of the charges against Bacon reveals that the majority of the instances involved gratuities given after a decision had been reached. However, in four specific cases, and some others by implication, Bacon confessed to receiving bribes from suitors *pendente lite* (while the litigation was pending). Despite these admissions, evidence suggests his decisions—often made in conjunction with other officials—remained just. In several instances, Bacon's judgment was rendered against the party who had provided the bribe. In the case of Lady Wharton, it appears Bacon knew his opinion would be adverse to her cause at the time he accepted the present. While Bacon viewed these practices as corrupt and believed his eventual fall would help purify the courts, he did not believe he had perverted justice for reward.

## Legal and Procedural Distinctions
In the context of federal prosecutions, a distinction is made between "public corruption" cases and other violent crime prosecutions. In public corruption cases, parties may be able to amicably work out a light sentence. However, in cases involving violent crimes with stiff penalties, this approach is opposed by some prosecutors who view the matter strictly as a criminal prosecution rather than a "collar" or corruption case.

Additionally, the USAO may deviate from standard requirements—such as the Ashcroft Memo's "most serious readily provable offense" requirement—under "Other Exceptional Circumstances." This may include situations where the office is over-burdened or a trial would be exceptionally long. While these case-by-case exceptions are permitted to address the practical limitations of the federal criminal justice system, they are intended to be rare to avoid jeopardizing equity and fairness.
