# The Customs

Customs systems serve as critical mechanisms for revenue generation and the regulation of trade across various global jurisdictions, ranging from the integrated systems of dual monarchies to the port-specific districts of the United States and the financial structures of independent republics.

## The Austro-Hungarian Customs System

In the Dual Monarchy of Austria and Hungary, customs are managed through a shared framework established by a customs and commercial agreement concluded in 1867. This agreement, which is renewable every ten years, ensures that both states adhere to the same principles regarding commercial affairs and customs legislation. This shared approach extends to legislation on duties closely linked to industrial production, specifically those concerning mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer.

Under a renewed compact in 1899, the monarchy was established as a single, identical commercial and customs territory. This territory includes the principality of Liechtenstein as well as Bosnia and Herzegovina.

### Financial Contributions and Budget
The net proceeds of the customs form a primary component of the common budget of the Dual Monarchy, alongside the revenues of joint ministries and proportional contributions known as the quota. This common budget funds the ministry of finance, the army, and the conduct of foreign affairs.

The quota for these expenditures is fixed for several years, typically coinciding with the duration of the commercial and customs treaty. The distribution of these contributions has shifted over time:
* Until 1897, Austria contributed 70% and Hungary 30% of the joint expenditure remaining after common revenues were deducted.
* From 1897 to July 1907, the quota was adjusted to 66-46/49 for Austria and 33-2/49 for Hungary.
* In 1907, Hungary's contribution was increased to 36.4%.

Additionally, 2% of total charges are debited to Hungary due to the incorporation of the former military frontier into that state. In 1905, the common administration's budget estimates listed customs revenue at L4,780,000, contributing to a total revenue of L20,762,410.

### Foreign Trade Statistics
The foreign trade of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy showed steady growth between 1900 and 1905. In 1900, imports were valued at L70,666,000 and exports at L80,916,000. By 1905, these figures rose to L89,430,000 for imports and L93,500,000 for exports.

## Customs in Other Jurisdictions

### Honduras
In the republic of Honduras, customs serve as a primary source of revenue, alongside monopolies on tobacco, gunpowder, and spirits. Between 1901 and 1905, the average revenue from these sources reached approximately L265,000. This revenue is used to fund expenditure in normal years of about L250,000, primarily supporting the departments of education, public works, finance, and war. Despite this, the republic has been practically bankrupt, with a foreign debt of L22,470,510 as of July 1909, much of which consists of arrears of interest.

### Buffalo Creek Customs District
In the United States, Buffalo serves as the port of entry for the Buffalo Creek customs district. As a major distributing center for raw products from the West to the East and manufactured products from the East to the West, it handles vast quantities of grain, flour, lumber, and livestock. In 1908, the imports for the Buffalo Creek customs district were valued at $6,708,919, while exports were valued at $26,192,563.

### Hong Kong
In contrast to the regulated customs systems mentioned above, Hong Kong operates as a free port. Because of this status, there are no official figures regarding the amount of trade, though the value of imports and exports is estimated at approximately L50,000,000 annually.
