# The Prussian Army under Frederick

## Organization and Training
In 1740, Prussia was a small, compact, and thoroughly organized power possessing an army of 100,000 men. At the time, it was viewed as one of Europe's minor armies, and few believed it could rival the forces of France or Austria. This perception was based on the army's limited recent experience, which consisted only of the desultory Rhine campaign of 1733-35.

Despite its size, the army was drilled to a level of perfection not previously attained. The Prussian infantry soldier was so well-equipped and trained that he could fire five shots for every three fired by an Austrian soldier. While the artillery and cavalry were considered less efficient, the overall triumph of Prussia as a military power was largely due to a fuller application of recognized principles of discipline and tactics. Furthermore, Prussia possessed a decisive advantage over Austria in administration.

## The Standing Army Theory
A primary initial advantage for Frederick was that his army had developed the standing army theory to its full effect, undisturbed by wars. This organizational superiority allowed Prussian regiments to take the field immediately. In contrast, the Austrians were forced to wait for drafts to complete their field forces. This readiness enabled Frederick to overrun Silesia almost unopposed.

## The Invasion of Silesia
On December 16, 1740, without a declaration of war, Frederick's army crossed the frontier into Silesia after concentrating quietly on the Oder. The available Austrian forces were small; their generals could only garrison a few fortresses before falling back to the mountain frontier of Moravia and Bohemia. The Prussian army soon held all of Silesia and invested the strong places of Neisse, Brieg, and Glogau.

By February 1741, the Austrians had collected a field army under Count Neipperg to reconquer the region. On the night of March 9, Prussian forces under Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Dessau stormed Glogau in one hour with a mathematical precision that earned universal admiration. Later, on April 10, 1741, Frederick's army fought its first pitched battle at Mollwitz. Although the Prussian right wing of cavalry was quickly routed, the infantry's tenacity and discipline retrieved the day. The Austrian infantry melted away before Prussian volleys, and the Austrian cavalry was shattered in repeated attempts to ride them down. Following this victory, Europe recognized the emergence of a new military power.

## Later Campaigns and the Seven Years' War
Frederick continued to engage in various campaigns, including the Second Silesian War beginning in 1744, which he initiated by secretly concluding a fresh alliance with Louis XV of France. In 1745, Frederick achieved one of his greatest victories at the battle of Hohenfriedberg (or Striegau) on June 4, where he led approximately 65,000 men to a complete defeat of Prince Charles's force of 85,000 Austrians and Saxons.

During the Seven Years' War, Frederick faced significant challenges from Austrian commanders. On June 18, 1757, Daun defeated Frederick for the first time in his career at the battle of Kolin. Frederick later won a brilliant victory at Leuthen in the same year, regaining the city of Breslau. However, the war of manoeuvre that followed saw Frederick suffer a severe defeat at Hochkirch on October 14, 1758, and was forced to raise the siege of Olmutz due to the actions of Major-General Loudon. In November of the following year, the Prussians suffered another blow when General Finck's entire corps was surrounded at Maxen and forced to surrender.

## General Military Character
The military history of this era was characterized by a disparity between grandiose political schemes and the limited means available to execute them. The small standing armies of the 18th century were capable of conquering territory by degrees but were unable to deliver a single decisive blow. Frederick was noted as the exception, succeeding completely because he possessed proportionate means to achieve a definite end. His success is attributed not only to his army's discipline but to his own personal ascendancy as an energetic and resolute leader.
