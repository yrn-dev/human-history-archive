# Badajoz

## Overview and Geography
Badajoz is a frontier province located in western Spain. Formed in 1833 from districts previously belonging to the province of Estremadura, it is the largest province in the entire kingdom, covering an area of 8,451 square miles. Its borders are defined by Caceres to the north, Cordova and Ciudad Real to the east, Seville and Huelva to the south, and Portugal to the west. As of 1900, the province had a population of 520,246.

The landscape of Badajoz is characterized largely by a desolate and monotonous plain that is either flat or slightly undulating, though low ranges of hills exist in various districts. The primary river is the Guadiana, which flows from east to west across the north of the province. Fed by numerous tributaries, the Guadiana's river-beds only fill with significant volumes of water during certain seasons, often allowing the river to be forded with ease.

The climate is marked by extreme temperatures, with fierce north and north-west winds during the winter and intense heat in the summer. In the Guadiana valley, intermittent fevers are common during the hot months. Rainfall is generally scanty, resulting in a landscape where only a small portion of the land is irrigated; the remainder is covered by thin forest, bush, or used as pasture.

## Economy and Industry
Badajoz possesses more livestock than any other Spanish province. It is particularly noted for its acorn-fed swine, which are celebrated throughout Spain for their bacon and hams. Additionally, goats and sheep thrive in areas where the pasture is too meagre to support cattle. Due to the lack of irrigation, agriculture and the cultivation of fruit—including olives and vines—remain in a backward state.

The province's mineral resources are under-exploited due to poor communication and a lack of water. In 1903, only 26 of nearly 600 registered mines were operational, producing small quantities of copper and lead. Local industries are of limited importance and include the production of oils, soaps, cork, leather, and coarse cotton and woollen stuffs.

Commercial interests are more significant than industrial ones, primarily due to the transit trade with Portugal, which is facilitated by seven custom-houses. However, development is hindered by the fact that many areas are only accessible by road, and these roads are in poor condition.

## The City of Badajoz
The capital city of Badajoz is situated on the left bank of the river Guadiana, close to the Portuguese frontier and the Madrid-Lisbon railway. In 1900, its population was 30,899. The city serves as the official residence of the captain-general of Estremadura and is the see of a bishop.

The city is built on a slight eminence and is characterized by its strong defensive appearance, featuring bastions, a broad moat, outworks, forts on surrounding heights, and a strong wall. It is overlooked by the ruins of a Moorish castle. A granite bridge crossing the Guadiana, originally built in 1460 and rebuilt in 1833, connects the castle-hill to the fort of San Cristobal. The cathedral, dating from 1258, also resembles a fortress with its massive embattled walls.

Historically, the city rose to prominence under Moorish rule and became the capital of a small Moorish kingdom in 1031. It was captured by Alphonso IX of Leon in 1229 after a period of Portuguese control (beginning in 1168). As a frontier fortress, it faced numerous sieges, including attacks by the Portuguese in 1660 and the Allies in 1705 during the War of the Spanish Succession. During the Peninsular War, the French unsuccessfully attacked in 1808 and 1809, but the city surrendered to Marshal Soult on March 10, 1811, after the Spanish commander, Jose Imaz, was bribed. A British army under Marshal Beresford attempted to retake the city, defeating a relieving force at Albuera on May 16, though the siege was abandoned in June.

The city is the birthplace of the painter Luis de Morales (1509-1586) and the statesman Manuel de Godoy, duke of Alcudia (1767-1851). Two retouched paintings by Morales are kept in the cathedral. Local industry in the capital includes pottery and the manufacture of leather, woollen, and linen goods.

## Other Towns
The province includes several other notable towns:
*   **Don Benito:** A modern town dating from the 15th century, founded by refugees from Don Llorente to avoid Guadiana floods. Located on the Madrid-Badajoz-Lisbon railway, it has a population of 16,565 (1900) and trades in fruit (especially melons), wine, and wheat, while manufacturing soap, flour, oil, brandy, cloth, and linen.
*   **Azuaga:** A town with a population of 14,192 (1900) that serves as a central market for livestock from upland pastures watered by the Bembezar and the Matachel. It manufactures pottery and coarse woollen goods.
