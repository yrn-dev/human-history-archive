# Baluchistan

Baluchistan is a sparsely populated region that covers a larger area than any Indian province except for Bengal, Madras, and Burma. It is characterized by a diverse geography and a complex political status, such that it can no longer be regarded as a distinct entity among Asiatic nations in the way that Afghanistan is.

## Political Status and Administration

The region is divided into several administrative and political zones. British Baluchistan is an official province within the Baluchistan Agency, which extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. This province is leased from Kalat and is brought under the ordinary civil administration of British India. Officially, British Baluchistan includes the districts of Thal-Chotiali, Sibi, and Peshin. While these districts were nominally handed over to Kalat forty years prior to 1879, they were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, as they had originally been Afghan.

Beyond the official province lies "independent Baluchistan," which remains under British political control. The degree of independence in these districts generally varies in proportion to their distance from Quetta. Although the political officer's reach extends to all parts of Baluchistan, he is not frequently seen in many areas. Supreme control is practically exercised by the agent to the governor-general of India and a staff of political assistants. The political status of the province as a whole is described as being almost precisely that of the native states of the Indian peninsula.

## Geography and Climate

Baluchistan is divided into two distinct parts by the railway line from the Indus to Quetta, along with its associated plains and passes. 

The northern section consists of long ridges of picturesque highlands and the central ranges of the Suliman system. This area is hedged between the plains of the Indus and Afghanistan. The average breadth of this Pathan district is 150 miles, narrowing to less than 100 miles at the Gomal and expanding to over 200 miles at the railway line. The climate here is clear and bracing with no great extremes of temperature, and vegetation is often alpine.

The southern section is a square block of territory measuring roughly 300 miles by 300 miles. This area is primarily the home of the Baluch and the Brahui. It includes the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, where drainage cuts deep gorges into the hills before passing east into the plains of Sind. Other streams either run to the Arabian Sea north of Karachi or disappear into inland swamps. 

British Baluchistan specifically comprises an irregularly shaped block of upland territory. It includes the Toba plateau, the upper Lora tributaries, the basins of the Nari and Bolan as far as Sibi, and the Thal Chotiali valley. The climate of this province is dry and exhilarating, though extreme temperatures can lead to severe forms of fever.

Quetta is situated on an open plain 5,500 feet above sea level, surrounded by grand peaks including Takatu to the north, Khalifat to the east, Chahiltan to the south-west, and Murdar to the south, with heights ranging from 10,500 to 11,500 feet.

## Geology

The mountain ranges are composed chiefly of Tertiary and Cretaceous beds arranged in a series of folds. These folds form a system stretching from Mount Ararat to Peshawar, with a subsidiary loop at Sibi forming the Bugti Hills and the Suliman. Jurassic rocks are found in the hills of British Baluchistan, and Triassic beds are located along the south side of the upper Zhob. Most deposits, excluding later Tertiary beds, are marine. North-western Baluchistan features volcanic tuffs and conglomerates. A line of recent, mostly extinct, volcanic cones extends from western Baluchistan into eastern Persia.

## Ethnography and Population

The region is home to a variety of races and tribes:

*   **Rind Confederation:** True Baluchis, including the Bugti and Marri sections, occupy the curved wrinkles ending the Suliman system. They claim Koraish extraction and Arab origin. The Bozdars are another Rind clan located to the north.
*   **Pathans:** Prominent tribal divisions include the Dumars and Kakars of Pishin and the Mando Khel of Zhob.
*   **Persian and Other Races:** A underlying Persian (Tajik) population is scattered throughout the country, often serving as agricultural bondsmen or slaves (such as the Nakibs, Dehkans, or Durzadas). The Naushirwanis of the Mashkel and Kharan deserts are a purely Persian race who entered Baluchistan in historic times.
*   **Nomads:** The Rekis (desert people) are found in the central desert (also known as the Kir, Kej, Katz, or Kash Kaian). The Mohamadani are the most numerous of these nomads and are likely of Arab origin.

## Persian Baluchistan

Persian Baluchistan is a province of Persia covering approximately 60,000 square miles along the northern shore of the Arabian Sea. It is bounded by Kerman to the west, Seistan and the central Persian desert to the north, and independent and British Baluchistan to the east. The land is largely composed of barren hills, stony deserts, and waterless plains. Its principal rivers, the Bampur and Mashkid, flow away from the sea and are lost in depressions called *hamuns*. 

Administratively, it is a division of the province of Kerman, subdivided into twenty districts, including Makran (the country of the Ichthyophagi). The total population is under 200,000. The province remained practically independent until Persian troops occupied Bampur in 1849, with supremacy over some eastern districts not recognized until 1872.
