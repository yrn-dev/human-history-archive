# The Central Depression of Asia: Gobi and Lop Nor

The central depression of the Asian continent is a vast interior region that extends laterally along the 40th parallel north. This depression begins east of the Gobi desert and reaches westwards through Mongolia into Chinese Turkestan, eventually extending to the foot of the Pamir plateau. To the west of Kashgar, the depression is bounded by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol, with the Sarikol serving as the eastern face of the Pamir.

## Physical Geography and Topography
The region is characterized as a vast, almost waterless interior space where local drainage is typically lost in swamps or deserts. The level of this depression, which is believed to have once been a vast inland sea, generally does not exceed 2,000 feet above sea level. Modern research indicates that in the central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, the elevation may actually be below sea level.

To the north, the continental level rises gradually from the low flats of Siberia toward a great central water-parting. This divide stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and Elburz to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, continuing to the Bering Strait. While some parts of this divide, such as the Altai, Khangai, and Hindu Kush, reach great heights, other portions remain below 5,000 feet.

Between the low-lying sands of the Gobi and Tibet lies a system of elevated valleys known as Tsaidam. Situated 8,000 to 9,000 feet above sea level, Tsaidam forms an intermediate steppe. This region is bounded to the south by the Kuen-lun mountains, which extend from the frontiers of Kashmir north of Leh eastward to the Chinese province of Kansu. The Kuen-lun mountains serve as the northern limit of the Chang Tang, the elevated highland steppes of Tibet. From this chain, a minor system branches northeast at approximately 83 degrees E. longitude, culminating in the Altyn Tagh and extending as a continuous water-divide to the Nan Shan mountains, north of the Koko Nor basin.

## Exploration and Discovery
The geography of this region has been clarified through the work of several explorers and scientists. 

### Nicolai Prjevalsky
Between 1871 and 1873, the Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky crossed the Gobi desert from the north to Kansu in western China. During this period, he mapped the hydrography and defined the geography of Tsaidam, the region from which the great rivers of Burma, Siam, and China emanate. Prjevalsky's later travels included:
* **1876:** A visit to Lop Nor and the discovery of the Altyn Tagh range.
* **1879:** An expedition following the Urangi river to the Altai Mountains. During this journey, he crossed and named the Dzungarian extension of the Gobi desert and traversed the Gobi from Hami to Sachu. He also visited the sources of the Salween and the Hwang-ho (Yellow River).
* **1883-1885:** A journey to Sining, a major trade center of the Chinese borderland, passing through northern Tibet, crossing the Altyn Tagh to Lop Nor, and traveling via the Cherchen-Keriya trade route to Khotan, from where he followed the Tarim to Aksu.

### Sven Hedin and Dr. M.A. Stein
The researches of Sven Hedin revealed the periodic shifting of the Lop Nor system. Together with Dr. M.A. Stein, Hedin demonstrated that the sand-strewn wastes of the Turkestan desert once housed highly civilized centers of industry and Buddhist art.

## Geological Hypotheses
Russian scientists have investigated the depression westward of the Aral and Caspian basins. Previous theories suggesting an ancient connection between the Caspian and the Oxus have been displaced by a hypothesis proposing that the Caspian Sea extended eastwards into Trans-Caspian territory during the post-Pleiocene age. 

Evidence for this hypothesis includes the discovery of shells—species currently living in the Caspian—found 100 meters inland at altitudes between 140 and 280 feet above the current Caspian level. Additionally, the Kara-kum sands appear to be a purely marine formation, lacking the fluviatile deposits that would be expected if they were delta deposits of the Oxus.

## Nomenclature
Terminology regarding the mountain ranges of the region varies. The Altyn Tagh is etymologically correct as Astin-tagh or Astun-tagh, meaning the "Nearer" or "Lower Mountains." In contrast, Ustun-tagh refers to the "Farther" or "Higher Mountains," a term that would appropriately describe the more southerly of the twin border-ranges of the Tibetan plateau. Other regional terms include the Pe-shan (the Northern Mountains in the Gobi) and Arka-tagh (meaning the "Remoter" or "Farther Mountains").
