# Belgium

## Early History and Unification
The history of the Belgian people is traced back to the time of Caesar in 57 B.C., who described the Belgians as the bravest of all the people of Gaul. While Germanic invasions in the fourth and fifth centuries introduced new ethnical elements to the population, the region remained politically divided for centuries. The east, known as Lotharingia, was part of the medieval German empire, while the west, Flanders, was subjugated to the kingdom of France. These two distinct parts were separated by the river Scheldt. 

The unification of the Belgian duchies and counties under a single dynasty was achieved in the fifteenth century by the dukes of Burgundy. Later, the Treaty of the Barriers imposed obligations on Belgium toward the United Provinces of Holland. Under this treaty, the Belgian sovereign had to allow Dutch garrisons in the fortresses of Knocke, Ypres, Warneton, Furnes, Tournai, and Namur to protect Holland from France. Belgium was required to pay a heavy yearly subsidy for these garrisons and recognize the closing of the Scheldt, as mandated by the Treaty of Munster (1648). Additionally, Holland claimed the right to block Belgian trade with the Indies. In an attempt to restore national trade, Charles VI founded the “Compagnie d’Ostende” via an imperial charter for trade with America, but the company was eventually disbanded due to opposition from England, France, and Holland.

## The United Kingdom of the Netherlands and the 1830 Revolt
Following the French conquest of Belgium, England sought to prevent France from controlling Antwerp, which Lord Castlereagh suggested would necessitate a continuous state of war. To create a bulwark against France, the idea of a neutral kingdom of the Netherlands was proposed by England and supported by Prussia and the Duke of Wellington. On July 31, 1814, the Belgian provinces, including Luxemburg and Liège, were handed over to the Prince of Orange.

However, the union between the Dutch and Belgians was strained by differences in religion, habits, and ideas. The Belgian people, including Walloons and Flemings, were deeply devoted to the Catholic faith, while the influence of the French republic and empire had left a mark on the population, making the French language common in public proceedings and law courts. Conflict arose over the Fundamental Law promulgated by William I; Liberals opposed the king's extensive powers, and Catholics condemned the principles of freedom of the press and worship.

In 1830, a revolt led to the declaration of Belgium as an independent state. A conference of powers held in London on November 4, 1830, saw Van der Weyer represent Belgian interests. On December 20, a motion by Lord Palmerston declared Belgium an “independent power.” The new Belgian constitution was based on medieval charters and privileges, specifically the “Joyeuse Entrée” of Brabant, and added modern liberties including freedom of association, the press, worship, and educational teaching. Equality before the law was established, and voting rights were granted to Belgians paying a certain amount in taxes.

## Neutrality and the First World War
The first King of the Belgians focused on developing economic resources and maintaining the permanent neutrality imposed by the Treaty of London. Queen Victoria of England acted as a protector, asserting in 1852 that any violation of Belgian neutrality would be a *casus belli* for her government.

In 1912, King Albert I and Premier Baron Charles de Brogueville obtained a new army bill to reinforce the Belgian army. On August 2, 1914, the German Minister to Brussels requested passage through Belgium for troops heading to France. The Belgian government refused the request, deciding to oppose the invaders with arms despite an army of only 115,000 men who lacked big guns and machine guns. This invasion of a neutral state, guaranteed by the great powers, led the British government to mobilize its army and fleet. On August 4, Britain declared war on Germany to defend its treaty obligations to Belgium.

## The Congo Free State
King Leopold II took a personal interest in the commercial and exploration development of equatorial Africa. In 1878, he financed the *Comite des Etudes du Haut Congo*, which became the International Association of the Congo, presided over by Colonel M. Strauch. Through the efforts of H.M. Stanley in Africa and King Leopold in Europe, the Association was recognized as an independent state by the powers between 1884 and 1885. 

The Belgian chambers authorized the king to lead this state, named the *Etat Independent du Congo*. While the union was officially personal, the state's headquarters were in Brussels and its officials were almost exclusively Belgian. In 1889, Leopold bequeathed the state to Belgium, and by 1890, the Belgian government acquired the right of annexation in exchange for financial aid. Immediate annexation was resisted by both the king and the Belgian public until reports of misgovernment in the Congo sparked international agitation for reform. This led to a new treaty of cession in November 1907.

## Academic and Cultural Contributions
The Bollandists, who continued their work after the dispersion of the Jesuits, operated in Antwerp until 1778 before moving to the monastery of canons regular of Coudenberg in Brussels. Their work ceased in 1788, and their library was acquired by the Premonstratensians of Tongerloo. In 1837, the work was resumed at the college of St Michael in Brussels with the support of the Belgian government and the suggestion of the Academie Royale of Belgium. Between 1845 and 1883, they published a new series of the *October* volumes, with contributions from several Jesuit fathers, including Joseph van Hecke and Benjamin Bossue.
