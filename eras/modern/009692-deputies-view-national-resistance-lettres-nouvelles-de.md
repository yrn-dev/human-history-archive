# Deputies with a View to a National Resistance

The concept of utilizing deputies or legislative bodies to organize national resistance appears in various historical contexts within the archive, ranging from the Roman Empire to the Napoleonic era and the French Revolution. These instances typically involve the mobilization of political representatives to counter perceived tyranny, foreign invasion, or the collapse of a regime.

## Roman Senatorial Resistance
During the reign of Emperor Maximin, the Roman senate assumed the reins of government to vindicate the cause of freedom through arms, reacting to a period of military license and wanton despotism. To organize the defense of Italy, the senate selected twenty consular senators, chosen for their merit and services, to command armies and conduct war. These leaders were tasked with fortifying highways and ports and disciplining the youth of Italy.

Simultaneously, the senate dispatched a number of deputies to the governors of various provinces. These deputies were selected from the equestrian and senatorian orders and were the most illustrious of their ranks. Their mission was to conjure the governors to assist their country and remind the nations of their ancient ties of friendship with the Roman people and senate. The zeal and respect with which these deputies were received indicated that the population feared the oppression of Maximin more than they feared resistance.

## The French Revolution and the National Assembly
In a different context of national resistance, the Third Estate of France asserted its role as the sole representative of the nation. After six weeks of dispute regarding whether voting should be conducted by head or by estate, the Third Estate declared that no taxation could be levied without its consent. 

When the king closed their meeting hall and suggested the deputies return home, they instead convened in a tennis-court. There, they took the "Oath of the Tennis Court," pledging not to separate until a constitution had been established for France. Although the king initially attempted to disperse the deputies by force, his soldiers refused to act. This led to the acceptance of the principle that the Three Estates would vote together as one National Assembly. Following subsequent attempts by the king to reverse these concessions—including the deployment of foreign regiments under Marshal de Broglie—Paris and France revolted, leading to the creation of the National Guard to resist the forces of the crown.

## Napoleon and the Chamber of Deputies
During the Hundred Days in 1815, Napoleon attempted to leverage the Chamber of Deputies to foster national resistance. Four days after the Battle of Waterloo, Napoleon urged his brother, Joseph, to "inspirit" the Chamber of Deputies for this purpose. 

However, the archive notes that Joseph lacked the strength and energy of his brother, being too supine and luxurious for such tasks. Consequently, Joseph's actions were insignificant; rather than mobilizing the Chamber for national resistance, he focused primarily on assisting Napoleon's plans to escape to America.

## Other Political and Literary "Lettres"
The archive contains various references to "Lettres" (letters) used for political or social commentary, though these differ from the formal diplomatic missions of deputies. For example, Alexandre Dumas wrote a *Nouvelle Lettre de Junius* and *Lettres sur les choses du jour* between 1870 and 1872, calling France to amendment and repentance during hours of humiliation. Similarly, the deputy Cormenin used a series of pamphlets and his *Lettres sur la liste civile* to oppose the government of Charles X and attack political and administrative abuses.
