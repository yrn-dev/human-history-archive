# The Battle of Carabobo and the Liberation of Venezuela

The Battle of Carabobo was a decisive military engagement that effectively ended the war for independence in Venezuela. It resulted in a complete rout of the Spanish forces and paved the way for the establishment of permanent political institutions in the region.

## Prelude to the Conflict
Following his return to Angostura, Simon Bolivar was hailed as the father and deliverer of his country. On December 17, 1819, he obtained the enactment of a fundamental law that united the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar serving as president. The seat of government was provisionally moved to Rosario de Cucuta.

Bolivar subsequently took the field with the best-appointed and most numerous army the republicans had assembled to date. He gained significant advantages over the Spanish forces commanded by Morillo. On November 25, 1820, an armistice of six months was concluded at Truxillo, intended to spare further bloodshed and encourage the Spaniards to reach terms. However, these hopes were disappointed. Morillo was recalled and replaced by General Torre. Once the armistice expired, a renewal of the contest became inevitable.

## The Battle of Carabobo
Determined to strike a decisive blow, Bolivar encountered General Torre at Carabobo. The resulting battle was a complete victory for the republican forces, routing the Spanish army. The shattered remains of the Spanish forces were forced to retreat and take refuge in Puerto Cabello.

The victory at Carabobo is considered the event that put an end to the war in Venezuela. Following the battle, Bolivar entered Caracas on June 29, 1821. By the end of that year, Spanish forces had been driven from every part of the province, with the sole exception of Puerto Cabello.

## Aftermath and Political Consolidation
Following the military victory, the focus shifted toward securing independence through permanent political institutions. On August 30, 1821, the constitution of Colombia was adopted with general approval. Under this new government, Bolivar continued as president, and Santander served as vice-president.

The Spanish forces remaining at Puerto Cabello held out for two years after the battle before eventually surrendering to Paez.

## Related Military Figures and Context
The liberation of Venezuela involved several key figures and previous campaigns. Earlier in the conflict, Bolivar had operated against Spanish troops on the Magdalena river and later entered Venezuela, where he issued a "war to the death" decree. He achieved a crushing defeat of Spanish troops under Monteverde at Lastoguanes, leading to his triumphant entry into Caracas on August 4, 1813. During that period, Monteverde had also taken refuge in Puerto Cabello.

Additionally, J.A. Paez played a critical role in the republican effort. In February 1817, after a three-day conflict that defeated the royalists, Paez and his *llaneros* encountered the retreating forces of Morillo, inflicting an additional and more complete overthrow.

## Later Disturbances
Despite the victory at Carabobo and the establishment of the Republic of Colombia, internal instability later emerged. Paez, commanding in Venezuela, eventually entered a state of open rebellion against the government after being accused of arbitrary conduct regarding the enrolment of citizens of Caracas into the militia. He was encouraged by a disaffected party in the northern departments seeking separation from the republic. In response to these disturbances, Bolivar returned to the region, reaching Puerto Cabello on December 31, 1826, where he issued a decree of general amnesty and held a friendly meeting with Paez.
