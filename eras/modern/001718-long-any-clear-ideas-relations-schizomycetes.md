# Schizomycetes

Schizomycetes, commonly known as bacteria, are minute vegetable organisms that lack chlorophyll. They are characterized by the absence of an ordinary nucleus and the absence of ordinary sexual reproduction. These organisms consist of single cells that multiply through repeated bipartitions. Their cellular shapes vary, appearing as spherical, cylindrical, or oblong, and they may also form filamentous structures or other aggregates of cells.

## Classification and Morphology
Due to their manner of division and the absence of a nucleus and sexual reproduction, Schizomycetes resemble the Schizophyceae (blue-green algae or Cyanophyceae). Consequently, these two groups are typically united under the class Schizophyta, reflecting the view that most typical bacteria are derived from Cyanophyceae. However, other relationships have been suggested: the group *Sarcina* shares algal analogues with the Palmellaceae (green algae), and the Myxobacteriaceae suggest a link to Myxomycetes. Furthermore, the formation of endospores and the existence of ciliated micrococci recall flagellate Protozoa such as *Chromulina* and *Monas*.

Morphologically, elongated forms may be straight, curved, or twisted into corkscrew-like spirals. Slight sinuosity defines the *Vibrio* form, while more pronounced spirals are known as *Spirochaete* or *Spirillum*. Some Schizomycetes form aggregates such as hollow or irregular branched colonies, or plates, which may result from cell displacement after division or divisions occurring in two or three planes, as seen in *Sarcina*.

The question of pleomorphism—the mutability of shape—has been a subject of intense discussion. In 1873, Lankester noted that *Bacterium rubescens* (later named *Beggiatoa roseo-persicina*) passes through various conditions that might be mistaken for separate species or genera, such as *Bacillus* or *Micrococcus*. While Billroth suggested in 1874 that all bacteria were different states of a single organism called *Cocco-bacteria septica*, it is now generally agreed that while some forms show different cell types during their life-history, the majority remain uniform.

## Distribution and Habitat
Schizomycetes are ubiquitous saprophytes. They are found in the sea, running streams, rivers, still ponds, and ditches, and are especially prevalent in soil, bogs, refuse heaps, and drains. Any solid food-stuff (such as vegetables or meat preserves) or liquid containing organic matter (including milk, beer, blood, and urine) will soon swarm with bacteria if moisture is present, the temperature is not abnormal, and the substance is exposed to air.

Their distribution in the air is uneven. In open country and high altitudes, particularly in the north, few or no Schizomycetes may be detected. In towns, they may exist in minute clouds with empty interspaces, whereas laboratories and closed spaces where cultivation is promoted may have air considerably laden with them. This distribution is influenced by wind, rain, temperature changes, and movement.

As parasites, certain Schizomycetes inhabit the organs of animals and humans. They exist in every part of the animal alimentary canal, except perhaps where acid secretions prevail. While some may be the forerunners of damage—such as those that destroy teeth—they are not always directly harmful. Plants are generally less subject to attack, possibly because the acid fluids of higher vegetable organisms are less suited for their development, though some parasitic forms on plants are known.

## The Germ Theory of Disease
The "germ theory of disease" gained acceptance from approximately 1870 onwards. Early thinkers had suggested resemblances between fermentation and certain diseases, proposing that a *contagium* or virus might be a minute organism capable of reproducing and spreading. This evolved into a definite shape through the ferment theories of Schwann (1837), Cagniard de la Tour (1828), and Pasteur.

Key milestones in the development of this theory include:
* **1850–1855:** P. F. O. Rayer, Davaine, and Pollender observed bacilli in the blood of animals dead of anthrax (splenic fever).
* **1863:** Davaine, influenced by Pasteur, proposed that anthrax bacilli caused splenic fever, which was later proved via inoculation.
* **1870–1871:** Pasteur proved a silkworm disease was caused by a bacterium, and Oertel showed that *Micrococcus* was intimately concerned in producing diphtheria.
* **1872:** Cohn grouped several "pathogenous" Schizomycetes together.
* **1876:** Koch published observations on Davaine's bacilli, confirming their causal relation to splenic fever and discovering their saprophytic phase and spores.

It is now clear that all infectious and contagious diseases result from the action of bacteria, or in some cases, protozoa or fungi. This includes tuberculosis, glanders, septicaemia, relapsing fever, and fowl-cholera.

## Fermentation and Putrefaction
The relationship between Schizomycetes and fermentation has been central to biological understanding. In 1857, Pasteur demonstrated that lactic fermentation depends on an organism. Earlier researches by Helmholtz (1843) and Schwann (1837) had already linked putrefaction and fermentation to organisms derived from the air. In 1862, Pasteur proved that the ammoniacal fermentation of urea was caused by a minute Schizomycete, a finding confirmed by van Tieghem (1864) and Cohn (1874).

By 1872, it became clear that no putrefaction can occur without bacteria or another living organism. This evidence, supported by researchers such as Tyndall, Lister, de Bary, Brefeld, Roberts, and Burdon-Sanderson, refuted the theory of "spontaneous generation." These investigations led to a better understanding of the high resistance of Schizomycetes spores to physical agents and the development of antiseptic treatments, which Lister and Koch applied to surgery.

## Ecological and Industrial Importance
The vast majority of Schizomycetes are not pathogenic; many are harmless or indispensable to natural operations. They play a supreme role in agriculture and daily life through useful fermentations. These include the preparation of tobacco, the soaking of skins for tanning, the production of indigo, and the creation of ensilage and hay. Additionally, some species are capable of fixing free nitrogen and carbon-assimilation, while others perform various unsuspected decompositions.
