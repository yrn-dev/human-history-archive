# Philosophy of Religions and Related Philosophical Works

The study of the philosophy of religion and associated philosophical problems in the late 19th century was characterized by a diverse range of contributions from American, British, German, and Danish scholars. These works often intersected with theology, psychology, and ethics, reflecting a broad academic effort to synthesize spiritual concepts with philosophical inquiry.

## American Contributions
John Bascom (1827–1903), an American educationalist and philosophical writer, produced several works addressing the intersection of faith and reason. His publications include *Science, Philosophy and Religion* (1871) and *Philosophy of Religions* (1876). Bascom continued to explore these themes in later works such as *The New Theology* (1891), *Evolution and Religion* (1896), and *God and His Goodness* (1901). Bascom's academic career included serving as a professor of rhetoric at Williams College and as the president and professor of mental and moral philosophy at the University of Wisconsin from 1874 to 1887.

## British and European Perspectives
In Britain, the philosophy of religion was advanced by figures such as the Caird brothers. Edward Caird (1835–1908), a philosopher and theologian who co-founded a school of orthodox neo-Hegelianism with Thomas Hill Green, delivered the Gifford Lectures from 1891 to 1892, which resulted in the publication of *Evolution of Religion*. His brother, John Caird, authored *Introd. to the Phil. of Religion* (1880). Other notable British contributions to the field include J. Royce's *Religious Aspect of Philosophy* (1885) and *The Conception of God* (1897), as well as R. B. Haldane's *The Pathway to Reality* (1903) and H. Jones's *Idealism as a Practical Creed* (1909).

In Germany, the philosopher Lotze delivered annual lectures on various branches of philosophy, including the philosophy of religion. While he embodied much of his work in the *System der Philosophie* (with volumes on *Logik* and *Metaphysik*), a planned third volume intended to treat the principal problems of the philosophy of religion and art never appeared before his death in 1881. Additionally, Otto Pfleiderer published a *Philosophy of Religion* (with an English translation of volume ii appearing in 1887).

In Denmark, H. Höffding (b. 1843) emerged as a prominent contributor to psychology; his works *Philosophy of Religion* and *Problems of Philosophy* were translated into English in 1906.

## Interdisciplinary Philosophical Problems
Beyond the specific study of religion, the late 19th century saw a proliferation of works addressing general "problems in philosophy" and specialized branches of the discipline:

*   **Logic and Psychology:** Works such as F. H. Bradley's *Principles of Logic* (1883) and J. Dewey's *Psychology* (1886) sought to define the structures of thought and mental processes.
*   **Ethics:** This period saw the publication of F. H. Bradley's *Ethical Studies* (1876), J. Dewey's *Ethics* (1891), and J. S. Mackenzie's *Manual of Ethics* (1900).
*   **Empirio-criticism:** Richard Heinrich Ludwig Avenarius (1843–1896) attempted to coordinate thought and action in *Kritik der reinen Erfahrung* (1888-1890), proposing an idealistic dualism known as Empirio-criticism.

## Historical Context of Philosophical Method
The evolution of these philosophical inquiries can be traced back to earlier methodological shifts, such as those introduced by Bacon. Bacon viewed natural philosophy as the "mother of all the sciences," dividing human knowledge into memory (history), imagination (poesy), and reason (philosophy). Within this framework, philosophy was concerned with God, nature, or man. Natural philosophy was further divided into speculative (theoretical) and operative (practical) branches, with the speculative side encompassing physics (efficient and material causes) and metaphysics (formal and final causes).
