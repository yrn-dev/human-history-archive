# British Baluchistan: Peshin, Sibi, and Thal-Chotiali

British Baluchistan is an irregularly-shaped block of upland territory that serves as a distinctive province under the ordinary forms of civil administration in British India. It is leased from Kalat and is managed by the agent to the governor-general of India, who exercises supreme control with a staff of political assistants. The province is incorporated with British India as of a resolution dated November 1, 1887.

## Territorial Composition and Boundaries
The territory of British Baluchistan is defined by several geographical markers. To the west, it rests on the Kwaja Amran (Kojak) Range, including an advanced loop that encompasses the Chaman railway terminus. It reaches south through Shorarud to Nushki and extends an arm to the east to embrace the Thal Chotiali valley. To the south-east, it includes the basins of the Nari and Bolan as far as Sibi. The northern boundary follows the main water-divide between the Lora and the Zhob, and the province includes the Toba plateau and all upper Lora tributaries.

The broader Baluchistan Agency extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. While British Baluchistan is a formal province, other areas of Baluchistan exist in varying degrees of independence, though all remain subject to British control. The level of independence in these districts generally varies in direct proportion to their distance from Quetta.

## The Districts of Peshin, Sibi, and Thal-Chotiali
British Baluchistan officially includes the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These areas were originally Afghan, though they had nominally been handed over to Kalat forty years prior to 1879. The nebulous claims of Afghanistan to Pishin and Sibi were formally disposed of by the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, which transferred these districts to British authority.

Following the incorporation of the region into British India in 1887, the territory was divided into two administrative districts: Thai Chotiali and Quetta-Pishin. These are administered by a regular staff and a deputy-commissioner.

## Infrastructure and Geography
The region's infrastructure has been significantly developed through the projection of railways, telegraphs, and the construction of roads. The Sind-Pishin railway was aligned from the plains to the plateau via the Bolan route. From Sibi, the line originally followed the Bolan stream, but due to destructive floods, it was rerouted through the Mashkaf valley, which debouches into the plains near Sibi, before joining the Bolan at Mach. An alternative, longer route for ordinary traffic exists via the Harnai valley, starting from Sibi in the opposite direction of the Bolan and entering the hills at Nari.

The climate of British Baluchistan is described as dry, bracing, and exhilarating, and is generally favorable for Europeans, although extreme temperatures can lead to severe forms of fever.

## Local Populations and Governance
The administrative staff of the province is usually composed of military officers. The region is home to various groups, including the Bugti, a Baluch tribe of Rind (Arab) origin numbering approximately 15,500. The Bugtis occupy the hills between Sibi and Jacobabad, east of the Sind-Peshin railway. Historically described as fine horsemen and hereditary robbers, the Bugtis were not brought under control until the rule of Sir Robert Sandeman. They have become fairly orderly since the construction of the railway, which outflanks their country.

The broader region of Baluchistan has a population that is largely nomadic, as evidenced by the annual Brahui migration, where as many as 15,000 camels have been counted in the Bolan Pass in a single month. As of 1901, the total population of Baluchistan was 914,551 over an area of 132,315 square miles. The population is overwhelmingly Mahommedan; out of every 100,000 inhabitants, 94,403 are Mussulman and 4,706 are Hindus, with the remainder consisting of Sikhs, Christians, and other denominations.
