# The Dispute Between Chile and Bolivia Regarding Territorial Occupation

The territorial dispute between Chile and Bolivia centered on the occupation of Bolivian provinces and the subsequent loss of Bolivia's access to the sea. This conflict was the result of a series of wars, secret agreements, and treaties spanning several decades of the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

## Origins and Early Agreements
The roots of the tension lay in economic interests and boundary agreements. Under a treaty from 1866, Bolivia recognized the 24th parallel as the boundary of Chilean territory. This agreement granted Chile full trading facilities and a half share of the customs on the coast between the 23rd and 24th parallels. Additionally, Chile was permitted to mine and export products from this district without tax or hindrance from Bolivia. In 1870, Bolivia further granted an Anglo-Chilean company the right to work nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel in exchange for $10,000.

These arrangements created discontent within Bolivia and Peru, as Chilean capital established a strong hold in the rich Tarapaca district. Consequently, on February 6, 1873, Bolivia and Peru entered into a secret agreement for mutual defense and the preservation of their territorial integrity against potential exterior aggression, specifically contemplating further encroachments by Chile.

## The War of 1878–1881 and Initial Losses
Conflict erupted in a war between 1878 and 1881. During this period, Bolivia took little to no part in the struggle throughout 1880, while the war was primarily maintained at sea between Chile and Peru. In January 1881, the battles of Miraflores and Chorrillos took place, characterized by heavy slaughter and savage excesses by Chilean troops. These events led to the surrender of Callao and Lima, leaving Chile as the practical master of Peru.

Following these events, the Bolivian army remained at Oruro while the Chilean army of occupation concentrated at Arequipa. Negotiations led to a peace treaty signed on December 11, 1883. Under the terms of this treaty, Bolivia ceded its entire sea-coast to Chile, including the port of Cobija. Additionally, the railways of the Litoral department, totaling 282 miles in length, passed under Chilean control.

## The Dispute over Tacna and Arica
Following the initial conflict, a long-standing dispute persisted regarding the Chilean occupation of the former Bolivian provinces of Arica and Tacna under the Parto de Tregna of April 4, 1884. By 1895, there was a possibility that Chile might concede an outlet to the sea for Bolivia in exchange for Bolivia recognizing Chilean ownership of Tacna and Arica. However, Chile abandoned this conciliatory stance after discovering secret negotiations between Bolivia and Argentina.

Bolivia found itself in a weak position, unable to compel concessions from Chile or venture into hostilities. The situation was further complicated when the boundary dispute between Argentina and Chile was settled, removing Bolivia's hope for Argentine support.

## The Treaty of 1905 and Final Settlement
Recognizing the necessity of yielding, President Pando and his successor, Ismail Montes (who took office in 1904), sought the best possible terms for a settlement. A treaty was ratified in 1905. While the republic of Bolivia was compelled to cede the maritime provinces occupied by Chile since the war of 1881—effectively leaving Bolivia without a seaport—the treaty provided several advantages.

Chile agreed to the following terms:
* The construction of a railway from Arica to the Bolivian capital, La Paz, at Chile's own expense. This line was 295 miles long, with the Bolivian section becoming Bolivian property fifteen years after completion.
* The granting of free transit for Bolivians through Chilean territory to specific coastal towns.
* The payment of a cash indemnity to Bolivia.
* The provision of pecuniary assistance for the construction of other interior railways to help open the country. Chile agreed to pay interest (not exceeding 5%) on capital invested in certain interior railways if constructed within thirty years, with a yearly cap of £100,000 and an aggregate limit of £1,600,000.

## Geography and Impact
As a result of these repeated losses of territory in boundary disputes with neighboring states, Bolivia became an inland republic. It is the third largest political division in South America, bounded by Brazil to the north and east, Paraguay and Argentina to the south, and Peru and Chile to the west. Because of these territorial revisions, official estimates of Bolivia's area vary, with figures ranging from 515,156 to 703,633 square miles.
