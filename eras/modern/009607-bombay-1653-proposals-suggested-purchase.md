# Bombay

Bombay is the chief seaport of western India and the capital of the Bombay Presidency. Situated at 18 deg. 55' N. and 72 deg. 54' E., the city is located on an island of the same name, which is part of a group now linked to the mainland via causeways. As of 1901, the population of the island and town was 776,006, with a 1906 estimate of 977,822. It is the second most populous city in the Indian empire, trailing Calcutta. While the region lacks the fertility of the Ganges valley or a comparable great waterway, Bombay is a major railway center, a primary port due to its proximity to Europe, and holds a monopoly on the cotton industry.

## Early History and British Acquisition

The first English settlement in the Bombay presidency occurred in 1618, when the East India Company established a factory at Surat under a charter from Emperor Jahangir. The English recognized the value of Bombay as a naval base, leading to the battle of Swally (1614-1615) and an unsuccessful attempt to gain possession of the island in 1626. By 1653, proposals were suggested for the purchase of the island from the Portuguese. In 1654, the Company's directors highlighted the safety of the harbor from land attacks to Cromwell.

Prior to British control, the island did not prosper under Portuguese rule. Lands were parcelled into fiefs via a system called *aforamento*, granted to individuals or religious corporations in exchange for quit-rents or military service. The Jesuits and Franciscans divided the northern districts and built several churches. Due to the intolerance of this rule, the settlement's population was only 10,000 by 1661.

The island was ceded to the English crown in 1661 as part of the dower of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. However, the British did not actually occupy the island until 1665, facing significant opposition from the Portuguese and religious orders.

## Administration and Expansion

The English crown's administration of Bombay was unsuccessful, and the acquisition was lightly esteemed in England. Consequently, in 1668, the crown transferred Bombay to the East India Company for an annual payment of £10, placing it under the factory of Surat. The Company was granted powers for the administration of justice and defense. A European regiment was enrolled, and fortifications were built, which successfully deterred a Dutch attack in 1673.

In 1687, Bombay became the head of all Company possessions in India, though it became subordinate to the government of Calcutta in 1753. The English first collided with the Mahratta power in 1774. This led to the 1782 treaty of Salbai, where Salsette was ceded to the British and Broach was handed to Sindhia. Following the second Mahratta war, which ended in 1803, the East India Company received the districts of Kaira and Broach; Surat had previously been annexed in 1800.

## Infrastructure and Military Organization

Bombay serves as a central hub for the province's railways. Most lines concentrate at Bombay City, including the Indian Midland, Great Indian Peninsula, Rajputana-Malwa, Southern Mahratta, and Bombay, Baroda & Central India systems. In 1905, the Bombay government had 7,980 miles of railway open for traffic (excluding the North-Western line in Sind). Outside of Sind, the presidency's water supply is generally unsuitable for large irrigation works.

In 1904, Lord Kitchener abolished the old Bombay command, replacing it with the Western army corps under a lieutenant-general. This corps was split into three divisions:
* **4th Division:** Headquartered at Quetta, covering Sind and Quetta districts.
* **5th Division:** Headquartered at Mhow, covering the Bombay district north of the Tapti and several other districts including Nagpur and Deesa.
* **6th Division:** Headquartered at Poona, covering the Bombay district south of the Tapti, Aden, and other districts including Belgaum and Aurungabad.

In September 1914, Bombay served as a transit point for military movements, including the dispatch of the third Division and seven battalions to France and German East Africa, as well as the exchange of British Indian regular artillery batteries for Territorial batteries.

## Bombay Furniture

The city, along with Ahmedabad and Surat, produces "Bombay blackwood furniture." This is crafted from Shisham or blackwood (*Dalbergia*), a dark-coloured, hard-grained timber that can be polished. While some pieces are considered clumsy due to original Dutch models, smaller items like ornamental stands and flower tables are highly prized for their graceful contours. The finest carving is lace-like and demonstrates the worker's ingenuity. These artisans often follow inherited traditions, with designs passed down through generations. Recent European efforts have attempted to integrate native work into Western designs, though conventional patterns remain dominant. Additionally, "Bombay boxes" feature distinctive geometrical inlays made from ivory, ebony, sapan wood, sandal wood, wire, and stags' horns.
