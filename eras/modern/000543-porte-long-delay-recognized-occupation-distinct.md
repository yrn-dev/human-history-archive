# Austrian Occupation of Bosnia and Herzegovina

## Initiation of Occupation
Following an arrangement made beforehand, Austria was requested at the Congress of Berlin to undertake the administration and occupation of Bosnia and Herzegovina. This task was considered arduous but honourable, necessitated by the fact that the provinces could not be left to the Turks, nor could Austria allow them to fall under the influence of Russia. 

The occupation began immediately on July 29, with 60,000 Austrian troops crossing the frontier under the command of General Philippovich.

## Military Resistance and Challenges
The process of establishing control proved more difficult than anticipated. The Mahommedans offered strenuous resistance, and military operations were complicated by the mountainous terrain of the region. To crush this resistance, Austria required a total of 200,000 men, and the fighting remained obstinate for several months.

Losses on both sides were very heavy. Even after the capture of Serajevo in August, resistance continued. In addition to those killed in battle, a significant number of insurgents were put to death under military law.

## Financial and Political Impact
The difficulties of the occupation led to strong opposition within the Delegations at the end of the year. Consequently, the government received a credit to cover the expenses for 1879 that was less than half of the original request. Furthermore, a supplementary estimate of 40,000,000 gulden for 1878 was not voted until the following year.

## Recognition by the Porte and Administration
In 1879, after a long delay, the Porte recognized the occupation. This recognition was based on the distinct understanding that the sovereignty of the sultan was acknowledged. 

Following this recognition, a civil administration was established. The provinces were not attached to either half of the empire but were instead placed under the control of the joint minister of finance.

## Social and Regional Unrest
The first two years of the new government were not very successful. The Christian population expressed disappointment upon discovering that they were still required to pay rent to the Mahommedan begs, as they had in the past. Additionally, tensions existed between members of the Greek Church and Roman Catholics.

In 1881, disturbances that began in Dalmatia spread across the frontier into Herzegovina, necessitating the deployment of another expedition to restore order.
