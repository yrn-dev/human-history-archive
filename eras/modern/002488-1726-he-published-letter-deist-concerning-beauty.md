# John Balguy

John Balguy (1686–1748) was an English philosopher and divine. Born in Sheffield on August 12, 1686, he received his early education at the Sheffield grammar school before attending St John's College, Cambridge, where he graduated with a B.A. in 1706. He was ordained in 1710 and, the following year, obtained a small living in Tanfield and Lamesley, Durham. Balguy married in 1715.

## Early Career and the Bangorian Controversy
Balguy began his career as an author during the "Bangorian controversy," which was sparked by a famous sermon on "The Kingdom of Christ" delivered by Bishop Hoadley in 1715. Writing under the pseudonym Silvius, Balguy entered the debate to support Hoadley against several High Church opponents.

## Philosophical Works and Moral Virtue
In 1726, Balguy published *A letter to a Deist concerning the Beauty and Excellency of Moral Virtue, and the Support and Improvement which it receives from the Christian Religion*. The primary objective of this work was to demonstrate that while the highest principle of morality is a love of virtue for its own sake, religious punishments and rewards serve as sanctions of conduct that are most valuable and, in certain instances, absolutely indispensable.

Following this, Balguy published a tractate titled *The Foundation of Moral Goodness*, the first part of which appeared in 1727 and the second part, *Illustrating and enforcing the Principles contained in the former*, in 1728. This work pursued two main goals: the refutation of Hutcheson's theory regarding the basis of rectitude, and the establishment of the theory held by Clarke and Cudworth. Balguy argued that virtue consists of conformity to reason, specifically acting according to fitnesses that emerge from the immutable and eternal relations of objects to agents.

## Later Life and Divine Rectitude
Balguy's professional advancement continued through his friendship with Bishop Hoadley, who made him a prebendary of Salisbury in 1727. In 1729, he became the vicar of Northallerton in the county of York.

His subsequent work, an essay titled *Divine Rectitude: or, a Brief Inquiry concerning the Moral Perfections of the Deity, particularly in respect of Creation and Providence*, explored the moral principles of the Deity. In this text, Balguy attempted to show that the same moral principles that should guide human life also underlie the ways and works of God. He posited that goodness in the Deity is not simply a disposition toward benevolence, but rather a regard for harmony, beauty, and order.
