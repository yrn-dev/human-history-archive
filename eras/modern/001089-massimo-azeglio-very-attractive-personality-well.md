# Massimo d'Azeglio

Massimo Taparelli, Marquis d'Azeglio (1798–1866), was an Italian author and statesman born in Turin in October 1798. Descended from an ancient and noble Piedmontese family, he was the son of Cesare d'Azeglio, a high-ranking court official and officer in the Piedmontese army.

## Early Life and Artistic Pursuits
At the age of sixteen, Massimo accompanied his father to the Vatican as an extra attache following the return of Pope Pius VII to Rome after the fall of Napoleon. Although he was granted a commission in a cavalry regiment, he soon gave it up due to health reasons. During his time in Rome, d'Azeglio developed a passion for music and art. Despite the disapproval of the "stiff and narrow" Piedmontese aristocracy to which his family belonged, his father eventually allowed him to settle in Rome to pursue painting. For several years, d'Azeglio lived an abstemious life, supporting himself through his art while meditating on the political state of Italy.

## Literary Career and Political Awakening
D'Azeglio returned to Turin in 1830 and moved to Milan in 1831 following his father's death. For twelve years, he was active in Milan's artistic and literary circles, becoming an intimate of the novelist Alessandro Manzoni. After marrying Manzoni's daughter, d'Azeglio shifted his primary focus from art to literature. He wrote two historical novels, *Ettore Fieramosca* (1833) and *Niccolo dei Lapi* (1841), which were written in imitation of Manzoni. These works contained pronounced political tendencies intended to reawaken national feeling and highlight the evils of foreign domination in Italy. He also celebrated a combat between French and Italian knights during the 1503 siege of Barletta in his work *Disfida di Barletta*.

His political activities intensified in 1845 when he visited Romagna as an unauthorized envoy to report on the region's conditions and predict troubles following the death of Pope Gregory XVI. This resulted in the publication of his famous pamphlet *Degli ultimi casi di Romagna* in Florence, which led to his expulsion from Tuscany.

## Political Career and Statesmanship
During the first war of independence, d'Azeglio wore the papal uniform and participated in the defense of Vicenza under General Durando, where he suffered severe wounds. While recovering in Florence, he was expelled from Tuscany a second time due to his opposition to the ruling democrats.

In early 1849, King Charles Albert of Sardinia invited d'Azeglio to form a cabinet. He initially refused because he believed it was impossible to renew the campaign and did not wish to sign a peace treaty with Austria under such poor external and internal conditions. However, after the defeat of Novara on March 23, 1849, and the succession of Victor Emmanuel II, d'Azeglio accepted the call to form a cabinet. He successfully concluded a peace treaty, dissolved the Chamber, and summoned a new one to ratify the agreement.

For the next three years, d'Azeglio managed the country's affairs with ability and tact, opposing the claims of the Roman Curia and improving diplomatic relations. In 1850, he invited Count Cavour into the ministry. D'Azeglio eventually resigned in 1852 because he disapproved of declarations made by Cavour and Farini that moved the ministry toward an alliance with the Left. Although he formed a second ministry at the king's request (excluding Cavour and Farini), he resigned again in October of that year due to ill health and dissatisfaction with colleagues, suggesting Cavour as his successor.

## Later Years and Ideology
D'Azeglio was described as an absolutely honest patriot and a characteristic example of the best type of Piedmontese aristocrat, though he was noted as being cautious, conservative, and somewhat of an amateur in politics. He believed in an Italian confederation under papal auspices, similar to the views of Balbo and V. Gioberti.

In his later years, he was deeply affected by the deaths of Cavour in 1861 and his two brothers in 1862. He lived a more retired life but remained active as a writer and deputy. His primary interests were the relations between the kingdom of Italy (formerly Piedmont) and revolutionists like Mazzini, as well as the "Roman question." He argued that Italy should be unified solely by the Franco-Piedmontese army, eschewing conspirators. He proposed that the pope maintain spiritual independence and nominal sovereignty over Rome, while the capital of Italy be located elsewhere and Romans be granted Italian citizenship. He strongly disagreed with the 1864 convention between the pope and the Italian government.

D'Azeglio spent his final years at his villa of Cannero writing his memoirs. He died of fever on January 15, 1866. His autobiographical work, *I Miei Ricordi*, was published posthumously in 1866, though it remained incomplete.
