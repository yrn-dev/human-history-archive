# Deep Channels of the Swedish Coast and the Baltic Proper

The Baltic Sea is a body of water extending between 54° and 66° N. latitude and 9° and 30° E. longitude. It is surrounded by the territories of Denmark, Germany, Russia, and Sweden. The sea reaches a greatest length of approximately 960 miles and a greatest breadth of about 400 miles, with a total coastline measuring 5,000 miles. Its central axis runs generally from the south-west to the north-east.

## The Swedish Coastal Channel
Along the coast of Sweden, a deep channel runs in a northward direction, originating from outside the island of Oland. This specific channel is entirely cut off to the east and south by a bank that sweeps northward and eastward from the vicinity of Karlskrona; the island of Gotland is situated upon this bank. At its northern extremity, this coastal channel communicates with the Gotland deep. The deepest hole in the Baltic is located near this junction, opposite Landsort, reaching a depth of 420 metres (230 fathoms).

## Basins and Hollows of the Baltic Proper
East of the plateau, the Baltic proper is characterized by a series of troughs or hollows:

*   **Bornholm Deep:** Located east of the island of Bornholm.
*   **Gotland Deep:** Separated from the Bornholm deep by the Middelbank.
*   **Danziger Tiefe:** An isolated depression situated to the south-east of the Middelbank.
*   **Gotland Basin:** The deepest and largest of all the basins, extending north-eastwards toward the Gulf of Finland.

## Water Circulation and Composition
The circulation of water within the Baltic proper is distinct from the circulation found in the channels that connect it to the North Sea. The dividing line for these two systems is the plateau connecting the coast of Ruegen and Mecklenburg with the islands of Moen and Falster.

In the great hollows and basins from Ruegen to the Gulfs of Finland and Bothnia, the upper layers of water—ranging from 30 to 70 metres (16 to 38 fathoms) in thickness—maintain nearly the same salinity throughout, with vertical circulation driven by convection currents. Beneath these upper layers lie masses of salter water. These deeper strata are practically stagnant, surcharged with carbonic acid, and deficient in oxygen. 

These salter waters originated from outside the Baltic and passed over the plateau between Mecklenburg and Falster. However, their horizontal extension is restricted by the ridges that separate the various deep hollows of the Baltic. The inflow into these deep basins is intermittent, characterized by a long period of reflux and flux.

## Connection to the North Sea
The Baltic is connected to the North Sea via a winding channel located between the Cimbrian peninsula and the south of Scandinavia. This connecting area is divided into two parts: the Skagerrak (west of a line joining the Skaw with Christiania fjord) and the Kattegat (east of that line). 

The Kattegat is blocked at its southern end by Danish islands and connects to the Baltic proper through the Little Belt, the Great Belt, and the Sound. The physical boundary between the North Sea and the Baltic is formed by the prolongation of the plateau from the islands of Moen and Falster to the coasts of Ruegen and Mecklenburg, as well as the plateau where the islands of Laaland, Funen, and Zealand are situated.
