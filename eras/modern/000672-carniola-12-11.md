# Carniola

Carniola, also known by the German name *Krain*, is a duchy and crown-land of Austria. Its modern name is derived from the Slavonic word *Krajina*, meaning frontier. The province covers an area of 3,856 square miles and is bounded to the north by Carinthia, to the northeast by Styria, to the south and southeast by Croatia, and to the west by Istria, Trieste, and Gorz and Gradisca.

## Geography and Topography
Carniola is primarily a mountainous region. The northern portion is occupied by the Alps, specifically the Julian Alps, the Karawankas, and the Steiner Alps. The highest point in the Julian Alps is the Triglav (or Terglou), which reaches 9,394 feet; its northern declivity contains the province's only glacier. The Triglav serves as a dividing range between the Alps and the Karst Mountains, acting as a barrier between the Italian, Slavonic, and German races. Other notable peaks include the Mangart (8,784 ft.), the Jaluz (8,708 ft.), and the Stou or Stuhlberg (7,344 ft.) in the Karawankas. The Steiner Alps feature the Grintovc (8,393 ft.), located on the boundary of Styria, Carniola, and Carinthia.

The southern region consists of the northern ramifications of the Karst Mountains, including the Krainer Schneeberg (5,890 ft.), the Nanos (4,275 ft.) in the Birnbaumer Wald, the Hornbuchl (3,608 ft.) in the Hornwald, and the Uskokengebirge (3,874 ft.). The Karst region is characterized by subterranean streams, funnels, and caves, most notably the grottos of Adelsberg and Planina, and the Kreuzberghohle near Laas.

The province's hydrology is dominated by the watershed of the Save (or Sau), which rises within the duchy at Radmannsdorf. Major affluents include the Kanker, Steiner Feistritz, Zeyer (Sora), Gurk, and the Laibach. The Laibach river is particularly remarkable for its subterranean courses, passing through the Adelsberg grotto and appearing on the surface near Planina as the Unz before disappearing again and resurfacing near Oberlaibach. Other water features include the Rothwein Fall cascades and lakes such as the Wochein, Veldes, and the periodical lake of Zirknitz, known to the Romans as *Lacus Lugens*. The Idria and Wippach rivers are exceptions to the Save watershed, flowing instead into the Isonzo and the Adriatic.

## History
During the Roman Empire, Carniola was part of Pannonia and Noricum. The city of Laibach (known then as Æmona) was situated northeast of the Julian Alps, making the territory a natural point of dispute between the sovereigns of Illyricum and Italy. The Slavonic population settled in the region between the end of the 6th century and the beginning of the 7th century. Following the conquest by Charlemagne, much of the district was given to the duke of Friuli. By the 10th century, a family based in the castle of Kieselberg near Krainburg began using the title of margrave of Carniola, though other lords, including the bishop of Freising and the duke of Carinthia, held various parts of the territory.

By the end of the 14th century, Rudolph IV of Austria acquired all separate portions of the territory through bequest or inheritance and took the title of duke of Carniola. The duchy remained an Austrian possession except from 1809 to 1813, when it was incorporated into the French Illyrian Provinces under Napoleon. These provinces—which also included Dalmatia, Istria, Fiume, Trieste, Görz and Gradisca, and parts of Croatia and Carinthia—were ceded to Italy at the peace of Schönbrunn on October 14, 1809. After French occupation ceased in 1813, the territories reverted to Austria. In 1816, the kingdom of Illyria was formed, comprising Carniola, Carinthia, Istria, Trieste, and Görz and Gradisca, until Carniola became a separate crown-land in 1849.

## Demographics and Administration
As of 1900, Carniola had a population of 508,348, with a density of 132 inhabitants per square mile. The population was 95% Slovene and 5% German, with 99% belonging to the Roman Catholic Church. The capital is the autonomous municipality of Laibach, which had a population of 36,547 in 1900. Other significant towns include Oberlaibach, Idria, Gurkfeld, Zirknitz, Adelsberg, Neumarktl, Krainburg, and Gottschee.

Administratively, the province is divided into eleven districts. Its local diet consists of thirty-seven members, including the bishop of Laibach as an *ex officio* member. Carniola sends eleven deputies to the Reichsrat in Vienna.

## Language and Politics
The Slovenes, who form the majority in Carniola, sought the use of their language in education and government. In 1882, they won a majority in the diet of Carniola and the town council of Laibach. This allowed for the introduction of Illyrian as the official language and the renaming of streets in Illyrian. The government subsequently recognized Slovenian as an official language for the whole of Carniola, the district of Cilli in Styria, and mixed districts in southern Carinthia. Additionally, a Slovene gymnasium was established in Laibach to exist alongside the German one.

The political ideal of the Slovenes was an "Illyrian" kingdom encompassing Croatia and other southern Slavs of the coast district. While a movement attempted to establish a unified Illyrian language for Croats and Slovenes, other elements, such as the Orthodox Serbs, remained attracted to Russia and the hope of a great Servian kingdom.

## Economy and Mining
The town of Idria is a significant mining center located 25 miles west of Laibach. It is known for rich quicksilver (mercury) mines discovered accidentally in 1497, which have been managed by the government since 1580. The mercurial ore, found as cinnabar or mingled with schist in clay slate, is noted for its high yield of pure metal. These mines are ranked second in the world for annual extraction, following Almaden in Spain. A white crystalline mineral wax called idrialin (C40H28O) is also found accompanying the mercury ore in Idria.
