# The Liberal Administrations of Robert Baldwin and L. H. Lafontaine

The political career of Robert Baldwin, a Canadian statesman born in York (now Toronto) on May 12, 1804, was defined by his persistent advocacy for a responsible executive. Baldwin, the son of William Warren Baldwin—an Irishman who arrived in Canada in 1798 and opposed the political and religious oligarchy of the time—was called to the Bar in 1825. After an initial period in the parliament of Upper Canada starting in 1829, Baldwin spent six years advocating for a responsible executive as the primary solution to the economic and political problems of the era, earning him the reputation as "the man of one idea."

## Early Political Career and Executive Roles
Baldwin's entry into executive governance began in 1836 when he was called to the executive council by the lieutenant-governor, Sir Francis Bond Head. However, Baldwin resigned within a month because he lacked influence and was forced to support measures he opposed. Despite his identity as a reformer, Baldwin strongly disapproved of the rebellion that occurred between 1837 and 1838. Following the union of the two Canadas in 1841, he served as a member of the executive council under Lord Sydenham, though he resigned shortly thereafter due to the issue of responsible government.

## The First Liberal Administration
In 1842, Robert Baldwin formed the first Liberal administration in connection with Mr. L. H. Lafontaine (later Sir L. H. Lafontaine). This administration was short-lived, ending the following year. Baldwin resigned after a quarrel with the governor-general, Sir Charles Metcalfe, regarding a question of patronage. Baldwin believed that the principle of responsible government was involved in this dispute. In the general election that followed this resignation, Sir Charles Metcalfe was sustained by a narrow majority.

## The Second Liberal Administration and Reforms
The Liberals returned to power in 1848, leading to the formation of a second administration under Lord Elgin, headed by Baldwin and Mr. Lafontaine. This government implemented several significant reforms, including:
*   The introduction of an important municipal system into Upper Canada.
*   The freeing of the Provincial University from sectarian control.

## Political Opposition
During the mid-1840s, the Reform party led by Baldwin and Lafontaine faced opposition from figures such as Sir Charles Metcalfe. In 1844, during Metcalfe's contest with the Reform party, Macdonald was elected as a Conservative member for Kingston to the provincial assembly. Macdonald entered the assembly on November 28 as a supporter of the Draper government, expressing a firm belief that Canada's prosperity depended upon its permanent connection with the "mother country."
