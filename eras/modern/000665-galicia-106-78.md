# Galicia

The term Galicia refers to distinct geographical and political regions located in both Spain and the former Austrian Empire.

## Galicia in Spain
Galicia is a region of Spain located in the north, bordering the Atlantic Ocean to the west and the Portuguese province of Entre Minho e Douro to the south. The river Minho (Spanish *Mino*) forms the northern boundary of the Portuguese province and flows through Galicia. Other rivers originating in Galicia include the Limia (Spanish *Linia*) and the Tamega, the latter of which is a large right-hand tributary of the Douro.

Historically, Galicia served as a refuge for older Spanish races due to its rugged and isolated terrain. Following the defeat of the Visigothic king Roderick in 711, most of Spain fell to the Moors in 713, with the exceptions of Asturias and Galicia. While Galicia was eventually lost in 734, the resistance maintained by the Asturians under Pelayo the Goth ultimately allowed the people of Castile, Leon, and Galicia to recover their liberty.

Lugo, formerly the capital of Galicia, is an episcopal see situated on the left bank of the river Miño. The town is characterized by a massive Roman wall, 20 feet thick and 30 to 40 feet high, which features semi-circular towers. The city's architecture includes a 12th-century Gothic cathedral and a 14th-century conventual church of Santo Domingo. The primary industries in Lugo include the manufacture of woollen cloth, linen, and tanning.

## Galicia in Austria
Galicia was a region within the Austrian Empire characterized by significant nationalist movements and ethnic diversity. The population included Poles and Ruthenes, and the region's political structure was influenced by these racial divisions. In the allotment of constituencies, tax-paying capacity was considered, and separate registers were established for different races to ensure that electors voted for candidates of their own race. This system prevented, for example, Poles from being defeated by Ruthenes.

### Political and National Movements
During 1868, nationalist movements gained ground. An extreme party led by Smolka sought to imitate the Czechs by refusing to attend the Reichsrath in Vienna. Polish parties collectively demanded that the powers of the Galician diet be increased and that Galician members cease attending the Reichsrath for matters the Galician diet was qualified to handle. 

To secure the support of Polish deputies for the Ausgleich, Beust promised several concessions:
* The appointment of a special minister for Galicia.
* The creation of a separate board for Galician education.
* The establishment of Polish as the language of instruction in all secondary schools.
* The use of Polish, rather than German, as the official language in public offices and law courts.
* The limited use of Ruthenian, which was permitted only in elementary schools.

## Commercial Centers and Geography
The town of Brody, located in Austrian Galicia 62 miles east of Lemberg by rail, served as a vital commercial center, particularly for trade with Russia, due to its proximity to the Russian frontier. Created as a town in 1684, it was raised to a free commercial city in 1779. However, its trade diminished after its charter as a free commercial city was withdrawn in 1879. As of 1900, Brody had a population of 17,360, approximately two-thirds of whom were Jews.

The region's economic ties extended beyond its borders; for instance, the town of Kremenets in south-west Russia (Volhynia) engaged in a brisk trade of grain and tobacco exported to Odessa and Galicia.
