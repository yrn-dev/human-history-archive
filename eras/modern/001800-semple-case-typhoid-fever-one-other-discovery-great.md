# Typhoid Fever

Typhoid fever, also referred to recently as "enteric fever," is an infective disease caused by a specific bacillus. It is characterized by a systemic infection that affects multiple organs and has been a subject of extensive bacteriological research and diagnostic development.

## Etiology and Bacteriology
The identification of the organism responsible for typhoid fever occurred during a period of rapid advancement in human knowledge between 1880 and 1890. This progress was stimulated by Louis Pasteur's demonstrations that bacteria could produce definite diseases and was furthered by Robert Koch's introduction of the "plate-method" and solid culture media in 1881. These methods simplified the cultivation and separation of bacteria, allowing the typhoid bacillus to be identified and its relationship to the disease established.

## Pathological Effects and Organ Involvement
In the case of typhoid fever, the bacteria exhibit a selective character in how they distribute lesions within the body. The disease involves a secondary invasion of the mesenteric glands. Clumps of bacilli are also found within internal organs, with the spleen being particularly affected, although there may be minimal tissue change surrounding these clumps.

The disease can also impact other systems:
*   **Kidneys:** Like other acute specific diseases, typhoid fever can produce a temporary nephritis during the height of the infection.
*   **Bone and Joint:** In some instances, aches or pains near a joint may occur during convalescence from typhoid fever. If these progress, the bone and the surrounding membrane (periosteum) may become swollen, hot, red, and excessively tender. This condition can lead to high temperatures (up to 104 deg.), violent delirium, convulsions, and potentially fatal complications such as pericarditis, septic pneumonia, or other forms of blood-poisoning.

## Diagnosis and Treatment
The foundation of serum diagnosis for typhoid fever was established by the findings of Widal and Gruenbaum. They demonstrated that the serum of patients suffering from the disease, even in its early stages, agglutinated the typhoid bacillus. Agglutination refers to the process where bacteria distributed in a fluid aggregate into clumps, a change that can be observed via microscope or seen by the naked eye as sedimentation.

Regarding treatment, serum therapy has been employed for malignant diseases, including typhoid fever. This approach involves introducing a modified form of the poisons that produce the disease into the system of a healthy individual.

## Public Health and Military Management
In military contexts, such as within the organization of the German Army's hospital services, the management of infectious diseases is handled by a Consulting Sanitary Officer. This officer, often a university professor in peacetime, utilizes a bacteriological laboratory to investigate individual cases of typhoid fever and similar diseases. The objective is to ascertain the patient's origin, isolate the individual, and attempt to eradicate the source and cause of the infection to prevent outbreaks.
