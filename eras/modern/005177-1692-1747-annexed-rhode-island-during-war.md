## Bristol, Rhode Island
Bristol is a shire-township located in Bristol county, Rhode Island, situated on a peninsula between Mount Hope Bay to the east and Narragansett Bay to the west, approximately 15 miles south-southeast of Providence. The township encompasses an area of 12 square miles and includes a small island known as Hog Island. The landscape is generally gently undulating and well-suited for agriculture, with a particular emphasis on the cultivation of onions. Mount Hope, reaching a height of 216 feet on the eastern side, provides views of the river, bay, and landscape.

The township's origins date back to the end of King Philip's War in 1676, when Mount Hope Neck—the former seat of the vanquished sachem—and most of the current township were awarded to the Plymouth Colony. In 1680, after Plymouth conveyed the "Neck" to a company of four, the village was laid out. It was named Bristol, after the town in England, in 1681 due to its anticipated commercial importance. Bristol became a shire-township in 1685. Its jurisdiction shifted to Massachusetts in 1692 before it was annexed to Rhode Island in 1747.

During the War of Independence, the village faced British military action. It was bombarded by the British on October 7, 1775, though it suffered little damage; however, it was visited and partially destroyed by a British force on May 25, 1778.

The principal village of Bristol serves as a port of entry with a deep and capacious harbour. It is a known centre for yacht-building, specifically the Herreshoff yards, where defenders of the America's Cup such as the "Reliance" and "Columbia" were constructed. The town also hosts woollen goods and rubber manufactories. As of 1910, the population was 8,565, following a population of 7,512 in 1905 (state census) and 6,901 in 1900 (of whom 1,923 were foreign-born). The area is served by the Rhode Island Suburban and the New York, New Haven & Hartford railways and is connected to the island of Rhode Island via ferry.

## Notable Figures of Rhode Island
The archive records several prominent individuals associated with the state:

**Esek Hopkins (1718–1802):** Born in Scituate, Rhode Island, to a prominent New England Puritan family, Hopkins became the first admiral of the United States navy. A skilled trader and sailor, he became the commodore of seventeen merchantmen after marrying into a prosperous Newport family. During the Seven Years' War, he served as a captain of a privateer. In Rhode Island politics, he supported his brother Stephen against the Ward faction. 

At the start of the War of Independence, Rhode Island appointed him brigadier-general. In December 1775, the Continental Congress commissioned him as commander-in-chief of the navy. In January 1776, he hoisted his flag as admiral of eight converted merchantmen. Despite an initial cruise that acquired material of war, Hopkins and two captains were censured by Congress for breach of orders, though they were defended by John Adams. Following complaints from officers and mutual distrust, Congress dismissed him from his command and the navy in 1777. He spent the remainder of his life in Rhode Island, where he remained active in state politics.

**George Washington Greene (1811–1883):** An American historian born in East Greenwich, Rhode Island, and grandson of Major-General Nathanael Greene. He attended Brown University starting in 1824 but left during his junior year due to ill-health. He spent twenty years in Europe, with the exception of 1833–1834, when he served as principal of Kent Academy in East Greenwich. From 1837 to 1845, he was the U.S. consul at Rome. He later served as an instructor in modern languages at Brown University (1848–1852) and a non-resident lecturer in American history at Cornell University (1871–1875). His works include *Short History of Rhode Island* (1877), *Life of Nathanael Greene* (1867–1871), and *Historical View of the American Revolution* (1865).

## Religious and Political History
Rhode Island's early governance was characterized by constitutions dating from original charters of 1662. In the mid-17th century, the region was linked to the movement of antipaedobaptists. Francis Doughty, an English Baptist who spent time in Rhode Island, worked in the New Netherlands in 1656, where he baptized converts before being banished. Similarly, William Wickenden of Providence was banished and fined after administering ordinances and evangelizing at Flushing in 1656. Additionally, Baptists settled on Block Island around 1663.

## The Island of Rhodes (Greece)
Separate from the American state, the archive contains records of the Greek island of Rhodes. According to legend, the island was settled by Althæmenês, a Kretan and son of King Katreus, who founded the temple of the Atabyrian Zeus on Mount Atabyrum.

In antiquity, Rhodes was a significant naval power. It was a member of the Athenian confederacy but later revolted from Athens, contributing to the Social War (357–355). During the Peloponnesian War, the Peloponnesians, invited by principal men in Rhodes, persuaded the island to revolt from the Athenians and join them. 

Later, Rhodes faced conflict with Sparta. After revolting from Sparta and establishing a democratical government, an opposition party sought Spartan aid. The Lacedæmonians sent forces under Ekdikus and later Teleutias. Teleutias, leading a fleet of 37 ships, established a fortified post on the island to support the oligarchy.
