# Ormonde

The name Ormonde is associated with a long line of Irish nobility, specifically the Butler family, who served as statesmen and soldiers for the English crown. The most prominent figure of this lineage was James Butler, the first duke of Ormonde, a loyal statesman who commanded for the king in Ireland.

## The First Duke of Ormonde
James Butler, the grandson of a previous Ormonde, united the Ormonde titles and estates through his marriage to his cousin, Elizabeth Preston. He served as a commander in Ireland, navigating the conflicts between Protestant parliamentarians and Catholic rebels. In 1649, he proclaimed Charles II, but after being defeated at Rathmines and seeing his garrisons broken by Cromwell, he left Ireland at the end of 1650.

Following the Restoration, James Butler was appointed lord-lieutenant. His estates were restored to him, and he was granted the county palatine of Tipperary, which James I had previously taken from his grandfather. His titles expanded over time: he was created a marquess in 1632, received the English earldom of Brecknock in 1660, and was granted an Irish dukedom of Ormonde in 1661. In 1682, he received a patent for an English dukedom of the same title.

## Political Struggles and Later Life
The first duke's tenure as lord-lieutenant was interrupted for seven years due to the intrigues of Buckingham. In 1670, he was the target of a desperate assassination attempt in St James's Street, where a group of ruffians dragged him from his coach in an attempt to take him to the gallows at Tyburn. It is suggested that the threat made by his son, Ossory—who claimed he would pistol Buckingham even if he were behind the king's chair—may have prevented the assassination.

Upon the accession of James II, the duke was removed from active employment. He died in 1688 at his house in Dorsetshire. He was succeeded by his grandson, James, the second duke of Ormonde. The second duke was a leader of the London Jacobites and was attainted in 1715, resulting in the forfeiture of his estates and honours. He spent thirty years in exile, primarily in Avignon, and died without surviving issue in 1745.

## History of the Ormonde Earls
The Ormonde earls were frequently appointed to the chief government of Ireland in roles such as lords-lieutenant, lords-keeper, deputies, or lords-justices. They maintained a tradition of loyalty to English custom and the English crown, often warring with native Irish populations. Some members of the family were noted scholars; the 6th earl was an ambassador to various courts and a master of every European tongue.

The lineage faced various disputes over titles and estates. In 1515, Sir Piers Butler ("red Piers") assumed the earldom of Ormonde and seized Irish estates. Although he was initially styled as "pretending himself to be earl of Ormonde," he was made lord deputy and chief governor of Ireland in 1522. By February 1537/8, he obtained a patent for the Ormonde earldom. His son, James the Lame, later secured an act of parliament in 1543/4 that confirmed the grant and restored the "pre-eminence" of the ancient earldom of 1328.

## Conflicts and Rivalries
The Butlers were hereditary enemies of the Geraldines. This rivalry manifested in several conflicts, including those involving Gerald, who succeeded to the earldom in 1558. Ormonde and the 15th earl of Desmond engaged in open war, culminating in a battle at Affane on the Blackwater, where Desmond was defeated and captured. 

Later, James Fitzmaurice Fitzgerald attempted to usurp the earldom of Desmond, seeking foreign assistance by giving the movement an ultra-Catholic character. He allied himself with several figures, including Sir Edmund Butler, the brother of Ormonde. However, the submission of the Butlers followed the appearance of Ormonde and Sidney in the south-west.
