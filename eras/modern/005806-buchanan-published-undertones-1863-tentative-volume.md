# Robert Williams Buchanan

Robert Williams Buchanan (1841–1901) was a British dramatist, novelist, and poet. Born on August 18, 1841, in Caverswall, Staffordshire, he was the son of Robert Buchanan (1813–1866), a journalist and Owenite lecturer. Buchanan was educated at the university and high school in Glasgow, where he formed a close friendship with the poet David Gray. The two traveled to London in 1860 seeking fame, an experience Buchanan later detailed in an essay on Gray originally published in the *Cornhill Magazine*.

## Poetic Works
Following a period of disappointment and struggle, Buchanan published *Undertones* in 1863, a volume described as "tentative." This was followed by several other poetic collections: *Idyls and Legends of Inverburn* (1865), *London Poems* (1866), and *North Coast and other Poems* (1868). In these works, Buchanan demonstrated a sympathetic insight into the humbler conditions of life and a faculty for poetic narrative. In 1870, he attempted a more ambitious work of mysticism titled *The Book of Orm: A Prelude to the Epic*.

Other notable poems include "The Drama of Kings" (1871), "Balder the Beautiful" (1877), "The City of Dream" (1888), "The Outcast: a Rhyme for the Time" (1891), and "The Wandering Jew" (1893). He also published "St Abe and his Seven Wives" anonymously in 1872, which is a lively tale set in Salt Lake City. His poems were collected into three volumes in 1874, a single volume in 1884, and finally as *Complete Poetical Works* in two volumes in 1901.

## Literary Criticism and Controversy
Buchanan was a frequent contributor to periodical literature. He gained notoriety in October 1871 through an article in the *Contemporary Review* titled "The Fleshly School of Poetry," written under the pseudonym Thomas Maitland. This critique was expanded into a pamphlet in 1872. The article provoked responses from Mr. Swinburne in *Under the Microscope* (1872) and from D.G. Rossetti, who wrote a letter to the *Athenaeum* on December 16, 1871, titled "The Stealthy School of Criticism." Buchanan later regretted the violence of his attack; Rossetti is the "old enemy" to whom the work *God and the Man* is dedicated.

## Novels and Drama
Buchanan began a long series of novels in 1876 with *The Shadow of the Sword*, which is considered one of his best. This and his 1881 novel *God and the Man*—a striking tale concerning a family feud—are noted for their simplicity and breadth of treatment. His subsequent novels include *The Martyrdom of Madeline* (1882), *Foxglove Manor* (1885), *Effie Hetherington* (1896), and *Father Anthony* (1898).

As a playwright, Buchanan authored several successful plays, including *Lady Clare* (1883), *A Man's Shadow* (1890), *The Charlatan* (1894), and *Sophia* (1886), the latter being an adaptation of *Tom Jones*.

## Other Writings
Buchanan's contributions to periodical literature are contained in several volumes: *David Gray and other Essays, chiefly on Poetry* (1868), *Master Spirits* (1873), *A Look round Literature* (1887), and *A Poet's Sketch Book* (1883), the latter of which reprints his essay on David Gray. He also wrote *The Land of Lorne* (1871), a two-volume vivid record of his experiences yachting on the west coast of Scotland.
