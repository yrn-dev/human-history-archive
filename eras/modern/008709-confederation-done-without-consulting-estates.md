# Confederation

The term confederation refers to various historical arrangements of political unions, alliances, and collective governance across different eras and regions, ranging from ancient Greek city-states to 19th-century European and American conflicts.

## Ancient Greek Confederations
In ancient Greece, confederations often balanced the autonomy of individual cities with collective federal obligations. The Bœotian confederation embraced the entire Bœotian territory. In this system, larger cities served as primary members—including Thebes, Orchomenus, Lebadeia, Korôneia, Haliartus, Kôpæ, Anthêdôn, Tanagra, Thespiæ, and Platæa (prior to its secession)—while smaller locations acted as dependencies. For example, Chæroneia and Hyêttus were dependencies of Orchomenus, while Siphæ and Thisbê were dependencies of Thespiæ. 

The Bœotian confederacy was managed by annually chosen magistrates known as Bœotarchs. During the Peloponnesian War, there were eleven Bœotarchs, two of whom were from Thebes. These officials consulted with four different senates on important matters. The confederation also held a periodic religious festival and general concilium called the Pambœotia at Korôneia. Despite these structures, the affairs of the confederation were substantially managed by Thebes to maintain Theban ascendency through superior bravery and force.

Other alliances, such as the Spartan confederacy, faced internal dissent regarding treaty conditions. While a majority of Spartan confederates may have ratified a peace, powerful minorities—including the Corinthians, Megarians, Bœotians, and Eleians—often protested. Specific grievances included the failure to regain territories such as Sollium, Anaktorium, and Nisæa, or the restoration of Panaktum to Athens. A primary point of contention in such alliances was the provision allowing leading powers, such as Athens and Sparta, to amend treaties by mutual consent without consulting their allies.

## The Germanic Confederation and the Habsburg Empire
In the 19th century, the Congress of Vienna in 1815 declared that Bohemia should be part of the newly formed Germanic Confederation. This action was taken without consulting the estates of the country, departing from the custom of consulting them during serious constitutional changes, a practice that had persisted even after the battle of the White Hill.

The constitutional position of Bohemia shifted significantly following the Austro-Prussian war of 1866. A treaty signed at Prague on August 23, 1866, excluded all lands ruled by the house of Habsburg from Germany, leading to a decline in German influence within the Austrian empire and Bohemia. While Hungary achieved complete independence, the new constitution of 1867—applicable to the Slavic and German parts of the empire—maintained centralization. This was energetically opposed by the Bohemians.

## The American Confederate Conflict
In the mid-19th century, Confederate forces engaged in military conflicts with Union divisions. In February 1862, Union forces invested a fort, leading to a series of engagements. On February 15, General Pillow attacked the Federal division of McClernand, driving it from the Nashville road, though he subsequently halted and retired. General C. F. Smith's division then assaulted the lines, capturing works that the Confederates were unable to reclaim. Following the escape of senior Confederate generals Floyd and Pillow by steamer on February 16, General S. B. Buckner surrendered the remaining infantry unconditionally. While Colonel Forrest's Confederate cavalry escaped by road, approximately 15,000 prisoners were taken from an original total of 18,000.

## Internal Political Confederates
The term "confederates" also appears in the context of internal political maneuvering. In one instance, a king entered into secret discussions with his confederates to produce a proposition that placed all fiefs under the control of the crown for taxation purposes. This ordinance allowed fiefs to be let or sold to the highest bidder, effectively shattering the nobility's privileged position by removing their exclusive right to possess fiefs.
