# William Count Slavata and the Bohemian Uprising

## Role in the Bohemian Government
William Count Slavata (1572-1652) was a government official and a faithful servant of the house of Habsburg. He served during a period of intense religious and political tension in Bohemia, where the succession to the throne was a primary concern. King Matthias, who was childless, sought to secure the succession for his cousin, Ferdinand, duke of Styria. Ferdinand was characterized as a cruel persecutor of Protestants in Styria and a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome.

Under the influence of Ferdinand, the policies of King Matthias became more pronouncedly ultramontane. The king's councillors, including those who adhered to the Church of Rome, expressed hope that the Catholic Church would regain its ancient hold over Bohemia.

## The Bohemian Uprising of 1618
Tensions escalated in December 1617 when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague ordered the suppression of Protestant religious services in churches located on their domains. This action was viewed as a direct infringement of the agreement signed by the Romanist and Utraquist estates upon King Rudolph's signing of the Letter of Majesty.

In response, Protestant members of the diet, led by Count Thurn, assembled in Prague on May 21, 1618. They proceeded in full armour to the Hradcany palace to present their complaints to the councillors of Matthias. Following a secret meeting on May 22—attended by Thurn, Ulrich of Kinsky, Wenceslas of Ruppa, and other members of the advanced party—it was decided that the most influential of Matthias's councillors should be put to death. On May 23, the representatives of the Bohemian Protestants proceeded to the Hradcany. During the ensuing events at the beginning of the Bohemian uprising, William Count Slavata was one of the government officials who were thrown from the windows of the Hradcany palace.

## Later Life and Historical Works
Despite the events of 1618, Slavata lived a long life, eventually passing away in 1652. In his later years, he dedicated himself to writing historical accounts of the period. In 1637, he published his memoirs, titled *Pamety*, which focused exclusively on the events of 1618 and 1619, years in which he played a significant role.

During the leisure of his final years, Slavata composed a more extensive work titled *Historicke Spisovani* (historical works). This vast collection consists of fourteen large manuscript volumes, two of which contain his previously written memoirs. These two volumes have since been edited and published by Dr. Jos. Jirecek.

## Legacy and the Bohemian Language
Slavata is noted as a historian and a political opponent of Paul Skala ze Zhore (1582-c. 1640), an official for the "winter king" Frederick of the Palatinate. While Skala was an adherent of the nationalist party, Slavata represented the imperialist party.

The year 1620, which saw the downfall of Bohemian independence, marked the start of a decline for the national tongue. By the 18th century, the Bohemian language was almost extinct as a written language. Following the deaths of Slavata, Skala, and Comenius, no works of importance were written in the Bohemian language for a considerable period, as the new Austrian government sought to discourage its use. The language only began to see a modest revival at the start of the 19th century through the romantic movement and the work of Joseph Dobrovsky.
