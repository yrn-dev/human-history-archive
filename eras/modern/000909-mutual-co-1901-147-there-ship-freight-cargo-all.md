# Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co. (1901)

*Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co.* (1901, 1 K.B. 147) is a legal case concerning the liability of insurers in maritime commerce, specifically regarding the application of general average (G.A.) when multiple interests in a voyage belong to a single individual.

## Legal Context of General Average
In modern maritime law, "average" refers to damages or expenses resulting from navigation accidents. This is categorized into two types: particular average and general average. Particular average involves partial loss or damage to freight, goods, or the ship caused by an unavoidable or fortuitous accident; such losses are borne by the affected party or their insurers. 

General average, conversely, occurs when expenditures are incurred or sacrifices are made to preserve the ship, freight, and cargo from the effects of a peril of the sea. This process implies a subsequent rateable contribution from all concerned parties, based on the values of their respective interests, to compensate for the loss.

## Comparison with the Brigella Case
The ruling in *Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co.* departed from the precedent set in the case of the *Brigella* (1893, P. 189). In the *Brigella* case, a shipowner incurred expenses that would typically be subject to G.A. contributions. However, because the shipowner was the only person interested in the voyage, there were no other contributories. The shipowner attempted to claim from the ship's insurers what would have been the ship's G.A. contribution if other parties had been involved with the cargo or freight. This claim was disallowed, as the court held that there could be no G.A. without the possibility of contribution. In that instance, the insurer's liability depended on the fact of contribution rather than the nature of the loss.

## Ruling in Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co.
The circumstances of *Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co.* were similar in that the ship, cargo, and freight all belonged to the same person. In this case, the owner had insured the cargo but had not insured the ship. Damage was done to the ship when masts were cut away for the sake of general safety.

The court held that the cargo underwriters were liable to pay a contribution for the damage to the ship. The ruling established that the loss is theoretically spread across all interests at risk, and the underwriters had undertaken to cover the cargo's share of such losses. Consequently, the liability of the insurer did not depend on whether the interests belonged to one person or multiple people. This legal perspective aligns with the view taken in the United States.

## General Average Principles and Rules
The case operates within a broader framework of G.A. rules regarding sacrifices and contributions:

*   **Sacrifices and Damage:** Damage to sails and spars caused by driving a ship higher up the ground or forcing it off the ground for common safety is made good as G.A. Similarly, damage to boilers and machinery during refloating efforts is allowed if there was an actual intention to float the ship for common safety.
*   **Cargo and Freight:** Loss of freight resulting from the loss or damage of cargo is made good as G.A. when caused by a G.A. act. The amount made good for sacrificed goods is based on the market value at the termination of the adventure or the vessel's arrival.
*   **Operational Expenses:** The cost of discharging cargo is admitted as G.A. if it was necessary for common safety or to enable repairs required for the safe prosecution of the voyage. If discharge is admissible, the costs of reloading, storing, and storage charges are also admitted, provided the ship is not condemned and the voyage is not abandoned.
*   **Contributory Values:** Contributions are based on the actual values of the property at the end of the adventure, plus the amount made good as G.A. for sacrificed property. Deductions are made for port charges and crew wages that would not have been incurred had the ship and cargo been totally lost at the time of the sacrifice. Notably, personal effects and passengers' luggage not shipped under a bill of lading do not contribute to G.A.
