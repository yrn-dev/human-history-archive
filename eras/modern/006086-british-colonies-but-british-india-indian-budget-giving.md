# British India

## Acquisition and Administration
The British presence in India was established through the actions of a trading company. Following a mutiny, the Indian Empire was annexed to the British Crown. Under the legislation titled *An Act for the Better Government of India*, the administrative structure was reorganized: the Governor-General became a Viceroy who represented the Sovereign, and a Secretary of State for India, responsible to the British Parliament, replaced the company. In 1877, Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India by Lord Beaconsfield.

The resulting governance is described as an autocracy without an autocrat, combining the characteristics of absolute monarchy with the impersonality of democratic officialdom. Because the Emperor serves as a golden symbol, individuals with complaints have no visible monarch to approach and must instead rely on circulating pamphlets in England or prompting questions within the British House of Commons. Consequently, India's level of attention from the British government fluctuates based on how occupied Parliament is with British affairs, often leaving the region at the mercy of a small group of higher officials.

## Trade and Economy
Trade served as a primary cause of imperial expansion and a bond of union. For a significant period, the East India Company held an important monopoly. However, the trade of India was thrown open to competition in 1813. This shift was part of a broader transition in British trade policy; for nearly two hundred years, the mercantile system and restrictions like the Navigation Act of 1660 had curtailed commercial intercourse to benefit British merchants. Following the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 and the introduction of free trade in Great Britain, colonies that had not yet received responsible government were empowered to abolish differential duties on foreign trade in 1846.

## Legal and Military Frameworks
Insolvency within India is governed by the Indian Insolvency Act 1848, which was later extended by Act XI of 1889. 

In the military sphere, the British and native armies in India utilized a dust-colored fabric known as khaki (derived from the Urdu word *khak*, meaning dust). This canvas, drill, or holland fabric was first worn in 1848 by the Guides, a mixed regiment of frontier troops. The use of khaki spread to other regiments and was used by some British troops during the Indian Mutiny of 1857-58, eventually becoming the nearly universal dress for troops in Asia and Africa.

## International Representation
Under the Covenant of the League of Nations dated April 28, 1919, the league was composed of "states, dominions, or colonies" that were stipulated to be "fully self-governing." India appeared as a separate sovereign state within this framework, though the archive notes that the Indian representative was expected to be a British nominee.
