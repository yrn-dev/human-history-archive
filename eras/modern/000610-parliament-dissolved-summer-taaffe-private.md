# The Premiership of Count Eduard Taaffe

Count Eduard Taaffe served as the Austrian prime minister from 1879 to 1893. His tenure was characterized by a strategy of "unpolitical politics," through which he maintained power for fourteen years by positioning himself between the Right and the constitutionalists.

## Rise to Power and Parliamentary Reconciliation
Taaffe ascended to the premiership in 1879 during a period of significant political instability. Previous attempts to govern had failed; a ministry involving Counts Taaffe and Alfred Potocki had resigned in January 1870 after disagreements over how to handle the opposition. Subsequently, the House had been reduced to only 110 members—consisting of Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia—after the Polish club, Slovenes, and Tirolese departed.

Upon becoming first minister, Taaffe initially attempted to govern with the help of moderates from nearly all parties. However, after the Liberals voted against the government on a critical military bill, Taaffe turned to the Right for support. This led to the resignation of the German members of his government, who were replaced by Czechs, Poles, and Clericals. This coalition, known as the "iron ring," left the German Liberals in a minority.

## Negotiations with Bohemia
A primary objective of Taaffe's early administration was the reconciliation of the Slav races, specifically the Czechs. In the summer, following the dissolution of Parliament, Taaffe engaged in private negotiations. He first persuaded Bohemian feudal proprietors to grant a certain number of seats to Feudalists who had previously been excluded. He then succeeded where Potocki had failed by reaching an agreement with the Czechs. Although the Czechs had taken seats in the Prague diet in 1878, they had practiced "passive resistance" toward the parliament in Vienna.

Under Taaffe, the representatives of Bohemia agreed to take their seats in the Vienna parliament, though they did so with the reservation that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. In return for this concession, Taaffe's government removed several of the most gross anomalies in the Bohemian electoral system, which had previously heavily favored the German minority. Additionally, the government consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague to aid the country's intellectual development.

## Governance and the "Iron Ring"
Taaffe's political strength derived from his lack of strict party affiliation; he had previously served in a Liberal government and did not adhere to the principles of the Federalists or the Clerical party. By avoiding contentious legislation and ruling according to the constitution, he ensured that the Right continued to support him to prevent the return of a Liberal government.

During his era (1879–1893), there was a notable development of Slav influence, particularly among the Poles and Czechs. However, the Czechs remained unsatisfied as they sought the formal recognition of the Bohemian kingdom.

## Later Years and Resignation
By 1890, Taaffe attempted to reconcile opposing parties in Bohemia. He chaired conferences in Vienna involving the Czechs, the German party, and the Feudal party. An agreement was reached regarding a revision of the electoral law and a separation between Czech and German districts. However, these measures failed in the diet because the "Young Czechs"—a more impatient faction—had been excluded from the conferences and utilized obstruction to oppose the bills, arguing that the measures threatened the unity of the Bohemian country.

In October 1893, Taaffe departed from his cautious approach by introducing a reform bill. While maintaining the chambers of commerce and the *curiae* of feudal proprietors, the bill proposed granting the franchise to anyone who could read and write and had resided in one place for six months. This was opposed by the Liberals, who feared the growth of anti-Semitism and socialism. It was also opposed by the Poles, who feared increased influence for the Ruthenes, and the Conservatives, who did not wish to transfer power to the indigent. Having failed to consult his ally Hohenwart or the other parties, Taaffe found his position hopeless and resigned.
