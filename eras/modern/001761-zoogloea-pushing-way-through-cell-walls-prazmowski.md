# Zoogloea

Zoogloea is a specialized stage in the life history of Schizomycetes, characterized by the formation of a gelatinous matrix that encloses numerous bacterial elements, such as bacteria or cocci. This condition is compared to the "palmella" condition found in lower algae. Once considered a distinct genus, zoogloea is now understood to be a resting condition of the Schizomycetes.

## Formation and Composition
The zoogloea stage occurs when dense swarms of vegetative cells become fixed in a matrix created by their own contiguous cell-walls, which become enormously swollen and diffluent. This process is initiated by the active division of several mother-cells or a single mother-cell. The resulting progeny continue to secrete cell-wall substance, which absorbs water many times its own volume, creating a consistent matrix in which the cells come to rest.

The composition of this matrix varies depending on the species; in some instances, it consists primarily of a proteid substance, while in others it is mainly cellulose. The physical properties of the matrix also differ, ranging from a thick solution of gum to a horny and resistant substance. If the zoogloea forms on a solid substratum, it may become firm and horny, though immersion in water will soften it.

## Physical Forms and Characteristics
Young zoogloea can assume a variety of characteristic shapes, including:
*   Spherical
*   Ovoid
*   Reticular
*   Filamentous
*   Lamellar
*   Fruiticose

These forms may change as the mass increases in size or comes into contact with other masses. In environments where small quantities of iron exist in the medium, older zoogloeae may precipitate oxide of iron within the matrix. 

Zoogloea can appear as a membrane on the surface of a medium (such as the "Mycoderma" of Pasteur's *Micrococcus* or a pellicle of *Bacillus subtilis*) or as branched masses and irregular clumps submerged in the medium, sometimes reaching several inches across. Because of its gelatinous nature, foreign bodies can become entangled in the matrix. Additionally, compound zoogloeae may form when several distinct forms are apposed, a phenomenon commonly observed in macerating troughs.

Specific examples of zoogloea include the egg-shaped masses and reticulate forms of *Beggiatoa roseo-persicina* (*Bacterium rubescens*), the diffluent matrix enveloping colonies of *Myconostoc*, the branched fruticose zoogloea of *Cladothrix*, and the tablets of cocci found in *Bacterium merismopedioides*.

## Biological Role and Transition
The zoogloea serves as a resting state. However, when conditions become favorable, the elements within the zoogloea regain activity. They move out of the gelatinous matrix and distribute themselves into the surrounding medium, where they resume growing and multiplying.

## Interaction with Leguminous Plants
The zoogloea stage plays a critical role in the symbiotic relationship between certain bacteria and leguminous plants. In arable soil, motile rod-like bacteria known as *Bacterium radicicola* enter the root-hairs of these plants. The bacteria pass down the root-hair in the form of a long, slimy thread of zoogloea, which pushes its way through the cell-walls and penetrates the root tissues.

This invasion causes the tissues to become hypertrophied, resulting in the formation of a root nodule. Within these nodules, the bacteria multiply and develop, drawing material from the host plant. Some bacteria develop into "bacteroids," which are eventually broken down and absorbed by the plant. This process allows atmospheric nitrogen to be absorbed by the nodules, built into the bacterial cell, and subsequently handed over to the host plant. While this is a case of symbiosis, the green plant restricts bacterial development to the nodules and eventually absorbs the bacteria for its own use.
