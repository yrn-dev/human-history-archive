# The Development of Banking in the United Kingdom (Late 17th to 19th Century)

The evolution of banking in the United Kingdom was significantly shaped by the establishment of central institutions and a series of restrictive parliamentary acts that governed the formation of joint-stock banks and the issuance of currency.

## The Bank of England
Founded in 1694, the Bank of England originated from a loan of £1,200,000 to the government. This sum was subscribed in just over ten days, between June 21 and July 2, 1694. On July 10, the subscribers appointed Michael Godfrey as deputy-governor and Sir John Houblon as governor. Godfrey, who was killed on July 17, 1695, during the siege of Namur, authored a pamphlet explaining the bank's purpose and utility to the country. This document addressed contemporary fears that the bank might collaborate with King William III to make the monarch absolute and render parliaments useless.

From its inception, the Bank of England maintained a practical, though perhaps not entirely legal, monopoly on issuing notes in London. It was eventually surrounded by various private banks, some of which held considerable power.

## Banking in Scotland and Ireland
Following the establishment of the Bank of England, other regions sought similar public institutions. In 1695, the Scottish parliament passed an act to establish a public bank. The Bank of Scotland is the only Scottish bank created by an act of parliament. Its terms were more favorable than those of the Bank of England, as it was granted the exclusive privilege of banking for twenty-one years without providing any consideration. Due to the reign of William III or general national caution, the bank was prohibited under heavy penalty from lending money to the king under any circumstances. Early directors, including Thomas Coutts, began granting cash credit accounts and receiving deposits with interest; records of the mode of keeping these accounts date back to 1729.

In Ireland, efforts to form a public bank began in 1695 when principal merchants in Dublin met to propose a bank modeled after the Bank of England. However, this proposal lacked favor for many years. The Bank of Ireland was not established until 1783, with David La Touche, junior, serving as the first governor. The institution was highly successful but operated under extreme jealousy toward rivals. Legislation decreed that no company or society exceeding six members, other than the Bank of Ireland, could borrow or take up money on notes or bills payable on demand. This restriction was modified in 1821 to allow companies larger than six people to establish banks if they were 50 miles from Dublin, a limit that was not withdrawn until 1845.

## Legislative Restrictions and Joint-Stock Banks
English banking, which can be traced back to 16th-century London goldsmiths, was heavily influenced by parliamentary acts. The acts of 1694 and 1697 declared that no joint-stock bank was to be established within England while the Bank of England continued to exist. Furthermore, the act of 1708 (confirmed in 1800) prohibited any partnership or company exceeding six persons in England from borrowing or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand or in less than six months.

Significant changes occurred in 1826, when one act prohibited bank notes under £5 and another allowed co-partnerships of more than six persons (joint-stock companies) to be established beyond 65 miles from London. In 1833, joint-stock banks were permitted within the 65-mile limit, though they were forbidden from issuing notes within that radius. Because the power to issue notes was the primary introduction to deposit-banking, banks without this power could scarcely exist outside of London.

## Note Issuance and the Bank Act of 1844
The Bank Act of 1844 aimed to concentrate note issuance within the Bank of England to secure its monopoly in England and Wales. By 1906, nearly all provincial banks in England had lost their right of issue. This differed from the Scottish and Irish systems, where local issues provided an advantage to industry.

The Bank Charter Acts of 1844 and 1845 established a specific hierarchy for note issuance:
* **Within a 3-mile radius of the city of London:** The Bank of England held an absolute monopoly.
* **Between 3 and 65 miles from the city:** A concurrent right existed for banks of six or fewer persons established and issuing notes before 1844.
* **Beyond 65 miles:** The privilege was available to all banks established and issuing notes before 1844, provided they had not lost the right through abandonment, bankruptcy, or temporary suspension.

While some authorities suggest the Companies Consolidation Act 1908 allowed banks within the 3-to-65-mile radius to increase their membership from six to ten persons without losing note-issuing power, the general trend of absorption by larger joint-stock banks minimized the output of notes other than those from the Bank of England.

## Other Financial Institutions
Beyond the major public banks, various provincial banks existed. Smiths' of Nottingham claims a founding date of 1688, predating the Bank of England. Other notable institutions include the Bank of Bristol (founded 1750), the Hull Old Bank (founded 1754), and Gurney & Co., established at the end of the 18th century. Additionally, Nicholas Barbon, an economist and M.P., founded a land bank that was united with that of John Briscoe in 1696.
