# Carpathians

The Carpathian Mountains (known in Latin as *Monies Sarmatici* or *Montes Nivium*) constitute the eastern wing of Europe's great central mountain system. After the Alps, they are the most extensive mountain system on the continent, covering an area of 72,600 square miles. The system begins on the Danube near Pressburg and ends on the Danube near Orsova, surrounding Transylvania and Hungary in a large semicircle that opens toward the south-west. The total length of the Carpathians exceeds 800 miles, with a width that varies between 7 and 230 miles.

## Orography and Structure
The Carpathians are not an uninterrupted chain but are composed of several geologically and orographically distinct groups. The system is divided into two primary groups: the mountains of Transylvania and the Carpathians proper. The Carpathians proper are further divided into an inner wall, which fills Upper Hungary, and an outer wall. This outer wall forms the frontier between Hungary and adjacent provinces of Austria; it is a roughly circular mass extending approximately 600 miles from Pressburg to the Golden Bistritza and the valley of the Viso. The Poprad divides this outer wall into the eastern (or wooded) Carpathians and the western Carpathians.

In terms of altitude, the Carpathians only reach over 8,000 feet in a few locations. They lack the glaciers, perpetual snow, high waterfalls, and bold peaks characteristic of the Alps. While they share some similarities in flora, structure, and appearance with the middle region of the Alps, they are considered less magnificent in scenery.

## Lakes and Passes
Mountain lakes are numerous throughout the Carpathian system, though they are smaller and less beautiful than Alpine lakes. The most numerous and largest lakes are found in the Tatra Mountains; local populations refer to these as "eyes of the sea" due to a belief that they communicate with the sea via subterranean channels.

Accessibility varies by region, with the central and western Carpathians being more accessible than the Transylvanian Mountains and the eastern Carpathians. Key passes include:
*   **Western Carpathians:** The Jablunka pass (1,970 ft.), which serves as the primary route between Hungary and Silesia and is crossed by the Breslau-Budapest railway, as well as the Jordanow, Lissa, Wlara, Hrozinkau, and Strany passes.
*   **Central Carpathians:** The Tylicz pass (from Tarnow to Bartfeld), the Telgart pass (from the Gran to the Poprad), and the road from Neumarkt to Kesmark through the High Tatra.
*   **Eastern Carpathians:** The Stiol pass in Bukovina, the Dukla pass, the Uszok pass (crossed by the road from Sambor to Ungvar), the Mezo-Laborcz pass (crossed by the railway from Przemysl to Tokaj), the Vereczke pass (crossed by the railway from Munkacs to Lemberg), and the Delatyn or Korosmezo pass (3,300 ft.), also known as the Magyar route, which is crossed by the railway from Debreczen to Kolomea.

## Regional Geography and the Danube
The Carpathians are separated from the Balkan Mountains at Orsova and from the Silesian and Moravian chains by the valleys of the Oder and March. They meet the Alps at only two points: the Bakony Mountains at Vacz (Waitzen) and the Leitha Mountains at Pressburg.

The Danube river plays a significant role in the region's geography. In prehistoric times, much of the Hungarian plains were an inland sea; when this sea burst its bounds, the Danube forced its way through the Carpathians at the Kazan defile. In this defile, the river is hemmed in by precipitous rocks, narrowing to 162 yards in width with a depth of 150 feet.

Budapest, the capital of Hungary, is situated where the outlying spurs of the Alps (the Bakony Mountains) meet the Carpathians. The city dominates the strategic approach from the west to the great Hungarian plain. The city's geography is defined by the Danube, which is 300 to 650 yards broad at this point. On the right bank lie the hills of Buda and O-Buda, while the left bank consists of the flat sandy plain of Pest and Köbánya.
