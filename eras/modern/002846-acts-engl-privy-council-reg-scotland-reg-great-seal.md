# Acts of Engl. Privy Council; Reg. P.C., Scotland; Reg. Great Seal of Scotland

The *Acts of Engl. Privy Council*, *Reg. P.C., Scotland*, and *Reg. Great Seal of Scotland* are historical records and registers associated with the administration and governance of England and Scotland. These documents serve as primary sources for historians, appearing in citations alongside other state papers, such as the *Letters and Papers of Henry VIII* (1540-1545), the *Hamilton Papers*, *Border Papers*, and the *Cal. of Scottish State-Papers* by Bain and Thorp.

## The Privy Council and the Lord President
The Privy Council in England is an institution whose formal direction was historically managed by various officials. Prior to 1679, the council was directed by the lord keeper of the seal, the lord chancellor, or specific court officials. While a special lord president of the council was appointed during the reign of Charles I, the position remained unfilled during the subsequent reign. It was not until 1679 that the office of lord president became a permanent fixture.

The lord president is a member of the ministry and the cabinet, appointed by the sovereign through a declaration made in council. The holder of this office is invariably a member of the House of Lords. Historically, the lord president held significant importance when the Privy Council's powers were more extensive, exercising authority through various committees. For instance, committees of the lords of the council previously handled duties that are now the responsibility of the Board of Trade and the secretary of state for foreign affairs.

Until 1855, the lord president was responsible for the education department, as well as duties regarding agriculture (now under the Board of Agriculture and Fisheries) and public health (now under the Local Government Board). Following the creation of the vice-president of the council in 1855, the duties of the lord president shifted to drawing up council minutes on subjects not belonging to other state departments and presiding over the council's infrequent meetings. The office is often held in conjunction with other roles; for example, in 1903, the lord president was also the president of the Board of Education, and in Gladstone's fourth ministry, the role was held by the secretary of state for India.

## The Privy Council of Scotland
The Privy Council of Scotland functioned as a body capable of adjudicating charges and appointing officials. In one instance, the council acquitted Graham of charges regarding oppression and exaction, while simultaneously condemning Sir John Dalrymple to imprisonment and a fine for interfering with Graham's proceedings. Following these events, Graham was nominated to the privy council of Scotland in May 1683.

## The Great Seal and Legal Requirements
The Great Seal serves as a formal instrument of royal consent and authentication. Under the Royal Marriage Act of 1772 (12 Geo. III. c. 11), the Great Seal was central to the legality of marriages involving descendants of George II. The act stipulated that no descendant of George II—excluding the issue of princesses married into or marrying into foreign families—could contract matrimony without the consent of the king, his heirs, and successors, which had to be signified under the Great Seal.

However, the act provided a mechanism for descendants over the age of twenty-five to marry without this consent. Such an individual could contract a marriage if they provided twelve months' notice to the privy council. Such a marriage would be considered good unless both Houses of Parliament declared their disapprobation.

## Notable Figures and Appointments
Various high-ranking nobles have held positions within these administrative structures. Evelyn Pierrepont (c. 1655-1726), the 5th earl and 1st duke of Kingston, served as a commissioner for the union with Scotland and was created marquess of Dorchester in 1706. He was appointed as a privy councillor and later served as lord president of the council and lord privy seal.
