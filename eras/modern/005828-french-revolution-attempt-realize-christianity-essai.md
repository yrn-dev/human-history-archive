# The French Revolution and Christianity

## Efforts Toward Reconciliation
During the period of the French Revolution, efforts were made to demonstrate that Catholic Christianity was not irreconcilable with modern conceptions of political liberty. A primary figure in this effort was Gregoire, who worked wholeheartedly to prove this compatibility. However, these efforts were ultimately defeated. This failure is attributed in part to the Revolution's lack of experience in the proper use of liberty, which caused it to transition into a military despotism. This military regime subsequently allied itself with the spiritual despotism of Rome.

Furthermore, when the Revolution was overthrown, the parties of reaction sought salvation through the "union of altar and throne." It is suggested that Gregoire's Gallicanism may have been fundamentally irreconcilable with the Catholic idea of authority. Nevertheless, his perspective made traditional religion possible for many French Catholics who remained passionately attached to the benefits brought by the Revolution. Had his views prevailed, it might have prevented the "fatal gulf" between Catholicism and Liberalism that was later sought to be made impassable by Pius IX's Syllabus of 1864.

## Military and Political Context of the Revolution
The intelligence of the French army during the Revolution was largely the result of a period of intelligent military writing and thinking. This trend began among French soldiers following the defeats of the army of Louis XV during the Seven Years War, and Napoleon himself showed traces of this inspiration. 

Diplomatically, the French monarchy faced challenges such as the Declaration of Pillnitz. This was a diplomatic démarche by Austrian statesmanship intended to save the collective face of European monarchies by announcing sympathy for the French Bourbons. The declaration included a proviso that unanimity must be secured before any intervention was attempted, though most historians and French opinion overlooked this proviso and focused on the announcement. During this era, there was a deep divergence of interests between France and Great Britain; for instance, it is considered impossible that the Whig nobles and Pitt would have been allies of the France of 1793.

## Christian Socialism and Historical Theory
In the aftermath of the revolutionary era, other thinkers attempted to synthesize Christian values with social organization. Philippe Joseph Benjamin Buchez (1796-1865), a French author and politician, developed what he regarded as a "Christian socialism." Buchez had previously been a member of the Saint-Simonian Society but left due to his aversion to the "strange religious ideas" developed by its "Supreme Father," Enfantin. To advocate for his principles of Christian socialism, Buchez founded the periodical *L'Européen*.

Buchez also contributed to the science of history in his 1833 work, *Introduction à la science de l'histoire*. His theory proposed a division of historical development into four great epochs, each originated by four universal revelations. He further divided each epoch into three periods—desire, reasoning, and performance—and subdivided those into theoretical and practical ages.

## General Perspectives on Christian Principles
While not specific to the French Revolution, broader historical analysis notes that the animating spirit of Christianity, as found in the gospels, was flatly opposed to both slavery and private property. However, the actual attitude of Christians was less definite, as they generally focused on ameliorating these conditions rather than abolishing them. Patristic theory went as far as to justify slavery as a result of the Fall.

In later periods, the prestige of Christianity was further undermined by the "Darwinian crisis" and the narrowness of priestcraft. This occurred at a time when the mechanical revolution brought different races and political ideas into close contact, and different classes became aware of mutual economic antagonism. Consequently, the fundamental teaching of brotherhood was discredited, and official, orthodox Christianity ceased to be a power in politics and social questions.
