# W.W. Rockhill

W.W. Rockhill was an American traveller and researcher whose explorations and writings earned him a significant reputation in the annals of Asiatic research. Between 1889 and 1891, Rockhill commenced a series of Tibetan journeys. During these travels, he attempted to reach the city of Lhasa, though he was unsuccessful in doing so.

## Observations on Trade and Commerce
During his travels, Rockhill provided detailed accounts of regional trade, specifically regarding musk. Writing from Lusar on February 15, 1892, while traveling from Sining, Rockhill noted an increase in the musk trade, with traders from Ssŭ-ch’uan and Canton purchasing the product. He observed that high-quality musk could command a price of four times its weight in silver. 

Rockhill identified that the best time for purchasing musk is from the seventh to the ninth moon, which corresponds to the period from the latter part of August to the middle of November. He noted that the purity of the musk is tested by examining its color and observed that Tibetans sometimes adulterate the substance by mixing it with blood and tsamba. In his research, he recorded that Mongols refer to musk as *owo* and Tibetans call it *latsé*. He specifically mentioned "white musk," known as *latsé karpo* in Tibetan and *tsahan owo* in Mongol, though he remained uncertain if the term "white" referred to the musk itself or the hair on the skin of the musk pouch.

## Religious and Cultural Research
Rockhill's research extended to the study of Tibetan religious sects, particularly the Bönbo. He encountered Bönbo Lamas at Kumbum and Kao miao-tzŭ, describing them as wearing red gowns and possessing "huge mops of hair," noting that they appeared dirtier than the general population. 

Upon examining volumes of Bönbo sacred literature, including a funeral service, Rockhill observed that the "Bönbo jargon" was three-fourths Buddhistic in its nomenclature. His findings indicate that while Bönbo Lamas are popular among agricultural Tibetans, they are less so among pastoral tribes, who primarily belong to the Gélupa sect of the orthodox Buddhist Church.

## Linguistic and Ethnographic Notes
Rockhill contributed to the understanding of regional terminology and demographics. Regarding the city of Sining, he noted that Tibetans refer to it as *Seling K’ar* or *Kuar*, and Mongols call it *Seling K’utun*, with *K’ar* and *K’utun* meaning "fortified city." 

In his notes on the Tangut people, Rockhill cited Chinese authorities stating that the name Tangut was originally Turkish and belonged to a people living in the Altaï. He described the Tangut population as a mixture of various groups, including Turks, Tibetans, Chinese, Uighúrs, and Tukuhuns.

Additionally, Rockhill documented local dietary habits and wildlife. He noted that in Tibet and the Kokonor country, a substance called *krut* or *chura* is softened in tea and eaten alone or mixed with *tsamba* (parched barley meal). While visiting the Hsi-ningites, he observed a fondness for cracked wheat (*mei-tzŭ fan*) and oatmeal (*yen-mei ch’en*), the latter of which is eaten with neat’s-foot oil or the water used to cook mutton. In his zoological notes, he recorded that the wild ass is known as *Kyang* in Tibetan and *Holu* or *Hulan* in Mongol, while the Chinese refer to it as *yeh ma* ("wild horse").

## Historical Analysis
Rockhill also engaged in the analysis of historical accounts regarding the inhabitants of Tibet. He specifically addressed frequent accusations of cannibalism found in old accounts. Rockhill noted that, to his knowledge, such charges were not made against the Tibetans by Oriental writers, although Armenian historians of the thirteenth century and Arab travellers of the ninth century claimed that the Chinese practiced cannibalism.
