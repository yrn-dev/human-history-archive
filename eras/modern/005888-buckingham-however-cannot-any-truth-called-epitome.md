# Buckingham

## Definition of Epitome
The term "epitome" originates from the Greek *epitome* (from *epitemnein*, meaning "to cut short"). It refers to an abstract, summary, or abridgment that provides the salient points of a law case, book, or specific event. Through transference, the word is also used to describe a miniature representation of a larger concrete or abstract thing. An example of this usage is Ruskin's description of St Mark's as the "epitome of Venice," as the site contains architectural examples spanning from the 10th to the 19th centuries.

## Character and Career of Buckingham
Despite descriptions suggesting a man so various that he seemed to be "all mankind's epitome"—characterized as a buffoon, statesman, fiddler, and chymist who was "stiff in opinions" and "always in the wrong"—it is argued that Buckingham cannot truthfully be called the "epitome of mankind." Instead, his life was defined by insignificance, imperfection, aimlessness, and a neglect of his talents and opportunities. 

Brian Fairfax noted that while Buckingham "saw and approved the best," he too often followed the inferior (*deteriora sequi*). Buckingham himself reflected on this waste of time; in his youth, he wrote of "wanton houres" fleeing and laughing at him, and his final recorded words upon facing death were: "O! what a prodigal have I been of that most valuable of all possessions—Time!"

## Political Conflict and the Petition of Right
Buckingham was an active member of the House of Lords during the parliamentary battle regarding the Petition of Right. He resisted the demands of the Commons that the king abandon the claim to imprison individuals without showing cause. When the king provided an unsatisfactory answer to the petition on June 2, the Commons suspected Buckingham had dictated the response.

Coke eventually identified the duke as the "grievance of grievances" and the cause of all the nation's miseries. Although the king granted a satisfactory answer on June 7, the Commons continued their remonstrance and demanded on June 11 that Buckingham be removed from office. King Charles refused and subsequently prorogued parliament in anger. This led to excited popular feeling and the circulation of lampoons. During this period, a quack doctor and astrologer named Dr Lambe, who was believed to influence Buckingham, was murdered in the streets of London.

## Literary Works
Buckingham possessed undeveloped but undoubted poetical gifts, producing satires and occasional verses. A collection of these was published by Tom Brown in 1704, though it included pieces not written by Buckingham. He authored *The Rehearsal* (first performed December 7, 1671, and published in 1672), a popular satire on the heroic drama and Dryden, which later influenced Sheridan's *Critic* and Fielding's *Tom Thumb the Great*.

His other published works include:
* *The Chances* (1682), adapted from Fletcher.
* *The Restoration or Right will take place* (1714), adapted from Beaumont and Fletcher's *Philaster*.
* *The Battle of Sedgmoor* (1704).
* *The Militant Couple* (1704).

A work titled *A Demonstration of the Deity* is also mentioned by Wood, though no trace of it currently exists. The most recent edition of his works was published by T. Evans in 1775.

## Other Historical Figures and Entities
The archive contains records of other individuals and entities associated with the name Buckingham:

**Henry Stafford, 2nd Duke of Buckingham (1454-1483):** The son of Humphrey Stafford and grandson of the 1st duke, Henry had royal blood on both sides. He married Catherine Woodville, the queen's sister, in 1466. He became a knight of the Garter in 1474 and served as high steward at the trial of George, duke of Clarence, in 1478. He later assisted Richard, duke of Gloucester, in obtaining possession of the young king, for which he was rewarded as the constable of all royal castles in the Welsh Marches and the principality, as well as justiciar and chamberlain of North and South Wales. He urged citizens at the Guildhall on June 24, 1483, to accept Richard as king. However, by August, he withdrew to Brecon.

**The Earl of Bristol:** Created earl on September 15, he was involved in marriage treaty negotiations that were wrecked in March 1623 by the arrival of Buckingham and Charles. Due to Buckingham's "implacable jealousy" regarding a letter Bristol wrote to James about Buckingham's unpopularity with Spanish ministers, Bristol was forbidden from court and confined to Sherborne in 1624. After the death of James, Charles I removed him from the privy council. Bristol eventually took his seat in the Lords in 1626 to demand justice against Buckingham, leading to an accusation of high treason and imprisonment in the Tower until March 17, 1628.

**Geography and Landownership:** Following the Conquest, Walter Giffard, the first earl of Buckingham, was one of only two Englishmen to retain estates of importance. In the county of Buckinghamshire, the family of Hampden is the only one claiming an unbroken line of estate ownership from Saxon times.
