# The Siege of La Rochelle and Naval Operations under Richelieu

## The Siege of La Rochelle
During the early 17th century, tensions rose between England and France, exacerbated by King Charles's persistence in seizing French vessels, which prompted French reprisals. In this climate of impending war, Cardinal Richelieu sought to prevent the English fleet from gaining access to La Rochelle. To ensure the city remained closed to English entry, Richelieu laid siege to this stronghold of the French Huguenots.

In response to the siege, Buckingham departed from Portsmouth on June 27, 1627. He led a numerous fleet and a considerable land force with the objective of relieving the besieged city.

## Naval Development at Brest
Beyond the conflict at La Rochelle, Cardinal Richelieu recognized the strategic advantages of Brest as a seaport town. In 1631, he constructed a harbour featuring wooden wharves, which subsequently became a station for the French navy. These facilities were later improved by Colbert, who replaced the wooden wharves with masonry, and further enhanced by Vauban's fortifications between 1680 and 1688.

The naval importance of Brest continued to grow throughout the 18th century. The location saw significant engagements, including a miserable defeat of an English squadron under John, 3rd Lord Berkeley, in 1694 during an attempted landing. Conversely, in 1794 during the revolutionary war, the English admiral Howe thoroughly beat the French fleet under Villaret de Joyeuse at the same location.

## Political Strategy and the "Independent Belgium" Plan
Cardinal Richelieu also pursued geopolitical strategies to undermine Spanish power in Europe. In 1634, France and Holland entered into a special treaty against Spain. Richelieu proposed the creation of an independent and permanently neutral Belgium, which he believed would destroy the cornerstone of Spanish power. Under this scheme, Belgium would have the right to conclude offensive alliances, though its territorial integrity would not be guaranteed.

This plan failed because the Belgians did not revolt against Spanish rule, due to the presence of strong Spanish armies within their borders. The idea was later revisited in 1658 by Richelieu's successor, Cardinal Mazarin. However, Mazarin's proposal faced strong opposition from England and Holland, who feared the extension of the French menace. Additionally, the Dutch "Staatspensionnaris" De Witt opposed the plan, fearing that an independent Belgium with a free Scheldt would ruin Dutch trade.

## Administrative and Literary Influence
Richelieu's influence extended into the cultural and administrative spheres of France. He gathered a band of writers to execute his literary ideas, including Jean Desmarets (Sieur de Saint-Sorlin). Desmarets, who was introduced to Richelieu around the age of thirty, wrote for the theatre at the Cardinal's request, producing works such as the comedy *Les Visionnaires* (1637), the prose-tragedy *Érigone* (1638), and the verse tragedy *Scipion* (1639). Desmarets eventually attained the official position of secretary-general of the fleet of the Levant.

## Legacy of Centralization
Cardinal Richelieu is credited with preparing the foundation of absolute power that was later built upon by Cardinal Mazarin under King Louis XIV. Richelieu was a contemporary of King James I of England. Through the efforts of Richelieu and Mazarin, the French monarchy successfully tamed the aristocracy, transforming great nobles into court servants and officials. This centralization of power shifted the burden of taxation onto the common people, as the nobility and clergy remained exempt from many taxes.
