# Army and Navy

The organization, discipline, and operational readiness of land and sea forces vary significantly across different nations and services. While armies often rely on a system of mobilization to reach full fighting strength, navies are generally structured for immediate availability.

## Organizational Structure and Mobilization
The fundamental difference between the organization of a fleet and an army lies in their readiness. Armies typically maintain only a small proportion of soldiers in regular service during peace, who serve as the framework for battalions, guard duties, and the training of recruits. An army is only ready to fight once the order to mobilize is given, calling up trained men living in civil life.

In contrast, navies are largely always ready. The British Navy, for example, permanently manned its best ships with whole-time men known as active service ratings. Nearly three-quarters of its ships could enter action without calling upon reserves. Mobilization in the fleet was limited to the oldest and most obsolete ships, which were manned by the Naval Reserve—men who had returned to civil life.

Other national structures vary; for instance, the military forces of the Bulgarian principality were organized by Russian officers between 1879 and 1885. Under a law dated January 1, 1904, the Bulgarian army was divided into the active or field army (comprising the active army and the active army reserve), the reserve army, and the *opltchenïe* (militia). While the first two could operate outside the kingdom, the militia operated only within the frontier for defense.

## Naval Roles and the United States Marine Corps
The responsibilities of a navy can be vast. The British Navy, for example, faced the burden of protecting food, raw materials, and commerce across thousands of miles of ocean, as the nation could not subsist on its own produce nor defend its soil with a regular army that might be ordered to India. This role included the right of capture of private property at sea, which exposed merchant ships to attacks from warships or converted armed-merchantmen.

In the United States, the Marine Corps exists as a separate military body under the control of the Navy Department. Established in 1775, its strength was fixed by an Act of Congress on March 3, 1899, at 211 officers and 5,920 non-commissioned officers and men. Commanded by a brigadier-general, the corps follows the British model of organization but the dress of the U.S. army. Marines are stationed at shore barracks for drilling and police duties before being sent to ships as a "guard." On ships, they perform sentry and orderly duties and are trained in naval signaling, rowing, and gun drill. In combat, they serve as riflemen or man the guns. They are also used to garrison places under navy control beyond U.S. territorial limits or to guard legations and American interests abroad.

## Rank and Symbolism
The term "ensign" carries different meanings across services. Generally, it refers to a military or naval standard, banner, or emblem. In the British Navy, an ensign is a flag with a red, white, or blue ground and the Union Jack in the upper corner. The white ensign is reserved for the royal yacht squadron and the royal navy; the blue and red ensigns are used by the naval reserve and the merchant service, respectively.

Historically, "ensign" was also a rank. Until 1871, it was the lowest grade of commissioned officer in British infantry regiments, tasked with carrying the regiment's colours. In the 16th century, the term was sometimes corrupted to "ancient." In the United States Navy, the title of ensign replaced "passed midshipman" in 1862 and ranks equivalent to a second lieutenant in the army.

## Discipline and Legal Frameworks
Desertion is a serious offense punishable by death in all armies while on active service. In Europe, compulsory service makes desertion infrequent, usually occurring when a soldier intends to leave the country entirely. The English army previously tattooed the letter "D" on deserters to prevent re-enlistment, though this was abandoned due to public misconceptions that the process involved torture.

Similar provisions for desertion exist in the Naval Deserters Act 1847 and the Navy Discipline Act 1866. In the U.S. Navy, "straggling" refers to absence without leave where there is no probable intent to desert. The U.S. government offers rewards of $20 to $30 for the arrest of army and navy deserters. In the British merchant service, desertion is defined as the unjustified abandonment of duty by quitting a ship before the engagement ends with the intent of not returning.

## Strategic Cooperation and Reform
Effective military operation often requires inter-service cooperation. Reports have noted "perfect cooperation" between the Navy in European waters and the Army. Administrative success in these efforts is often attributed to departments such as the Adjutant General's Department, the Inspector General's Department (responsible for discipline), and the Judge Advocate General's Department, which handles complex legal and international problems.

Naval power is also subject to periods of intense reform. The Royal Navy underwent significant changes under Fisher, who forced the service to review its existence and move from a state of complacency to intense activity, though this period was marked by internal hostility and intrigue. Simultaneously, other nations sought to expand their power; for example, the German Kaiser pursued a "great navy" to match the height of his army, converting the island of Heligoland into a naval fortress and utilizing a "mailed fist" approach to expansion.
