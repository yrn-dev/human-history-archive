# Canada

## Military History and Defense
Canada's military history within the British Empire includes periods of both imperial support and local administration. Between 1802 and 1805, Sir Isaac Brock served in Canada with his regiment. He returned in 1806 due to the imminent threat of war between the United States and Great Britain. From September 1806 until August 1810, Brock was responsible for the garrison at Quebec. By 1810, he assumed command of the troops in Upper Canada and subsequently became the provisional lieutenant-governor, taking over the civil administration of the province.

During the war of 1812, Sir Isaac Brock defended Upper Canada against invasion by the United States. Despite facing disaffection and various difficulties, he organized the province's militia and repelled the invaders. On August 16, 1812, Brock led a force of approximately 730 men and 600 Indians, commanded by their chief Tecumseh. This force compelled General William Hull's American force of 2,500 men to surrender at Detroit. This victory earned Brock a knighthood of the Bath and the title "the hero of Upper Canada." Brock was later killed on October 13, 1812, at the battle of Queenston Heights.

## Imperial Contributions and Global Conflict
Canada has contributed significant military forces to broader imperial efforts. During the initial movements of the Imperial concentration, approximately two divisions were transported from Canada to England. Additionally, four Canadian and Australian divisions completed their training in England and Egypt. These forces were considered to be in a more advanced state of preparation than the 24 divisions of the New Armies being raised by Lord Kitchener or the 10 divisions of Territorials remaining in England. The transport of these great convoys from Canada, along with forces from India and Australia, was conducted while enemy cruisers remained active in the outer seas.

## Commercial Relations and Imperial Sentiment
In the late 19th century, Canada played a leading role in shaping commercial relations within the British Empire. At the Colonial Conference in Ottawa in 1894, views were expressed regarding the relationship between Great Britain's foreign trade in manufactured goods and the markets for colonial raw materials. It was noted that injury to Great Britain's foreign trade would necessarily react upon the colonies.

Canada became the first important British colony to give substantial expression to this new imperial sentiment in commercial matters. In 1897, Canada introduced an imperial tariff. This system granted a deduction of 25% on customs duties imposed on British goods without requiring any reciprocal advantage. Canada offered the same advantage on equal terms to all other British colonies trading with her.
