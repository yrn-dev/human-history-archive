# Seine: Prefectural Administration and the Parisian Mob

The department of the Seine, which encompasses the city of Paris and surrounding areas such as the town of Asnières, has been the site of significant political volatility and administrative challenges throughout French history. The interaction between government officials and the Parisian populace, often characterized as "the mob," has frequently defined the stability of the ruling regime.

## The Administration of Odilon Barrot
Following the revolution of July 1830, Camille Hyacinthe Odilon Barrot was appointed as the prefect of the department of the Seine. Barrot had previously been an active participant in the revolution, having joined the National Guard and served as the secretary of the municipal commission at the hotel-de-ville, which functioned as a provisional government. In that capacity, he had been tasked with delivering a protest to the chamber of deputies that outlined the terms advanced Liberals wished to impose on the newly elected king.

During his tenure as prefect of the Seine, Barrot's governance was marked by a specific approach to the unrest in Paris. He was noted for his extreme gentleness toward individuals who demanded the prosecution of the ministers of Charles X. Additionally, he made concessions to the Parisian mob. These actions and his lenient disposition eventually led to an unfavorable outcome.

## The Role of the Parisian Mob in Political Upheaval
The "mob" of Paris has historically acted as a catalyst for systemic change and violence. In July 1789, the passions of the crowd were inflamed by Camille Desmoulins, who, after announcing the dismissal of Necker outside a café in the garden of the Palais Royal, called the people "To arms!" This event triggered a surge through Paris where the crowd procured arms by force, leading to the organization of the Parisian militia (later the National Guard) and the taking of the Bastille on July 14.

Further instability occurred in October, when a mob marched from Paris to Versailles, resulting in the violent transport of the king and queen back to Paris. This lawlessness was viewed with disgust and "divine rage" by observers who abhorred the violence of the Parisian insurgents and the naked doctrines of right presented in the French assembly of 1789.

## Escalation and Violence
The influence of the Parisian populace continued to grow, often sweeping away moderates in favor of more radical elements. By June 20, 1792, the mob flooded the Tuileries, and on August 10, the Swiss guard was massacred while the royal family was imprisoned. This period was characterized by murders in prisons during September and the eventual execution of the king in January 1793.

The power of the Parisian populace was such that government officials often felt threatened. François Nicolas Léonard Buzot, a Girondist deputy, attempted to defend the Convention against the populace of Paris by demanding a national guard formed from the departments. This proposal caused the Parisians to become extremely bitter toward Buzot and the Girondists, contributing to the environment that led to the proscription of the Girondins in June 1793.

## Geography of the Department
The department of the Seine includes various urban and industrial centers. One such location is Asnières, a town situated on the left bank of the Seine, approximately 1-1/2 miles north-northwest of the fortifications of Paris. As of 1906, Asnières had a population of 35,883. The town grew rapidly as a favorite boating center for Parisians and developed industries in perfumery, the manufacture of colours, and boat-building.
