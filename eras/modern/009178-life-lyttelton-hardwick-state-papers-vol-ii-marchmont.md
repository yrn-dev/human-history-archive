# George Lyttelton, 1st Baron Lyttelton

George Lyttelton, 1st Baron (1709–1773), was an English man of letters and statesman. Born at Hagley, Worcestershire, he was a descendant of the great jurist Sir Thomas Littleton. His father, Sir Thomas Lyttelton, 4th bart. (who died in 1751), was recognized as one of the most capable Whig debaters in the House of Commons during the reign following the revolution of 1688.

## Early Life and Education
Lyttelton received his education at Oxford and Eton. In 1728, he embarked on the grand tour, during which he spent significant amounts of time in Rome and Paris. Upon returning to England, he entered public life in the same year as Pitt, serving as a member of parliament for Okehampton, Devonshire.

## Political Career
Lyttelton's political career included several high-ranking offices. From 1754 back to 1744, he served as a lord commissioner of the treasury. In 1755, he became the chancellor of the exchequer, succeeding Legge. He left this office in 1756, the same year he was raised to the peerage as Baron Lyttelton of Frankley, in the county of Worcester. 

During the political crisis of 1765, prior to the formation of the Rockingham administration, there was a suggestion that Lyttelton be appointed to head the treasury; however, he declined to participate in the scheme.

## Later Life and Death
The final years of Lyttelton's life were primarily dedicated to literary pursuits. He died on August 22, 1773.

## Family and Succession
George Lyttelton was the eldest son of Sir Thomas Lyttelton, 4th bart. His brother was Charles Lyttelton (1714–1768), who served as president of the Society of Antiquaries and bishop of Carlisle.

He was succeeded as 2nd baron by his son, Thomas (1744–1779). Thomas was known as the "wicked Lord Lyttelton" due to his notorious prodigal and loose habits, in contrast to his father, who was referred to as "the good lord." Because Thomas left no lawful issue, the barony became extinct upon his death.

The barony was revived in 1794 for William Henry (1724–1808), the uncle of the 2nd baron and the 1st baron of the new creation. William Henry had served as an ambassador to Portugal and as governor of Jamaica and S. Carolina. The title subsequently passed to his sons:
* The 3rd baron (1782–1837).
* George William Lyttelton, 4th baron (1817–1876), a scholar who married Miss Mary Glynne (sister-in-law to W. E. Gladstone) and performed significant work regarding poor law and educational reform.

The 4th baron had eight sons. The eldest, Charles George (b. 1842), became the 5th baron. In 1889, following the death of the 3rd duke of Buckingham and Chandos, Charles George succeeded to the viscounty of Cobham, and the barony of Lyttelton was merged into that title. Other notable sons included Alfred Lyttelton (b. 1857), who served as secretary of state for the colonies from 1903 to 1906 and was a distinguished tennis player and wicket-keeper; Edward Lyttelton (b. 1855), who served as headmaster of Eton and Haileybury; and Arthur Temple Lyttelton (d. 1903), who was a bishop-suffragan of Southampton and warden of Selwyn College, Cambridge.
