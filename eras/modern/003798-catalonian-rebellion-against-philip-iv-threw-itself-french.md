# Catalonian Rebellion against Philip IV

## Background of Barcelona
Barcelona, known in antiquity as Barcino, is traditionally associated with the Carthaginian Hamilcar Barca, who is credited as its founder in the 3rd century B.C. Following the Roman conquest, the city was named Julia Faventia by Augustus (27 B.C.–A.D. 14) and granted the status of a Roman colony. It grew rapidly to become a primary mart of the western Mediterranean by the 2nd century A.D., competing with Massilia (Marseilles) and Tarraco (Tarragona).

The city's early medieval history includes the founding of a bishopric in 343 and its selection as a temporary capital by the Visigoths in 415 and 531. After being captured by the Moors in 713, Barcelona and the rest of Catalonia passed under the dominion of the Franks in 801. By 874, the counts of Barcelona began ruling as independent monarchs.

## Commercial Zenith and Decline
The wealth and fame of Barcelona reached their peak following the union between Aragon and Catalonia in 1149. During this era, the city's merchant ships traded as far east as Alexandria and as far west as the Baltic and North Seas, competing with the maritime powers of Venice, Ragusa, and Genoa. In 1258, James I of Aragon authorized the city to issue the *Consulado del Mar*, a code of maritime law that became authoritative for many European states. Barcelona was also an early adopter of marine insurance and maintained consuls in major Mediterranean commercial centers.

This supremacy declined following the union of Aragon and Castile in 1479, which benefited other Spanish cities. The discovery of America in 1492 further shifted commercial dominance toward the ports of western Spain.

## Rebellion against Philip IV
The shift in economic power led the citizens of Barcelona to attribute their misfortunes to the "Castilian" government. This resentment fostered a strong political faction within the city that favored annexation by France.

In 1640, Barcelona became the center of a Catalonian rebellion directed against Philip IV. During this uprising, the city threw itself under French protection. This period of French alignment lasted until 1652, when Barcelona returned to its allegiance. However, the city was captured again in 1697 by the duke of Vendome. This occupation ended with the peace of Ryswick in the same year.
