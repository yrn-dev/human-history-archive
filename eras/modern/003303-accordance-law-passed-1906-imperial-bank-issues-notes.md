# Imperial Bank of Germany Note Issuance and Regulation

## The Law of 1906 and Note Values
In accordance with a law passed in 1906, the Imperial Bank (Reichsbank) began issuing notes, known as *Reichsbanknoten*, in denominations of 20 marks (£1) and 50 marks (£2, 10s.). These were added to the existing circulation of notes valued at 5, 10, 100, and 1,000 marks (£5, £10, £5, and £50 respectively). Prior to this law, imperial paper currency in the 20 and 50 mark denominations existed only as treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*), which were withdrawn from circulation as a result of this change.

## Banking Law Amendments and Capital
The banking law of Germany, originally passed in 1899, was amended to affect the position of the Reichsbank as well as four other banks that issued notes. Under the bill of that year, the capital of the Reichsbank was raised to £9,000,000. Additionally, the reserve fund was increased to £3,240,000 using surplus profits, surpassing the £3,000,000 requirement established by the act of 1899. The amending act also increased the share of dividends obtained by the government while diminishing those receivable by the stockholders of the Reichsbank.

## Regulation of Note Circulation
The circulation of notes is a significant factor in the Reichsbank's business and is governed by the law of 1875 and the amending law of 1899. This system corresponds in some degree to the Bank of England's regulation under Peel's act of 1844. 

The law establishes an uncovered limit for notes not represented by specie. Originally set at £12,500,000, this limit was increased to £14,811,450 following the lapse of issues from other banks, and was further extended to £23,641,450 by the act of June 5, 1902. To cover notes issued without specie, the bank must hold discounted bills (maturing no later than three months after being taken) with at least two, and usually three, good indorsements. Alternatively, it may hold notes of issuing banks reckoned as specie or treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*), which are the legal tender notes of the empire.

Furthermore, at least one-third of the notes in circulation must be covered by gold in bullion, foreign coin (reckoned at £69, 12s. per pound fine), money, current German notes, or notes of the imperial treasury.

## Redemption and Financial Status
The Reichsbank is legally bound to redeem its notes in current German money, which may consist of silver thalers, gold coin, or bar-gold at a rate of 1,392 marks per pound fine of gold (based on a reckoning of 20 marks to £1). While the importance of this obligation is admitted, the Reichsbank has not always provided facilities for payment in gold in practice.

As of the balance-sheet for 1906, the financial status of the bank was as follows:
*   **Average notes issued for the year:** £69,000,000
*   **Bills held:** £67,000,000
*   **Loans and advances:** £14,200,000
*   **Gold held (as of December 30, 1906):** £24,069,000
*   **Silver held (as of December 31, 1906):** Approximately £9,000,000

If business conditions require note circulation to exceed legal limits, the bank is permitted to do so by paying 5% on the surplus. This automatic statutory power differs from the English act, which allows no such provision.

## Comparative Standing
In 1906, the note circulation of the Imperial Bank of Germany (£69,000,000) was considerably larger than that of the Bank of England (£28,890,000), though smaller than that of the Bank of France (£186,300,000). Regarding capital, the Bank of England's £18,000,000 exceeded the £12,200,000 of the Bank of Germany and the £9,000,000 of the Bank of France.
