# Middle Period of the Tokugawa Bakufu

The Middle Period of the Tokugawa Bakufu spanned from the accession of the fifth shogun, Tsunayoshi, in 1680 to the death of the tenth shogun, Ieharu, in 1786. This era was characterized by significant shifts in educational status, administrative changes, and an ongoing struggle between the government's ideals of frugality and a prevailing trend toward luxury.

## Accession of Tsunayoshi
The transition to the Middle Period began in 1680 following the dangerous illness of the fourth shogun, Ietsuna. A council of chief Bakufu officials was convened to determine a successor. During this meeting, the Bakufu prime minister, Sakai Tadakiyo, suggested following the precedent of Kamakura by inviting an Imperial prince to take the office of shogun. This proposal was vehemently opposed by Hotta Masatoshi, a junior minister, who argued that the dignity belonged solely to Tsunayoshi, as he was the only brother of Ietsuna and the legitimate son of the late shogun, Iemitsu. Following this confrontation, Sakai Tadakiyo retired from the council, and Tsunayoshi ascended to the shogunate.

## Learning and Education under Tsunayoshi
Shogun Tsunayoshi is regarded as the leader among all Tokugawa shoguns regarding matters of learning. Under his auspices, numerous books were published that remain standard works. One of his most significant contributions was the elevation of the status of Confucian scholars and physicians. Previously, these professionals were required to shave their heads and use titles similar to Buddhist priests, which excluded them from being treated as samurai and barred them from State honors.

Tsunayoshi addressed this by obtaining the rank of junior fifth class from the Court in Kyoto for the great Confucian scholar Hayashi Nobuatsu. Nobuatsu was further appointed as the chief instructor at Kongo College and the minister of Education. This change allowed Confucianists to stop shaving their heads and made them eligible for official posts. Consequently, ten of Hayashi's disciples were appointed as retainers to the shogun and were tasked with delivering periodic lectures at the Bakufu court.

## Administrative Structure and Evolution
The administrative framework of the Bakufu, which had been developed into a consistent whole by the third shogun, Iemitsu, underwent radical alteration during the time of the fifth shogun. The original system featured a cabinet of seniors known as *roju*, with one serving as the *dairo* (great senior), a role corresponding to a Western prime minister. State business was conducted in the *Go-Yo-beya*, located near the shogun's sitting-room to keep him informed of administrative affairs.

Judicial matters were handled by a court of justice called the *Hyojo-sho*, established at Tatsunokuchi in Yedo. While the organization was largely completed under the third shogun, it required further supplementation by the eighth shogun and was not fully perfected until the eleventh.

## Economic Trends and the Virtue of Frugality
Throughout the Tokugawa rule, the Bakufu emphasized frugality as an essential virtue. Successive shoguns made strenuous efforts to encourage economy; however, the long period of peace led to a constant tendency toward increasing luxury and extravagance. While official injunctions of economy saw some success under the first three shoguns and the eighth shogun, Yoshimune, the general trend of the era was one of growing extravagance.

## Transition to the Late Period
The Middle Period concluded with the death of the tenth shogun, Ieharu, in 1786. He was succeeded by Ienari, a great-grandson of Yoshimune and son of Hitotsubashi Harunari. Because Ienari was only fifteen years old at his accession, he was unable to manage the administrative, economic, and financial problems of what is described as the darkest period of Tokugawa sway.

To address these crises, Matsudaira Sadanobu—the grandson of Yoshimune and son of Tayasu Munetake—was appointed prime minister in 1788. Sadanobu, described as a man of great insight, courage, and energy, worked to restore national confidence by issuing rules for the storage of grain to combat the threat of famine. He also reformed the ministry; previously, ministers had been drawn almost exclusively from the families of Hotta, Sakai, and Ii, but these individuals had allowed their duties to be usurped by the shogun's personal attendants. Sadanobu remedied this by appointing the heads of the three Tokugawa families of Owari, Kii, and Mito to the council of State.
