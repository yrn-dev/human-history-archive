# Operations in Italy (1745–1747)

The military campaigns in Italy between 1745 and 1747 were characterized by significant troop concentrations and shifting alliances, moving beyond a mere "war of posts."

## Allied Concentration and Early Victories
In March 1745, a secret treaty was established, aligning the republic of Genoa with Spain, France, and Naples. This alliance facilitated a series of strategic movements: De Gages moved from Modena toward Lucca, while Marshal Maillebois led French and Spanish forces from the Alps through the Riviera to the Tanaro. By mid-July, these forces concentrated between the Tanaro and the Scrivia, reaching a total of 80,000 men.

The allies executed a swift march on Piacenza, which drew the Austrian commander away from other positions. This maneuver allowed the allies to attack the Sardinians at Bassignano on September 27, resulting in a complete defeat of the Sardinian forces. This victory led rapidly to the capture of Casale, Valenza, and Alessandria. Jomini described this concentration of forces as the most remarkable of the entire war.

## Austrian Counter-Offensives
Despite the initial success, Maillebois was unable to capitalize on the victory due to the complex politics of the region. In early 1746, Austrian troops—having been freed by a peace agreement with Frederick—entered Italy via Tirol. These forces launched a brusque attack on the Franco-Spanish winter quarters, which resulted in the capitulation of a French garrison of 6,000 men located at Asti.

Simultaneously, an Austrian corps led by Count Browne struck the allies on the lower Po. This action severed the allies' communications with their main body in Piedmont. A series of minor actions subsequently destroyed the large concentration of allied forces.

## The Battle of Piacenza
Following the destruction of their concentration, the allies separated; the Spaniards marched against Count Browne while Maillebois covered Liguria. Browne received prompt and heavy reinforcements, forcing the Spaniards to entrench themselves at Piacenza. The Spanish Infant, serving as supreme commander, called for Maillebois's assistance.

The French forces, marching rapidly and under skillful conduct, rejoined the Spaniards. However, their position remained critical, as they were pursued by the army of the king of Sardinia and faced the principal Austrian army. This culminated in the pitched battle of Piacenza on June 16. The engagement was hard fought, and Maillebois had nearly achieved victory before receiving orders.

## Earlier Italian Conflicts (1744)
Prior to the 1745 campaign, the Italian war had become serious in 1744. A grandiose campaign plan sought to unite the army on the lower Po with the army in Dauphine, utilizing a road into central Italy secured through the adhesion of Genoa. However, the French and Spanish generals were hampered by orders from their respective governments.

During this period, Lobkowitz took the offensive and drove Count de Gages's Spanish army toward the Neapolitan frontier. In response, the king of Naples assisted the Spaniards, forming a combined army at Velletri that defeated Lobkowitz on August 11. Following this, Lobkowitz moved to Piedmont to assist the king against Conti. 

Conti achieved victories at Montalban and Villefranche on April 20, and later defeated the king of Sardinia in a great battle near Coni (Cuneo) at Madonna del Olmo on September 30. However, Conti failed to take the fortress of Coni and was forced to retire to Dauphine for winter quarters. Because of this, the two armies failed to combine, leaving the Austro-Sardinians positioned between them.
