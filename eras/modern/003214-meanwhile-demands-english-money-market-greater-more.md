# The English Money Market

The English money market, described as the clearing-house of the world, is characterized by demands that are more continuous and greater than those of any other money market globally. Due to the smallness of its reserve, it is exposed to fluctuations more severe than those of any other country. These fluctuations may be driven by domestic trade or the necessity of meeting the requirements of other nations for specie.

## Structure and Organs of the Market
At the beginning of the 20th century, the working organs of the London money market were divided into two primary groups based on their level of activity:

*   **Constantly Active Organs:** This group includes the Bank of England, private and joint-stock banks (including several large foreign banks), and bill-brokers and discount houses.
*   **Intermittently Active Organs:** This group consists of certain finance houses and great merchants, as well as certain members of the Stock Exchange. 

While the members of the Stock Exchange vary in their activity, they are never entirely outside the market. Many of the great merchant houses are registered as bankers, though they do not typically perform standard banking functions in the United Kingdom. Instead, they often focus on finance business, such as issuing capital for companies and making loans to foreign governments. These capitalists may lend large sums of money they hold temporarily in the money market or on the Stock Exchange.

## The Bank of England and the Bank Act of 1844
The Bank of England's accounts are divided into two departments: the issue department and the banking department. The notes held in the banking department are technically referred to as the "Reserve." If the Bank is called upon for a sum in specie or notes that exceeds this Reserve, the government must grant permission to "suspend the Bank Act" to meet the demand, regardless of the amount of specie available in the issue department.

Since the Bank Act of 1844 came into force, authority for suspension has been granted three times: during the crises of 1847, 1857, and 1866. In 1857, the limits of the act were actually exceeded; on the other two occasions, the granting of permission served to stay the alarm. While there has been no anxiety regarding the payment in specie of note circulation since 1844, the division of specie into two parts has disadvantages. Specifically, it causes the smaller part of the reserve to bear the full strain of any demands for specie or notes, which has greatly increased the liability to constant fluctuations in the Bank's rate of discount.

Between the operation of the act in 1844 and the end of 1906, there were more than 400 changes in the rate. Consequently, the hopes expressed by Sir Robert Peel in 1844—that commercial crises would cease following the act—were not realized.

## Market Dynamics and External Influences
The money market is subject to the general tendencies of all markets to seek an equilibrium between the quantity of commodities offered and those desired. When equilibrium is attained and undisturbed, prices remain practically unchanged. However, equilibrium can be disturbed by changes in supply, changes in demand, or simultaneous changes in both. Bagehot described the money market as being "sometimes extremely excited" and "often very dull."

External factors significantly influence the British market. In 1906, there was a recorded excess of imports over exports totaling £147,000,000. Although this is eventually balanced by "invisible" exports, it grants foreign nations a level of power over the British money market that is unprecedented. This outward flow of currency is further enhanced by the increase in foreign banks maintaining branches in England.

## Modern Management of Market Disturbances
Modern methods for handling disturbances to the money market are exemplified by the enormous war loans raised by Japan between 1904 and 1906. These loans were issued by three banks: a Japanese institution representing the government, a leading London bank, and the principal British bank operating in China. 

To minimize disturbance to the London market, "application money" withdrawn from other banks was lent back into the short loan market as quickly as possible, usually on the afternoon of the issue. When loan instalments became due, the cash was made available to borrowers within a few hours. To avoid embarrassing the London money market with huge balances, the Japanese government transferred a portion of the funds to the Bank of England and lent the remainder.
