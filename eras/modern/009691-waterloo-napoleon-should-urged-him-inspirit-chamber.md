# The Battle of Waterloo and its Aftermath

The Battle of Waterloo (1815) served as the final defeat for Napoleon, ending his return to power during the period known as the Hundred Days. The conflict took place in Belgium, where Napoleon sought to strike the British, Prussians, and Dutch forces swiftly to achieve victory before the arrival of Russian and Austrian reinforcements.

## The Military Engagement
Prior to the main engagement at Waterloo, Napoleon achieved a victory over the Prussians at Ligny, though the success was not sufficient to secure the campaign. At Waterloo, Napoleon faced the British army under Wellington. The French forces were ultimately defeated by the tenacity of the British, while the Prussians, led by General Blücher, arrived on Napoleon's right flank as the day progressed. The result was a rout that left Napoleon without hope or support.

Among the French officers involved in the campaign was Baron de Marbot, who had been promoted to general of brigade during the Hundred Days and was wounded during the battle. Another key figure, Count d'Erlon, commanded the I. army corps as part of the Army of the North. Due to a series of misunderstandings, d'Erlon's corps did not participate in the battles at Quatre Bras or Ligny, though Napoleon did not hold him accountable for this failure.

## Immediate Political Consequences
Following the defeat, France fell away from Napoleon. A provisional government in Paris ordered him to leave the country, granting him twenty-four hours to depart. In the wake of the battle, Bignon, who had returned to Napoleon's service during the Hundred Days, served as the minister of foreign affairs under the executive commission. In this capacity, Bignon signed the convention of July 3, 1815, which resulted in the surrender of Paris to the allies.

In a notable post-battle interaction, four days after Waterloo, Napoleon urged his brother Joseph to inspirit the Chamber of Deputies to encourage national resistance. However, Joseph's contributions were insignificant; he primarily focused on assisting the emperor's attempts to escape to America.

## Napoleon's Exile and Death
Napoleon attempted to reach America but arrived at Rochefort, where British cruisers were watching. He boarded the British frigate *Bellerophon*, requesting to be received as a refugee, but he was instead treated as a prisoner. He was transported to Plymouth and subsequently exiled to the tropical island of St. Helena. He remained a British prisoner there until his death in 1821.

Napoleon's mother, Letizia (Madame Mere), who had joined him in Elba and returned to Paris during the Hundred Days, took up residence in Rome after the final downfall at Waterloo. There, she was protected by Pope Pius VII. In 1818, she wrote a letter to the powers at the congress of Aix petitioning for Napoleon's release, citing his mortal illness. The powers did not answer the letter, suspecting it was a political move concerted with Napoleon.

## Regional Impacts and the Congress of Vienna
The victory of the allies led to a general effort to restore the state of affairs that had been disrupted by the French Revolution. A great Congress of the victorious allies met at Vienna to reorganize Europe, maintaining a period of "exhausted effort" peace for nearly forty years.

In the aftermath, difficulties arose in the new kingdom of the Netherlands. In 1814, Holland had adopted a strongly Protestant constitution. A commission consisting of eleven Dutch, eleven Belgians, and two delegates from Luxemburg attempted to adapt this into a constitution for the new kingdom. While the Dutch passed the proposed constitution unanimously, the Belgians rejected it by a vote of 1,603 to 527, largely due to a refusal to legalize religious equality. King William I eventually forced the act through using a method the Belgians termed "Dutch arithmetic," counting those who abstained as voting in favor.

## Commemoration
The battlefield of Belle Alliance remains a site of historical interest near Brussels. A great hall erected outside Waterloo houses a gigantic panorama measuring 110 metres in circumference. This display depicts dramatic moments of the battle, including Marshal Ney’s onslaught with the French Guards against the infantry and artillery positions of Wellington, as well as Napoleon on his white charger and General Blücher with the Prussians.
