# Hong-Kong

## History and Administration
The island of Hong-Kong was formerly an integral part of China. It was first ceded to Great Britain in 1841, a cession that was confirmed by the treaty of Nanking in 1842. The charter for the colony is dated April 5, 1843. 

The colony is administered by a governor, an executive council, and a legislative council. The executive council consists of holders of specific offices and other members nominated by the crown; in 1890, this council had nine members. The legislative council is composed of the same officials and six unofficial members. Of these unofficial members, three are appointed by the governor—two of whom were members of the Chinese community at the time of the record—while one is elected from the justices of the peace and one from the chamber of commerce.

## Population and Society
The population of Hong-Kong consists of Chinese and brown people, numbering 306,130. The Chinese are noted as one of the most important races inhabiting the Eastern dependencies. 

The colony provides education through a few government schools and a large number of schools that receive grants-in-aid. The Queen's College offers secondary education for boys, and the foundation-stone for Hong-Kong University was laid in March 1910, with buildings provided as a gift from colonial broker Sir Hormusjee Mody. Public health is supported by several hospitals, including one government institution. Security is maintained by a strong military garrison and a police force composed of Chinese, Indian Sikhs, and Europeans.

## Economy and Trade
Hong-Kong operates as a free port. While official trade figures are unavailable, the annual value of imports and exports is estimated at approximately £50,000,000. Principal goods traded include salt, tea, silk, opium, flax, sugar, cotton and cotton goods, earthenware, oil, amber, ivory, sandal-wood, betel, granite, vegetables, and live stock. There is also an extensive passenger trade involving the Chinese. 

Shipping activity has shown growth over several decades:
*   1880: 8,359,994 total tonnage (3,758,160 British)
*   1890: 13,676,293 total tonnage (6,994,919 British)
*   1898: 17,265,780 total tonnage (8,705,648 British)
*   1902: 19,709,451 total tonnage (8,945,976 British)

British ships lead in trade volume, followed by Chinese, Japanese, and German ships.

## Industry and Finance
Agricultural industry is minimal on Hong-Kong island and in Kowloon, where only 400 acres are cultivated in each area. However, the "new territory" has 90,000 acres under cultivation, including over 7,000 acres of sugar-cane as of 1900. 

The colony's chief industries include:
*   Manufacturing: Cement, paper, bamboo and rattan ware, umbrellas, jinrickshas, and products connected to matches and kerosene oil.
*   Crafts: Wood and ivory carving, gold-beating, and the production of silver, gold, and sandal-wood ware.
*   Metalwork: Working in iron and copper.
*   Other: Sugar-refining, granite quarrying, and the introduction of cotton manufacture.
*   Maritime: Ship and boat building, as well as sail and rope making, which are noted as being less subject to depression than other industries.

Financial services include the Hong-Kong savings bank, which holds deposits of about $1,100,000. The colony's revenue is derived primarily from land sales, the post-office, rent of government property, and licences. Revenue grew from $1,069,948 in 1880 to $4,901,073 in 1902. A public debt of about £340,000 exists for public works, managed via a sinking fund.

The legal tender includes the British dollar, the Hong-Kong dollar, the Mexican dollar, and other authorized silver dollars. Small copper and silver coins are legal tender for amounts up to one and two dollars, respectively. Paper currency is issued by the National Bank of China, Limited, the Hong-Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, and the Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China.

## Geography and Environment
The flora of Hong-Kong consists of species where approximately three-fifths are common to the Indian region, with the remainder being local or Chinese forms. 

Hong-Kong maintains a relationship with other regional ports. It is connected to Macao by a daily steamer, and Macao is often used as a resort to escape the oppressive heat of Hong-Kong. Additionally, the British colony of Hong-Kong serves as a model for the Chinese imperial customs' collection of duties on vessels trading with Macao, and Great Britain cooperates in opium revenue work at Macao in the same manner as it does at Hong-Kong.
