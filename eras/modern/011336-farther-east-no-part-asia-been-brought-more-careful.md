# Hydrography and Regional Studies of Farther Asia

## Hydrography of the Tibet-Burma-China Border
The mountain wilderness dividing China from Tibet and Burma has been subject to more careful investigation than any other part of Asia further east. Through the combined efforts of travelers, native surveyors, and reconnaissance parties working on commercial projects, the hydrography of this region is now well understood.

It has been established that the Brahmaputra and the great Tsanpo of Tibet are the same river. North of the point where this river effects a great countermarch from east to west, the sources of several major rivers are located. From west to east, these are the Salween, the Mekong, the Yang-tsze-kiang, and the Hwang-ho (Yellow River). To the south of this point, the dual sources of the Irrawaddy rise between the Salween and the extreme eastern edge of the Brahmaputra basin.

The distance from the water-divide separating the easternmost affluent of the Brahmaputra to the deep gorges of the upper Yang-tsze-kiang (which flows north to south at that point) is approximately 100 miles. Within this span, the Mekong and Salween rivers send torrents toward Siam and Burma. These three rivers flow parallel to one another for about 300 miles, hidden in narrow, precipitous troughs. They begin to spread apart where the Yang-tsze turns eastwards, not far north of the 25th parallel.

## Cultural and Political Influences
European influence in Asia has been particularly strong during two distinct epochs: following the conquests of Alexander the Great and from the 16th century onward. Alexander's campaigns led to the creation of Perso-Greek kingdoms that hellenized their immediate areas and influenced the religion and art of China and India. Following this, eastern Europe spent a long period combating Asiatic invasions, which limited the "Europeanizing" of the East. While the Crusades maintained communication with the Levant and established the power of the Roman Church, intercourse with farther Asia remained limited to a few travelers.

In western Asia and eastern Europe, Asiatic influences have generally prevailed, with Islam remaining paramount. However, western Europe has asserted power in middle and eastern Asia due to improvements in communication.

## Civilizational and Religious Characteristics
Asia is often considered the birthplace of civilization because its records precede those of Europe. However, this originality is not absolute; for example, the Aryans brought civilization to India from the west. Historically, both Asia and Europe saw small aristocratic or feudal states consolidate into monarchies. A key difference is that in Europe, royalty was often replaced by popular government, whereas in Asia, revolution typically resulted only in a change of dynasty. Arabia is noted as a case where non-monarchical government persists as a very ancient condition.

Asia is characterized by its religious diversity, being the origin of the world's great religions—Islam, Christianity, and Buddhism—as well as Taoism, Parseeism, and Judaism. While Christianity originated in Asia, its present form was largely shaped on European soil, particularly through the Roman Church. Christianity has seen little success farther east than Asia Minor; earlier attempts by Jacobites and Nestorians in India, China, and central Asia received respectful hearing but did not achieve the success of Islam or Buddhism. Conversely, Buddhism has made little impression west of India.

## Ethnic Groups and Nomadic Populations
The northern half of Asia is inhabited largely by nomadic hunters, shepherds, and herdsmen known as Mongolians. The most peaceful of these occupy Siberia. Further south, the Turks, Moguls, Mongols proper, and Manchus are collectively known as Tatars (and were called Scythians by the ancients), occupying the zone between the 40th and 50th parallels of North latitude.

*   **Turks:** Mahommedans whose tribes extend to the Caspian, the borders of Persia and Afghanistan, and into Russia (as Kirghiz). They use Persian letters.
*   **Manchus and Mongols:** Chiefly Buddhist, using letters derived from ancient Syriac. The Manchus are gradually losing their nomadic habits and language under the influence of Chinese civilization.
*   **Chinese:** A branch of the Mongolian family, they are settled traders and agriculturists. They are partially Buddhist and use a monosyllabic, uninflected language with symbol-based writing.

## Trade and Exploration
In the 15th century, the Italian navigator John Cabot visited Mecca, which was then the world's greatest mart for exchanging Western and Eastern goods. Cabot discovered that spices, precious stones, perfumes, and silks were brought by caravan from the north-eastern parts of farther Asia. This led him to hypothesize that these goods could be brought to Europe more quickly by sailing across the western ocean. Around 1484, Cabot moved to London and later worked with Bristol merchants to find a western route to Asia, initially attempting to reach the island of Brazil or the Seven Cities.
