# Civil Unrest and Political Violence in Colonial and Imperial Contexts

The history of various colonial and imperial administrations is marked by instances of political violence, including the assassination of officials and the imprisonment of native leaders, often coinciding with periods of social distress or administrative transition.

## Violence in Poona and Bombay
In the city of Poona, during 1897, two European officials were assassinated. This period of unrest was accompanied by the confinement of two leaders of the Brahman community and the sentencing of the editor of a prominent native paper to imprisonment on charges of sedition. These events occurred during a time when the region was severely affected by bubonic plague, which first broke out in Bombay City in September 1896. 

The plague caused massive mortality, with 531,000 deaths recorded from the start until the end of October 1902. While many submitted to government sanitary regulations, the method of segregation was misunderstood by the population, leading to deplorable outbreaks. Further violence occurred in March 1898 in Bombay, where a riot initiated by Mahommedan weavers resulted in several Europeans being fatally injured. Additionally, in January 1898, a mob brutally murdered the native chairman of the plague committee in the Nasik district.

## Portuguese Administration in Macao
In Macao, the Portuguese established factories in 1557 and later organized a senate in 1583. For a significant period, the Portuguese remained largely under Chinese control; the Chinese maintained their territorial rights through mandarins and insisted that European criminals be handed over to them. 

This dynamic changed in 1849 when the Portuguese governor, Ferreira do Amaral, ended this arrangement, limiting the authority of Chinese officials in the peninsula to that of representatives from other foreign nations. Although his antagonists successfully procured his assassination on August 22, his successors continued to implement his policies.

## Political Assassination in Japan
In Japan, the government took steps toward legal reform in 1875 by organizing a Senate (Genro-in) to discuss and revise laws. However, the Senate lacked the power of initiative and suffered in public credit because its members were largely men who could not find posts in the administration.

In the spring of 1878, the prominent statesman Okubo Toshimitsu was assassinated. The individuals responsible for the murder claimed to be sympathizers of Saigo, citing the government's failure to establish representative institutions as their primary motive. Although the killers had little knowledge of Saigo's actual sentiments, the Japanese public viewed them as champions of a constitutional government. This event prompted the authorities to act with greater expedition, leading to the proclamation of elective assemblies in cities and prefectures two months after Okubo's death.

## Other Instances of Assassination and Fear
Beyond colonial administration, historical records note the psychological impact of the fear of assassination. In one instance, a figure who had previously been interested in philosophy, history, and art developed a dread of society and a fear of assassination, accompanied by delusions that he was being followed.
