# Banking and Commercial Development in Ireland

## Early Banking Efforts and the Bank of Ireland
The early development of banking in Ireland was characterized by legislation that was less favorable to the creation of a dependable and steady system than the legislation found in England. In 1695, several of the principal merchants in Dublin met with the intention of forming a public bank for Ireland, intending to use the Bank of England as their model. However, this proposal did not receive favor for many years.

It was not until 1783 that the Bank of Ireland was established and began its business operations. The first governor of the institution was David La Touche, junior; additionally, two other members of the La Touche family served among the first board of directors. While the bank achieved very great success, it was marked by extreme jealousy toward rival establishments.

## Legislative Restrictions on Competition
The act that formed the Bank of Ireland included specific provisions to hinder the establishment of other banks. It enacted that no society or company exceeding six members in number—with the sole exception of the Bank of Ireland—could borrow or take up money on their notes or bills payable on demand.

These restrictions were modified over time:
*   **1821:** The act was modified to allow the establishment of banking companies with more than six members, provided they were located at a distance of 50 miles from Dublin.
*   **1824:** An act was passed to explain the 1821 legislation due to ambiguity.
*   **1845:** The restriction regarding the 50-mile limit was finally withdrawn.

## Dublin and Regional Commercial Centers
Dublin served as a central hub for various professional and commercial activities. The city was home to merchants, such as the father of the painter Nathaniel Hone and the father of the singer and composer Michael Kelly (who was a dancing-master and wine-merchant). 

Beyond the city center, other areas of County Dublin developed significant commercial infrastructure. Kingstown, originally named Dunleary, evolved from a fishing village into a large seaport and popular watering-place. This transformation followed the construction of an extensive harbour begun in 1817 and completed in 1859. The harbour features a western pier of 4,950 feet and an eastern pier of 3,500 feet, enclosing approximately 250 acres. Kingstown became a vital link for the City of Dublin Steam Packet Company's mail steamers to Holyhead. Its trade includes the import of provisions and corn, and the export of cattle.

## Other Irish Urban Centers
In the province of Ulster, Belfast emerged as a seaport of the first rank and the county town of County Antrim. Situated at the entrance of the river Lagan into Belfast Lough, it is located 112 3/4 miles north of Dublin by rail. Belfast developed into an important railway center with terminal stations for the Northern Counties (Midland of England), Great Northern, and Belfast & County Down railways. 

The city's growth was marked by rapid commercial development that often outpaced architectural improvement. By the close of the 19th century, squalid districts were replaced by handsome buildings and new thoroughfares, such as Royal Avenue. Notable structures include the city hall, opened in 1906, and a free public library opened in 1888.

In County Kerry, the market town of Killarney developed as a tourist destination due to its scenery. While its industry originally owed its origin to iron-smelting works fueled by neighboring forests, its primary manufacture became fancy articles made from arbutus wood.
