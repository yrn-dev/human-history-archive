# Estates and Legal Declarations in Historical Context

The term "estates" appears across various historical and legal contexts within the archive, referring to landed property, social orders of government, and the distribution of assets during bankruptcy.

## Landed Estates and Royal Service
In the 17th century, the recovery and management of estates were often tied to political loyalty and royal service. John Ashburnham (c. 1603-1671), an English Royalist and son of Sir John Ashburnham of Sussex, recovered the Ashburnham estates that had been alienated by his father. Ashburnham served as a groom of the bedchamber and represented Hastings in parliament, including the Long Parliament of 1640, where he reported proceedings to the king. 

During the Civil War, Ashburnham served as treasurer of the royal army, a role that caused friction with Hyde, the chancellor of the exchequer. Following the flight of Charles I from Oxford in April 1646, Ashburnham traveled abroad to Paris and the Hague. In November 1647, following Charles I's escape from Hampton Court, Ashburnham advised the king to take refuge in the Isle of Wight under the governor Robert Hammond. This decision led to the king's capture and resulted in Ashburnham being accused of disloyalty, though he was later acquitted by both Charles I and Charles II.

## The Estates as Political Orders
In the context of Danish history, "Estates" refers to the social and political orders of the realm. In September 1660, the *Rigsdag* opened in Copenhagen to address the aftermath of war. The government proposed an excise tax on consumption and subsidiary taxes on poultry and cattle in exchange for abolishing old direct taxes. This proposal created a conflict between the nobility, who sought total exemption, and the "Lower Estates"—comprising the clergy and burgesses—who demanded equality in taxation.

Internal divisions within the Lower Estates, specifically regarding the privileges of Copenhagen and its bishops, initially hindered a unified front. However, Hans Nansen (the burgomaster) and Hans Svane (bishop of Copenhagen) led a reform movement to abolish these dividing privileges. On September 16, the two Lower Estates sent a memorandum to the *Rigsraad* stating they would renounce their privileges if the nobility did the same. This coalition became known as the "Conjoined Estates." By September 30, the First Estate (the nobility) renounced its privileges.

This political shift culminated in the *Kongelov* (Royal Law), dated and subscribed on November 14, 1665. This document declared that the monarchy originated from the Estates surrendering their supreme authority to the king, establishing an absolute monarchy.

## Bankruptcy and the Distribution of Estates
In legal proceedings regarding bankruptcy, the "estate" refers to the assets of the bankrupt party. Following the payment of preferential debts and administration costs, a trustee is required to distribute the estate to creditors. The first dividend must be distributed within four months of the first creditors' meeting, with subsequent dividends occurring at intervals of no more than six months.

The process involves gazetting a notice of the intention to declare a dividend, which must be sent to creditors who have not yet proved their claims. Once a dividend is declared, creditors are notified of the amount due and the place of payment. In the case of a bankrupt firm, joint creditors cannot receive dividends from the separate property of the bankrupts until all separate creditors have been paid in full.

## Financial and Legal Concepts
The archive also details the legal treatment of interest related to debts and securities. In England and America, "compound interest" (interest on interest) was historically viewed with horror, similar to usury. Lord Eldon did not recognize agreements to pay compound interest as valid, and Chancellor Kent maintained that it could only be taken via a special agreement made after simple interest became due. In Scotland, compound interest is not permitted as a form of damages.

Under the Civil Procedure Act 1833, juries may grant interest as damages on debts payable at a specific time, or from the date of a written demand. In the United States, the granting of interest is left to the discretion of the court based on the equity of the case.
