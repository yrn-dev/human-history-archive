# Lucien Bonaparte

Lucien Bonaparte (1775–1840), the prince of Canino, was born on May 21, 1775, in Ajaccio. Educated at the schools of Brienne and Autun, he originally intended to join the French army. However, due to defective sight, he was directed toward the church and attended the seminary at Aix in Provence in 1786. His volatile and excitable nature clashed with the discipline of the seminary, and following the 1789 Revolution, he became an eager supporter of anti-clerical and democratic movements.

## Early Political Career
Upon returning to Corsica, Lucien became a prominent speaker for the Jacobin club in Ajaccio. He urged his brothers to break ties with Paoli, the leader of the conservative party that sought an alliance with England against the French regicide republic. Lucien led a Corsican deputation to France to solicit aid for the democrats and denounce Paoli. When the Paolists eventually gained control, the Bonapartes left the island for Toulon.

In southern France, Lucien continued his Jacobin activities in the town of St Maximin (renamed Marathon), where he was known as "Brutus." On May 4, 1794, while still a minor and without family consent, he married Mlle Catherine Boyer, an act that led to a period of penury and near-disgrace. Following the *coup d'etat* of Thermidor on July 28, 1794, he fled St Maximin and took a small post at St Chamans. After a period of imprisonment, he was released through the influence of Napoleon and served as a commissioner in the French army in Germany. Dissatisfied with this role, he returned to Corsica and later entered the Council of Five Hundred in Paris in 1798.

## Relationship with Napoleon
Lucien played a pivotal role in Napoleon's rise to power. During the conspiracy to replace the Directory with three Consuls, Napoleon lost his nerve when deputies of the Lower House (the Council of Five Hundred) shouted at and hustled him. Lucien saved the situation by bringing in soldiers to disperse the council.

Despite this, Lucien remained suspicious of his brother. In early letters to Joseph, Lucien noted that Napoleon possessed an ambition that surpassed his love for the general weal and suggested that Napoleon would attempt to "ride on the crest of events" in the event of a counter-revolution. After helping Napoleon triumph over parliamentary institutions, Lucien's suspicions grew, and relations became strained during the consulate (1799–1804). Although he served as minister of the interior, he was soon removed due to personal and political differences with the First Consul.

## Diplomatic Tenure and Conflict
In November 1800, Napoleon appointed Lucien as ambassador to the court of Madrid to soften the blow of his removal from the ministry. During this time, France and Spain were preparing to partition Portugal, and Spanish forces had begun invading the territory. However, the court of Lisbon reportedly used bribes to induce Lucien Bonaparte and the Spanish minister, Godoy, to sign preliminaries of peace at Badajoz on June 6, 1801. This frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon. Consequently, the First Consul remonstrated with Lucien, who resigned and returned to Paris to join the opposition in the Tribunate.

The final breach between the brothers occurred over Lucien's personal life. Following the death of his wife in 1800, Lucien became enamored with Mme Jouberthou in 1802. Despite an express prohibition from the First Consul—who was pressing Lucien to marry the widow of the king of Etruria for reasons of state—Lucien secretly married Mme Jouberthou at Plessis on October 23, 1803. Upon discovering this, Napoleon ordered Lucien to leave French territory. Lucien departed for Italy with his wife and infant son, further annoying Napoleon by publicly giving his wife the name of Bonaparte.

## Later Life and Legacy
Lucien lived in Italy, primarily in Rome, and displayed marked hostility toward the emperor. In December 1807, Napoleon offered to recognize Lucien as a French prince if he would annul his marriage, but Lucien refused. He eventually left for America but was captured by a British ship and held under surveillance in Malta and England until the peace of 1814. During the Hundred Days in 1815, he returned to Rome and offered his help to Napoleon, remaining by his side at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris and defending his prerogatives during the second abdication. He died in Rome on June 29, 1840. He authored a two-volume epic titled *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree* (1814), as well as *La Verite sur les Cent Jours* and unfinished *Memoirs*.

## Descendants
Lucien had four sons and six daughters. Among them were:
* **Charles Lucien Jules Laurent** (1803–1857), a scientist and prince of Canino who published works on ornithology and zoology, including *American Ornithology*. He was a proponent of Italian independence and the expulsion of Austrians before leaving Italy in 1849.
* **Louis Lucien** (1813–1891), who spent his youth in England and later served as a deputy for Corsica and the Seine. He was named a senator and prince by Napoleon III but later withdrew to England to study philology, publishing works on the Basque language.
