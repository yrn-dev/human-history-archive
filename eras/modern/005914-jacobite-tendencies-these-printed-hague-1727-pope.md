# Jacobite Tendencies and Related Historical References

## Literary Censorship and Alexander Pope
In the early 18th century, the works of the poet Pope were subject to censorship due to alleged Jacobite tendencies. In 1721, Edmund Curll released a pirated edition of Pope's works, which led to Curll being brought before the House of Lords for a breach of privilege. Subsequently, an authorized edition was produced in 1723 under Pope's superintendence. However, the authorities removed two specific pieces from this authorized version: "The Feast of the Gods" and the "Account of the Revolution," citing their Jacobite tendencies.

These two excluded works were eventually printed at the Hague in 1727. Following this publication, Pope disingenuously claimed he had no knowledge of the contents. Pope's works continued to be published in various editions in 1723, 1726, 1729, 1740, and 1753, and his *Poems* were later included in other editions of British poets, including those by Johnson.

## Political Context and Bolingbroke
The political climate regarding Jacobitism extended to other figures such as Bolingbroke. After retiring to France in June (following a period of disappointment and baffle), Bolingbroke resided primarily at the chateau of Argeville near Fontainebleau. He authored the *True Use of Retirement* and *Letters on the Study of History*, the latter of which was printed privately before his death and published in 1752.

During a visit to England in 1738, Bolingbroke became a leading adviser and friend to Frederick, prince of Wales. For this purpose, he wrote *The State of Parties at the Accession of George I.*, *The Spirit of Patriotism*, and *The Patriot King*. These works were entrusted to Pope and remained unpublished for a time. It was later discovered that Pope had secretly printed 1,500 copies of *The Patriot King*, prompting Bolingbroke to publish a correct version in 1749. Despite his political involvements, Bolingbroke showed no sympathy for the Jacobite expedition of 1745.

## Religious and Cultural Definitions of Jacobites
Separate from the political movements in Britain, the term "Jacobite" is used in historical accounts of the Kingdom of Mausul, located on the south-east frontier of Armenia. In this region, the population includes people described as Jacobite and Nestorian Christians. 

These Christians are governed by a Patriarch known as the Jatolic. The Jatolic is responsible for creating Prelates, Abbots, and Archbishops, whom he sends to various quarters, including Cathay, Baudas, and India. This administrative structure is noted as being similar to the way the Pope of Rome operates in Latin countries. While there are many such Christians in these regions, the texts specify that they are not Christians in the fashion enjoined by the Pope of Rome, as they fall short of the Faith in several points.

## Other Historical References to the Hague and the Pope
The Hague served as a significant diplomatic and printing center during the 18th century. Beyond the printing of Pope's censored works in 1727, the city was a site of Russian diplomatic activity. Prince Boris Ivanovich Kurakin, a Russian diplomat and pupil of Peter the Great, represented Russia at the Hague between 1708 and 1712. Additionally, the Treaty of the Hague (1790) settled the status of the Belgian republic, restoring Austrian rule in the "Austrian Netherlands" while providing guarantees for ancient institutions.

The office of the Pope also appears in diplomatic contexts. In 1707, Prince Kurakin was dispatched to Rome with the specific objective of inducing the pope to decline the recognition of Stanislaus Leszczynski, the candidate of Charles XII, as the king of Poland.
