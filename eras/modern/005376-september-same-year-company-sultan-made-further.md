# Imperial British East Africa Company

The Imperial British East Africa Company was a chartered organization established in the late 19th century to manage and develop British interests in the mainland territories of the sultanate of Zanzibar. Its formation and subsequent dissolution were closely tied to the shifting political influence of Great Britain in East Africa and the administrative decisions of the British government.

## Origins and Formation
British influence in Zanzibar was substantial during the final quarter of the 19th century. The sultan of Zanzibar, Bargash, relied heavily on the counsel of Sir John Kirk, the British representative. In 1877, Bargash offered a 70-year lease for the administration and customs of his mainland dominions—including certain rights of sovereignty—to William Mackinnon, the chairman of the British India Steam Navigation Company. This offer was initially declined due to a lack of support from the foreign office.

By 1884 and 1885, the acquisition of numerous concessions by Germans on the East African coast prompted renewed British interest in maintaining regional influence. Following an 1886 agreement with Germany that ratified a British claim to districts behind Mombasa, Sultan Bargash granted a concession in May 1887 to an association formed by Mackinnon. This concession covered the administration of the sultan's mainland territory, excluding the region recognized as the German sphere of operations. International agreements defined these mainland territories as extending 10 miles inland from the coast.

The association's primary objective was to open the hinterland beyond this ten-mile strip. In April 1888, through a founder's agreement, the association became the Imperial British East Africa Company. It received a royal charter in September 1888, and the sultan provided a further concession in October 1888. Based on these charters and concessions, £240,000 was subscribed, and the company formally took charge of its concessions.

## Administration and Operations
The company's operations were marked by early difficulties. One of its notable employees was Sir Frederick John Dealtry Lugard, who was engaged by the company in 1889. During his service, Lugard explored the neighboring region and the Sabaki river. He also developed a scheme intended for the emancipation of slaves held by Arabs on the Zanzibar mainland.

In 1890, the company dispatched Lugard to Uganda. There, he worked to secure British predominance and end civil disturbances, a process that involved severe fighting, including an unprovoked attack by a "British" faction against a "French" faction. While administering Uganda, Lugard mapped a large area of the country, journeying around Ruwenzori to Albert Edward Nyanza. He also visited Albert Nyanza, where he brought away several thousand Sudanese who had been left there by H. M. Stanley and Emin Pasha.

## Decline and Extinction
The company eventually faced financial and political pressures that led to its extinction. A significant blow occurred in June 1892, when the British government—which had taken Zanzibar under its protection in November 1890—declared the sultan's dominions to be within a free trade zone. 

This declaration had several devastating effects on the company:
* It extinguished all treaties regulating duties and tariffs with foreign powers.
* It established free trade along the entire coast, which swept away the dues the company relied upon for revenue.
* The company was left responsible for the costs of administration and the payment of rent without the necessary revenue from the abolished dues.

Furthermore, the company attempted to draw up a scheme of taxation to recover its losses, but this proposal failed to receive approval from the foreign office.
