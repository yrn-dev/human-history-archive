# Brazilian Frontier

The boundary between Brazil and Bolivia is a complex line shaped by colonial treaties, geographical features, and territorial disputes over natural resources. Its origins lie in the limits established between Portuguese and Spanish colonies via the treaties of San Ildefonso (1777) and Madrid (1750), with subsequent modifications occurring in 1867 and 1903.

## Boundary Delineation and Course
The boundary line begins at the outlet of Bahia Negra into the Paraguay river (lat. 28 deg. 08' 35" S.). From this point, the line ascends the river to a location on the west bank situated 9 kilometres below Fort Coimbra. It then moves inland 4 kilometres to a point at long. 58 deg. 04' 12.7" W. and lat. 19 deg. 45' 36" S.

From there, the frontier follows an irregular course toward the North and East of North, passing through Lakes Uberaba, Gaiba (or Mandiore), and Mandiore. The line continues up the San Matias river and moves North along the Sierra Ricardo Franco until it reaches the headwaters of the Rio Verde, a tributary of the Guapore. This specific section of the boundary was shifted inland from the Paraguay river to ensure that settlements on the west bank, including Corumba and Fort Coimbra, remained under Brazilian jurisdiction. In 1903, this area was modified again, with approximately 1158 sq. m. ceded to Bolivia to improve commercial facilities on the Paraguay.

The line then follows the Guapore, Verde, Madeira, and Mamore rivers down to the mouth of the Abuna (approximately lat. 9 deg. 44' S.), as established by the treaty of 1903.

## Territorial Modifications and the Treaty of Petropolis
The northern section of the frontier has undergone significant changes. The original colonial boundary extended down the Madeira river to a point midway between the Amazon and the Beni, then ran due West to the Javary. The treaty of 1867 shifted the starting point to the mouth of the Beni (lat. 10 deg. 20' S.) and established a straight line to the source of the Javary, which granted Brazil a large amount of territory.

However, the discovery of valuable rubber forests in the upper Purus led to Brazilian invasions and demands for further boundary modifications. This was resolved in 1903 through the treaty of Petropolis. This agreement stipulated that the line should ascend the Abuna river to lat. 10 deg. 20' S., then follow that parallel West to the Rapirran river. The boundary follows the Rapirran to its principal source, then moves due West to the Ituxy river, following it to its source. From there, it proceeds to the source of Bahia Creek, follows it to the Aquiry (or Acre) river, and continues up that river to its source.

## Regional Geography and River Systems
The frontier region is heavily influenced by the river systems of Bolivia, which are divided into the Central Plateau, La Plata, and Amazon regions. The Amazon system covers more than half of the republic and includes rivers flowing into the Madeira, as well as tributaries of the Purus and Acre in the north. The Madeira is formed by the union of the Beni and Mamore rivers at lat. 10 deg. 20' S.

The Mamore river, which rises on the north-east slopes of the Sierra de Cochabamba, is approximately 600 m. long and largely navigable. Its principal Bolivian tributary is the Guapay (or Grande), which rises in the Cordillera Oriental and follows a tortuous course of over 700 m. Other significant tributaries rising on the north-east flanks of the Andes include the Yacuma, Secure, Chapare, and Manique (or Apere).

## Infrastructure and Communication
To address the isolation of the region, Brazil entered into a 1903 treaty with Bolivia to improve communications. Brazil agreed to pay L2,000,000 in cash for Bolivia to use on internal railway construction. Additionally, Brazil agreed to build a railway approximately 180 m. long around the falls of the Madeira to provide north-eastern Bolivia with access to the Amazon.
