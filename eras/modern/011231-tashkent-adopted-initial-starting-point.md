# Tashkent and Russian Geodetic Surveys

## Geodetic Surveys in Russia
The progress of geodetic surveys within the Russian Empire was historically established across its European territories. A direct system of triangulation, executed with high scientific precision, connected St Petersburg with Tiflis, located on the southern slopes of the Caucasus. Furthermore, St Petersburg was connected to Greenwich through European triangulation systems. Consequently, Russia adopted the Greenwich meridian as the zero point for all of its longitude values.

## Challenges in Asiatic Russia
Beyond the eastern shores of the Caspian Sea, the implementation of first-class triangulation for direct geodetic measurements was not possible. This created a separation between the surveys of Europe and those of Asiatic Russia, caused by the width of the inland sea. The rigorous geodetic survey methods used in Europe were hindered by the arid nature of the trans-Caspian deserts, which proved to be an insuperable obstacle. As a result, Russian geography in central Asia relied on means other than direct measurement to determine the coordinate values of latitude and longitude for any specific point.

## The Role of the Tashkent Observatory
To address these challenges, the astronomical observatory at Tashkent was adopted as the initial starting-point for the trans-Caspian triangulation of Russia. This specific triangulation is ranked as second-class and has extended to the Pamir frontier beyond Osh. The longitude of the Tashkent observatory was determined differentially with Pulkova via telegraph.

## Military and Strategic Context
The Russian presence in the region was the result of long-term territorial and mercantile ambitions. Russia established relations with Khiva in the 17th century, following raids by the Cossacks of the Yaik. In 1717, Peter the Great sent a military force to Khiva to release Russian subjects, open mercantile relations with India through Turan, and investigate reports of auriferous sand in the bed of the Oxus. Although that expedition and a subsequent 1839 expedition under General Perovsky ended in disaster, Russia continued its advance.

In 1847, the Russians founded a fort at the mouth of the Syr-darya (or Jaxartes), which provided a base for further operations and deprived the Khivans of territory and tax-paying Kirghiz. Following operations against Khokand, the Russians captured Tashkent in 1865. 

## Expansion into Khiva
With the capture of Tashkent, Russia redirected its focus toward Khiva. In 1869, Krasnovodsk was founded on the east shore of the Caspian, and between 1871 and 1872, the country leading to Khiva from various parts of Russian Turkestan was surveyed and explored. In 1873, a large-scale expedition of 10,000 men under General Kaufmann was organized. This army operated from three different bases: Orenburg, Krasnovodsk, and Tashkent. The resulting occupation of Khiva led to the annexation of all territory on the right bank of the Oxus, totaling 110,000 souls and 35,700 square miles.
