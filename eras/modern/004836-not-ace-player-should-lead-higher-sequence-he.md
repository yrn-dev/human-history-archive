# Card Play Strategies in No-Trump Declarations

In the context of card games such as bridge, specifically during a no-trump declaration, the primary objective is to bring in a long suit. To successfully establish such a suit, certain favorable conditions are preferred: one hand should possess at least five cards of that suit, and the two hands combined should hold eight cards (unless they possess a sequence of high cards) to increase the probability of establishing the suit within three rounds. Additionally, the hand containing the strong suit must have sufficient cards of re-entry, and the suit should not contain too many possible tenaces, which could make opening it disadvantageous.

## Leading and Trick Management
The goal of playing a suit is not to secure tricks early, but to maximize the total number of possible tricks. In some instances, it is strategically sound to give away a trick to create a favorable opening for a finesse. If it is uncertain which hand should perform the finesse, it is advisable to delay the action as long as possible, as an adversary's failure or the falling of a specific card may disclose the suit. Finessing against a card that must be unguarded is generally considered unsound.

When a player lacks cards of re-entry, they should avoid leading winning cards that would exhaust the suit from their partner's hand. For example, if one hand holds seven cards headed by the ace and king while the other holds only two, it is often correct to give the first trick to adversaries despite the chance of making seven tricks.

## Leading Conventions and Sequences
Against no-trumps, a leader should generally avoid playing winning cards unless there is a strong chance of clearing the suit without partner assistance. It is often advisable to give away the first trick—particularly if the leader lacks a card of re-entry—so the partner may have a card of that suit to return upon gaining the lead. However, the player may lead their best card if they hold:
* Ace, king, and queen.
* Ace and king with seven cards in the suit.
* Ace, king, knave, and ten with six cards in the suit.

Regarding sequences, if a player holds three honours and any two are in sequence (excluding the ace), the higher of the sequence should be led. The highest card should also be led from the following combinations:
* Queen, knave, ten.
* Queen, knave, nine.
* Knave, ten, nine.
* Knave, ten, eight.
* Ten, nine, eight.

In other scenarios, the player should lead a small card, typically the fourth best according to usual convention. This allows the dealer and partner to credit the leader with three cards higher than the one led.

## The "Eleven" Rule and Information
The "eleven" rule is used to determine the number of cards higher than the led card that are not in the leader's hand. This is calculated by subtracting the pips of the led card from eleven. For example, if a seven is led, the calculation (11-7=4) indicates there are four cards higher than the seven not held by the leader. If three of those cards are already seen (such as the queen, nine, and eight), the dealer must hold the remaining one.

The dealer is encouraged to provide as little information as possible about their hand by playing frequent false cards, though they usually play the higher or highest of a sequence. Conversely, leading the highest of a sequence through the dummy often signals to the third player that a good finesse is available. Leading the lowest of a sequence through the dealer may explain the position to the third player while keeping the dealer uninformed.

## Finessing and Suit Establishment
When positioned on the dummy's left, finessing against a card not in the dummy's hand is futile. However, if a player holds the ace and knave and the dummy holds either the king or queen, the knave should usually be played. This is done because the other high card might be in the leader's hand, or to maintain a tenace over the dummy if the finesse fails.

Players attempting to establish a long suit should keep every card of that suit if possible, regardless of whether it was already opened or is a suit they want their partner to lead. However, if the primary goal is to establish the partner's suit, the player does not need to keep their own long suit and should instead focus on guarding other suits. In some circles, a discard from a suit is understood to indicate strength, though this convention can lead players to discard valuable cards.
