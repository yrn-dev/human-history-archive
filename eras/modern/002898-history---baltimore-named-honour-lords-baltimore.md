# Baltimore

Baltimore is a major city and seaport serving as the metropolis of Maryland, USA. Located at the head of tide-water on the Patapsco River and its north-west and middle branches, the city sits 12 miles from the entrance of these waters into the Chesapeake Bay. It is situated approximately 172 miles by water from the Atlantic Ocean and is connected by rail to Washington (40 miles N.W.), Annapolis (26 miles N. by W.), Philadelphia (97 miles S.W.), and New York (184 miles). As of the 1910 census, the population was 558,485.

## Origins and Early Development
The city was named in honor of the Lords Baltimore, the founders of the province of Maryland. George Calvert, the 1st Baron Baltimore (c. 1580–1632), was an English statesman and secretary of state who became a Roman Catholic in 1625. While he established a settlement in Newfoundland in 1621 called Avalon, he died in 1632 before securing a new concession for Maryland; the charter subsequently passed to his son, Cecilius, the second Lord Baltimore, who founded the colony.

Despite the colony's planting, no settlement was made at the site of Baltimore for nearly 100 years. Two other town-sites had previously been given the name, but neither became permanent. In 1729, the provincial legislature directed seven commissioners to purchase 60 acres of land on the north side of the Patapsco to be laid out in sixty equal lots as the town of Baltimore. In 1732, Jones-Town (also known as Old Town) was established on the opposite side of Jones's Falls, and the two towns were consolidated in 1745.

Growth was initially slow; in 1752, the city consisted of only twenty-five houses and two hundred inhabitants. However, development accelerated as Germans rapidly developed the resources of the interior, for which Baltimore served as a trade center. By the start of the War of Independence, the population had reached 6,755.

## Economic and Political Evolution
Baltimore became an important shipping center early in its history. It was designated a port of entry in 1780. During the War of Independence and the War of 1812, the city dispatched many privateers. In the interval between these conflicts, Baltimore ship-owners participated in the global carrying trade, gaining fame for their "Baltimore clippers."

Until 1797, the city was governed from Annapolis through commissions with limited powers. In 1797, Baltimore received its first charter and annexed the Fells' Point settlement, which had been founded around 1730 by a ship carpenter named William Fells.

## Urban Geography and Architecture
The city is divided by Jones's Falls. East Baltimore consists of Old Town to the north and west, and Fells' Point to the south and east, the latter being a manufacturing and shipbuilding quarter. Old Town retains some eighteenth-century architecture, including post-houses that served travelers using the old Cumberland Road.

The western side of Jones's Falls contains the more modern and inviting portions of the city, with principal residential districts in the northern half. The chief business thoroughfare is Baltimore Street, which intersects with Charles Street. The wholesale, financial, and shipping districts are located near this intersection. Wealthy citizens reside along North Charles Street, which features imposing residences. Wide streets with squares, fountains, and lawns include Broadway, Washington Place, Mount Vernon Place, and Eutaw Place.

Notable civic buildings located along East Fayette Street include the court-house (completed 1899), the post-office (completed 1890), and the city hall (completed 1875). The city hall is built in the Renaissance style with white Maryland marble and an iron dome 260 feet high. Other significant structures include the Roman Catholic cathedral, the First Presbyterian church, and the Grace Episcopal church.

## Education, Health, and Social Services
Baltimore is home to numerous professional schools, including the University of Maryland and Baltimore University, both of which offer courses in dentistry, medicine, and law. Other institutions include the Baltimore Medical College, the Woman's Medical College, and St. Mary's Seminary, established in 1791. The city's principal libraries include the Enoch Pratt, the Peabody Institute, and those of the Maryland Historical Society and Johns Hopkins University.

The city's charitable network is extensive, featuring the Johns Hopkins hospital, the Sheppard asylum, the Baltimore orphan asylum, and the Bay View asylum. Specialized care is provided by the Thomas Wilson sanatorium for children under three and the Woman's Industrial Exchange. Additionally, the McDonogh farm school, established via a bequest from John McDonogh, prepares poor boys for college.

## Demographics and Notable Residents
According to 1900 data, the population included 79,258 negroes and 68,600 foreign-born residents. The largest foreign-born groups were natives of Germany (33,208), Russia (10,493), and Ireland (9,690).

The city was also home to a branch of the Bonaparte family. Jerome Bonaparte married Elizabeth Patterson, the daughter of a Baltimore merchant, on December 24, 1803. Their son, Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte (1805–1870), resided chiefly in Baltimore. His own son, also named Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte (1832–1893), served in the French army in Italy and the Crimea.
