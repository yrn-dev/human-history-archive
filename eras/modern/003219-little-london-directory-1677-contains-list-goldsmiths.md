# The Little London Directory (1677) and the Goldsmiths of London

The *little London Directory* of 1677 is a historical record that contains a list of goldsmiths. These individuals and firms were specifically noted for keeping "running cashes."

## Evolution of Goldsmiths into Bankers
In England, the origin of bank notes can be traced back to the notes issued by goldsmiths. These goldsmiths would receive deposits of money and, in return, provide receipts or notes for those sums, which were payable on demand. This practice of providing deposit-receipts or notes continued among London bankers until approximately 1781, at which point they began providing customers with books of cheques instead.

Prior to the introduction of cheque-books, the issuance of notes was viewed as the primary feature of banking. Consequently, any prohibition on the issuance of notes was seen as an effective way to bar banking activities. This is evident in the act of 6 Anne, c. 50 (1707) and the Bank of England Act 1708 (7 Anne, c. 30, s. 66), both of which contained prohibitory clauses preventing more than six persons from issuing promissory notes. The intent of these clauses was to prevent the formation of any bank with more than six partners, which effectively stopped the creation of joint-stock banks at that time.

## Notable Goldsmiths and Clients
Some goldsmiths operated substantial businesses. Edward Blackwell, an eminent banker and goldsmith during the reign of Charles II, maintained a high-profile clientele. His records indicate that he did business with the king, the queen mother Henrietta Maria, the prince of Orange, James, duke of York, and Samuel Pepys. Additionally, he held accounts for the Goldsmiths' Company, other city companies, the East India Company, and Sir John Houblon, who served as the first governor of the Bank of England.

Despite his prominence, Blackwell was ruined in 1672 following the closing of the exchequer. However, his family connection to banking continued; his son married into the family of Sir Francis Child, and his grandsons eventually became partners in Child's Bank.

## Continuity of Firms
The *little London Directory* of 1677 provides a benchmark for the longevity of these early financial houses. Of the firms listed in 1677, five were still conducting business in 1876. Three of these houses—or firms that descended directly from them—continued to operate beyond that period:
* Child & Co. of Temple Bar
* Martin & Co. of Lombard Street (operating as Martin's Bank, Ltd.)
* Hoare & Co. of Fleet Street

Other firms mentioned in the 1876 period were later absorbed. Barnetts was absorbed by Lloyds Bank in 1884, and Willis, Percival & Co. was absorbed by the Capital and Counties in 1878.
