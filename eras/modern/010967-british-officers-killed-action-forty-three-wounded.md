# Military Casualties and Engagements in Archive Records

The Human History Archive contains various records of military engagements across different eras and regions, detailing the personnel involved and the resulting casualties of these conflicts.

## British Campaigns in Africa and India

In a campaign to crush a rebellion following the relief of Kumasi, Colonel Willcocks led a force consisting of 134 British officers, 35 British non-commissioned officers, and 3,368 natives, which included Sikhs from Central Africa and Yaos, as well as Ashanti levies. The forces achieved a complete victory over the Kumasi at Obassa on September 30. By December 28, the final two remaining rebel chiefs in the field were captured, restoring peace by the start of 1901. The total casualties for the campaign, including deaths from disease, numbered 1,007. Among the British, nine officers were killed in action, forty-three were wounded, and six died of disease. Following the campaign, Colonel Willcocks was promoted and created a K.C.M.G.

Earlier British military history in India records a battle at Assaye on September 23, 1803. Major-General Wellesley (later the Duke of Wellington) led a force of 4,500 men against combined Mahratta forces under the rajah of Berar and Sindhia. The Mahratta force was significantly larger, consisting of 50,000 men and 100 pieces of cannon operated by French artillerymen. Despite the disparity in numbers, the English achieved a complete victory. The Mahratta forces suffered 12,000 killed and wounded, while General Wellesley lost 1,657 men, killed and wounded, representing one-third of his force.

## World War I: British and Allied Operations

Records from the Great War detail various engagements involving British and Allied forces. In one naval action, British casualties were limited to approximately forty wounded and thirty-five killed, with no British ships sunk or seriously injured. In contrast, over a thousand Germans perished, and 224 desperately wounded Germans were rescued by Commodore Keyes on the destroyer *Lurcher*. This action had a decisive effect on the German Emperor, who subsequently issued orders to restrict the initiative of the North Sea Fleet Commander-in-Chief to avoid further losses of ships.

In land engagements, the British Expeditionary Army operated in harmony with the French General Staff. On August 26, British troops guarded the left flank of the French Armies at Cambrai [Le Cateau]. Later, on September 1, the 1st British Cavalry Brigade and the 4th Guards Brigade engaged in a sharp action near Compiègne. The Guards Brigade bore the brunt of the fighting, losing approximately 300 men killed and wounded, while ten German guns were captured.

Other records highlight the experience of the South African Brigade, which held a difficult post on the British front for six days and five nights under concentrated fire from three sides. The brigade began the engagement on July 14 with 121 officers and 3,032 men; by July 20, only a remnant of 143 remained, with a total of about 750 eventually assembled. Officer casualties included 23 who were killed or died of wounds, 47 wounded, and 15 missing. During this period, Lieutenant-Colonel Heal, commander of the 1st Regiment, was killed after having been wounded twice.

Naval casualties are also noted during an operation involving the ship *Iris*. A shell burst among fifty-six marines, killing forty-nine and wounding seven. Another shell hit the ward-room (serving as a sick bay), killing twenty-six men and four officers. The total casualties for the *Iris* were eight officers and sixty-nine men killed, and three officers and one hundred and two men wounded.

## American and Japanese Military Records

Archive data regarding American forces in Europe indicates a total of approximately 2,053,347 men, including organizations at Murmansk and units with the Italian Army. Of these, 1,338,169 combatant troops were in France. As of November 18, American losses included 36,145 killed in action, 14,811 died of disease, 2,204 unclassified deaths, 179,625 wounded, 2,163 prisoners, and 1,160 missing. The Americans captured approximately 44,000 prisoners and 1,400 trench mortars, howitzers, and guns.

Japanese military records include a battle against Russian forces where the Japanese suffered 318 killed (including five officers) and 783 wounded (including thirty-three officers). Russian casualties were 1,363 killed and 613 prisoners. The Japanese captured eight machine-guns, twenty-one quick-firing field-guns, and 1,021 rifles.

Additionally, an early Japanese record from 789 describes a campaign led by Kosami. Despite preparations involving the collection of salt and rice (including 175,000 bushels of hulled rice from Mutsu), the army faced severe climatic difficulties, including deep snow and floods. After the van was ambushed, Kosami suffered a heavy defeat. Although he commanded a large army, only 2,500 entered the action; casualties included 25 killed, 245 wounded, and 1,036 drowned.
