# The Duchess of Kendal and Related Political Figures

## The Duchess of Kendal
Ehrengarde Melusina (1667–1743), known as the duchess of Kendal, was the mistress of King George I. Born on December 25, 1667, in Emden, she was the daughter of Gustavus Adolphus, count of Schulenburg, who held significant positions under the elector of Brandenburg. Melusina first entered the household of Sophia, the electress of Hanover, and became the mistress of Sophia's son, the future king, around 1690. 

Following George's arrival in England in 1714, the duchess followed him and became his primary favorite, supplanting Charlotte Sophia, Baroness von Kilmannsegge. Her titles evolved over time; she was created duchess of Munster in 1716, then duchess of Kendal, and was later made a princess of the Empire by Emperor Charles VI in 1723.

The duchess was characterized as very avaricious. She generated wealth by selling patent rights and public offices and titles. One such sale involved the privilege of supplying Ireland with a new copper coinage, which she sold to William Wood, a Wolverhampton iron merchant. This resulted in the circulation of "Wood's halfpence," which prompted the writing of the *Drapier's Letters* by Swift.

## Political Influence and the Recall of Bolingbroke
The duchess of Kendal wielded considerable influence over King George I. In political matters, she received £10,000 for procuring the recall of Bolingbroke from exile. In 1723, Bolingbroke received his pardon through her mediation and returned to London in June or July. 

Upon his return, Bolingbroke offered his services to Walpole, though these offers—specifically regarding the accession of several Tories to the administration—were received coldly. While Walpole succeeded in keeping Bolingbroke excluded from the House of Lords, a peremptory order from the king enabled Bolingbroke to hold real estate, though he was not permitted to alienate it.

Later, Bolingbroke attempted to organize an opposition to the Walpoles in 1726 alongside Windham and Pulteney. During this period, he gained the support of the duchess of Kendal by providing a bribe of £11,000, which was sourced from his wife's estates.

## The Marlborough Family and Court Intrigues
The political landscape of the era was also shaped by the family of John Churchill, the 1st Duke of Marlborough (1650–1722). Churchill, born in Devonshire, rose through the court after serving as page of honour to the duke of York. He was created earl in 1689 and duke in 1702.

During the reign of Queen Anne, the political position of the Duke of Marlborough was undermined by party intrigues. While his friends were initially among the Tories, the ministry eventually introduced Harley and St John in May 1704. The duchess of Marlborough, influenced partly by her son-in-law, the earl of Sunderland, shifted her allegiance to the Whig cause.

The relationship between the duchess of Marlborough and the queen deteriorated due to the duchess's imperious manner. This breach was further widened by Harley and St John with the assistance of Abigail Hill, a cousin of the duchess who had been given a court position. Consequently, the family of Marlborough was humiliated; Sunderland and Godolphin fell in July-August 1710, followed by the dismissal of the duchess from her offices. The Duke of Marlborough's own fall occurred on the last day of 1711, leading him to live on the Continent from November 1712 until the death of Queen Anne in August 1714.

## Succession of the Marlborough Title
Following the death of the 1st Duke in June 1722, his titles passed to his eldest daughter, Henrietta, who became the duchess of Marlborough. As she died without sons, she was succeeded by her nephew, Charles Spencer, the 5th earl of Sunderland. Charles did not obtain Blenheim until the death of Sarah, the dowager duchess, in 1744. The lineage continued through George Spencer, the 4th duke, whose sons included the 5th duke (who took the name Spencer-Churchill), Lord Henry John Spencer, and Lord Francis Almeric Spencer.
