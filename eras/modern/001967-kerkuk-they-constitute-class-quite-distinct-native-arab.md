# Kerkuk

## Geography and Location
Kerkuk, also known as Qerquq, is the chief town of a sanjak located within the Mosul vilayet of Asiatic Turkey. The town is situated on both banks of the Khassa Chai, which is a tributary of the Tigris (known as Adhem in its lower course), and is positioned among the foothills of the Kurdistan Mountains at an elevation of approximately 1100 feet.

## Demographics and Population
The population of Kerkuk is estimated to be between 12,000 and 15,000 people. The inhabitants are chiefly Mahommedan Kurds. Historical records from the 14th century identify the Hasnani as one of the tribes that retained a leading place among the Kurds in Kerkuk and Arbil; tradition suggests the Hasnani originally came from Khorasan.

## Economy and Natural Resources
Due to its location at the junction of several routes, Kerkuk maintains a brisk transit trade. The town deals in various commodities, including cottons, Persian silks, hides, timber, fruit, and colouring materials. 

The town's principal importance, however, is derived from its naphtha and petroleum springs. Additionally, Kerkuk possesses natural warm springs that are used to supply baths and are reputed to have valuable medical properties. A burning mountain, which has been locally famous for many centuries, is located in the vicinity of the city.

## History and Landmarks
Kerkuk is evidently an ancient site. Its citadel is situated upon an artificial mound that reaches a height of 130 feet. The town served as a metropolitan see for the Chaldean Christians. Within the city, there is a Jewish quarter located beneath the citadel. Furthermore, one of the mosques in the town displays the reputed sarcophagi of the Hebrew children and Daniel.

## Regional Administration and Social Classes
In the broader administration of the province, Bagdad is governed by a pasha and a council. While the pasha and higher officials generally originate from Constantinople, a very large portion of other Turkish officials appear to come from the town of Kerkuk. 

These officials from Kerkuk constitute a class that is quite distinct from the native Arab population. This class, along with the Turkish government in general, is intensely unpopular among the Arabs. This unpopularity is increased by religious differences, as the Turks are Sunnites while the Arabs are, as a rule, Shi'ites.
