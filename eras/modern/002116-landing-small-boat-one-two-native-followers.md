# Landing and Shore Operations via Small Craft

The use of small boats for landing personnel, transporting goods, and conducting tactical maneuvers is a recurring theme across various historical and geographical contexts, ranging from ancient military crossings to cultural processions and modern wartime evacuations.

## Military Landings and Tactical Crossings
Small boats have frequently been employed to facilitate the movement of troops and animals from water to land during military campaigns. During the campaigns of Hannibal, infantry crossed rivers using small boats, rafts, and a specific type of gondola constructed from hollowed-out tree trunks. To ensure an immediate offensive upon landing, Hannibal placed completely harnessed horses into boats so the riders could charge the enemy immediately upon reaching the shore. To protect the smaller fleet and ease the passage, larger boats were positioned in a line at the top of the channel to break the force of the waves.

In other historical conflicts, small boats served as the primary means of rapid deployment or retreat. In the chronicles of Norway, King Hrorek and thirty men utilized two small boats in a channel. Upon encountering Thorer Lange and his company, Hrorek’s men turned toward the land and sprang ashore, though the king remained on the aft seat to bid farewell to his pursuers.

## Cultural and Ceremonial Shore Activities
Beyond military utility, small boats have been used for organized cultural processions. In the journey to the city of Bubastis, large numbers of men and women sail together in boats. As these vessels pass various cities along the river-bank, they bring the boats to land. During these landings, some women dance, some cry aloud and jeer at the women of the city, and others pull up their garments. These activities continue at every city along the bank until the travelers reach Bubastis to celebrate a festival involving great sacrifices and the consumption of grape wine.

## Transport and Commercial Vessels
Various regions have developed specialized small or native boats for river navigation and trade. In Egypt, the *dahabeah* (also spelled *dahabiya* or *dahabeeyah*) is a native passenger boat used on the Nile. These barge-like house-boats, often ornamented with gilding, were historically used by travelers and the wealthy classes. Modern versions may be made of iron, drawing approximately 2 feet of water and accommodating between two and a dozen passengers.

In the trade of Assam, native boats are a significant component of external commerce, particularly in the Surma valley, where they carry about 43% of both imports and exports. In other regions, such as the route to Babylon, round leather boats are used. These vessels are constructed with willow ribs from the land of the Armenians and covered in hides. Because the current is too swift to sail back up the stream, the boats are dismantled upon arrival at Babylon; the straw and ribs are sold by auction, and the hides are packed onto asses to be driven back to Armenia.

## Wartime Evacuations and Incidents
In more recent conflicts, small craft have been essential for fleeing combat zones. During the German advance on Antwerp, fugitives swarmed into Ostend to find transport to England and France. Because landing was prohibited at Dunkerque, vessels were forced to continue to Havre. In these desperate evacuations, any available craft was utilized, including launches, row-boats, and sailing boats.

Small boats have also been involved in naval engagements and intelligence activities. During a British submarine operation in the Marmora, a small steamer was stopped, leading to a "panic abandon ship" where the crew capsized their boats while attempting to put them out. An American gentleman on board was subsequently sent ashore. In another instance, the figure Therimenes is recorded to have sailed off in a small boat after handing over a fleet to Astyochus, after which he was lost.
