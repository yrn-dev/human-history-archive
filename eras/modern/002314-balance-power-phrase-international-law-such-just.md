# BALANCE OF POWER

The balance of power is a term used in international law to describe a "just equilibrium" among the members of the family of nations. The primary objective of this equilibrium is to prevent any single nation from becoming powerful enough to enforce its will upon the other members of the international community.

## Conceptual Origins and Development
The principle of the balance of power is rooted in experience and the instinct of self-preservation. As noted by Hume in his *Essay on the Balance of Power*, the concept is as old as history and was well-understood by ancient practical statesmen and political theorists. Polybius articulated this principle by stating that no single power should be allowed to grow so great that it becomes impossible to dispute manifest rights with them on equal terms.

While the instinct was ancient, the theory was formulated as a fundamental principle of diplomacy at the beginning of the 17th century. This occurred as the science of international law was developed by Grotius and his successors. Under this formulation, European states were viewed as a federal community. The essential condition of this community was the preservation of a disposition of affairs where no single potentate or state could absolutely predominate or prescribe laws to others. Because all states shared an interest in this settlement, it was considered the duty, right, and interest of every power to interfere—including through the use of armed force—if the conditions of this balance were assailed or infringed.

## Application in Political Science and History
Once formulated, the balance of power became an axiom of political science. It was emphasized by Fenelon in his *Instructions* to the young duke of Burgundy, proclaimed by Frederick the Great in his *Anti-Machiavel*, and clarified in 1806 by Friedrich von Gentz in *Fragments on the Balance of Power*.

Historically, this principle served as the basis for coalitions formed against Napoleon and Louis XIV. It also provided the occasion or excuse for the majority of wars that occurred in Europe between the Congress of Muenster in 1648 and subsequent conflicts. The Treaty of Westphalia (1648) gave expression to the European balance of power by granting legal recognition to Protestantism, which ended the era where the Pope could provide arbitration in international matters. Consequently, states had to rely on themselves; weaker states found protection by uniting against any power attempting to absorb them.

## Relation to International Law and Neutrality
According to Professor L. Oppenheim, an equilibrium between powers is essential for the existence of international law itself. In a system lacking a central authority, the only sanction for the code of rules defined in treaties or established by custom is the ability of powers to hold one another in check. Without this balance, a sufficiently powerful state could ignore international law to act solely according to its own interests and convenience.

The balance of power is also closely linked to the concept of neutralization. Certain geographical zones are predestined to become theaters of struggle between nations; when such a zone falls under the exclusive influence of one great power, it marks a breakdown of the European balance of power. To prevent this, treaties may place these zones in a position of permanent neutrality, creating a régime of peace based on voluntary and reciprocal restrictions of action.

Belgium serves as an example of this "wheel" in general policy. Established as perpetually neutral by the great powers after the revolt of 1830, Belgium's status involves reciprocal obligations. The contracting powers guarantee Belgium permanent peace and agree not to attack it or compel it to abandon its neutrality. In return, the neutralized state must:
* Defend its own neutrality.
* Prevent the passage of belligerent convoys or troops through its territory.
* Remain a truly independent state, as becoming dependent on another power would destroy the European balance of power.
