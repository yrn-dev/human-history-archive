# Gold and Silver in Historical Economic Systems

The use of gold and silver as currency and assets has historically been associated with shifts in governance, social morality, and state finance across various civilizations. While some states embraced these metals to facilitate trade and government spending, others viewed them as sources of corruption or "imported ruin."

## The Spartan Prohibition of Precious Metals
In ancient Sparta, the institutions of Lykurgus strictly prohibited the use of gold and silver money. The state instead mandated the use of heavy iron pieces as currency. This iron money was produced by dipping red-hot iron into vinegar, a process that rendered the metal brittle and useless for other purposes. Because this currency was heavy and difficult to transport, it was only portable in trifling amounts, which served to limit the accumulation of wealth.

The introduction of gold and silver into Sparta is attributed to the proceedings of Lysander, who brought home a large sum of money received from Cyrus for the prosecution of war. This admission of precious metals was met with strong opposition from some ephors and citizens, such as Skiraphidas (or Phlogidas), who argued that such a move was a flagrant transgression of Lykurgean ordinances and fatal to Spartan morality. Despite this, the treasure was accepted into the public treasury under the proviso that it be used exclusively for government purposes. For a time, it remained a penal—and potentially capital—offense for any private citizen to possess gold or silver. However, once these metals were tolerated for the purposes of the government, enforcing the prohibition against individuals became impracticable.

## Taxation and Currency in Japan
In Japan, gold and silver were integrated into a complex taxation system involving various forms of payment, including money, rice, and labor.

### Guild and Business Taxes
The government imposed numerous miscellaneous taxes on businesses and expert labor. To facilitate collection in cities, merchants were organized into guilds. Specific taxes were levied on these guilds in gold; for example, a guild of sake-brewers was taxed one thousand gold ryo, while a guild of wholesale cotton dealers paid five hundred ryo.

### Regional Payment Methods
The manner of paying taxes varied by locality. In the Kwanto region, taxes for uplands were paid in money at a rate of one gold ryo per two and a half koku of rice. In the Nankai-do, Kinai, and western provinces, the tax was split, with one-third paid in money. In these areas, the value of a koku of rice was fixed at forty-eight mon of silver, which was equivalent to four-fifths of a gold ryo.

### Shogunate Reserves and Expenditures
The treasury of the shogunate saw significant fluctuations based on the frugality of its leaders. Ieyasu practiced strict economy, leaving behind a reserve of nearly two million ounces (troy) of silver and 150,000 gold oban (one and a half million ryo). His son, Hidetada, spent large sums on the repairs of Yedo Castle and distributed 182,000 ryo of silver and 4,217,400 ryo of gold among barons during progresses to Kyoto. The third shogun, Iemitsu, was more open-handed, making donations of 5,000 kwamme of silver to the citizens of Yedo and Kyoto, and spending a million ryo on the mausolea at Nikko.

## Precious Metals in Other Colonial and State Contexts
In the La Plata region, the Spanish colonists' interest in Buenos Aires was initially overshadowed by the "stores of gold and silver" in Peru, which dazzled the imagination of the king, his councillors, and merchants. This focus on Peruvian wealth led the Spanish crown to take little interest in the trading station of Buenos Aires, initially believing one small shipment of supplies per year was sufficient.

In other contexts, such as Bolivia in the early 20th century, the state derived revenue from various duties and taxes, though export duties were specifically applied to minerals with the exception of silver.

## Costs of War and Public Works
The financial burden of maintaining state power often necessitated heavy taxation. In Athens, despite a commanding naval position, the cost of war became a serious burden. This was exacerbated by the need for a perpetual coast-guard to protect commerce from privateers from Ægina, leading to the imposition of a heavy direct property tax at home. Similarly, in Japan, the Bakufu imposed "aids" (otetsudai) and military dues (buke-yaku) on feudatories for great works or military purposes, which were often very onerous.
