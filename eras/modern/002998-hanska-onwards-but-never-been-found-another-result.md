# Honoré de Balzac: Works and Personal Life

## The Comédie Humaine and Literary Production
Honoré de Balzac produced an immense and varied body of work, the core of which was his "Comedy" (the *Comédie humaine*). This vast structure, which includes subdivisions such as *Etudes philosophiques* and *Scenes de la vie parisienne*, was conceived as a single huge structure. Balzac began carrying out this idea of a unified structure in 1842, with the earliest volumes of the *Comédie humaine* appearing between 1842 and 1846 in sixteen volumes.

Balzac's approach to writing was characterized by a unique relationship with his production; it has been suggested that his entire output existed in his head together. This mental cohesion led to a process of constant re-connecting, re-shaping, and altering of different parts of his work. He frequently corrected small printed originals into long novels during the proofing stage. His working habits were intense, often writing from midnight until the following day, sometimes in stretches of sixteen hours that continued for weeks.

## Lost and Posthumous Works
Due to the nature of his creative process, some of Balzac's planned works never transitioned from his mind to print. There are numerous titles mentioned in his letters as being more or less finished for which only fragmentary manuscript sketches remain or no trace exists at all. One notable example is a considerable book titled *La Bataille*, which focused on the battle of Essling. Balzac visited the site of the battle and referred to the work as being in progress from the time of his early letters to Madame Hanska onwards, but the book has never been found.

Beyond his novels, Balzac wrote political pamphlets, miscellaneous articles, and several dramas. His plays include *Mercadet le faiseur*—which found some success after his death—as well as *La Maratre*, *Vautrin*, *Les Ressources de Quinola*, and *Pamela Giraud*. He also nearly wrote a periodical titled the *Revue parisienne*.

## Relationship with Madame Hanska
Work remained the primary focus of Balzac's life, often taking precedence over business, friendship, and love. This was evident in his relationship with Madame Hanska; he occasionally resisted her summons to visit her so that he could continue working.

The two had a formal agreement as early as a Genevan meeting in 1833, where Madame Hanska promised to marry Balzac should her husband die. Following her husband's death at the end of 1841, she did not allow Balzac to visit her until the following year. Although the engagement was renewed during a trip to St Petersburg, the wedding was indefinitely postponed. For several years, the couple met in various cities, including Paris, Rome, Brussels, Wiesbaden, and Baden.

In September 1847, Balzac was invited to her estate in the Ukraine, Wierzschovnia, as her future husband. After a visit lasting more than two years, interrupted by one trip to Paris, they were married on March 14, 1850.

## Final Days and Death
By the time of his marriage, Balzac's health had been ruined by his enormous labors, his travels across Europe, and the Russian cold. Following the wedding, the couple moved to a house in Paris on the rue Fortunee, which Balzac had purchased for his wife and filled with his collections, arriving at the end of May.

Balzac died on Sunday, August 17, at half-past eleven at night. He was attended by his mother, though his wife was not present. He was buried on August 20 in Pere La Chaise. His pall-bearers included Dumas, Sainte-Beuve, the statesman Baroche, and Victor Hugo. In a funeral discourse, Hugo described Balzac's work with the phrase "observation and imagination."

## Bibliography and Editions
The bibliography of Balzac is extraordinarily complicated. The *Edition definitive* (published from 1869 onwards) includes his miscellaneous works in four volumes and a volume of correspondence. It does not, however, reprint the *Oeuvres de jeunesse*. These early works were included in a different edition consisting of fifty-five small 24mo volumes published by Michel (Calmann) Levy, which was the most well-known edition for many years and was referred to by Browning as "all Balzac's novels fifty volumes long."

Other significant bibliographic resources include:
*   *Histoire des oeuvres* (1875 and later), attached to the *Edition definitive* by M. Spoelberch de Lovenjoul.
*   *Repertoire des oeuvres de Balzac* (1893), compiled by MM. Cerfbeer and Christophe, which tracks the history of the personages of the *Comédie*.
*   *Oeuvres posthumes*, the first volume of which appeared in 1899 (an instalment of *Lettres a l'etrangere*) and a second in 1906, featuring illustrations and a portrait of Madame Hanska.
*   English translations edited by Saintsbury (1895-1898), which provide summaries of the bibliography, general criticism, and a short Memoir.
