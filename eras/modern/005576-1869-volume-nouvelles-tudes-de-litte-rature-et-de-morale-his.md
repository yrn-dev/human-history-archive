# Jacques Victor Albert, Duc de Broglie

Jacques Victor Albert, Duc de Broglie (1821–1901), was a French statesman, diplomat, and man of letters. Born in Paris on June 13, 1821, he was the eldest son of the previous duke. Before ascending to the dukedom, he was known as the prince de Broglie.

## Literary Career and Early Works
The prince de Broglie began his professional life with a brief diplomatic career, serving in Rome and Madrid. However, the revolution of 1848 led him to withdraw from public life to focus on literature. His early scholarly contributions included a translation of the religious system of Leibnitz, published in 1846.

He established his reputation through writings for *Le Correspondant* (a clerical and Orleanist organ) and the *Revue des deux Mondes*. These contributions were later compiled into two volumes: *Études morales et littéraires* (1853) and *Questions de religion et d'histoire* (1860). In 1869, he supplemented these works with a volume titled *Nouvelles études de littérature et de morale*. His work *L'Église et l'empire romain au IVe siècle*, published between 1856 and 1866, earned him a seat in the Academy in 1862, succeeding Lacordaire.

## Political and Diplomatic Service
In 1870, he succeeded his father as the duc de Broglie. The following year, he was elected to the National Assembly representing the department of the Eure. Shortly thereafter, on February 19, he was appointed as the ambassador to London. His tenure in London ended in March 1872 when he resigned his post following criticisms of his negotiations regarding commercial treaties between France and England.

Upon returning to the National Assembly, the duc de Broglie became the primary figure in the monarchical campaign directed against Thiers.

## Ministerial Roles and Governance
Following the replacement of Thiers by Marshal MacMahon, the duc de Broglie was appointed minister for foreign affairs and president of the council in May 1873. On November 26, during a reconstruction of the ministry after the passing of the septennate, he moved to the ministry of the interior.

His time in office was characterized by extreme conservatism. This political stance resulted in bitter hatred from the Republicans. Simultaneously, he alienated other political factions: the Legitimist party was alienated by his friendly relations with the Bonapartists, while the Bonapartists were alienated by his attempts to find a compromise between rival claimants to the monarchy. These tensions led to the fall of his cabinet on May 16, 1874. Three years later, on May 16, 1877, he was once again entrusted with the formation of a new cabinet.
