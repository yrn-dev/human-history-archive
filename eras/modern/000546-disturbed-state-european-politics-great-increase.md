# The Disturbed State of European Politics and Military Expansion

The political landscape of Europe has been characterized by periods of instability, shifting alliances, and a recurring drive toward military strengthening. These disturbances have often been linked to the ambitions of individual monarchs, the rise of nationalism, and the competition for raw materials and territorial influence.

## Military Strengthening in Austria-Hungary
The disturbed state of European politics and the increase in military establishments in other countries prompted Austria to strengthen its own military resources. Despite financial difficulties that limited the scale of these measures, compulsory military service was introduced in both Hungary and Austria in 1868, fixing the available wartime army at 800,000 men. 

The military structure was divided: a joint army operated under a joint ministry of war, while separate militias and ministers for national defence existed in each part of the monarchy. In Hungary, the national force, known as the *honved*, remained distinct from the ordinary army. In Austria, the *Landwehr* was organized as part of the standing army, with the exception of Tirol and Dalmatia, which maintained separate local militias.

Further developments occurred in 1882, when compulsory service was extended to Herzegovina and Bosnia. This period saw a reorganization based on the Prussian plan, introducing local organization and army corps to facilitate speedy mobilization, despite risks that national and local spirits might enter the army. In 1886, a law created the *Landsturm*, which provided for the organization and arming of the entire male population up to age forty-two during emergencies. Additional increases in recruits followed in 1889 and between 1892 and 1893. Despite these steps, the army establishment of Austria-Hungary showed a small increase compared to other continental powers. A point of unity for the monarchy remained the use of German as the common language of the army, a policy the monarch and government refused to concede despite protests in the Hungarian parliament.

## Diplomatic Instability and the Ambitions of Louis XIV
Earlier European instability was often driven by the ambitions of powerful leaders, such as Louis XIV of France. In the 17th century, the instability of the European situation hindered the efforts of Chancellor Griffenfeldt to restore Denmark as a leading international power. Louis XIV, resolved to conquer the Netherlands, worked to isolate the country by dissolving the Triple Alliance. 

In April 1672, France concluded a treaty with Sweden, which stipulated that France would not include Denmark in its alliances without Swedish consent, indicating that Sweden held more weight in French calculations than Denmark. Following the French invasion of the Netherlands in June 1672, Denmark was invited to join an alliance between the elector of Brandenburg and Emperor Leopold, while simultaneously negotiating to renew a Dano-Dutch alliance.

## Balkan Disturbances and the "Eastern Question"
The outbreak of disturbance in the Balkans ended a period of calm and created a difficult political problem for Vienna. Insurrections in Bosnia and Herzegovina led to a large influx of refugees into Austria. The government of Vienna faced a conflict of interest: while the Slav inhabitants of the empire sympathized with Christians in Turkey, the Hungarians—whose memories of Turkish conquest had been erased by the events of 1848—sympathized with the Turks. 

Austrian policy was governed by the cardinal principle that new Slav states could not be allowed on its southern frontier, especially as disturbances were being fomented by Russian agents and the Pan-Slav party. While German mediation initially preserved the understanding between Austria and Russia, and a 1875 note required Turkey to institute reforms, the rebellion spread to Bulgaria. 

A secret understanding was reached in early 1877 where Russia promised not to annex territory or take steps injurious to Austria. However, the Russian army's advance on Constantinople threatened Austrian influence. This led Andrassy to demand a European conference in Vienna to determine peace terms. When the peace of San Stefano violated Russian engagements, Andrassy mobilized a small portion of the army and requested a credit of 60 million gulden.

## Economic Drivers and Global Expansion
In the latter quarter of the nineteenth century, European political disturbances were mirrored by a "fresh scramble" for politically unprotected lands. This was driven by an increase in European populations, necessitating the search for staple foods, and the growth of scientific industrialism, which created demands for rubber, fats, greases, and other raw materials.

While Great Britain, Portugal, and Holland already controlled many tropical products, Germany (after 1871), France, and Italy began seeking Oriental countries for modernization or unannexed raw-material areas. This expansion occurred globally, except in the American region, where the Monroe Doctrine barred such adventures. 

## The Role of Great Britain and Neutrality
By the early twentieth century, Great Britain maintained a distinct position among European states. In correspondence from November 1911, Winston Churchill noted that England, almost alone among European powers, sought no territorial expansion and retained supremacy of the sea. During the "lamentable struggle" of that period, Britain declared its neutrality, though it expressed a desire to maintain friendship with Turkey, provided the Turkish government did not revert to oppressive methods or disturb the existing British status quo.
