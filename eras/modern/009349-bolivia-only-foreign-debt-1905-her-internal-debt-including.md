# Public Debt and Finance of Bolivia

The public debt of Bolivia is described as being comparatively small. The nation's financial history during the late 19th and early 20th centuries was characterized by periods of borrowing for internal development, subsequent bankruptcies, and various arrangements with creditors.

## Foreign Debt and External Loans
In the early part of the late 19th century, European and American investors provided capital for the internal development of the republic, attracted by its valuable resources and relative immunity from revolution. In 1871, the government contracted a loan in London for £1,000,000, followed by an additional loan of £2,400,000 in 1872 specifically for the construction of railways.

By 1887, the outstanding foreign debt reached £2,691,300, with interest arrears totaling £2,119,500. While an arrangement with creditors was reached in 1888, the republic became bankrupt again in 1895. A new arrangement was sanctioned in March 1897, which reduced the interest on £1,475,000 to 2-1/2% and the interest on £525,000 to 3%. This agreement stipulated that amortization would begin in 1917 at a rate of £10,000 per year.

By 1904, the external debt amounted to £2,500,000, a figure that included £500,000 in arrears of interest; however, the service of this debt was suspended that year. Following the war with Chile, Bolivia held an indemnity debt due to Chilean citizens totaling 6,550,830 bolivianos. This debt was nearly liquidated in 1904 when Chile assumed the unpaid balance, leaving this as Bolivia's only foreign debt.

## Internal Debt and Treasury Issues
In 1904, the total internal debt was recorded at £815,000. By 1905, the internal debt amounted to 6,243,270 bolivianos (£546,286), which included 1,998,500 bolivianos in treasury bills. 

Additionally, the government authorized the issue of treasury notes in 1903 for the National Territory and the department of Beni, totaling one million bolivianos (£87,500). To ensure the redemption of these notes, 10% of the customs receipts from these two districts are set aside.

## National Revenue and Expenditure
Bolivian revenue is derived from several primary sources. Approximately half of the national revenue comes from customs. Other significant contributors include stamps, railways, and monopolies on tobacco and salt. More detailed records indicate that revenues are also drawn from:
* Duties and fees on imports.
* Export duties on minerals (excluding silver), rubber, and coca.
* Excise taxes on sugar, wines, spirits, and tobacco.
* General and mining taxes.
* Taxes on the profits of stock companies.
* Postal and telegraph revenues.
* Fees for patents and licenses.

While Bolivia is sometimes referred to as a "free-trade country" due to moderate nominal import duties (ranging from 10 to 40% ad valorem), this is considered a misnomer. Actual costs include a 1-1/2% consular fee for invoices exceeding 200 bolivianos, a consumption tax of 10 centavos per quintal (46 kilogrammes), fees for viseing certificates for merchandise in transit, and various "octroi" taxes on monopoly-controlled merchandise, alongside municipal and departmental taxes.

In the financial year 1904-1905, revenue was £503,000 against an expenditure of £390,000. Chief expenditures include official salaries, interest on the public debt, public instruction, justice, military expenses, subsidies, public works, and support for missions and the church. For 1905, specific appropriations were allocated as follows:
* War: 2,081,119 bolivianos.
* Government and fomento: 2,021,428 bolivianos.
* Justice and public instruction: 1,878,941 bolivianos.
* Finance and industry: 1,462,259 bolivianos.

## Currency and Coinage
The public debt does not include the republic's paper currency, as bank-notes are issued by four private banks. As of June 30, 1903, the official estimate of currency in circulation was 9,144,254 bolivianos (£800,122), issued on a par with silver.

The national coinage consists of copper, nickel, and silver. 
* **Copper:** 1 and 2 centavos.
* **Nickel:** 5 and 10 centavos.
* **Silver:** 5, 10, 20, 50 centavos, and 1 boliviano (100 centavos).

The Potosi mint produces approximately 1,500,000 bolivianos in silver coins annually, and silver mining companies are legally required to send 20% of their product to the mint. Because paper currency is cheaper, silver bolivianos are rarely seen in circulation. To prevent the exportation of silver coins, fractional denominations have been slightly debased.
