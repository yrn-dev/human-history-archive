# Kumasi

Kumasi (also spelled Coomassie) is the capital of Ashanti in British West Africa. Located at 6° 34´ 50´´ N., 2° 12´ W., the town is situated on a low rocky eminence extending across a valley to an opposite hill. It is positioned in a clearing within the dense forest that covers much of Ashanti. The town occupies an area approximately 1½ miles in length and over 3 miles in circumference. Its name, more correctly Kum-ase, means "under the okum tree," referring to the abundance of such trees in its streets. As of 1906, the population was 6,280, increasing to over 12,000 when including the suburbs.

## Geography and Climate
The land immediately surrounding Kumasi was once marshy but has since been drained. The small river Dah, a headstream of the Prah, lies to the north-west. Approximately 25 miles south-east of the town is Lake Busumchwi, the sacred lake of the Ashanti, which is nearly circular with a maximum diameter of 6 miles and is surrounded by forest-clad hills reaching 800 feet.

The elevation of the region generally remains below 2,000 feet, rising toward the north. The climate is less unhealthy than that of the West African coast towns, though it is considered unsuited for the prolonged residence of Europeans. The mean temperature in Kumasi is 76° F, with a mean annual rainfall of 40 inches. A rainy season characterized by frequent and violent storms lasts from the end of May until October.

## Infrastructure and Architecture
Kumasi is connected to the coast by a 141-mile wide road from Cape Coast and a railway line from Sekondi (168 miles). While the railway was cut through dense forest, other communications consist of narrow, tortuous forest paths unsuitable for wheeled vehicles. Ancient caravan routes lead from Kumasi to inland trading centers, and ferries are used to cross swamps and rivers. Travel through the bush is often conducted via palanquins borne by four carriers. The town is also linked to the Northern Territories and coast towns via telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service.

The most imposing structure in the town is the fort, built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner and can house a garrison of several hundred men. Other facilities include cantonments, officers' quarters, stations for the Wesleyan and Basel missions, and both native and European hospitals. Native houses are constructed from red clay in a style common to Ashanti, often featuring rich ornamentation and compounds for the better classes. Leading mercantile houses are located near the railway station, and some principal Ashanti chiefs own European-style houses which they lease to strangers.

## Early British Relations and Conflict
British missions to Kumasi began as early as 1817, when Frederick James, T.E. Bowdich, and W. Hutchinson were sent following Ashanti invasions of Fantiland. These negotiations were strained by the king's demands for quit rents for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort. Later, in 1820, Consul Joseph Dupuis arrived in Kumasi and signed a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory, though this was later disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith.

Hostilities escalated in 1824 when Sir Charles M'Carthy, the first crown-appointed governor, was defeated by the Ashanti at Essamako. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was subsequently used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. Following a signal defeat of the Ashanti at Dodowah in 1826, Ashanti power over coast tribes declined. By 1831, the king purchased peace from administrator George Maclean for 600 oz. of gold and provided his son as a hostage.

## 19th Century Wars and British Administration
In June 1869, an Ashanti force captured several Europeans, including Monsieur M.J. Bonnat and members of the Basel mission—Mr. J. Kuhne and Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer and his wife. These captives were taken to Kumasi. While negotiations for their release began, the transference of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872, sparked war, as the Ashanti viewed Elmina as their territory. On January 22, 1873, Ashanti forces invaded the British protectorate. In response, Sir Garnet Wolseley was dispatched as British administrator to march on Kumasi.

Following the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was named to the Ashanti throne. Captain Donald W. Stewart was installed as a British resident at Kumasi. While other confederacy states kept their kings, Kumasi was administered by chiefs under British guidance. During this period, a fort was built and garrisoned by the Gold Coast constabulary.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Despite an outward appearance of submission, Kumasi chiefs remained unreconciled to British rule. Tensions peaked on March 28, 1900, when Governor Sir F. Hodgson demanded an annual payment of 4,000 oz. of gold and the surrender of the "golden stool," which the Kumasi had hidden since 1896. This led to a rebellion involving the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes, who commanded a force reported at 40,000.

The rebels attacked a party of Hausa and later threatened the Kumasi fort, where Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, along with Mr. Ramseyer and other Basel missionaries, took refuge. The garrison was eventually reinforced by Gold Coast and Lagos constabulary, reaching a total of 700 men. 

The fort was eventually relieved on July 15, 1900, by Colonel James Willcocks. Leading a force of 1,000 West African fighting men, 60 white officers, and various artillery pieces (including Maxims and 75-millimetre guns), Willcocks fought through impenetrable bush and mire. After a bayonet charge by Yoruba troops against the main rebel stockade, the relieving column entered Kumasi to find the garrison exhausted and the town outside the fort consisting of putrid bodies and burnt-down houses.
