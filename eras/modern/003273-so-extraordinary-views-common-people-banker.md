# Banking Practices and Legal Frameworks

## History of Banking in Ireland
The development of banking in Ireland closely mirrored the process seen in England, where the circulation of notes preceded and fed deposits. The system was built gradually through the ready acceptance of notes, which attracted customers to bank counters. However, the early history of Irish banking was marked by significant instability. Sir Robert Peel, in a speech on June 9, 1845, cited a 1804 report from the committee of Irish exchanges stating that there were fifty registered banks at that time, all of which failed, resulting in "the most fearful distress."

Public perception of banking during these periods could be extreme. In one instance, a Dublin banker named Beresford became so unpopular that a large group of "ignorant country people" collected his notes and publicly burned them. As the promises to pay on demand were destroyed, the crowd shouted, "What will he do now; his bank will surely break." Despite this volatile history, legislation in 1845 led to a period of steadiness and success, bringing Irish banking to a footing equal to other parts of the United Kingdom.

## The Banker-Customer Relationship
The legal relationship between a banker and a customer is primarily that of debtor and creditor. While it is often suggested that a cheque is a prerequisite for withdrawing money, this is not the case; balances at a bank can be attached as unconditional debts via garnishee orders, as seen in *Rogers v. Whiteley* (1892). 

Beyond the debtor-creditor relationship, a banker is obligated to honor a customer's cheques, provided there is a sufficient and available balance. If a banker dishonors a cheque despite having sufficient funds, they are liable for substantial damages without the customer needing to prove actual injury (*Rolin v. Steward*). If multiple cheques are presented simultaneously and funds are insufficient for all, the banker is not required to discriminate and should pay as many as the funds cover.

## Cheque Processing and Customs
Bankers may refuse to pay cheques that are not drawn in a recognized form or those that raise doubts regarding validity. This includes cheques drawn to "petty cash or order" or "wages or order." While these are sometimes viewed as payable to bearer, some banks refuse them, and such a refusal is considered justified. Additionally, money deposited too shortly before a cheque's presentation to be passed through the bank's books is not treated as available for drawing.

The "custom of bankers" often informs legal interpretations. For example, the practice of refusing "stale" cheques—those presented an unreasonable time after their date—is common, though the definition of "stale" varies between six and twelve months across different banks. Similarly, the right of a banker to summarily debit a customer's account for a returned cheque is attributed to such customs. Any person entering business relations with a banker is presumed to implicitly agree to conduct business according to these general and reasonable customs.

## Banker's Liens and Overdrafts
A distinguishing feature of banking advances is the banker's lien, part of the law merchant. This allows a banker, unless otherwise agreed, to retain and apply any of the customer's securities that come into their possession as a banker to discharge the customer's liability. This includes cheques and bills paid in for collection (*Currie v. Misa*). However, the lien does not extend to money paid in for specific bills or goods deposited for safe custody.

Regarding overdrafts, a banker is under no obligation to permit a customer to overdraw unless there is an express or implied agreement based on the course of business. Drawing a cheque without sufficient funds is considered an implied request for an overdraft. Interest is chargeable on these overdrafts based on universal banking custom. Compound interest may be charged if the system is brought to the customer's attention via a pass-book and is not objected to.

## Liability, Fraud, and Collection
Bankers are generally not held responsible for payments made on a forgery of a customer's signature. However, if a customer accredit the forgery or fails to warn the banker after discovering it, the customer is estopped from disputing the debit (*Vagliano v. Bank of England*).

When collecting crossed cheques, bankers are granted qualified immunity under section 82, provided they meet specific conditions:
* They must receive the cheque from a customer with whom they have existing business relations.
* The cheque must already be crossed generally or specially to the banker.
* The banker must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care for the true owner. To avoid this, the banker must ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct and must not take a cheque for a private account if it indicates the holder is an agent or official. Protection is lost if the banker acts as the owner of the cheque rather than a "conduit pipe" for the customer. For instance, crediting cheques as cash before the money is actually received is considered taking them as an owner, debarring the bank from protection.

## Alternative Savings Associations
Outside of traditional banking, building and loan associations grew rapidly after 1840, numbering nearly 6,000 by the start of the 20th century. A 1893 U.S. Department of Labour investigation found that total dues paid on instalment shares reached $450,667,594. This business was conducted without experienced bankers in charge, suggesting that common people were competent in managing their own savings. The report noted that only thirty-five associations experienced a net loss in their latest fiscal year, totaling just over $23,000.
