# The Great Depression: The Global Economic Collapse of the 1930s

## Overview
The Great Depression was the most severe economic downturn in the history of the industrialized Western world. While often associated with the U.S. stock market crash of October 1929, the Depression was a complex global phenomenon resulting from a convergence of structural weaknesses in the post-WWI international economy. 

The crisis was characterized by a catastrophic collapse in GDP, skyrocketing unemployment rates (reaching nearly 25% in the U.S.), and a deflationary spiral that decimated agricultural prices and industrial production. In the United States, President Franklin D. Roosevelt attempted to stabilize the economy through the "New Deal," a series of federal programs aimed at relief, recovery, and reform. Globally, the crisis fueled political instability, contributing to the rise of totalitarian regimes in Europe and Asia as citizens sought strongman solutions to economic misery.

## Key Facts
*   **The Monetary Debate:** Historians and economists debate the primary cause of the Depression's longevity. Monetarists, such as Milton Friedman, argue that the Federal Reserve failed by allowing the money supply to contract, turning a typical recession into a depression. Conversely, Keynesian economists argue that a collapse in aggregate demand necessitated massive government spending to stimulate recovery.
*   **The Dust Bowl:** In North America, the economic collapse coincided with a severe ecological disaster. Poor farming practices and a decade-long drought created massive dust storms in the Great Plains, forcing thousands of "Okies" to migrate toward California.
*   **Global Interconnectedness:** The crisis spread via the Gold Standard. As countries attempted to protect their gold reserves by raising interest rates, they inadvertently choked off investment and trade, exporting the depression from the U.S. to Europe and Latin America.

## Did You Know?
1. **Bank Runs:** Before the creation of the FDIC in 1933, if a bank failed, depositors lost every cent of their savings, as there was no federal insurance for deposits.
2. **The "Hoovervilles":** Shanty towns built by homeless people were named "Hoovervilles" after President Herbert Hoover, whom many blamed for the government's perceived inaction.
3. **Agricultural Surplus:** Paradoxically, farmers often burned their crops or poured milk into ditches during the 1930s because the cost of transporting the food to market was higher than the price they could get for it.
