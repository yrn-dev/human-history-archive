# Bank of France

The Bank of France is described as the most powerful financial institution in France. Established in 1800 by Napoleon I with the assistance of his minister Mollien, the bank was created to supply loanable capital at a moderate charge and to support French industry and trade. Since its inception, it has functioned as a bank of deposit, a category of institution that receives deposits and makes loans to associate itself with a country's general industries and trade.

## Historical Development
Prior to the establishment of the Bank of France, the first bank of issue in the country was the *Banque Generale*, founded in 1716 by John Law. This institution, which Law associated with the *Systeme* and the *Mississippi Scheme*, was converted into the *Banque Royale* in 1718. Its notes were guaranteed by King Louis XV, but the bank ended in 1721. A subsequent attempt to reconstruct a bank in 1767 was suppressed in 1793. While other banks issued notes afterward, they lacked real power.

Following negotiations in 1796 that were hindered by financial difficulties, the Bank of France was founded in 1800. Initially, its operations did not cover the entire country. Under a law adopted in 1803, departmental banks with the privilege of issue were formed; by the end of 1847, nine such banks existed in larger towns. These were absorbed into the Bank of France in 1848, granting the Bank of France an exclusive privilege of issue. In 1863, the bank further expanded by taking over the Bank of Savoy following the union of that province with France.

## Operational Mechanisms
At the present time, the Bank of France operates chiefly through its enormous note circulation. Most business transactions in France are carried on by means of these notes, which are preferred over specie due to their convenience. This differs from the United Kingdom, where large transactions are typically settled by cheque.

The bank manages a variety of accounts. As of the recorded data, the bank held a total of 66,569 accounts, an increase from 59,182 at the end of 1903. These accounts are categorized as follows:
*   Current accounts with power of discount: 11,178
*   Simple current accounts: 4,576
*   Current accounts with advances: 26,709
*   Deposit accounts: 24,106

## Note Circulation and Reserves
The circulation of notes issued by the Bank of France has been extended over several decades. In 1848, the limit was 350 million francs (March 15) and 452 million francs (April 27 and May 2). By December 2, 1849, it reached 525 million francs. Significant increases occurred in 1870, rising to 1,800 million francs on August 12 and 2,400 million francs on August 14. The limit continued to grow to 2,800 million francs in 1871, 3,200 million francs in 1872, 3,500 million francs in 1884, 4,000 million francs in 1893, and 5,000 million francs in 1897. By 1906, the limit reached 5,800 million francs, which converts to £232,000,000 (at a rate of 25 francs to £1). In 1906, the average note circulation was £186,300,000.

The bank maintains a large specie reserve in its vaults, which in 1906 averaged £115,000,000 in gold and £42,000,000 in silver. This reserve is generally larger than that held by the Bank of England; for instance, during the Baring crisis in the autumn of 1890, the Bank of England borrowed £3,000,000 in gold from the Bank of France.

## Economic Impact
The large specie reserve provides stability to French trade and allows the bank to conduct business without the frequent fluctuations in the rate of discount seen in England. While maintaining such a large amount of specie imposes a heavy burden on the bank's shareholders, the resulting low rate of interest for borrowed capital serves as a significant advantage to the trade and industry of France.
