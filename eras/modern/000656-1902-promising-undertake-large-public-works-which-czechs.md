# Public Works and Political Conflict in Early 20th Century Austria

## The Koerber Administration and Public Works
Following the general election of 1901, the history of Austria was defined by efforts toward franchise reform to restore the normal working conditions of parliament. The premier, Dr. von Koerber, sought to overcome political obstruction and establish a compromise between Germans and Czechs. To induce the Chamber to sanction the contingent of recruits, estimates, and other "necessities of state" for 1901 and 1902, Koerber promised the undertaking of large public works of mutual interest to both Czechs and Germans.

These projects primarily focused on the development of canals, including:
* A canal from the Danube to the Oder.
* A ship canal from the Danube to the Moldau near Budweis, alongside the canalization of the Moldau from Budweis to Prague.
* A ship canal from the projected Danube-Oder canal near Prerau to the Elbe near Pardubitz, including the canalization of the Elbe from Pardubitz to Melnik.
* A navigable connection between the Vistula, the Dniester, and the Danube-Oder Canal.

The construction of these four canals was estimated to take twenty years, funded by a 4% loan amortizable over ninety years. Additionally, the cabinet and the Chamber sanctioned a "second railway route to Trieste." This line was designed to shorten the distance between Salzburg, South Germany, and the Adriatic by passing under the central and southern Austrian Alpine ranges, specifically boring tunnels through the Wochein, Karawanken, and Tauern hills. While the project was intended for completion by 1909 and is considered one of Europe's most interesting railway routes, the costs greatly exceeded parliamentary estimates. Some contend that the adoption of the 1901-1902 Budget cost the state £100,000,000 for public works that were often hastily begun due to political expediency.

## Ethnic and Linguistic Tensions in Bohemia
The struggle between Czechs and Germans was most vehement in Moravia and Bohemia, where the two populations were nearly equal. For twenty years, there had been a constant encroachment by Czechs on Germans, partly aided by government action. An 1880 ordinance required government offices and law courts to deal with business in the language in which it was introduced, granting Czech a position within the government service. In 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid frequent translations. Further steps included the founding of Czech elementary and middle schools, the introduction of Czech classes in German schools, and the 1882 start of Czech classes at the university of Prague.

Despite these linguistic concessions, the Czechs sought the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom. While older party leaders might have been satisfied with their influence, the "Young Czechs" were more impatient. In 1890, Taaffe attempted a reconciliation through conferences in Vienna involving the Feudal party, the German party, and the Czechs. An agreement was reached regarding a revision of the electoral law and the separation of German and Czech districts. However, the Young Czechs, who were not invited to the conference, opposed the measures on the grounds that they threatened the unity of the Bohemian country. By the 1891 elections, the Young Czechs were completely victorious.

## Parliamentary Obstruction and Governance
By December 1902 and January 1903, Dr. von Koerber's attempts to promote a compromise proved futile. He proposed dividing Bohemia into 10 districts (5 Czech, 3 German, and 2 mixed) and dividing 234 district tribunals (133 Czech, 94 German, and 7 mixed). The Czechs rejected this, demanding that German and Czech languages be placed on an equal footing for all official purposes throughout Bohemia. The Germans refused this as it would recognize Czech as a language of internal service.

Consequently, Koerber was unable to secure votes for the budgets of 1903, 1904, or 1905. He reverted to sanctioning estimates by imperial ordinance under paragraph 14 of the constitution. Until his fall on December 31, 1904, Koerber governed practically without parliament, summoning the Chamber only as a pretext for using paragraph 14. The Czechs employed noisy obstruction and "urgency motions," with one sitting lasting 57 hours without interruption. In response to this aggressiveness, German parties—including the Christian Socialists, the Constitutional Landed Proprietors, the German Populists, and the German Progressists—formed a supreme committee of four members and a joint executive committee to protect German racial interests.

## Regional Profiles: Budweis and Krumau
Within Bohemia, specific towns reflected these demographic and economic trends. Budweis (Czech: Bud[ve]jovice) served as the principal commercial center of South Bohemia and a river port at the junction of the Moldau and the Maltsch. By 1900, it had a population of 39,630 and a diverse industry including boat-building, chemicals, and beer exports to America. While it was a center for a German enclave, the Czech element increased, reaching 60% of the population by 1908.

Krumau (Czech: Krumlov), situated on the Moldau, had approximately 8,000 inhabitants of mixed Czech and German nationality. It was known for its ancient castle, the stronghold of the Rosenberg family. Peter I. of Rosenberg raised the location to a city by 1349, and later, Peter Vok of Rosenberg, an adherent of the Utraquist party, sold the town shortly before his death in 1611 due to the establishment of Jesuits in the area.
