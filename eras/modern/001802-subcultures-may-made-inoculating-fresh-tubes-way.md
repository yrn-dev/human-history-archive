# Bacterial Cultivation and Immunity

## Principles of Bacterial Cultivation
Cultivating bacteria outside the body requires the preparation of media that serve as food material. These media must be sterilized by heat and are designed to mimic the natural habitat of the organisms as closely as possible; for pathogenic bacteria, this means simulating the natural fluids of the body. Media can be prepared in fluid or solid forms. Solid media are created through a process of coagulation or by adding a gelatinizing agent, such as agar or gelatine. Examples of suitable media include blood serum solidified at a specific temperature or those based on meat extract, which may include non-coagulable proteids (commercial "peptone") to replace proteids lost during coagulation.

The reaction of the media must be carefully managed, typically remaining neutral or slightly alkaline. For delicate work, titration methods may be used to standardize the reaction. These media are stored in flasks and then placed in small flasks or glass test-tubes protected by cotton-wool plugs and sterilized by heat. Solid media are generally preferred because accidental contamination is more easily recognized and bacterial growth appears as a discrete mass.

## Inoculation and Maintenance
Cultures are established by using a sterile platinum wire to transfer a small amount of material containing bacteria into the medium. Once inoculated, the tubes are kept at suitable temperatures—usually 20°C (a warm summer temperature) or 37°C (the temperature of the body)—until growth is visible. To maintain these constant temperatures, regulating apparatuses known as incubators are used.

Growth can be maintained for an indefinite period through the creation of "subcultures," which are made by inoculating fresh tubes. When only one variety of bacterium is present, a "pure culture" can be obtained.

## Anaerobic Cultivation
The standard methods of cultivation apply to organisms that grow in the presence of oxygen. However, strictly anaerobic bacteria grow only in the absence of oxygen. To cultivate these, oxygen must be excluded. This can be achieved through "deep cultures," where inoculation occurs deep within a solid medium, particularly if the medium contains a reducing agent like glucose. For fluid cultures or the plate method, air is displaced by an indifferent gas, typically hydrogen.

## Immunity and Inoculation
Immunity is defined as non-susceptibility to a specific toxin, bacterium, or disease. It is a relative term that depends on present conditions; for example, an animal might be susceptible to a disease via experimental inoculation but not in nature if the necessary conditions of infection are absent. Immunity can be natural or acquired. Animals may acquire immunity by successfully passing through a disease attack or through various inoculation methods.

There are two primary varieties of artificial immunity:
*   **Active Immunity:** This is produced by a reaction or series of reactions in the animal's body, usually via injections of bacteria or their products. It is based on the principle that recovery from certain infective diseases provides protection against subsequent attacks. Immunity can be established by using properly adjusted doses of poison without causing symptoms of the disease.
*   **Passive Immunity:** This is produced by transferring serum from an actively immunized animal to a fresh animal. This method involves no active change in the tissues of the second animal and is the basis for serum therapeutics.

## Methods of Producing Active Immunity
Active immunity can be established through several methods:
1.  **Attenuated Virus:** Inoculation with bacteria whose virulence has been diminished. Attenuation can be achieved by growing the organism outside the body under unsuitable conditions, such as in the presence of weak antiseptics or at temperatures higher than the optimum. Some organisms lose virulence when grown on ordinary artificial media, a process that can be accelerated by passing air over the growth. Additionally, virulence may decrease when a bacterium is passed through the body of a different species.
2.  **Graduated Vaccines:** Immunity can be increased by using vaccines of increasing virulence, eventually reaching a high degree of immunity using "supervirulent" cultures attained through the method of passage.
3.  **Dead Bacteria:** Immunity against living organisms can be produced by the injection of the bacterium in a dead condition.

## Experimental Testing
To test the effects of bacteria, researchers typically use smaller rodents, such as mice, guinea-pigs, and rabbits. However, some animals are not susceptible to certain bacteria, or the disease manifests differently than in humans. For example, monkeys have been used for relapsing fever and Malta fever, but no lower animals have been found susceptible to leprosy.

For inoculation, young vigorous cultures are used, either as a fluid culture or mixed with an indifferent fluid. Injections are administered via hypodermic syringe into a vein, the subcutaneous tissue, serous sacs, or other specific body parts. Following injection, the animal is kept in favorable surroundings and monitored. Post-mortem examinations and microscopic analysis of affected tissues are then used to determine the relationship between the bacteria and structural changes.
