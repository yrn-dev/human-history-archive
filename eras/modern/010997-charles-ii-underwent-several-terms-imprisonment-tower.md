# Charles II

## Overview and Early Reign
Charles II was the English monarch who succeeded Charles I. During his time in power, he dealt with various internal and external conflicts, including the activities of the Covenanters in Scotland and the religious landscape of England and its colonies.

## Military and Political Administration in Scotland
In 1665, Thomas Dalyell returned to the service of Charles II. On July 19, 1666, Charles II appointed Dalyell as the commander-in-chief in Scotland. His primary objective was to subdue the Covenanters, a task he executed with great cruelty, leading to his name becoming a terror to the peasants. Following his victory at Rullion Green, Dalyell obtained several forfeited estates and was appointed a privy councillor on January 3, 1667.

Later, in June 1679, the duke of Monmouth was chosen as commander-in-chief. This decision incensed Dalyell; however, Charles II confirmed Dalyell in his original appointment. Despite this confirmation, Dalyell did not appear at Bothwell Bridge until after the engagement had closed.

## Religious Policy and the Baptist Church
The government of Charles II was characterized by intolerance toward certain religious groups, specifically the Baptists. In 1663, John Myles, a Welsh Baptist and former one of Cromwell's Tryers, fled to Massachusetts with his congregation to escape this intolerance. 

Even after the establishment of a church covenant by English Baptists in Boston in May 1665, the group faced severe persecution, including fines and the imprisonment of members. In July 1679, Charles II issued a mandate to the Massachusetts authorities. In this document, the king sharply rebuked the authorities for denying others the liberty to secure, noting that the Massachusetts authorities themselves had once gone into exile. This mandate, however, produced little effect.

## Relations with Advisors and Royalists
Charles II provided acquittals to those who had served his father, Charles I. One such individual was John Ashburnham, an English Royalist and former treasurer of the royal army. Ashburnham had been accused of treachery and disloyalty due to his role in placing Charles I in the hands of Robert Hammond, the governor of the Isle of Wight, following the king's escape from Hampton Court on November 11, 1647. While Clarendon viewed Ashburnham's actions as a result of folly and excessive self-confidence, both Charles I and Charles II acquitted him.

Additionally, Charles II maintained ties with George Villiers, the 2nd Duke of Buckingham. On September 19, 1649, Charles II conferred the Garter upon Buckingham, and on April 6, 1650, he admitted him to the privy council. Buckingham served as an advisor, though he often clashed with other advisors like Hyde. He accompanied Charles II to Scotland in June 1650 and was present with the king at the battle of Worcester on September 3, 1651.
