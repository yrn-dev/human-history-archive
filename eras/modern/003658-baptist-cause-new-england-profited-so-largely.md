# The Baptist Cause in New England

The Baptist movement in New England experienced significant fluctuations in growth and social standing, shaped largely by its relationship with the "standing order" and its response to major historical upheavals such as the Great Awakening and the War of Independence.

## Impact of the Great Awakening
The Baptist denomination profited more from the Great Awakening (1740–1743) than almost any other denomination. Prior to this period, the region had seen a time of religious declension characterized by a "Socinianized Arminianism" that hindered evangelistic efforts. This was evident in several prominent churches: the First Church in Providence had become Arminian, the First Church in Boston had become Socinianized, and the First Church in Newport had been divided by Arminianism.

During the Great Awakening, the "standing order" in many New England communities bitterly opposed the new evangelism. Those influenced by the revival felt compelled to organize "New Light" or "Separate" churches. These groups faced severe persecution from the dominant party and were denied the few privileges that Baptists had previously managed to secure. 

The transition from "Separate" churches to Baptist churches occurred primarily over the issue of membership and baptism. Because the "Separates" insisted on personal regeneration as a requirement for membership, many concluded it was inconsistent to administer baptism to unconscious infants. Consequently, several entire "Separate" churches determined that infant baptism lacked Scriptural warrant and viewed it as a cornerstone of "state-churchism," leading them to transform into Baptist churches.

## Leadership and Religious Liberty
The Great Awakening produced several key Baptist leaders who had previously been part of Congregationalism. Among them was Isaac Backus (1724–1806), who served as the historian of the denomination and became the champion for equality and religious liberty in New England. Other influential "New Light" evangelists who became Baptists included Shubael Stearns and Daniel Marshall (d. 1784). Their efforts were instrumental in multiplying Baptist churches and spreading Baptist principles throughout the southern colonies.

## The War of Independence and Decline
Despite the gains made during the Great Awakening, the Baptist cause failed to achieve similar success during the War of Independence. While a large portion of New England Baptists cooperated heartily with the cause of independence, the denomination as a whole failed to secure the popularity associated with successful leadership.

The "standing order" in New England represented the popular and patriotic party. In contrast, Baptists lost favor when they threatened to appeal to England for the redress of their grievances at a time when the colonies were determined to resist English oppression. This misalignment with the patriotic sentiment of the era resulted in a failure to secure religious liberty and a period of slow growth.

## Early Persecutions and Foundations
The history of the movement in the region was marked by early conflict with authorities. In 1649, a group of Baptists at Rehoboth, led by Obadiah Holmes, found the work impracticable due to the intolerance of the authorities and subsequently joined the Newport church. In 1651, Joseph Crandall, Obadiah Holmes, and Clarke visited Lynn, Massachusetts, where they were arrested for holding a meeting in a private house. They were imprisoned, fined, and maltreated for refusing to participate quietly in the services of the standing order. In 1652, Clarke published *Ill News from New England*, which provided a reasoned plea for liberty of conscience and detailed the proceedings against the brethren at Lynn.

Additionally, Henry Dunster (1612–1659), the first president of the college at Cambridge (Harvard), became convinced by 1653 that baptism should be reserved for "visible believers only." Due to this conviction, he relinquished his position in 1654 under conditions of considerable hardship.
