# BALLOT

The ballot is a modern method of secret-voting utilized in judicial, legislative, and political assemblies, as well as within the proceedings of corporations and private clubs. The term is derived from the Italian *ballotta* (a diminutive of *balla*, meaning a ball), referring to the historical practice of dropping a small ball into a specific receptacle to indicate a choice. In contemporary usage, the term applies to any system of secret-voting, regardless of whether a physical ball is used.

## Ancient and Classical Precedents
In ancient Athens, dicasts used metal (*sponduli*) or stone (*psephi*) balls to deliver verdicts. White or unpierced balls signified acquittal, while black or pierced balls signified condemnation. These were typically placed in a brass box, while a wooden box was used for unused balls. Secret-voting was also employed in the assembly for cases of *privilegia*, including the release of state-debtors, the naturalization of foreigners, and ostracism. Additionally, Syracuse practiced petalism, which involved voting via words written on olive-leaves.

At Rome, the ballot was introduced to the comitia through the *Leges Tabellariae*. These included the *Lex Gabiana* (139 B.C.) for the election of magistrates, the *Lex Papiria* (131 B.C.) for the repeal and enactment of laws, and the *Lex Cassia* (137 B.C.) for *judicia populi*. Romans used wooden *tabellae* placed in a wicker box called a *cista*. For proposed laws, the marks used were A. (*antiquo*) and U. R. (*uti rogas*); for public trials, D. (*damno*) and L. (*libero*). In elections, *puncta* were marked opposite the initials or names of candidates. Roman *judices* also used *tabellae* to express judgments using the letters N. L. (*non liquet*), C. (*condemno*), and A. (*absolvo*).

## European Applications
In Italy, the ballot was part of the free constitutional government introduced by Charles Albert in March 1848 in Piedmont, later extending to Italy in 1861. Under the law of 20th November 1859, voting for the Italian chamber of deputies occurs in public halls. Qualified voters present a certificate of inscription from the mayor to receive a stamped blue official paper (*bolletino spiegato*). The elector writes the name of a candidate—or has a confidential friend do so in exceptional cases—and hands the folded paper to the president of the bureau, who places it in the *urna* (box) and presides over the public *squittinio dei suffragi*.

Greece is the only European country that continues to use the ball-ballot. Voting takes place in churches, where each candidate has a box divided into "yes" (white) and "no" (black) sections. After citizenship or voting rights are verified, the voter receives a leaden bullet or ball for each candidate from a wooden bowl. The voter drops the ball into the chosen division via a funnel. While the vote is secret, the bullets are imitable, and there is no check to prevent "yes" votes for all candidates.

## Development in the United States
Early American elections were conducted *viva voce*, though several colonies adopted written or printed ballots. By 1775, ballots were used in Delaware, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, South Carolina, and the New England states. New Jersey adopted them in 1776 and New York in 1778. At the time of the U.S. Constitution's adoption, *viva voce* voting remained only in Georgia, Virginia, and Maryland. Later, Illinois (1848), Arkansas (1846), and Georgia and Maryland also adopted the ballot. Kentucky continued *viva voce* until 1819, and Virginia did not provide for the ballot until 1869. In Missouri, the ballot was introduced to some localities in 1845 and generally adopted by 1863.

Initially, ballots were written by voters or provided by candidates. Eventually, political committees prepared ballot-tickets for party-lists. This system made voting a mixed ticket difficult and compromised secrecy, as ballots were received near the polls, facilitating bribery and intimidation.

## The Australian Ballot and Reform
The "Australian ballot" was introduced in Canada following its adoption in Great Britain. In the United States, serious agitation for the system began in 1885. While New York and Michigan urged bills in 1887, Massachusetts enacted the first complete law in 1888. This statute required the state to print and distribute ballots containing the names of all candidates arranged alphabetically by office, which electors marked.

The movement for reform gained momentum after the 1888 presidential election, where it was alleged that large sums of money were used to purchase votes. In 1889, nine states enacted new laws: Montana, Tennessee, Missouri, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Indiana, Rhode Island, and Connecticut. The Connecticut law offered little improvement, providing only official envelopes for unofficial party ballots. Indiana, Missouri, and Michigan adopted a party-group system (or "blanket" ballot) where a single mark could vote for all candidates in a party-group. Other states followed the Massachusetts model of a blanket ballot arranged by office.

Further adoptions of the Australian system included Iowa (1892), Kansas and Alabama (1893), Virginia (1894), Florida (1895), and Utah and Louisiana (1896). In 1895, New York adopted a blanket ballot with candidates in party columns. Missouri was the only state to abandon the blanket ballot, returning to separate ballots in 1897 without providing booths for secret marking.
