# The Austro-Hungarian Monarchy and Related Regional Contexts

## Political Structure and History
The Austro-Hungarian monarchy, often referred to as the Dual Monarchy, was established in 1867 following the defeat of old Austria by Prussia at Sadowa in 1866. This period, along with the foundation of the new German empire in 1871, marked the beginning of Austro-Hungarian history properly so called. The polity functioned as a dual system, though this interpretation was later discredited by the introduction of universal suffrage in Austria and a crisis occurring between 1903 and 1907.

Foreign policy was generally peaceful and unambitious, maintaining a close connection with Germany. To manage national enmities and passions, discussions on foreign policy were conducted in Delegations rather than in parliaments. Key diplomatic figures included Count Kalnoky, who retired in May 1895 following a difference with the Hungarian premier, Banffy, and his successor, Count Goluchowski. In 1898, the Austrian premier, Count Thun, expressed irritation regarding the expulsion of Austrian subjects from Prussia during a period of Anti-Polish policy. The government focused on maintaining influence in the Balkan states through commercial treaties with Bulgaria, Servia, and Rumania, as well as the opening of the Danube for navigation and the construction of railways.

## Economic and Financial Systems
The monarchy operated as a single customs and commercial territory, which included the principality of Liechtenstein and Bosnia and Herzegovina. This was based on a customs and commercial agreement concluded in 1867 and renewed in 1899. Under this agreement, both states followed the same principles regarding commercial affairs, customs legislation, and duties on mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer.

The Austro-Hungarian Bank, founded in 1816 as the Austrian National Bank and renamed in 1878, held the exclusive legal right to issue bank-notes. Originally entirely Austrian in direction, it was converted into a joint-stock company by a 1899 statute with a stock of L8,780,000. Management was divided equally between Austria and Hungary, with head offices in both Vienna and Pest. By the end of 1900, the bank's accounts showed a capital of L8,750,000 and a note circulation of L62,251,000; by 1907, note circulation had risen to L84,501,000.

Currency reforms begun in 1892 aimed to substitute a gold standard for a silver standard (the gulden), redeem *Staatsnoten* (government notes issued during the 1866 war), and resume cash payments.

## Military Strength
As of 1905, the Austro-Hungarian army on a peace footing consisted of 20,564 officers and 336,818 men, supported by 64,887 horses and 1,048 guns. The force was divided between the Common Army, the Austrian Landwehr, and the Hungarian Honveds. The Common Army was the largest component, comprising 15,863 officers and 285,733 men. This total included 376 officers and 6,372 men stationed in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

## Regional Observations: Bucharest and Poland
In the capital of Rumania, Bucharest, the population in 1900 was 282,071. This population included 43,274 Jews and 53,056 aliens, the majority of whom were subjects of Austria-Hungary. The city, situated on the river Dimbovitza, underwent gradual reform starting in 1866 under Prince Charles, which included the installation of electric light, sewerage, and tramways.

In contrast, Poland was described as a poor, agricultural country that had not developed a Grand Monarchy. Its government was republican in nature, featuring an elected life-president (king). The Polish population consisted largely of a "savagely ignorant" peasantry and "great masses of very poor Jews."

## Post-Empire Transitions
Following the dissolution of the empire, the Republic of Austria assumed sole responsibility for war debt held outside the former empire. The Austro-Hungarian Bank was liquidated by the Reparation Commission. New governments in separated territories were required to stamp currency notes from the former bank and replace them with new currencies within twelve months. Additionally, Austria agreed to negotiate the return of artistic, scientific, or historic objects to their districts of origin if they formed part of the intellectual patrimony of those ceded districts.
