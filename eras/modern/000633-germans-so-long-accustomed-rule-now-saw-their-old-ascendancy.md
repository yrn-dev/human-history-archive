# The German Ascendancy and National Struggle

## Political Dominance and the Party of Centralization
For three hundred years, Germans held the position of rulers within the Austrian monarchy. This ascendancy was championed by the party of centralization, initially the Liberal German party—supported by some Ruthenes and Italians—which later evolved into the National German party. This group sought to maintain German rule through a common parliament and the spread of the German language via schools, aiming to create a shared Austrian nationality.

The National German party viewed the German language as the essential medium for communication between the monarchy's various races and insisted it remain the language of the parliament, the civil service, and the army. They believed that the ascendancy of the Germans was the only way to preserve the unity of the monarchy and emphasized a historic mission to bring German culture to the "half-civilized races of the east." Consequently, they demanded that universities and higher schools remain German and that elementary schools be Germanized, viewing the German schoolmaster as an "apostle of German culture."

## Threats to German Influence
The German population constituted less than one-third of the total population, meaning their rule required significant political ability. Their position was further complicated by internal divisions, specifically opposition from the Feudal party and the clergy.

The Germans viewed any grant of self-government to various territories as a threat that would diminish their influence and restrict the use of the German language. In Bohemia, they feared that full self-government would result in Germans becoming the "subject race." This prospect was considered intolerable, particularly as the national power of non-Austrian Germans was increasing and Germans were becoming the primary race in Europe.

## The Rise of Slav National Individuality
The decline of German ascendancy was evident in census data and election results, driven by the growth of Slav races. This growth was not solely due to government assistance but was influenced by economic factors, such as greater migration from the countryside to towns and a higher increase in the Czech population. Additionally, a nationalist and romantic movement beginning around 1830, combined with the introduction of parliamentary government and popular education, allowed these groups to reassert their national individuality.

The shift was stark in urban centers. Prague, once a German city, had only 36,000 Germans compared to 120,000 Czechs by the 1880 census. In Pilsen, where Germans held a majority in 1861, they made up less than a quarter of the population by 1880. This occurred despite the fact that the generation demanding national rights had been educated in German schools.

## Defensive Measures and Political Conflict
As their ascendancy was threatened, Germans defended it with increasing energy. In 1880, they established the *Deutscher Schulverein*, a society dedicated to assisting and establishing German schools. This organization achieved unprecedented success for a private society in Austria, reaching 100,000 members and an income of nearly 300,000 gulden.

Political efforts to secure the language were also pursued in the Reichsrath. German Liberal parties introduced a motion to declare German the state language and regulate the recognition of other idioms; however, this motion and a subsequent 1886 bill failed to progress.

In Bohemia, where Germans had been in a minority since 1882, they proposed dividing the country into two parts: one where German was the sole language and another where Czech was recognized. This proposal was rejected without discussion on a motion by Prince Karl Schwarzenberg. The Czechs opposed the plan because it contradicted their policy of the "indissoluble unity of the Bohemian kingdom," where German and Czech were to be equal and parallel languages. Following this rejection, the Germans left the diet, imitating an action previously taken by the Czechs when the latter had been the minority.

## Historical Context of Disunity
The history of German political cohesion has been marked by periods of instability. Following the death of Emperor Frederick II, the last of the Hohenstaufens, Germany fell into disunion, leading to a period known as the German interregnum. This ended in 1273 with the election of Rudolf of Habsburg as the first Habsburg Emperor.

Later, during the 16th century, the Emperor faced difficulties uniting German princes against the Turks, who had killed the king of Hungary in 1526 and nearly took Vienna in 1529. Internal struggles further hindered unity; Protestant princes formed the Schmalkaldic League to break away from Rome. This era was characterized by a "sanguinary irrational bickering of princes" for ascendancy, a cycle of intrigue and war that continued to desolate Central Europe into the nineteenth century.
