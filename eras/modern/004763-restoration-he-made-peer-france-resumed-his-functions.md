# Peers of France During and After the Restoration

The designation of peer of France was a high dignity granted to various statesmen, military leaders, and courtiers during the period of the Bourbon Restoration and the subsequent reign of Louis Philippe. The appointment of peers often reflected the shifting political landscape of France as it transitioned from the Napoleonic era through multiple restorations.

## Appointments During the Bourbon Restoration

Following the fall of Napoleon, several individuals were elevated to the peerage as part of the restored monarchy's administration. Henri Evrard, the marquis de Dreux-Brézé (1762–1829), who had served as master of the ceremonies to Louis XVI in 1781, was made a peer of France at the Restoration. In this capacity, he resumed his duties as the guardian of an antiquated ceremonial.

Other figures who gained the peerage during this era included François, marquis de Barthelemy (1747/50–1830). A former member of the Directory who had been deported to French Guiana, Barthelemy returned to France and eventually abandoned Napoleon in 1814. He participated in the creation of the constitutional charter and was named a peer of France. After the second Restoration, he obtained the title of marquis and, in 1819, proposed a motion in the chamber of peers to make the electoral law more aristocratic.

Marmont was also made a peer of France and a major-general of the royal guard upon the restoration of the Bourbons, though he remained distrusted by his countrymen. Similarly, the philosopher and politician Louis Gabriel Ambroise, vicomte de Bonald (1754–1840), was made a peer in 1823 after serving as a deputy and minister of state. However, Bonald lost this dignity in 1830 due to his refusal to take the oath.

## Peers of the Napoleonic Era and the Hundred Days

Some individuals were granted the peerage during the brief return of Napoleon known as the Hundred Days. Carnot, a general of division and governor of the fortress of Antwerp, was made a peer of France on June 2, 1815. However, following the second Restoration, Carnot was proscribed and spent the remainder of his life in Magdeburg.

## Appointments Under Louis Philippe

Following the revolution of 1830, the government of Louis Philippe continued the practice of appointing peers, often including those who had been exiled or sidelined during the Bourbon Restoration.

Joseph Dominique, Baron Louis (1755–1837), a financier and former minister of finance under both the Bourbons and Louis Philippe, was made a peer of France in 1832. Similarly, the duc de Bassano (Maret), who had served as Napoleon's private secretary and had been exiled after the second restoration of the Bourbons, was made a peer of France by Louis Philippe after the 1830 revolution.

Military figures also received the honor during this period. Dumas, who had served as intendant-general of the army during the Russian campaign of 1812 and had been excluded from the council of state in 1822, was created a peer of France after taking an active part in the revolution of 1830. Additionally, Baron Jean Baptiste Antoine Marcelin de Marbot (1782–1854), a soldier who had been exiled at the second restoration, was made a member of the Chamber of Peers in 1845. He remained in this position until the fall of Louis Philippe, at which point he retired from private life.
