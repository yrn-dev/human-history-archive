# Spurious Memoirs and Forgeries in Historical Records

The archive's records indicate several instances where memoirs, letters, and literary works published under the names of historical figures have been identified as spurious or forgeries. These occurrences span various eras and genres, ranging from political memoirs and royal correspondence to lyrical poetry.

## Spurious Political and Personal Memoirs

Certain published memoirs attributed to historical figures have been deemed fraudulent. The *Memoirs* published under the name of the individual associated with the works of Prince de Ligne (*Memoire sur le comte de Bonneval*, 1817) and A. Vandal (*Le Pacha Bonneval*, 1885) are noted as spurious. Similarly, the *Memoires de Billaud-Varenne ecrits a Port-au-Prince*, published in Paris in 1821 in two volumes, are considered probable forgeries.

In the case of the French historian Pierre Emmanuel Albert Ducasse (1813–1893), some of his edited works are viewed with skepticism. Ducasse compiled *Les Rois freres de Napoleon* (1883) and the *Journal de la reine Catherine de Westphalie* (1893) from papers belonging to the imperial family. While these publications provided insight into Napoleon I and his entourage, they were edited with little care and are not considered entirely trustworthy. Furthermore, Ducasse's *Souvenirs d'un officier du 2^e Zouaves* and *Les Dessous du coup d'etat* (1891) are described as containing piquant anecdotes that occasionally degenerate into "mere tittle-tattle."

## Forgeries of Royal Correspondence

The historical record of Marie Antoinette has been significantly impacted by the insertion of forgeries into various collections. A large number of these fraudulent documents appeared in the works of F. Feuillet des Conches (*Louis XVI., Marie Antoinette et Madame Élisabeth, lettres et documents inédits*, 1864–1873) and Paul Vogt d'Hunolstein (*Correspondance inédite de Marie Antoinette*, 1864). Consequently, most works regarding Marie Antoinette published prior to the publications of Arneth in 1865 were based, in part, on these forgeries.

To address these issues of authenticity, detailed examinations were provided in the appendix of A. Geffroy's *Gustave III. et la cour de France* (1869) and in the introduction to the *Lettres de Marie Antoinette. Recueil des lettres authentiques de la reine* (1895–1896), published by M. de la Rocheterie and the marquis de Beaucourt.

## Spurious Literary and Academic Works

Spurious attributions are also prevalent in classical and lyrical poetry. In the *Canzoniere* of Dante, many poems bearing the poet's name are identified as spurious. Some of these works actually belong to Dante de Maiano, who utilized a harsher style. Others attributed to Dante are referable to his sons, Pietro or Jacopo, or his grandsons, while some are ascribed to predecessors and contemporaries such as Cino da Pistoia. A collection by Fraticelli highlights this disparity, containing seventy-eight genuine poems, eight doubtful ones, and fifty-four spurious entries.

In the field of epigraphy, Georg Friedrich Grotefend (1775–1853) wrote a preface in 1836 for Wagenfeld's translation of the *Sanchoniathon* of Philo Byblius. This work was identified as spurious and had been alleged to have been discovered in 1835 at the Portuguese convent of Santa Maria de Merinhao.

## Controversial Authorship

Some historical documents occupy a middle ground where authorship is a matter of controversy rather than outright forgery. An example is the *Memoires secrets pour servir a l'histoire de la Republique des Lettres*, a register of news and scandals from the *salon* of Madame Doublet de Persan. While the name of Louis Petit de Bachaumont (1690–1771) is commonly connected with the first volumes of this 36-volume set (published 1774–1779), his exact share in the authorship remains a subject of controversy.
