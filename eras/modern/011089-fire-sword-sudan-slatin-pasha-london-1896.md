# Fire and Sword in the Sudan

*Fire and Sword in the Sudan* is a work authored by Slatin Pasha and published in London in 1896. The text serves as a primary source of information regarding the history and geography of the Sudan, with a particular focus on the region of Darfur and the political upheavals involving the Mahdi.

## Content and Regional Information
The first ten chapters of the work provide extensive details concerning the country of Darfur and its history. Slatin Pasha offers a full account of the overthrow of Egyptian authority by the Mahdi. The book is cited as a key resource for understanding the region, joining the accounts of other travelers and explorers such as William George Browne, who visited Kobbe between 1793 and 1795; Sheik Mahommed-el-Tounsi, who traveled through Darfur in 1803; and Gustav Nachtigal, who spent several months there in 1873.

## The Mahdi and the Khalifa
The text provides personal sketches of the khalifa Abdulla, the successor to the mahdi. The khalifa is described as a man of great energy and iron will who possessed military skill. While he was noted for being tyrannical, impatient of opposition, and appearing to delight in cruelty, these traits were framed by the necessity of overawing the various tribes of the Nile valley, who were often hostile to him.

In his public life, the khalifa maintained that the power he inherited from the mahdi was divine, and he used this to inspire his followers to perform acts of valour. Slatin Pasha's accounts note that the khalifa possessed a marked vanity, which was ministered to by the presence of European captives in Omdurman. Although he treated many of these captives with terrible severity, he never executed any of them. In contrast to his public persona, the khalifa showed significant affection for his family in his private life.

## The Ashraf and the Mahdist State
The work is also a source for information regarding the Ashraf (or Sherifs). In the context of the period between 1885 and 1898, under the rule of the khalifa Abdulla, "Ashraf" was the name used to describe the adherents and the family of the mahdi. The family of the mahdi claimed to be Ashraf, a title applied to descendants of the prophet Mahomet (who call themselves Beni Hashin). This is distinct from the Ashraf of Tokar, a small tribe of African "Arabs" settled in the valleys of the Baraka and Gash and the Amarar country north of Suakin, who remained loyal to Egypt during the troubles in the Sudan.

## Historical Context of the Region
The events described in Slatin Pasha's work occur against a backdrop of shifting colonial and local powers. Kordofan, for example, remained under Egyptian rule until 1882, when Mahommed Ahmed, the mahdi, led the country into revolt. This revolt saw the annihilation of the army of Hicks Pasha in November 1883. The Baggara of Kordofan became the primary supporters of the mahdi, and the khalifa Abdulla himself was a Baggara. The khalifa eventually met his death in Kordofan in 1899, after the region had passed to the new Sudan government.
