# Austria

## Early History and Foundation
The foundation of Austria is attributed to Otto the Great, who was elected German king in 936. Following a victory over the Magyars on the Lechfeld in August 955, Otto freed Bavaria from their presence and refounded the East Mark to defend his kingdom. In 976, Emperor Otto II entrusted the government of this mark—which would become known as Austria—to Leopold of the Babenberg family.

The Babenbergs ruled Austria until their family became extinct in 1246. Under their administration, the mark grew in importance among the German states. The Babenbergs expanded the eastern frontier down the Danube valley through colonization, moving their capital from Poechlarn to Melk, then to Tulln, and finally to Vienna around 1140. In 1156, the district between the Inn and the Enns was added to the mark, and King Frederick I raised Austria to the rank of a duchy. This status included exceptional privileges: the investiture was granted to Duke Henry and his second wife, Theodora, and the duchy could descend to females if male heirs failed. The duke's primary obligations to the Empire were to send a contingent to the imperial army for campaigns in bordering countries and to attend any diet held in Bavaria. Further expansions occurred in 1192, when Styria and upper Austria were brought to the Babenbergs via a treaty made by Duke Leopold I in 1186, and in 1229, when Duke Leopold II became lord of Carniola after purchasing lands from the bishop of Freising.

## The Ausgleich and Commercial Policy
In the dual state of Austria-Hungary, financial and economic reforms were managed through a general settlement known as the "Ausgleich" (compact or compromise), which occurred every ten years. This settlement included the determination of the Quota and functioned as a bargain where concessions in commercial policy were often offset by gains in financial agreements. For example, in 1877, Austria granted Hungary a share in the management of the bank, while Austria benefited from the arrangement for paying the bonus on exported sugar.

The Customs Union, established in 1867, was a central part of these periodical agreements. Initially, the customs tariff was based on commercial treaties with nations such as Great Britain, France, Italy, and Germany. These treaties included "most favoured nation" clauses and specified maximum duties, resulting in a system that nearly lacked customs duties. However, after 1873, Austrian manufacturers—facing competition from Belgian, English, and German goods—pushed for protective duties, reflecting a broader reaction against free trade doctrines.

## Trade Disputes and Tariff Shifts
Hungary initially favored free trade because it lacked significant manufacturing industries and required secure markets for its agricultural produce. When commercial treaties expired after 1875, Hungary gave notice to terminate the commercial union with Austria. Negotiations in 1877 resulted in a new treaty where raw materials remained duty-free, but low duties were placed on corn and textile goods, and excises on brandy and sugar were increased. Additionally, all duties were required to be paid in gold, which led to a considerable increase.

During this period, treaties with France and Great Britain were not renewed, and agreements with Germany failed due to the policy changes of Bismarck. This allowed Austria-Hungary to introduce tariffs based on legislation. Internal political strife complicated these matters; in 1877, many members of the Austrian parliament voted against essential duties on petroleum and coffee. This opposition, along with disputes over the treaty of Berlin and the occupation of Bosnia, forced Austrian ministers and Andrassy to seek support from the Poles, who subsequently became a government party.

## Financial Settlements and Later Agreements
The process of reaching the Ausgleich was often fraught with difficulty due to national jealousy and race hatred within the two parliaments. The first settlement took two years, from 1876 to June 1878. Disputes often arose over specific burdens; for instance, because increased duties on petroleum and coffee fell more heavily on Austria, the Austrians sought to persuade Hungary to pay a larger quota of common expenses. There was also a dispute regarding whether Hungary was responsible for a bank debt of 80 million gulden.

Later agreements included the renewal of the Bank charter until 1910, providing Hungarians with a larger share in its direction. While the Customs Union based on reciprocal treaties lapsed, both parties continued it in practice until December 31, 1907. In November of a subsequent settlement, the Quota-Deputations agreed that Hungary would pay 33-3/4%, a small increase. Because these arrangements could not be procured in the Austrian parliament, they were proclaimed by imperial warrant.

## Post-War Obligations
Following the World War, Germany was required to deliver to the allied and associated powers all sums deposited in Germany by Austria-Hungary and Turkey. Germany was also tasked with transferring all claims against Austria-Hungary, Bulgaria, or Turkey related to wartime agreements to the Allies. Furthermore, upon request from the Reparations Commission, Germany was to expropriate any rights or interests of its nationals in public utilities located in Austria-Hungary (as well as Turkey, China, Russia, and Bulgaria) and transfer them to the Commission.
