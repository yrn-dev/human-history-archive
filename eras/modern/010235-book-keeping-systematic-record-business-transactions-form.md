# BOOK-KEEPING

Book-keeping is the systematic recording of business transactions in a form that is conveniently available for reference. It is utilized by corporations and individuals engaged in financial or commercial operations to ascertain, with minimal dislocation to the business, the detailed particulars of transactions and their cumulative effect on the business and its financial relations with others. Described as both an art and a science, book-keeping is an evolutionary growth rather than a discovery, with crude early methods gradually improving to meet changing business requirements.

## Core Objectives and Verification
The ideal of any book-keeping system is to achieve the maximum amount of record with the minimum amount of labor. However, because systems must guard against dishonesty, an adequate system must allow records to be verified as complete and true statements of transactions. This verification process is known as an audit. In large or public concerns, professional accountants typically perform audits. In organizations with large book-keeping staffs, a "staff audit" or "internal check" may be performed by the staff itself, as the organization is often structured so that members check each other's work.

## The Ledger and Subsidiary Books
The ledger serves as the center of all book-keeping systems. Other books are maintained for practical convenience and are termed "subsidiary books." Because transactions are first recorded in these subsidiary books before being classified into the ledger, they are also called "books of entry" or "books of first entry." Books used solely for statistical purposes that do not form the basis for ledger entries are called "auxiliary" or "statistical books."

In early book-keeping, the ledger was not a complete record but contained only accounts deemed necessary to keep accessible, such as "personal accounts" for transactions with other business houses. Initially, transactions reducing indebtedness were recorded by date underneath those creating the debt, with the reduction subtracted from the total. This was eventually replaced by the use of a "contra account" to record transactions that extinguished or reduced debt. These two accounts were placed on opposite sides of the ledger, leading to the development of the "Dr." (debit) and "Cr." (credit) accounts. In a personal account, benefits received by a person from the business (creating indebtedness) are recorded on the left-hand (Dr.) side, while benefits imparted by that person (creating a liability for the business) are recorded on the Cr. side.

## Evolution of Entry Systems
While small businesses may use a single "journal" to record all transactions as they occur, larger modern businesses find this impracticable. This has led to two primary methods of organization:
1. **Intermediate Journals:** Common in continental Europe, where a journal acts as an intermediate book to focus transactions before they are recorded in the ledger.
2. **Multiple Books of First Entry:** Common in North America and Great Britain, where specific journals are used for different classes of transactions. These include:
    * **Sales journal (sales book or day book):** For goods sold.
    * **Bought journal (invoice book or purchases book):** For goods purchased.
    * **Returns inwards book:** For goods sold but returned by customers.
    * **Returns outwards book:** For goods purchased but returned.
    * **Bills receivable book:** For bills of exchange received from debtors.
    * **Bills payable book:** For bills of exchange given to creditors.

## Specialized Systems and Modern Adaptations
Various modifications have been introduced to increase speed and reduce clerical work:
* **Tabular Book-keeping:** This replaces usual forms with books ruled with numerous columns. In books of first entry, this allows one book to handle multiple transaction classes (such as separate columns for different trading departments). In ledgers, it allows many personal accounts to be recorded on a single opening if entries per individual are few.
* **The Slip System:** This involves creating a facsimile duplicate of an original entry (such as an invoice) using a carbon sheet. These movable slips can be sorted and handled separately before being posted to the ledger. In some cases, the filed slips themselves constitute the ledger record.
* **Card and Loose-leaf Ledgers:** Card ledgers place each account on an independent sheet of cardboard in cabinets, allowing "dead matter" to be eliminated from the active record.

## Accounting Principles and Methods
Historically, book-keeping was limited to accounts of receipts and payments of money, or the receipt and issue of goods before money existed. This "cash system" is still used by charitable institutions, local authorities, and governments. However, businesses must record movements of credit. While customers are usually charged at the date of sale, other expenses, such as wages, are often only recorded when paid. Consequently, to find actual expenditure for a period, one must adjust for payments made for previous work and add the value of unpaid work performed in the current period.

The principle of double-entry provides a complete record of every transaction. Since transactions are cross-dealings involving the simultaneous receipt and imparting of a benefit, an equal amount must be entered on both the debit and credit sides of the ledger. A "trial balance" is a list of all ledger balances used to ensure total debits equal total credits. However, the trial balance is a mechanical test and does not prove absolute accuracy; it cannot detect omitted transactions, records entered into the wrong accounts, incorrect monetary values, or fictitious entries.

## International Legal Requirements
Book-keeping practices are influenced by national legislation:
* **France:** The Code of Commerce requires a journal, an inventory, and a letter book.
* **Germany:** Accounts must be kept in bound books with consecutively numbered pages, and a balance sheet must be drawn up annually.
* **Belgium:** Merchants must keep a journal and balance book initialed by a prescribed officer; letters and telegrams must be preserved for ten years.
* **Spain:** The Commercial Code requires an inventory, journal, ledger, letter book, and invoice book.
* **Portugal:** Requires a balance book, journal, ledger, and copy-letter book.
* **Holland:** Requires records of commercial transactions, letters received, copies of letters sent, and an annual balance sheet.
