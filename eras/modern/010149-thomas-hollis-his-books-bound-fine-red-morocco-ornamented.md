# Thomas Hollis and the Art of Bookbinding

## The Bindings of Thomas Hollis
Thomas Hollis utilized fine red morocco for the binding of his books. These volumes were ornamented with small, well-cut stamps that were engraved by the medallist Thomas Pingo. The specific imagery included in these stamps consists of a figure of Britannia, a figure of liberty, a cap of liberty, and several smaller stamps.

## Development of Gold Tooling and Morocco
The use of morocco and gold tooling represents a significant evolution in the history of bookbinding. Following the introduction of printing to Europe in the middle of the 15th century, there was an initial reaction against the precious covers of illuminated manuscripts. Early printed books were often considered ugly and were generally bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf, using large panel stamps in blind.

Shortly thereafter, the art of gold tooling on leather emerged, likely brought to Venice from the East. The finest examples of this craft are found in Italian work from the late 15th century. In England, Thomas Berthelet, who served as the Royal Binder to Henry VIII and worked until approximately 1558, is believed to be the first binder to practice this art. Berthelet's early work displayed a strong Italian influence, suggesting he may have been trained by an Italian master.

## English Binding Styles and Traditions
English binding evolved through various distinct styles across several centuries:

*   **Tudor and Jacobean Periods:** During the 15th and 16th centuries, many fine printed books were bound in satin and velvet, some of which were embroidered or set with enamels. Royal books were frequently ornamented with heraldic designs, a fashion believed to have started under Edward IV. In the 17th century, the Jacobean style was characterized by heavy corners and semis of small stamps. While much fine work of this era was done in morocco, Henry, prince of Wales, preferred his books bound in calf.
*   **The 17th Century:** Notable specialized work occurred at Little Gidding, where an establishment founded by Nicholas Ferrar produced magnificent bindings in velvet stamped with silver and gold. Mary Collet and her sisters created harmonies of the Gospels and other Bible parts, using stamps identical to those of the Cambridge university printers, John and Thomas Buck.
*   **The 18th Century:** The middle of the century saw the emergence of the "Harleian style," used by Eliot and Chapman for the library of Robert Harley, earl of Oxford. These bindings featured morocco with diamond-shaped centre-pieces and broad, richly gold-tooled borders. Toward the end of the century, Roger Payne introduced the "straight grain" method of graining morocco and utilized richly gold-tooled corner-pieces and ornamental inside linings known as "doublures."

## International Influences and Notable Collectors
Beyond England, various European collectors and binders influenced the craft. In France, the Deromes family were prominent binders; Nicholas Denis ("Le Jeune") was celebrated for his "dentelles a l'oiseau," which featured birds interspersed among arabesque borders. Jean Charles le Monnier was noted for his skill with delicate coloured leather inlays, often signing his work in gold small capitals.

Italian collectors also left a mark on the archive of binding. Jean Grolier, the treasurer of France in 1545, collected books bound in pale brown morocco with gold tooling. Similarly, Demetrio Canevari, a physician to Pope Urban VIII, used deep red or dark green morocco featuring a coloured cameo stamp of Apollo driving a chariot with one black and one white horse toward a mountain with a silver Pegasus.
