# Timber

Timber production and forest growth vary significantly across different global regions, with specific species driving local economies, shipbuilding, and industrial manufacturing.

## North American Forests
In certain provinces of Canada, forest growth is rich, creating a steady demand for lumber in South America, Africa, Australia, China, and other Canadian regions. The Douglas fir is the primary timber of commerce, reaching heights of 300 feet and diameters of 8 to 10 feet; its tough, strong wood is highly valued for building and ships' spars. Other significant species include the red or giant cedar—used for interior work and shingles—and the western white spruce. The region's diverse flora also includes various spruces (white, Engellmann's, and Menzies's), pines (white mountain, black, and yellow), as well as western larch, red alder, large leaved maple, western white oak, giant cedar, yellow cypress or cedar, and western hemlock. In 1904, a total of 348,031,790 feet of timber was cut, with 325,271,568 feet from provincial lands and 22,760,222 feet from Dominion government lands. By 1905, the provincial cut increased to 450,385,554 feet.

In the United States, some forests are considered the finest of their kind remaining. In 1884, Professor C. S. Sargent estimated that long-leaf and short-leaf pines aggregated 26,558 million and 21,625 million feet, respectively. The value of timber products rose from $1,764,640 in 1880 to $17,294,444 in 1900, and further to $35,192,374 by 1905. The eastern pine belt, covering about 3,900 sq. m., consists of long-leaf pine and some loblolly. The south-western pine belt, covering about 4,200 sq. m., contains the world's heaviest growth of long-leaf pine. Short-leaf growth is particularly heavy in the north-western portion of the state. Additionally, the south of the state contains some of the largest and most heavily timbered cypress forests known, while hard-woods are found in river bottoms.

## British India and Burma
The flora of India is largely determined by climatic humidity. Dense, impenetrable forests are found in eastern Bengal, the Malay peninsula, Ceylon, and the west coast of the Indian peninsula, contrasting with the loosely-timbered drier regions of the north-western Himalaya and central India. The Tarai jungles at the foot of the eastern Himalaya feature luxuriant growth. In the administrative division of Kumaon, the uncleared forest tracts are under official supervision, featuring trees such as fir, alder, cypress, saindan, sal (iron-wood), and the chir (three-leaved Himalayan pine). In the feudatory state of Bamra in Bengal, most of the country is forest, which produces lac and timber.

Burma possesses some of the finest forests in British India and is the world's main source of teak for shipbuilding. Teak has supplanted oak in iron ships because it contains an essential oil that preserves iron and steel, whereas the tannic acid in oak causes corrosion. Due to its value, the government strictly preserves Burmese forests through a forest department that manages conservation, the planting of young trees, and the prevention of fires. While teak provides the bulk of revenue, other valuable woods include padauk, india rubber, sha (cutch), and pyingado (ironwood), the latter of which is used for railway sleepers. In Lower Burma, Sulpiz Kurz enumerated some 1,500 species in 1877.

## Other International Regions
In the Argentine Republic, the north-eastern corner of the Corrientes province is heavily forested. The town of Esquina exports firewood and timber from the nearby forest of Payubre. In Rumania, the city of Bacau serves as a commercial center in Moldavia with many large timber yards, benefiting from access to the forests of the Carpathians. In Asia Minor, the province of Bigha exports oak and pine timber through the port of Dardanelles.

## Specific Tree Characteristics: The Beech
The beech is characterized by a smooth olive-grey bark and a sturdy pillar-like stem often reaching 15 to 20 feet in girth. Its branches rise vertically with subsidiary branches spreading outwards to create a rounded outline. The tree produces inconspicuous flowers in May; male flowers hang in globular clusters from the lower leaves, while female flowers stand erect near the top. The resulting fruit consists of two or three chestnut-brown seeds enclosed in a tough, brown, four-sided cupule. While the beech's leaves enrich the soil, the tree is injurious to plants under its drip, causing shade-bearing trees like thuja, yew, and holly to suffer. Its timber is not noted for durability or strength; while formerly used in turnery and mill-work, it is currently used for bedsteads, chairs, and other minor articles. It is also used for charcoal and fuel.
