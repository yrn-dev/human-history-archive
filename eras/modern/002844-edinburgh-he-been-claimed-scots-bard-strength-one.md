# Edinburgh

Edinburgh serves as a primary center for Scottish legal, literary, and historical activity, hosting numerous influential publications, academic institutions, and professional bodies.

## Literary and Publishing History
The city has been a significant hub for the publication of Scottish poetry and prose. Robert Burns's *Poems chiefly in the Scottish Dialect* saw an enlarged edition printed in Edinburgh in 1787, followed by another enlarged two-volume edition in 1793 and a reprint in 1794. Burns's work also appeared in several Edinburgh-based publications, including *The Edinburgh Herald*, *The Edinburgh Advertiser*, *The Edinburgh Evening Courant*, and *The Caledonian Mercury*, as well as the *Edinburgh Magazine*.

The city is also associated with the *Edinburgh Review*. In January 1808, this publication featured an article by Brougham that criticized the author of *Hours of Idleness* and "his poesy." This critique prompted the author to write rhyming couplets on "British Bards," which eventually became the poem *English Bards and Scotch Reviewers*, published on March 1, 1809. The poem included a satire directed at Lord Holland, a patron of the *Edinburgh Review*, and its editor, Jeffrey.

Other notable literary figures connected to the city include Henry Glassford Bell (1803–1874), who was educated at Edinburgh University. Bell served as the editor of the *Edinburgh Literary Journal* starting in 1828, a publication that was later incorporated into the *Edinburgh Weekly Chronicle*.

## Legal and Academic Institutions
Edinburgh is a center for the study and practice of law. Bell's *Principles* of Scots Law (10th ed., 1899) was published in the city. The city's academic and professional landscape is further represented by Edinburgh University and the Advocates' library. The latter preserves the "Bannatyne Manuscript," a collection of Middle Scots poetry by 15th and early 16th century poets such as Alexander Scott, Lyndsay, Dunbar, and Henryson. This manuscript was originally compiled by George Bannatyne, an Edinburgh merchant and burgess (admitted 1587) who had retired to his native county in 1568 during a "pest" outbreak in the capital.

## Historical Events and Figures
The city has been the site of significant royal and political events. On August 8, 1503, the marriage of James IV of Scotland and Margaret, the eldest daughter of Henry VII of England, was celebrated in Edinburgh. 

The city is also the place of death for Balnaves, who died there in 1579. Balnaves had a varied career: he was a lord of session (restored to office in 1563), a reviser of the *Book of Discipline* appointed by the General Assembly, and one of the judges for Bothwell in 1567 regarding the murder of Darnley. Due to a single ballad titled "O gallandis all, I cry and call," printed in Allan Ramsay's *Evergreen* (1724-1727), Balnaves has been claimed as a Scots bard.

## Historical Societies and Publications
Edinburgh has hosted various clubs and publishers dedicated to the preservation of Scottish history. The Bannatyne Club was founded in Edinburgh in 1823 to publish historical and literary material from Scottish sources. In 1829, the club issued *Memorials of George Bannatyne*, which included an account of the Bannatyne MS. by David Laing and a memoir by Sir Walter Scott.

The city has also been the site of various historical publications, including:
* *Chronicle of Picts and Scots* (1867) and *Celtic Scotland* (1876-1880), both by W. F. Skene.
* *History of the House and Race of Douglas and Angus* (1644), written by David Hume of Godscroft.
* *The Douglas Book* (4 vols., 1885) by Sir William Fraser.
