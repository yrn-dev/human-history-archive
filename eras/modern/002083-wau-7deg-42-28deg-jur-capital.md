# Wau

Wau is a city located at 7deg 42' N., 28deg 3' E., situated on the Jur river. It serves as a key destination for steamboat communication from Khartum, a distance of approximately 930 m.

## Geography and the Jur River
Wau is positioned on the Jur, which is the most important of the many rivers that form the Bahr-el-Ghazal, the chief western affluent of the Nile in North-East Africa. The Bahr-el-Ghazal joins the Nile at 9deg 30' N., 30deg 25' E. 

The Jur is one of several southern affluents of the Ghazal. In their upper courses, these rivers flow across a plateau of ferruginous laterite and are characterized by valleys with steep banks. North of 7deg 20' N., where the currents are interrupted by rapids, the valleys open and the rivers wind through tortuous channels that are frequently choked by sandbanks. This alluvial region is subject to flooding during the rainy season.

Just above the confluence of the Lol and the Jur, the Jur broadens to form Lake Ambadi. This lake is over a mile broad at low water and 10 m. long, though it becomes much larger during flood times. Lake Ambadi is home to many "swimming" variety sudd plants, although ambach and papyrus are absent. The lake is also a habitat for large numbers of *Balaeniceps rex*, a bird that is rare elsewhere.

## The Bahr-el-Ghazal System
The Bahr-el-Ghazal is described as a drainage channel rather than a true river. It begins at the confluence of the Lol and the Jur and extends for about 200 m. to its junction with the Nile at Lake No. The river's course begins by flowing north with shallow, lagoon-like expansions that never exceed 13 ft. in depth. It then turns north-east, narrowing and deepening with occasional reaches of papyrus, before finally turning east toward Lake No. The banks in this section are typically marked by scrub and anthills; in one valley, these anthills are so densely packed that Sir William Garstin noted they resemble a "gigantic graveyard."

The Bahr-el-Ghazal is prone to being choked by sudd. During the rule of the Mahdists, the river became almost blocked by these accumulations.

## Navigation and Exploration
To establish reliable transport, British officers removed sudd blocks from the Jur, the Bahr-el-Ghazal, and other rivers starting in 1901 and in subsequent years. These efforts enabled uninterrupted steamboat communication between Khartum and Wau during the flood season.

Between 1905 and 1907, further exploration of the northern and western affluents of the Ghazal was conducted by British naval officer R. C. Bayldon, along with Capt. C. Percival and Lieut. D. Comyn. Their work helped clarify the nomenclature and hydrography of the tributaries.
