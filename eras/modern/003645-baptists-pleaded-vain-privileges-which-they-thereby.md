# The History and Development of the Baptist Denominations

Baptists are a body of Christians distinguished by their specific view regarding the ordinance of baptism. The central, defining belief shared by all Baptists is that baptism should be administered only to believers. While some groups, such as the Mennonites, practice baptism by sprinkling or pouring, modern Baptists are characterized by the use of immersion. This practice is not exclusive to them, as it is also recognized by Paedobaptists and practiced by the Orthodox Eastern Church.

## Early Settlements and Persecution in Massachusetts
In 1663, John Myles, a Welsh Baptist and former one of Cromwell's Tryers, fled the intolerance of Charles II's government and sought refuge in Massachusetts with his congregation. They were permitted to settle in Rehoboth, provided their meeting-place was located a considerable distance from that of the standing order. Myles contributed significantly to the growth of the Baptist Church in Boston and throughout Massachusetts.

By May 1665, several English Baptists and others who had adopted Baptist views in the neighborhood of Boston, including Thomas Gould of Charlestown, joined in a church covenant. This church faced severe persecution; members were frequently fined and imprisoned, and they were denied the use of a building they had constructed as a meeting-house.

Despite a July 1679 mandate from Charles II—which sharply rebuked Massachusetts authorities for denying others the liberties they themselves had sought in exile—little changed. Even after the Act of Toleration of 1689 was fully implemented in England, the Boston Baptists pleaded in vain for the privileges to which they were entitled. Recognition of their right to exist was only achieved through the earnest efforts of other dissenters and English Baptists.

## Expansion in North America
Baptist presence expanded through migration and missionary work. In 1682, Humphrey Churchwood and William Screven organized a small congregation in Kittery, Maine. Due to persecution, Screven and others migrated to South Carolina, organizing the First Baptist church in Charleston around 1684. This became a primary center for disseminating Baptist principles in the region, though it was nearly extinguished by 1746 due to the intrusion of Socinianism and the withdrawal of members.

In Virginia, General (Arminian) Baptists appeared from 1714 onward, fostered by missionaries from the English General Baptists, eventually reaching North Carolina by 1727. By the close of the 18th century, Baptists also established themselves in the Dominion of Canada via migrations from the United States, later reinforced by Scottish, Welsh, and English Baptists. By 1909, Canadian Baptists numbered 120,000.

## Theological Divisions and the Great Awakening
The Baptist tradition was historically split into two sections based on their views of redemption:
*   **General Baptists:** Held Arminian views and the doctrine of "General Redemption."
*   **Particular Baptists:** Agreed with Calvinistic theory and the doctrine of "Particular Redemption."

Over time, many General Baptists adopted Socinian or Arian theories. In 1770, the orthodox portion of the General Baptists, influenced by Dan Taylor, formed the General Baptist New Connection. Meanwhile, the "Old Connection" gradually merged into the Unitarian denomination. Particular Baptists saw a shift toward more moderate Calvinism toward the end of the 18th century, largely due to the writings of Andrew Fuller.

During the "Great Awakening" (1740-1743), the General Baptists of Connecticut and Rhode Island did not cooperate heartily because the movement was conducted on Calvinistic principles. However, the Baptists ultimately benefited from the Awakening. Many "Separate" or "New Light" churches, which were severely persecuted by the dominant party and denied the scanty privileges Baptists had already won, eventually converted to Baptist views. They concluded that infant baptism lacked Scriptural warrant and served as a cornerstone of state-churchism.

## Organizational Evolution and Modern Status
In England, the persecution of Baptists and other Protestant dissenters ended with the Revolution of 1688 and the Act of Toleration of 1689. In 1727, the "General Body of Protestant Dissenting Ministers of the three Denominations" (Baptists, Independents, and Presbyterians) was organized, granting its ministers the privilege of presenting addresses to the sovereign.

In 1891, under the influence of Dr. John Clifford, the General and Particular denominations united to form the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland. This union is voluntary; some prosperous churches remain aloof. Additionally, "Strict" Baptist churches exist as a separate denomination, further divided into "Vessel" and "Standard" parties based on their respective organs, the *Earthen Vessel* and *Gospel Standard*.

Regarding communion, while most Baptists historically required baptism for membership, some influenced by Bunyan argued that differences in water baptism should not bar communion. This practice of mixed communion gradually spread, with some churches now admitting any person professing faith in Christ to full membership.
