# Nervous System

The nervous system encompasses a wide range of biological structures across different species, varying from diffuse epidermal layers in simpler organisms to complex brains in ungulates. Its study involves detailed histological research into the continuity of cells and fibres, as well as pharmacological applications for the treatment of various disorders.

## Anatomical Variations in Different Species

In certain organisms, the nervous system is essentially epidermal in position and diffuse in distribution. A layer of nerve-fibres exists over the entire body surface at the base of the epidermis, situated immediately outside the basement-membrane that separates the epidermis from the subjacent musculature. In these organisms, special thickenings of this diffuse layer occur along certain lines and regions, such as the neck of the proboscis and the dorsal and ventral middle lines of the trunk. 

A concentration of nerve-cells and fibres is found in the collar-region, forming a closed medullary tube known as the collar nerve-tube, which opens via anterior and posterior neuropores. The central canal of this tube may be wide and uninterrupted, broken into small closed medullary cavities, or obsolete. In the *Ptychoderidae* family, this tube connects to the epidermis via unpaired outgrowths from the dorsal wall. These hollow roots terminate in the dorsal epidermis of the collar. Some views suggest the collar nerve-tube of the Enteropneusta is equivalent to the primary fore-brain of vertebrates or the cerebral vesicle of the Ascidian tadpole and *Amphioxus*.

Other species possess a nervous system consisting of a segmented neutral cord, a nerve ring, and a ganglion or brain located dorsally near the junction of the stomach and pharynx. In ungulates, the brain features elongated, subcylindrical hemispheres and large olfactory lobes that project in front of the hemispheres, with much of the cerebellum remaining uncovered.

## Histology and Research

Significant advancements in the understanding of the nervous system have come from histological studies. In 1845, Kölliker provided proof that nerve-fibres are continuous with nerve-cells, establishing a necessary basis for speculations regarding the actions of the central nervous system. He later utilized the Golgi method to further investigate the intricate patterns of cells and fibres within the spinal cord and brain.

Additionally, early anatomical documentation was provided by Sir Charles Bell, who published engravings of the brain and nervous system in 1802 and co-authored a volume on the anatomy of the nervous system and organs of special sense in 1804. Bell also studied the "Anatomy of Expression" to describe how the mind's influence is propagated to the muscular frame.

## Pharmacology and Medicinal Use

Various substances are used to treat the nervous system or manipulate its functions:

**Bromides**
Bromide of potassium is described as the safest and most generally applicable sedative of the nervous system. It is used as a hypnotic, a standard anaphrodisiac, and a sedative for mania, morbid mental excitement, and hyperaesthesia. It is considered the specific remedy for epilepsy (including *grand mal*). While potassium bromide is rapid and certain, it may depress the heart in morbid states; in such instances, the sodium salt may be used. Ammonium bromide is often used in cases of whooping-cough when both a sedative and a stimulant are indicated. Other conditions treated with bromides include insomnia, delirium tremens, asthma, migraine, hysteria, neuralgia, nymphomania, spermatorrhoea, and certain nervous disorders of the heart.

**Atropine**
Atropine acts as a local anaesthetic, anodyne, and anhidrotic. When applied to a raw surface or rubbed into the skin with alcohol or glycerine, it paralyses the terminals of pain-conducting sensory nerves and those determining perspiration. Once absorbed into the blood, it affects nearly every part of the nervous system. Most notably, it paralyses the terminals of nearly all secretory nerves, drying the skin and arresting the secretion of mucus and saliva in the throat and mouth (due to the paralysis of the *chorda tympani* nerve). It also reduces mucus secretion in the trachea and bronchi and diminishes or arrests milk secretion in the mammary gland. However, it does not exert appreciable sedative action on the nerves of pain when given internally.

## Systemic Interconnections

The nervous system maintains intimate relationships with other bodily systems. The mouth is closely linked to the general nervous system. The teeth, through extensive connections with the sympathetic nervous system and the largest cranial nerves, can cause irritation that results in profound reflex nervous phenomena. Such issues are often only curable by removing the local tooth disorder. Furthermore, certain saline chalybeate waters, such as those found in Griesbach, have been used since the 16th century to treat diseases of the nervous system.
