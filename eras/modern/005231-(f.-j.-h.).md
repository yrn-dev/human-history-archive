## Theory of Differentiable Functions
The archive contains detailed mathematical entries regarding the nature of functions. A function is considered "uniform" or "one-valued" when the rule of calculation assigns a single definite value for the function at each point in the domain of the argument.

### Differentiation and Limits
The differentiation of a continuous function is a process used to measure the rate of growth by comparing the increment of the function with the increment of the variable. For a function $[f](x)$ defined in an interval containing point $a$, the expression $[f](a + h) - [f](a) / h$ represents a function of $h$, denoted as $[\phi](h)$. 

There are four limits associated with this, known as the "four derivates" after Dini:
*   **Progressive differential coefficient:** Occurs when the first two limits are equal.
*   **Regressive differential coefficient:** Occurs when the last two limits are equal.
*   **Differential coefficient (or first derived function):** Occurs when all four limits are equal. This is denoted by $d[f](x)/dx$ or $[f]'(x)$.

### Continuity and Differentiability
A function that possesses a finite differential coefficient at all points of an interval is continuous throughout that interval. However, the archive notes that a function may be continuous without being differentiable. This distinction is attributed to G.F.B. Riemann, though the archive notes that Ampere's failed attempt to prove that every continuous function must be differentiable served as an initial step in the theory. Examples of continuous functions that are not differentiable have been provided by Dini, Weierstrass, Darboux, and Riemann.

## Analytic Functions and Integration
An analytic function is characterized by the continuity of $[f](x)$ and its first $n$ differential coefficients ($[f]'(x), [f]''(x), \dots [f]^{(n)}(x)$) within an interval. This leads to "Taylor's theorem," which expresses the function as a sum involving these coefficients and a remainder $R_n$.

### Improper Integrals
The archive defines "improper" definite integrals as those where the interval of integration is unlimited (extending to positively or negatively infinite values). For example, the integral of $(\sin x / x)dx$ from 0 to infinity is considered improper in two ways. 
*   **Convergent Integral:** An improper definite integral that exists even when the function becomes or tends to become infinite.
*   **Divergent Integral:** An expression that has no value because the limit does not exist when the function tends to become infinite at a point within the interval.
