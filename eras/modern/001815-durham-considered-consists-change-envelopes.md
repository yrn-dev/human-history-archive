# Agglutination and the Theories of Gruber and Durham

## Overview of Agglutination
Agglutination is a property that may be possessed by an anti-bacterial serum. This process is characterized by the aggregation of bacteria, which are uniformly distributed in an indifferent fluid, into clumps. If the bacterium is motile, its movement is arrested during this process. While the process is observed via a microscope, the resulting clumps eventually settle in the fluid to form a sediment, leaving the upper portion of the fluid clear. This visible change is referred to as sedimentation.

## Early Observations and Serum Diagnosis
The phenomenon of sedimentation was first demonstrated by G. E. H. Roger and B. J. A. Charrin using *B. pyocyaneus*. They observed that when a small quantity of homologous serum—defined as the serum of an animal immunized against the bacterium—was added to a fluid culture of the bacillus, growth formed a sediment rather than a uniform turbidity.

Subsequent research by Durham and Gruber showed that sedimentation occurred when a small quantity of homologous serum was added to a bacterium emulsion in a small test-tube. They found that this occurred in all cases where Pfeiffer's lysogenic action could be demonstrated. Shortly after these findings, Gruenbaum and Widal demonstrated that the serum of patients suffering from typhoid fever agglutinated the typhoid bacillus, even during the early stages of the disease. This discovery established the foundation for serum diagnosis. Similar phenomena have since been demonstrated in various other infections, including plague, cholera, Malta fever, infection with *B. coli*, and "meat-poisoning" caused by Gaertner's bacillus.

## The Durham and Gruber Theory of Action
Regarding the mode of action of agglutinins, Gruber and Durham proposed a specific theory. They considered that the process consists of a change in the envelopes of the bacteria, which causes them to swell up and become adhesive. 

While various facts support this view, other researchers have presented different findings. F. Kruse and C. Nicolle discovered that if a bacterial culture is filtered to be germ-free, an agglutinating serum still produces some change, causing suspended particles to gather into clumps. Based on this evidence, E. Duclaux considers agglutinins to be coagulative ferments.
