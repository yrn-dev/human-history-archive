# Sven Hedin and Central Asian Exploration

Sven Hedin was a Swedish explorer and traveler whose expeditions in Central Asia, particularly in Turkestan and Tibetan fields, significantly advanced geographical and historical knowledge of these regions. His work is noted for revolutionizing the understanding of the area north of the upper Tsanpo.

## Expeditions and Geographical Discoveries

Between 1899 and 1902, Hedin collected material in Tibetan and Turkestan fields. He later resumed his journeys from 1905 to 1908. His findings from the 1899–1902 period were published in the multi-volume work *Scientific Results of a Journey in Central Asia* (Stockholm, 1905–1907), which included a general map of Tibet on a scale of 1:1,000,000 and an elaborate atlas.

Hedin's explorations focused heavily on the complex hydrology and topography of the region. He conducted studies on the Khotan-Darya, a river that rises in the Kuen-lun Mountains from two headstreams, the Yurun-kash and the Kara-kash. These streams unite approximately 90 miles north of the town of Khotan, flowing 180 miles north across the Takla-makan desert before emptying into the Tarim.

One of Hedin's primary contributions was settling the "knotty point" regarding the Lob-nor. While traveling from Korla by a road at the foot of the Kurugh-tagh, he discovered milestones (potaïs)—clay and wood pyramids indicating distances in *lis*—which proved the existence of an ancient road with large traffic between Korla and a location to the south-east. On April 4, 1896, after following the Ilek river, Hedin found a large sheet of water in the valley at the location marked by Richthofen and Chinese topographers for the Lob-nor. This water mass is divided by natives into Arka Kul, Tayek Kul, Kara Kul, and Avullu Kul, though these are largely filled with reeds. Hedin also visited the Lob-nor described by Prjevalsky, reaching its western extremity, the Kara-buran, on April 17. He observed that Prjevalsky's lake was of recent formation and that the northern (Chinese) Lob-nor had gradually filled up since 1885, rendering the lake "vagrant."

## Archaeological Findings

In 1896, during a second journey through the Takla-Makan from Khotan to Shah Yar, Hedin explored ruins located between the Kiria Daria and the Khotan Daria. There, he discovered the remains of the city of Takla-Makan, which had been buried in sand. Hedin described the site as an "Asiatic Pompei" at least ten centuries old. His findings included:
* Figures of Buddha.
* Vestiges of habitations.
* A piece of papyrus containing unknown characters.

Hedin concluded that the city's inhabitants were of Aryan race, likely originating from Hindustan, and were Buddhist. He noted that the city predated the 8th-century Mahomedan invasion led by Kuteïbe Ibn-Muslim. In 1895, Hedin also followed a route from Yarkand, Yangi-Hissar, and Kashgar to Khotan, during which he spent nine days at Ilchi, estimating its population at 5,500 (comprising 500 Chinese and 5,000 Musulmans).

## Later Activities and Public Life

Outside of his exploration, Hedin was involved in public lecturing and political discourse. He was employed by the German Official Press Bureau and delivered lectures in Germany. During one such visit to Heidelberg, he was arrested and detained for several days by police who found his intense enthusiasm for Germany suspicious, leading to accusations of espionage. He was released after the Press Bureau established that he was not a spy.

Hedin also held distinct anti-English opinions. While these views were described as intensely irritating to British subjects, some contemporaries noted that his honesty in recording personal experiences—even facts that contradicted his own case—did not diminish the value of his factual records.

## Related Explorations of the Era

Hedin's work coincided with other significant explorations in the region. In 1903, Ellsworth Huntington explored the Alai range and the Tian-shan plateau. In 1904, Sir Francis Younghusband led a mission to Lhasa, which allowed Captains C.G. Rawling and C.H.D. Ryder to conduct a reconnaissance of the Indus and Upper Brahmaputra valleys and extended the Indian system of triangulation to determine the geographical position of Lhasa. Additionally, the work of Deasy in western Tibet was extended by Captain C.G. Rawling and Dr. M.A. Stein, increasing knowledge of ancient commerce and industry in Tibet and Turkestan.
