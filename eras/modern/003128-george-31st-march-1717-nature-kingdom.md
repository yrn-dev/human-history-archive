# The Nature of the Kingdom or Church of Christ

The discourse titled *The Nature of the Kingdom or Church of Christ* was a famous sermon preached on March 31, 1717, before King George I. It was delivered by Benjamin Hoadly, who had been newly appointed as the bishop of Bangor. The sermon served as a central element of the Bangorian Controversy, a theological dispute that began in 1716.

## The Bangorian Controversy
The controversy originated with the posthumous publication of *Constitution of the Christian Church, and the Nature and Consequences of Schism* by George Hickes, the bishop of Thetford. In this work, Hickes excommunicated all individuals except for the non-juring churchmen. Benjamin Hoadly responded to these principles by writing *Preservative against the Principles and Practices of Non-Jurors*. In this reply, Hoadly proposed sincerity as the only test of truth and recommended his own Erastian position.

## Teachings of the Sermon
In his sermon before George I, Hoadly presented a vision of the church that challenged existing ecclesiastical norms. He identified the church with the kingdom of Heaven, asserting that it was "not of this world." Based on this identification, Hoadly argued that Christ had not delegated His authority to any representatives. 

Furthermore, Hoadly used the discourse to:
* Impugn the idea that any visible church existed at all.
* Ridicule the value placed on tests of orthodoxy.
* Express contempt for the claims of the church to govern itself through the use of the state.

## Political and Ecclesiastical Aftermath
The sermon and Hoadly's accompanying book were reported on in May by a committee appointed by the Lower House of Convocation. While the bishops and the archbishop would have taken steps regarding the matter, the government intervened and prorogued Convocation until November. Hoadly denied that this intervention occurred at his request.

Following the dispute, Hoadly wrote *A Reply to the Representations of Convocation* and engaged with several critics. Among those who opposed him were Francis Hare (then dean of Worcester), Andrew Snape (provost of Eton), and Thomas Sherlock (then dean of Chichester). These three men, along with Robert Moss, the dean of Ely, were deprived of their royal chaplaincies. Hoadly chose not to answer William Law, who is described as the most brilliant of his antagonists, though Law was comparatively unknown at the time.

The most significant result of the controversy was the silencing of Convocation. Although the body had appeared to be returning to its proper work of addressing the real exigencies of the church, it did not meet again following the Hoadly dispute.
