# General Average Contributions and Values

General Average (G.A.) is a system of maritime law and practice used to calculate the amounts to be made good and paid by various interests involved in a maritime adventure. The fundamental principle governing the adjustment of G.A. is that the interests at risk must contribute in proportion to the benefits they received upon the completion of the adventure.

## Contributory Values and Calculation
According to Rule XVII, contributions to a G.A. are based on the actual values of the property at the termination of the adventure. To this actual value, the amount made good as G.A. for any property that was sacrificed is added. 

Several deductions are made during this calculation:
* **Shipowner's Interests:** Deductions are made from passage-money and freight at risk for crew's wages and port charges that would not have been incurred if the cargo and ship had been totally lost at the date of the sacrifice or G.A. act, provided these were not already allowed as G.A.
* **General Property:** All charges incurred regarding the property after the G.A. act are deducted, unless those specific charges are allowed in G.A.
* **Exemptions:** Passengers' personal effects and luggage that were not shipped under a bill of lading are not required to contribute to G.A.

## Principles of Adjustment
The adjustment of G.A. is a complex process focused on the results at the end of the adventure rather than the amounts at stake at the moment the sacrifice occurred. Consequently, an interest that is lost during the voyage after the sacrifice is made pays nothing, while an interest that has depreciated pays in proportion to its diminished value.

The liability to contribute is considered inchoate when the sacrifice is first made and becomes complete when the adventure ends, whether by arrival at the destination or by being broken up at an intermediate point, provided the interest survives.

An exception exists regarding G.A. expenditure, which is typically borne initially by the shipowner. Repayment to the shipowner is not entirely dependent on the safety of other interests at the ultimate destination. If some interests are realized or arrive at an intermediate port, contributions are proportional to those arrived or realized values. However, if all interests are lost, the burden does not remain solely with the party who first bore the expenditure; instead, all interests at stake at the time of the expenditure contribute in proportion to their values at that time.

## Valuation of Sacrificed Property
The objective of G.A. law is to place the party whose property was sacrificed on an equal footing with others, rather than a better one. For example, if goods valued at L100 are jettisoned for general safety, the owner does not receive the full L100 in contribution because they must bear a portion of the loss themselves, as their goods were part of the adventure. While the full L100 is treated as the amount to be "made good," the owner contributes toward that sum based on the L100 saved.

To prevent the owner of sacrificed goods from being put in a better position than those whose goods remained on board (by effectively withdrawing the sacrificed goods from subsequent voyage risks), the value of sacrificed property is estimated as it would have been on arrival. This estimate considers the condition the property would likely have been in had it remained on board throughout the voyage.

## Insurance and Liability
While the primary liability for G.A. contributions falls on the owners of the freight, goods, or ship, these contributions are typically paid by insurers in practice. Standard insurance policies often lack an express provision requiring underwriters to indemnify the assured against this liability, though they may include clauses recognizing the obligation, such as agreements that G.A. be payable "according to York-Antwerp Rules" or "as per foreign statement."

The legal ground for this insurance obligation has been debated. One theory suggested it was covered by the "sue and labour" clause (expenses incurred for the safeguard and recovery of the insured subject), but the House of Lords rejected this in *Aitchison v. Lohre* (1879). Another theory suggested that underwriters impliedly undertake to repay sums required by law to avert losses that would otherwise fall on the underwriter, but this was also negatived by the decision in *Aitchison v. Lohre*.

## The York-Antwerp Rules
The York-Antwerp Rules were introduced to provide uniformity and certainty in the adjustment of G.A. losses. They replaced varying customary rules with definite standards. For instance, Rule XIII established a specific scale for "new for old" deductions from repair costs, adapting these to modern shipbuilding. Rule XVII provided a standardized rule for contributory values, replacing the diverse rules previously used by different countries regarding how freight and ships should contribute.
