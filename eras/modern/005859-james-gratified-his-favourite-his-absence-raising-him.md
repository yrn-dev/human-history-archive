# The Elevation of Buckingham

## Rise to Power under James I
During the reign of James I, the figure of Buckingham rose to a position of significant influence. In an act of gratification toward his favourite, James raised Buckingham to a dukedom during the latter's absence. While the king's favour provided the initial foundation for his status, Buckingham's subsequent splendour was derived from other sources. He positioned himself at the forefront of a popular movement directed against Spain.

By February 1624, James summoned parliament after acknowledging, though reluctantly, that the recovery of the Palatinate could only be achieved through force. During this period, Buckingham, supported by the heir apparent, established an independent political position. This influence was such that James was both persuaded and half-driven to terminate all negotiations with Spain. Consequently, Buckingham became the most popular man in England for a time.

## Influence on the Reign of Charles I
Following the death of James I on March 27, 1625, the new king, Charles, and Buckingham remained aligned in their objectives. Both men were young, inexperienced, and shared a desire to recover the Palatinate and chastise Spain. However, Charles was characterized as being sluggish in action and lacking fertility in ideas, despite being obstinate once his mind was settled. As a result, Charles submitted his mind to Buckingham, whom the sources describe as a brilliant and versatile favourite who provided visions of future possibilities and was never uncertain of the next course of action.

Buckingham was dispatched to Paris to encourage the French court to convert its alliance into active co-operation. This mission was complicated by the rebellion of the Huguenots of La Rochelle, whom James had promised to help suppress with English ships. Although Buckingham initially seemed to consent to this, he later sought to mediate peace between the king of France and his subjects to prevent Charles from compromising his standing with parliament by attacking Protestants. Upon his return, his primary demands were refused, though he was given hope that peace with the Huguenots would be achieved. During his travels through France, Buckingham also made love to the queen of France.

## Other Historical Figures and "Favourites"
The archive contains various accounts of "favourites" across different eras and contexts. In Rome, Lucius Flamininus had a favourite whom he accompanied even while governing a province or commanding an army. To gratify this favourite's wish to see a man killed, Lucius ordered the execution of a condemned criminal in a banquetting chamber; however, Cato claimed that Lucius had instead slain a Gaulish deserter with his own hand.

In the English court of Queen Elizabeth, Fulke Greville became a great favourite, though he faced disgrace on several occasions for leaving the country against the queen's wishes. Later, under James II, Colonel Churchill (later Baron Churchill of Sandridge) and his wife were the favourite attendants of princess Anne. Despite the many favours showered upon him by James II, Churchill eventually betrayed the king, promising William of Orange that he would bring the troops over to the prince's side.
