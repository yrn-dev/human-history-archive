# Inner Temple, London

The Inner Temple is one of the four ancient societies in London known as the Inns of Court. These societies, which also include Gray's Inn, Lincoln's Inn, and the Middle Temple, serve as the mandatory professional bodies for barristers in England.

## History and Purpose
The rise of the Inns of Court as educational institutions can be traced back to the 13th century. This development is attributed to a clause in the Magna Carta that fixed the Common Pleas at Westminster rather than having them follow the king's court, which resulted in professors of law congregating in London. 

Lawyers formed associations and acquired their own houses, where students were educated in common law. These institutions conferred degrees of sergeant (comparable to a doctor) and barrister (comparable to a bachelor or apprentice). In England and Ireland, the term "barrister" refers to the highest class of lawyers, who possess exclusive audience in all superior courts. While the English barristers belong to the four Inns of Court, those in Ireland belong to the King's Inns.

## Evolution of Legal Education
During the 16th and 17th centuries, the corporate life of the Inns of Court experienced a decline in activity, mirroring a general decay seen in other crafts and gilds. As successful barristers grew more prosperous, they became less willing or able to dedicate time to the welfare of the profession. During this period, the Inns of Chancery ceased to function as places for student education, and the benchers of the Inns of Court stopped providing systematic teaching of law.

A revival occurred toward the middle of the 19th century. In 1852, the council of legal education was established. This body consists of twenty barristers and judges appointed by the four Inns of Court to oversee the education of students preparing to be called to the bar. 

The modern system emphasizes a compulsory examination that students must pass before being called to the bar. This examination serves to guide student reading and ensure a fixed standard of knowledge. The subjects covered in these examinations include:
* Common law
* Equity
* Real and Personal Property
* Evidence, Procedure, and Criminal law
* Constitutional law and legal history
* Roman law

To support this education, the council of legal education appoints assistant readers and readers, who are practicing barristers that hold classes and deliver lectures.

## Notable Associations
The Inner Temple has been associated with various legal and public figures. Sir John Rahere Paget, a Bencher of the Inner Temple and author of *The Law of Banking*, is one such figure. 

The Temple in London has also served as a site of study for international figures, such as Paul Dudley (1675-1751). After graduating from Harvard in 1690, Dudley studied law at the Temple before returning to Massachusetts, where he served as attorney-general from 1702 to 1718 and later as chief justice.

Conversely, some individuals joined the Inner Temple without pursuing a legal career. Eustace Budgell (1686-1737), the son of Dr. Gilbert Budgell, joined the Inner Temple after matriculating at Trinity College, Oxford, in 1705. However, Budgell devoted his full attention to literature rather than the study of law. He later became a contributor to the *Guardian*, the *Spectator*, and the *Tatler*, and held several administrative posts in Ireland, including accountant and comptroller-general of the revenue.
