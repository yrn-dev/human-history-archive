# Book-keeping

Book-keeping is the practice of recording financial transactions. While all methods of book-keeping are based on the same fundamental principles, their practical development varies by country, influenced by legislative requirements and the manner in which business is conducted.

## History and Evolution
The origins of accounting are obscure, though evidence suggests methods of keeping accounts have existed since remotest times. Babylonian records from as far back as 2600 B.C. were written with a stylus on small clay slabs, which often featured nail marks and cylinder seal impressions. In Egypt, records were commonly written on papyrus; contemporary imagery depicts scribes accounting for grain entering and leaving government store-houses.

Early accounting records were primarily narratives of events. Before the general employment of money, adequate book-keeping systems were not possible. Even after money was introduced, the use of Roman figures hindered ready calculation.

The transition to bound books occurred later in history. In the modern era, there is a trend toward abandoning bound books in favor of loose-leaf sheets or cards that can be rearranged for convenience. One of the earliest extant examples of a ledger is found in the Advocates' library at Edinburgh, dated 1697, which was ruled by hand.

## Systems of Entry
At present, book-keeping records are generally kept in three columns: the date of the transaction, its nature, and its money value. 

### The Ledger and Subsidiary Books
The ledger serves as the center of all book-keeping systems. Other books are considered "subsidiary books" and are kept for practical convenience. Because transactions are first recorded in these subsidiary books before being classified into the ledger, they are also called "books of entry" or "books of first entry." Books used solely for statistical purposes that do not lead to ledger entries are termed "auxiliary" or "statistical books."

In early book-keeping, the ledger was not a complete record but contained only accounts deemed necessary for accessibility, such as "personal accounts" regarding transactions with other business houses. Initially, reductions in indebtedness were recorded chronologically beneath the creation of that debt. This was later replaced by the use of a "contra account" to record reductions. Placing these two accounts on opposite sides of the ledger led to the development of the Dr. and Cr. account system used today.

### Double-Entry and Single-Entry
Double-entry book-keeping was in general use well before 1494. While often attributed to Lucas de Bergo, the system was likely in use for approximately 200 years prior to 1494. In that year, Luca Paciolo published *Everything about Arithmetic, Geometry and Proportion* in Venice, which provided an explanation of double-entry book-keeping as it was understood at the time.

In 1796, E.T. Jones published *Jones's English System of Book-keeping by Single or Double Entry* in Bristol. Jones claimed to have patented a new and improved system, securing thousands of subscribers via a prospectus; however, the system was found to be without merit.

## Regional Practices and Legal Requirements
Different nations impose various statutory requirements on how business records are maintained:

*   **France:** The Code of Commerce requires traders to maintain a letter book, an inventory, and a journal. The compulsory nature of the journal makes the use of multiple books of first-entry difficult due to duplication, which has left French book-keeping in a comparatively backward state.
*   **Germany:** Requirements are similar to France, though the journal is not compulsory. Accounts must be kept in bound books with consecutively numbered pages, which complicates the legal use of loose-leaf or card ledgers. A balance sheet is required annually, though inventories may be taken every two years if the stock-in-trade is difficult to measure.
*   **Belgium:** Merchants must keep a journal of daily transactions and a balance book, both of which must be initialled by a prescribed officer. Letters and telegrams (received and sent) must be preserved for ten years.
*   **Spain:** The Commercial Code requires a journal, ledger, invoice book, letter book, and inventory.
*   **Portugal:** Requirements include a journal, ledger, copy-letter book, and balance book.
*   **Holland:** Business men must record commercial transactions and maintain copies of letters sent and received, as well as prepare an annual balance sheet.

## Modern Developments and Tabular Book-keeping
As business grew, using a single book of first-entry became impracticable. Continental Europe generally treats the journal as an intermediate book to focus transactions before they enter the ledger. In North America and Great Britain, the journal is instead split into several specialized books of first-entry:
*   **Sales journal (day book/sales book):** For goods sold.
*   **Bought journal (purchases book/invoice book):** For goods purchased.
*   **Returns inwards book:** For goods returned by customers.
*   **Returns outwards book:** For goods returned to suppliers.
*   **Bills receivable book:** For bills of exchange received from debtors.
*   **Bills payable book:** For bills of exchange given to creditors.

Additionally, "tabular book-keeping" has been developed to reduce clerical work and increase speed. This involves substituting usual forms with books ruled with numerous columns. In books of first-entry, this allows a single book to handle multiple classes of transactions (such as separate columns for different business departments). In ledgers, it allows many personal accounts to be recorded on a single opening if the number of entries per individual is low.
