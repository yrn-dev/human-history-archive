# Political Conflict and National Identity in the Austro-Hungarian Empire

The political landscape of the Austro-Hungarian Empire during the late 19th and early 20th centuries was characterized by intense struggle between German interests and the nationalist movements of the Slav races, particularly in Bohemia and Galicia. This friction manifested in repeated constitutional crises, boycotts of legislative bodies, and disputes over language and regional autonomy.

## The Bohemian and Galician Declarations of 1868
In 1868, nationalist movements gained significant momentum. In Galicia, an extreme party led by Smolka sought to imitate the Czechs by avoiding attendance in Vienna. While outvoted, Galician parties agreed on a declaration demanding increased powers for the Galician diet and requesting that members cease attending the Reichsrath for matters the Galician diet was qualified to handle; failure to grant these demands would result in their departure from the Reichsrath.

Simultaneously, Czech leaders in Bohemia presented a "declaration" to the diet at Prague in 1868. This official statement of their claims, signed by eighty-one members—including bishops and many feudal nobles—demanded the full restoration of the Bohemian kingdom. The Czechs contended that the diet was not qualified to elect representatives for Vienna and that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia. They sought a separate settlement with Bohemia similar to the one established with Hungary. In response, the German majority declared that the signatories had forfeited their seats and ordered new elections. This period was marked by serious riots and the government's decree of exceptional laws to maintain order. Similar unrest occurred in Moravia and among the Bocchesi in Dalmatia.

## Parliamentary Instability and the Reichsrath
The struggle for control of the Reichsrath often centered on whether the empire should be governed by a German majority or a federalist system. In 1867, Beust became minister-president and ordered the diets to elect a Reichsrath according to a valid constitution. However, diets with Federalist majorities in Moravia, Carinthia, Tirol, and Bohemia refused to elect representatives on terms the government could accept.

By March 1870, after the rejection of new Galician demands, the Polish club, along with Slovenes and Tirolese members, left the House. This left the Reichsrath consisting of only 110 members—Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia—a composition that proved impossible to govern. Subsequent attempts at reconciliation by Minister-President Potocki failed, as Czechs and Feudals remained committed to the 1868 declaration. During the crisis of the July war between Germany and France, the emperor dissolved all diets and the Reichsrath. In the ensuing elections in Bohemia, the Czechs, supported by Clericals and Feudals, won a large majority. They took their seats only to declare the diet an informal assembly rather than a legal representative of the Bohemian kingdom, refusing to elect delegates for the Reichsrath. Consequently, the Germans left the diet.

## Electoral Reforms and Racial Distribution
The government attempted to manage these tensions through electoral changes. In 1872, the diet in Bohemia was dissolved, and the government used its influence to secure a German majority. This was achieved through the suppression of Czech newspapers, the transfer of Czech journalists' trials to German districts, and the formation of a society of German capitalists (popularly called *Chabrus*) to purchase estates with attached votes. This allowed Germans from Vienna to replace figures like the Schwarzenbergs and Thuns.

A new electoral law in 1873 transferred the right to elect Reichsrath members from the diets to a direct vote of the people. This deprived Federalists of the ability to use the legal representatives of a territory to refuse the appointment of deputies. While the Poles withdrew from the Reichsrath in protest, the law passed 120 to 2.

Later revisions of the allotment of seats formally ended the fiction that Austria was predominantly a German country. The Germans were left in a permanent minority with 233 seats, compared to 259 Slav seats. The "German-Latin block" (including Rumanian and Italian seats) totaled 257, but this block ceased to exist in practice as Italians began cooperating with the Slavs. The Ruthenes saw their representation trebled, though it remained disproportionate to their numbers.

## Language and Cultural Conflict
As German ascendancy waned, German parties fought to maintain their influence. In 1880, they founded the *Deutscher Schulverein* to establish German schools, which grew to 100,000 members. In the Reichsrath, German Liberals proposed that German be declared the language of state, though the bill failed to progress. In Bohemia, Germans proposed dividing the country into two parts—one where German was the sole language and another where Czech was recognized. This was rejected by Prince Karl Schwarzenberg and the Czechs, who insisted on the indissoluble unity of the Bohemian kingdom with both languages recognized as equal. Following this rejection, the Germans rose and left the diet.

Conversely, Slovene agitation led to the 1882 victory of Slovenes in the Carniola diet and the Laibach town council. This resulted in Illyrian becoming the official language and the renaming of streets in Illyrian, which caused riots in Istria. In Prague, Czech victory was marked by the removal of German street names and a by-law forbidding private individuals from using German street name tablets.
