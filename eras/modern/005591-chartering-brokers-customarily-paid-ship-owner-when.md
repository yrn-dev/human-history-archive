# Brokers

## Definition and General Role
In a primary sense, a broker is a mercantile agent categorized as a general agent. The fundamental office of a broker is to bring intending buyers and sellers together to facilitate a contract between them. For this service, the broker receives remuneration known as commission or brokerage. While originally brokers negotiated contracts only for the purchase and sale of commodities—such as produce, wool, or cotton brokers—the term now encompasses various other mercantile agents, including stockbrokers, ship-brokers, insurance-brokers, and bill-brokers. Pawnbrokers are distinguished from this group as they deal as principals rather than acting as agents.

## Relations with Principals and Third Parties
A broker's primary duty is to execute a principal's instructions with skill, diligence, and perfect good faith. This includes ensuring that the bargain's terms regarding price, quantity, and quality align with the principal's orders and that the resulting contract is legally enforceable. A broker must not accept commissions from third parties or enter positions where their interests oppose those of the principal. Unlike a factor, a broker does not have possession of the principal's goods and cannot buy or sell in their own name unless expressly authorized. Once the contract is formed, the broker's authority and duty generally cease.

Regarding third parties, a broker who signs a contract note "as broker" on behalf of a principal is not personally liable. However, if the broker structures the contract to make themselves a party to it, the third party may sue either the broker or the principal. While brokers generally lack the authority to receive payment, in trades where this is customary, a buyer may use payment to a seller's broker as a defense if the broker becomes insolvent or absconds. Additionally, brokers may be held liable for damages in tort for the conversion of goods if they negotiate a sale for a principal who lacks title to those goods.

## Remuneration and Liability
Brokerage typically takes the form of a percentage of the total price of the goods bought or sold. If a broker guarantees the solvency of the other party, they are employed on "del credere" terms and are entitled to a higher rate of pay. In certain trades, the selling broker is paid by the buyer or the buyer's broker, in which case the broker must account to the principal for the purchase money. 

Brokers are entitled to reimbursement for money properly expended and indemnity for liabilities incurred on the principal's behalf. Because they do not possess the goods, they have no lien to enforce these rights. If a broker fails to perform their duty, they lose their right to indemnity, reimbursement, and remuneration, and may be sued for damages for breach of the employment contract.

## Specialized Types of Brokers
### Ship-Brokers and Chartering
Ship-brokers act as commission agents and often serve as ships' managers. They function similarly to house-agents, procuring purchasers for ships or ships for purchasers. They also find charterers for ship-owners or available ships for charterers, effecting the "charter-party." 

Chartering brokers are customarily paid by the ship-owner upon the effectuation of the charter-party, regardless of whether the broker was originally employed by the charterer or the owner. To prevent disputes over payment, charter-parties often include a specific provision for a percentage (such as 2½%) of the estimated freight to be paid to the broker upon signing. Because the broker is not a party to the charter-party contract, they cannot sue on it, but the inclusion of such a clause practically secures their right to payment. When handling a ship's business in port, the broker acts as the "ship's husband," which includes clearing the vessel at customs.

### Insurance and Bill Brokers
An insurance broker is an agent who effects marine insurance policies. Employed by the person with the interest to insure, the broker pays premiums to the underwriter and receives payment for losses. By trade custom, the underwriter looks only to the broker for premium payments and has no right of action against the assured; conversely, the underwriter pays the broker's commission, despite the broker being employed by the assured. If an underwriter refuses to pay a loss, the broker drops out of the process and the assured sues the underwriter directly.

A bill-broker was originally an agent who procured the discounting of bills for country bankers in London. Over time, this evolved; today, a bill-broker typically buys bills on their own account at a discount and borrows money on their own security at interest, profiting from the difference. In this capacity, the bill-broker acts as a principal rather than an agent.

## Historical Context
In London, from the reign of James I until 1884, brokers were regulated by statute and licensed by the corporation. It was common for a single broker to act as the agent for both the buyer and seller. Following the passage of the Statute of Frauds under Charles II, brokers began recording contracts in a formal book and signing on behalf of both parties. They would then issue a "bought note" to the buyer and a "sold note" to the seller.

In Europe, the city of Antwerp became a major commercial center by the end of the fifteenth century, establishing the first exchange in Europe in 1460. Unlike Bruges, where monopolies existed for money-changers and brokers, Antwerp kept these professions open to all. In this environment, the local population often served as intermediaries, including brokers, charterers of vessels, and forwarding agents.
