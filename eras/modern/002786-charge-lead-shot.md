# Gravimetric Density of Charge of Lead Shot

## Definition and Purpose
Gravimetric density (G.D.) is a term used specifically by artillerists. It is employed to create a distinction between the specific gravity (S.G.) of a powder filling a specific volume while in a state of gas and the specific gravity of the individual solid cord or grain of powder.

## Calculation for Lead Shot
The gravimetric density of a charge of lead shot is determined by the arrangement of the material. When a lump of solid lead with a given specific gravity is formed into a charge of lead shot consisting of closely packed equal spherules, the G.D. is calculated using the following formula:

G.D. of charge of lead shot / S.G. of lump of solid lead = 1/6 [pi] [sqrt]2 = 0.7403.

For comparison, a bundle of cylindrical sticks of cordite has a G.D. relative to the S.G. of a cordite stick of 1/6 [pi] [sqrt]3 = 0.9067.

## Application in Interior Ballistics
In the study of interior ballistics, the primary challenge is the adjustment of the charge's gravimetric density in relation to the weight of the shot. This adjustment is necessary to ensure that the shot advances during the combustion of the charge, which prevents the maximum pressure from exceeding safe limits. 

For example, in a 6-in. range table, a charge may be described with a weight of 13 lb 4 oz. and a gravimetric density of 55.01/0.504. In this instance, the G.D. is 0.504, and the figure 55.01 represents the specific volume of the charge (measured in cubic inches per lb) when filling the chamber in a state of gas. The product of these two figures is 27.73. If the shot were not free to move, a closed chamber pressure resulting from a charge with a G.D. of 0.5 would be nearly 49 tons per sq. in., a level considered too great to be safe.

## Pressure Measurement and Experimental Data
To experimentally measure pressure within the bore of a gun, a crusher-gauge is utilized. This device records maximum pressure via the compression of an internal copper cylinder and can be placed in the base of the shot or the powder-chamber. Other methods for determining pressure include using chronographic appliances to register the passage of the shot to infer energy and velocity, or exploding powder charges in closed vessels. The latter method, used by Benjamin Robins in 1743 and Count Rumford in 1792, was later revived by Dr. Kellner, who used steel bicycle ball-bearing spheres as safety-valves to check crusher-gauge indications.

The relationship between pressure and G.D. is further detailed in ballistic tables. For instance, at a G.D. of 0.05, the pressure is 20.00 tons per sq. in., whereas at a G.D. of 1.66, the pressure is 63.33 tons per sq. in.
