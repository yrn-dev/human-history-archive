# Bohemia

## Geography and Topography
The interior of Bohemia is characterized as an undulating plateau, generally over 1,000 feet high, consisting of a succession of terraces that slope gradually from south to north. The region is divided into highland (south of a line passing through Hohenmauth-Prague-Komotau) and lowland (north of that line). The lowest point in Bohemia is located where the Elbe escapes into Saxony, at an altitude of 367 feet.

The region is bordered by several mountain ranges. To the north-west are the Erzgebirge (Czech *Rudo Hori*), which average 2,600 feet in altitude and reach their highest point at the Keilberg (4,080 feet). The highest place in Bohemia and central Germany is the village of Gottesgab, situated at 3,345 feet. The north-east frontier is formed by the Sudetes, which include the Lausitzergebirge, the Jeschkengebirge, the Isergebirge (peaking at the Tafelfichte at 3,683 feet), and the Riesengebirge. The Riesengebirge, or *Kroknose*, are among the highest mountains in central Europe after the Alps, reaching 5,264 feet at the Schneekoppe. Other ranges include the Heuscheuergebirge, the Adlergebirge, and the Bohemian-Moravian Hills, a plateau of primitive rocks reaching 2,500 feet. Interior ranges include the Brdywald, Tepler Gebirge, Karsbader Gebirge, and the volcanic massif of the Mittelgebirge.

## Education and Language
Education in Bohemia is well advanced, and the region possesses the lowest proportion of illiterates among the Austrian provinces. The highest educational institutions are the two universities in Prague, one Czech and one German. 

Language and education have been points of significant political importance. A law from 1867 mandated that elementary education in Slav districts be conducted in Slovenian or Czech. Under the minister of education Prazak, the government granted the demand that Slav minorities in towns be provided with their own schools rather than being compelled to attend German schools. While Germans viewed German as the *Kultur-sprache* and hoped education would lead the population to cease using local languages, the movement of the Slav population into towns and manufacturing districts has instead spread their language. This has led to demands for nationality-based government officials and the introduction of Slavonic into higher and middle schools.

## Administration and Urban Centers
For administrative purposes, Bohemia is divided into ninety-four districts and two autonomous municipalities: the capital, Prague (population 204,478), and Reichenberg (34,204). Other significant towns include Pilsen (68,292), Budweis (39,360), Aussig (37,255), Schonau (24,110), Eger (23,665), Brux (21,525), Warnsdorf (21,150), Gablonz (21,086), Kladno (18,600), Asch (18,675), Pardubitz (17,029), Saaz (16,168), Komotau (15,925), Kolin (15,025), Kuttenberg (14,799), Trautenau (14,777), Carlsbad (14,640), Pribram (13,576), Jungbunzlau (13,479), Leitmeritz (13,075), Chrudim (13,017), Dux (11,921), Bodenbach (10,782), Tabor (10,692), Bohmisch-Leipa (10,674), Rumburg (10,382), and Weipert (10,037).

Politically, Bohemia sends 130 deputies to the Reichsrat at Vienna. The local diet consists of 242 members, including the two university rectors, the archbishop, and three bishops, who belong *ex officio*.

## Military and Political History
Bohemia has been the site of various conflicts and political shifts. In 1431, a crusade against the Hussites ended in failure near Domazlice. After the crusaders had committed atrocities in Most and failed to capture Tachov, they were routed by the Hussites under Prokop the Great, whose forces were identified by their war-wagons and the song "All ye warriors of God."

In the 17th century, the Bohemian crown became vacant following the death of Matthias. In 1619, the Bohemian estates deposed Ferdinand and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as king. However, in September 1620, an imperialist army led by the duke of Bavaria, Tilly, and Bouquoi entered Bohemia. On November 8, 1620, the Bohemians were defeated at the "Bila Hora" (White Hill), leading to the occupation of Prague and the flight of King Frederick.

In the 18th century, during a campaign involving the French and the elector of Bavaria, Prague was stormed on November 26, 1741. The elector of Bavaria was crowned king of Bohemia on December 19, 1741, and later elected imperial throne as Charles VII.
