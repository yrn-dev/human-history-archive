# Buck, Don Juan, and the Name of José

## The Term "Buck" and the Dandy
The word "buck" originates from Old English terms *buc* (a he-goat) and *bucca* (a male deer), referring to the male of various animals, including rabbits, hares, and specifically fallow-deer. In a different linguistic vein, the term also derives from a root common to Romance and Teutonic languages (such as the French *buée* and German *Bauch*) referring to lye or the bleaching of clothes in lye; this usage is preserved in the term "buck-basket," which describes a basket of clothes ready for washing. Additionally, the word is used as a verb meaning "to leap," seen in the term "buck-jumper" to describe a horse that leaps with its back arched and feet tucked.

In a social context during the 18th century, "buck" was used to describe a reckless, spirited young man of fashion. Over time, this evolved to refer to a "dandy," specifically regarding extravagance in dress. While earlier bucks were distinguished by rich materials, jewellery, and bright colors, the modern school of dandyism—led by Brummel—emphasized extravagant neatness and the superb fit of garments, typically in black.

## The Legend of Don Juan
Don Juan is a legendary character appearing in various European myths and literature. He was introduced into formal literature via the Spanish play *El Burlador de Sevilla y convidado de piedra*, printed in Barcelona in 1630 and usually attributed to Tirso de Molina. The core story involves a profligate man who invites a dead man to supper, only to have the invitation accepted. While some report that a dissolute gallant named Don Juan Tenorio lived in Seville, or another frequented the court of Peter the Cruel, there is no satisfactory evidence for their historical existence.

The character is viewed as an incarnation of arrogant blasphemy and perverse sensuality. The legend spread from Spain to Italy, where it was performed in pantomime, and then to France, influencing Moliere's *Le Festin de pierre* (1665). The legend's sustained popularity is attributed largely to Mozart's setting of Da Ponte's libretto, *Don Giovanni*. This version influenced the French romantic school, including writers such as Dumas, Merimee, Balzac, and Alfred de Musset. Additionally, Lord Byron created a masterpiece titled *Don Juan*, which uses a "humorous-realistic" style to depict life as a whole and analyze human nature.

## Historical Figures Named Don Juan
Distinct from the legend, Don Juan was the name of a Spanish governor appointed by Philip II to Belgium. The son of Charles V, this Don Juan was the victor of the naval battle of Lepanto in 1571. His tenure in Belgium was marked by a lack of sympathy for the Belgians and failed negotiations with rebels. By occupying the fortress of Namur against conventions to secure a stronghold, he was declared a traitor by the States-General under the influence of William the Silent.

## Brigandage in Spain
Brigandage was endemic in Spain, particularly south of the Sierra Morena and in Catalonia. In Catalonia, it began as a conflict between peasants and the feudal exactions of landlords, featuring a traditional hero named Roque Guinart. The War of the Succession (1700-1714) and the 1640 revolt against the house of Austria further stimulated these activities.

Efforts to suppress brigandage included the actions of Pedro Veciana, a country gentleman and hereditary *balio* of the archbishop of Tarragona. Veciana armed his farm-servants to resist brigands, forming a group known as the Mozos (Boys) of Veciana. The government of Philip V subsequently commissioned him to raise the "escuadra de Cataluna," a police corps led by the Veciana family for five generations. Later, around 1844, the duke of Ahumada organized "La Guardia Civil," which effectively kept brigandage down. In the south, "bandoleros" often found more sympathy, and lawlessness was sustained by tobacco smuggling from Gibraltar.
