# Lansdowne House and the Unionist Compact

## The Unionist Agreement
Following a period where the Unionists existed as a miserable minority in parliament—consisting of 157 members against 379 Liberals, 83 Nationalists, and 51 Labour members—the necessity for consolidation within the party became immediate. This led to negotiations between Mr. Chamberlain and Mr. Balfour. These discussions resulted in the patching up of an agreement, which was detailed in correspondence dated February 14th.

A few days after this correspondence, the agreement was confirmed during a meeting of the party held at Lansdowne House. This new compact was outlined in a letter from Mr. Balfour, who asserted that the first constructive work of the Unionist party must remain fiscal reform.

## Objectives of Fiscal Reform
According to the compact indicated by Mr. Balfour, the primary goals of fiscal reform were to achieve closer commercial union with the colonies and to secure more equal terms of competition for British trade. 

While Mr. Balfour noted that it was inexpedient to allow differences of opinion regarding specific methods to divide the party, and unnecessary to prescribe exact methods at that time, he identified certain measures that were not "in principle objectionable." These included:
* The imposition of a small duty on foreign corn.
* The establishment of a moderate general tariff on manufactured goods, provided it was not used to give artificial protection against legitimate competition or for the purpose of raising prices.

Mr. Balfour stated that such measures should be adopted if they were shown to be necessary for revenue purposes or for attaining the party's objectives. Following this agreement, Mr. Balfour's leadership of the entire party was confirmed, and he secured a seat in the City of London following the retirement of Mr. Gibbs.

**Political Appointments and Monuments**
In the early 20th century, Lord Lansdowne served as foreign secretary in the cabinet reconstituted by Prime Minister Mr. Balfour after July 11, 1902. Additionally, in Calcutta, an equestrian statue of Lord Lansdowne stands on the Maidan, facing a similar statue of Lord Roberts on opposite sides of the Red Road.

**Parliamentary Patronage**
In February 1830, Lord Lansdowne offered a seat at Calne—then considered a "pocket borough"—to the author of two articles on the Utilitarians and James Mill that had appeared in the *Edinburgh Review* in 1829. This author, Macaulay, entered parliament with the assurance from Lord Lansdowne that the patron did not wish to interfere with his freedom of voting.

**Military and Literary History**
During the English Civil War, a battle took place at Lansdown, near Bath. Sir Bevil Grenville was killed there at the head of the Cornish infantry. A monument to Grenville was later erected on the field of Lansdown. In literary records, George Granville, Lord Lansdowne, published a work in 1736 containing a *Vindication* of his kinsman, Sir Richard, against charges made by Clarendon.
