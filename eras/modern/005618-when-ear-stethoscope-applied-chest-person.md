# Auscultation of the Chest

In medical practice, the application of the ear or a stethoscope to the chest of a person allows for the detection of abnormal breath sounds, particularly in individuals suffering from specific respiratory attacks. These sounds are categorized based on their quality and the pathological condition of the bronchial tubes.

## Dry Sounds
During the earlier stages of certain respiratory attacks, sounds described as snoring or cooing may be heard. These are often mixed with sounds of a fine whistling quality or wheezing that accompany respiration. These are termed "dry sounds." In some instances, these sounds are so distinct and abundant that they can be heard by a bystander from some distance, and their vibrations may be felt by a hand applied to the chest.

The generation of these sonorous breath sounds is explained by the inflammation of the bronchial mucous membrane. This inflammation causes swelling and the presence of a tough secretion that adheres to the membrane, which together diminish the calibre of the tubes. The coarser dry sounds are generated in the large divisions of the bronchi, while the finer wheezing sounds are produced in the small divisions.

## Moist Sounds
As a disease progresses, dry sounds are largely replaced by sounds of a bubbling or crackling character, which are referred to as "moist sounds" or râles. This transition occurs when the discharge from the bronchial mucous membrane becomes less glutinous and more abundant, accumulating in the tubes until it is dislodged by coughing. The moist râles are caused by respired air passing through this fluid.

Because different portions of the bronchial tubes are affected at different times during the course of a disease, it is common for both moist and dry sounds to be heard abundantly in the same case.

## Other Chest Conditions
Beyond the sounds detected via the ear or stethoscope, other chest conditions are identified through different methods or symptoms. For example, the presence of an empyema—an abscess in the pleural space between the chest wall and the lung—can be detected through percussion, which yields a dull note on the affected side. This condition results from septic inflammation of the pleura caused by germs, such as those from typhoid fever or pneumonia.

Severe injuries to the chest, such as those caused by a wheel passing over a person, can result in broken ribs being driven into the lung. This leads to the collapse of the lung as air enters the pleural space, potentially causing death by asphyxia. Symptoms of such injuries include a cough accompanied by bright red blood or frothy, blood-stained mucus.
