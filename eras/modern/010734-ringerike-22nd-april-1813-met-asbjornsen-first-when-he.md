# Peter Christen Asbjornsen and Jorgen Engebretsen Moe

Peter Christen Asbjornsen (1812–1885) and Jorgen Engebretsen Moe (1813–1882) were collectors of Norwegian folklore. Their professional and personal lives were so closely intertwined that they are rarely named separately in historical records.

## Early Life and First Meeting
Peter Christen Asbjornsen was born in Christiania on January 15, 1812. He was a member of an ancient family from the Gudbrandsdal, a lineage believed to have ended with him. Jorgen Engebretsen Moe was born on April 22, 1813, at the Mo i Hole parsonage, located in Sigdal Ringerike. 

The two first met when Moe was fourteen years of age. This meeting marked the beginning of a close friendship that persisted until the end of their lives.

## Collection of Folklore
Asbjornsen began the process of collecting and recording legends and fairy stories in 1832, during his twentieth year. He later expanded his collection by traveling on foot across the entirety of Norway. Asbjornsen entered the university as a student in 1833.

In 1834, Asbjornsen discovered that Moe had independently begun searching for relics of national folklore. Following this discovery, the two friends decided to work in concert and began comparing their results.

## Professional Pursuits
While collaborating on folklore, both men pursued other professional paths. Asbjornsen became a zoologist by profession. Utilizing the support of the university, he conducted several investigating voyages along the coasts of Norway, with a particular focus on the Hardanger fjord.

Moe left Christiania University in 1839 to study theology. To support himself, he worked as a tutor in Christiania. During his holidays, Moe traveled through remote districts and mountains to collect stories.

## Published Works
The collaboration between Asbjornsen and Moe resulted in a major work titled *Norwegian Popular Stories (Norske Folkeeventyr)*. The first installment of this work appeared in 1842–1843 and was recognized across Europe as a significant contribution to literature and comparative mythology. A second volume followed in 1844, and a further collection was published in 1871. In 1859, Sir George Dasent translated many of the *Folkeeventyr* into English.

Apart from their joint efforts, Asbjornsen published a separate collection of Norwegian fairy tales (*huldreeventyr og folkesagn*) in 1845 without assistance from Moe.

## Later Activities
In 1856, Asbjornsen turned his attention to the issue of deforestation in Norway, successfully inducing the government to address the matter.
