# Methodism and the Ministry of Francis Asbury in America

## Early Life and Conversion
Francis Asbury (1745–1816) was an American clergyman born on August 20, 1745, at Hamstead Bridge in the parish of Handsworth, near Birmingham, Staffordshire, England. Born to poor parents, Asbury attended the village school of Barre for a brief period of study before being apprenticed to a maker of tongues, or "buckle chapes," at the age of fourteen. Asbury became converted to Methodism when he was thirteen years old, and his parents are believed to have been among the early converts of Wesley. By the age of sixteen, he had become a local preacher. Known as a fluent and simple speaker, Asbury's success led John Wesley to enroll him as a regular itinerant minister in 1767.

## Missionary Work in the American Colonies
In 1771, Asbury volunteered for missionary work in the American colonies, landing in Philadelphia in October of that year. At the time of his arrival, Methodism had been introduced to the colonies only three years prior, and the number of converts was fewer than 300. Asbury provided new life to the movement, and within one year, the membership across several congregations more than doubled. 

In 1772, John Wesley appointed Asbury as "general assistant" to oversee the work in America. Although he was superseded in 1773 by an older preacher named Thomas Rankin (1738–1810), Asbury remained practically in control of the operations.

## The War of Independence and Exile
The outbreak of the War of Independence brought challenges to the Methodist movement, which by that time numbered several thousand members. The group fell under unjust suspicion of Loyalism, primarily because they refused to take the prescribed oath. This political climate led many ministers, including Thomas Rankin, to return to England.

Asbury, however, chose to remain at his post due to his duties and sympathies toward the colonies. Despite being threatened and arrested once, he continued his itinerant preaching. Eventually, hostility from the authorities in Maryland forced Asbury into a two-year exile in Delaware, where he remained quietly but continued to be active.

## Later Appointments and Leadership
In 1782, Asbury was reappointed to supervise the affairs of the Methodist congregations in America. In 1784, John Wesley took a radical step by disregarding the authority of the Established Church to appoint superintendents, or "bishops," for the church in the United States; these appointments were given to Francis Asbury and the Rev. Thomas Coke (1747–1814).
