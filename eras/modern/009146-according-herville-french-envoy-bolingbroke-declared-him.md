# Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke

Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke (1678–1751), was an English writer and statesman. The son of Sir Henry St John and Lady Mary Rich, he was educated at Eton and traveled abroad in 1698 and 1699, where he gained an exceptional knowledge of the French language. In 1700, he married Frances, the daughter of Sir Henry Winchcombe.

## Early Life and Character
Bolingbroke's youth was marked by extreme extravagance and dissipation. According to his intimate friend Swift, Bolingbroke desired to be viewed as the Petronius or Alcibiades of his era, mixing high political responsibility with licentious orgies. His contemporaries noted his riotous behavior; one account describes him running naked through a park while intoxicated. Despite these morals, he was admired for his social qualities, wit, and good looks. His most significant talent was his eloquence, which Swift claimed was unrivaled across all political factions.

## Political Career and the Treaty of Utrecht
Bolingbroke rose to prominence within the Tory administration, though his relationship with Harley, the Earl of Oxford, eventually dissolved. While Harley became lord treasurer and an earl in May 1711, St John was created Viscount Bolingbroke and Baron St John of Lydiard Tregoze on July 7. 

In August, Bolingbroke traveled to France to conduct negotiations, signing a four-month armistice on August 19. This culminated in the Treaty of Utrecht, signed on March 31, 1713, by all allies except the emperor. Bolingbroke is held primarily responsible for the manner of this peacemaking, which involved underhand conspiracies with the enemy and the violation of international assurances, as well as the abandonment of the Catalans. Additionally, a commercial treaty he established with France for free trade was rejected in June.

## The Jacobite Plot and Fall from Power
By the time of Queen Anne's declining health, Bolingbroke and Oxford had maintained secret communications with James. Bolingbroke reached a position of supremacy where a Jacobite restoration appeared inevitable. He placed the Jacobite Sir William Windham as chancellor of the exchequer and put the faction in charge of important military posts. 

The situation changed abruptly with the death of the queen on August 1, and the appointment of Shrewsbury to the lord treasurership. Following these events, Bolingbroke declared to Herville, the French envoy, that he could have secured everything within six weeks. While Bolingbroke later claimed in *The Patriot King* that there were no designs to place the Pretender on the throne, this is described as a palpable falsehood. His primary objective was likely the acquisition and maintenance of supreme power by any means necessary.

## Exile and Later Years
Upon the accession of George I, Bolingbroke was immediately dismissed from office. He fled in disguise to Paris on March 28, 1715, to avoid an attack by Walpole regarding the Treaty of Utrecht. In July, he became the secretary to the Pretender. However, his advice was often ignored in favor of Irish priests and refugees. After the failed expedition of 1715 and subsequent petty intrigues, Bolingbroke was dismissed from his office and renounced all further efforts for the Pretender.

He sought a pardon from the English government, declaring his final abandonment of the Pretender in March 1716. He lived at the estate of La Source near Orleans, where he studied philosophy and criticized the chronology of the Bible. He married Marie Claire Deschamps de Marcilly in 1720. In 1723, he received a pardon through the duchess of Kendal and returned to London. Though he offered his services to Walpole, they were received coldly. He later resided at Dawley, where he wrote the philosophy for the *Essay on Man* and wrote a series of letters to the *Craftsman* as the "Occasional Writer" to attack the Walpoles.

## Literary Legacy and Works
Bolingbroke's written works include political tracts and philosophical essays, such as *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge* and *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*. While his style was often spirited and dignified, his philosophical essays are viewed as lacking solidity and permanent interest, often serving as vehicles to display his eloquence. 

His influence on posterity is considered weaker than his influence on his contemporaries. While George III and Bute derived political ideas from *The Patriot King*, his influence on figures like Burke, Gibbon, and Voltaire is viewed as problematical. Burke imitated Bolingbroke's style in *Vindication of Natural Society* but did so to refute his principles.
