# Baker and African Exploration

The archive contains records of two individuals named Baker involved in African ventures: Sir Samuel White Baker and his younger brother, Valentine Baker (known as Baker Pasha). While Sir Samuel was an explorer focused on the geography of the Nile, Valentine was a professional soldier whose career included military commands in various regions.

## Sir Samuel White Baker
Sir Samuel White Baker (1821–1893) was an English explorer born in London. After early experiences in commercial office work and a period in Mauritius, he traveled to Ceylon in 1846. There, he established a successful agricultural settlement at Nuwara Eliya with the help of his brother, bringing emigrants and choice cattle breeds from England. His time in Ceylon resulted in the publication of *The Rifle and the Hound in Ceylon* (1853) and *Eight Years' Wanderings in Ceylon* (1855).

Following a visit to the Crimea and Constantinople in 1856, Baker supervised the construction of a railway across the Dobrudja to connect the Black Sea with the Danube. In March 1861, he began his first tour of exploration in central Africa. His stated goal was to discover the sources of the Nile and to potentially meet Captains Speke and Grant near Victoria Lake. During this expedition, Baker spent a year on the Sudan-Abyssinian border, where he learned Arabic and explored various Nile tributaries, proving that Nile sediment originated from Abyssinia. In December 1862, he left Khartum to follow the White Nile, eventually meeting Speke and Grant at Gondokoro two months later.

## Valentine Baker (Baker Pasha)
Valentine Baker (1827–1887) was a British soldier who served in the Ceylon Rifles and the 12th Lancers, seeing active service in the Kaffir war of 1852-53. He later served in the Crimean War at the fall of Sevastopol and the action of Traktir. He commanded the 10th Hussars for thirteen years starting in 1860.

Baker's British military career ended following his arrest and sentencing to a year's imprisonment and a fine for the indecent assault of a young woman in a railway carriage. After leaving England, he entered Turkish service during the war with Russia. He commanded a division of infantry and was promoted to *Ferik* (lieutenant-general) after a brilliant rearguard action at Tashkessan against the troops of Gourko. He also commanded Suleiman's rearguard with distinction.

## Military Actions in the Sudan
During the Sudan War, Baker Pasha led a force of 3,500 men to relieve Tokar. At El Teb, his forces encountered the enemy under Osman Digna. The expedition ended in disaster; Baker's men became panic-stricken and were slaughtered, resulting in the annihilation of his force, though Baker and a few officers managed to fight their way out. 

Subsequently, Baker Pasha accompanied a British force led by Sir Gerald Graham to Suakin. He served as a guide for the march back to the site of his previous defeat. During the second battle of El Teb, which was desperately fought, Baker was wounded. He continued to serve as the commander of the Egyptian police until his death in 1887.

## Context of Equatorial Africa
The archive notes that the lake regions of equatorial Africa were first explored by Richard Burton and Speke, who discovered Lake Tanganyika in February 1858. Burton's work and his publication *Lake Regions of Equatorial Africa* (1860) served as the incentive for later explorations by Stanley, Livingstone, Grant, Speke, and Baker.

General records regarding military expeditions in equatorial Africa highlight extreme physical and logistical hardships. These include mountainous terrain, heavy rainfall, and unbridged rivers. Transport is hindered by "cotton soil" that turns to mud or dust, and animal transport is often destroyed by the tsetse fly. Furthermore, unacclimatized whites face fierce heat and tropical diseases bred by parasitic life and the malaria mosquito.
