# The Palatinate and the Political Career of Buckingham

The recovery of the Palatinate was a central point of political contention in England during the early 17th century. The issue gained significant public attention following a revolution in Bohemia, which increased English sympathy for continental Protestants, particularly as the son-in-law of King James had accepted the crown of Bohemia. Tensions escalated in the summer of 1620 when a Spanish force prepared to invade the Palatinate.

## Buckingham's Shifting Alliances
The Duke of Buckingham's involvement in the Palatinate crisis was marked by frequent and abrupt changes in political alignment. Initially, Buckingham threw himself into the popular movement supporting the Protestant cause. However, before the end of the summer of 1620, he shifted his position to align closely with Gondomar, the Spanish ambassador. This change occurred after Buckingham became incensed by injuries the Dutch had inflicted upon English sailors in the East Indies. It is suggested that his marriage to Lady Katherine Manners, the daughter of the earl of Rutland—who was a Roman Catholic at heart despite outward conformity to the English Church—may have influenced this shift.

By the winter of 1621 and into the following year, Buckingham was entirely under the influence of Gondomar. In May 1622, Laud had to argue Buckingham out of a resolution to declare himself a Roman Catholic. During this period, Buckingham supported the dissolution of parliament in December 1621. By May 1622, an understanding had been reached with the Spanish ambassador regarding a visit by the prince of Wales to Madrid the following year. The Spanish court hoped to secure the prince's conversion to Roman Catholicism before granting him the hand of the infanta Maria.

## The Madrid Expedition and Break with Spain
On February 17, 1623, Charles and Buckingham set out for Madrid, arriving on March 7. Both parties entered the agreement under false pretenses: Charles and Buckingham hoped the restitution of the Palatinate to James's son-in-law would be granted as a marriage gift to Charles, while the Spaniards expected Charles's conversion to Roman Catholicism and other extreme concessions. The mission failed due to these conflicting goals and personal disputes between Buckingham and the grandees, including Olivares. By the time the two men sailed from Santander in September, they had resolved to break entirely with Spain.

## Military Efforts and Failures
By February 1624, King James acknowledged that the Palatinate could only be recovered by force. Buckingham, now the most popular man in England and having put himself at the head of the movement against Spain, helped persuade James to end all negotiations with Spain. 

Buckingham attempted to launch a military campaign to recover the Palatinate, but he lacked the necessary funds. He arranged for 12,000 Englishmen to be sent through France (though the French insisted they march through Holland) under the command of a German adventurer, Count Mansfeld. The enterprise ended in disastrous failure, as the troops, lacking money and provisions, died in the winter frosts. Despite this, Buckingham continued to propose various schemes, including sending aid to Christian IV, king of Denmark, and launching English fleets to attack Spain and its ally, Genoa.

## Transition to the Reign of Charles I
Following the death of King James on March 27, 1625, the new king, Charles, and Buckingham remained united in their goal to chastise Spain and recover the Palatinate. Buckingham, described as versatile and brilliant, became the dominant influence over the inexperienced and sluggish Charles. 

Buckingham was sent to Paris to seek active co-operation from the French court. This was complicated by the rebellion of the Huguenots of La Rochelle; James had previously promised English ships to help suppress the rebellion. While Buckingham initially seemed to consent, he later sought to mediate peace between the king of France and the Huguenots to prevent Charles from compromising his standing with parliament by attacking Protestants. During his time in France, Buckingham also behaved insolently by making love to the queen of France.

## Parliamentary Conflict and Downfall
The recovery of the Palatinate and the associated costs created severe friction with the House of Commons. In 1621, parliament expressed anger that no help was being sent to the Palatinate and attacked the system of monopolies that benefited Buckingham's dependants and brothers. 

Under Charles I, Buckingham's guidance led to enormous expenditures for a continental war that was unpopular in the Commons. Parliament grew suspicious of marriage treaties and demanded the execution of laws against Roman Catholics. By August 1625, English ships had fallen into French hands and were used against La Rochelle. The Commons lost all confidence in Buckingham, demanding that trustworthy persons be admitted to the king's council. Charles refused and dissolved his first parliament on August 12.

Later, during the battle over the Petition of Right, Buckingham resisted the Commons' demand that the king abandon the claim to imprison without showing cause. This led Coke to name the duke as "the grievance of grievances" and the cause of all national miseries. Despite demands for his removal from office, Charles refused to surrender Buckingham and prorogued parliament in anger. This period was marked by high popular excitement, including the murder of Dr. Lambe, a quack doctor and astrologer believed to influence Buckingham.
