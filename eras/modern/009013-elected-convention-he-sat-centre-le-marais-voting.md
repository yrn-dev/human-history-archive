# François Antoine de Boissy d'Anglas

François Antoine de Boissy d'Anglas (1756-1828) was a French statesman who began his professional life focused on literature and membership in several provincial academies. After moving to Paris, he entered the legal profession, purchasing a position as an advocate to the parlement.

## Early Revolutionary Activity
In 1789, Boissy d'Anglas was elected as a deputy to the states-general by the third estate of the *senechaussee* of Annonay. He played a role in the early stages of the Revolution, being among those who induced the states-general to proclaim itself a National Assembly on June 17, 1789. Through various speeches, he expressed approval for the taking of the royal family to Paris in October 1789 and the capture of the Bastille. During this period, he published pamphlets regarding finance and called for strict measures against royalists intriguing in the south of France. Later, during the Legislative Assembly, he served as *procureur-syndic* for the directory of the department of Ardeche.

## Role in the Convention
Upon being elected to the Convention, Boissy d'Anglas sat in the centre, a group known as "le Marais." During the trial of Louis XVI, he voted for the king's detention until such time as the state judged deportation to be expedient. 

Following this, he was sent on a mission to Lyons to investigate frauds related to the supplies of the army of the Alps. During the period of the Terror, Boissy d'Anglas was among the deputies of the centre who provided support to Robespierre. However, he was eventually won over by members of the Mountain who were hostile to Robespierre. This shift in support, shared by other leaders of the *Marais*, helped make the 9th Thermidor possible.

## Later Career and Public Service
Boissy d'Anglas was subsequently elected as a member of the Committee of Public Safety, where he was tasked with the superintendence of the provisioning of Paris. He was responsible for presenting the report that supported the decree of the 3rd Ventose of the year III, which established liberty of worship.

He demonstrated significant courage during the critical days of Prairial and Germinal of the year III. On the 12th of Germinal, while reading a report on food supplies from the tribune, the hall of the Convention was invaded by rioters; once they had withdrawn, he quietly resumed his report where he had been interrupted. On the 1st of Prairial, he presided over the Convention, remaining unmoved despite the menaces and insults of insurgents. This composure was maintained even when the head of the deputy Jean Feraud was presented to him.
