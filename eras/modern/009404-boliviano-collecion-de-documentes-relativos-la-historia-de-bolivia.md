# Boliviano. Collecion de documentes relativos a la historia de Bolivia

The *Boliviano. Collecion de documentes relativos a la historia de Bolivia* is a historical collection of documents authored by Vicente de Ballivian y Rizas and published in Paris in 1872. It serves as one of the referenced sources for the study of the history of Bolivia.

## Government and Political Structure
Bolivia is organized as a "unitarian" or centralized republic. While representative in form, the system is described as autocratic in certain important particulars. The government operates under a constitution adopted on October 28, 1880 (in force as of 1908).

The executive branch is led by a president and two vice-presidents, elected by direct popular vote for four-year terms; these individuals are ineligible for re-election in the immediately succeeding term. The president is supported by a cabinet of five ministers of state overseeing the following portfolios:
* Foreign relations and worship
* Finance and industry
* Interior and fomento
* Justice and public instruction
* War and colonization

Every executive act requires the countersignature of a minister of state, who is responsible for the act's enforcement and may be prosecuted by the supreme court for illegality.

The legislative branch consists of a national congress with two houses:
* **The Senate:** Composed of 16 members (two from each department) elected by direct popular vote for six-year terms, with one-third retiring every two years.
* **The Chamber of Deputies:** Composed of 72 members elected for four-year terms, with one-half retiring every two years.

The Chamber of Deputies is responsible for electing supreme court justices and acting as the prosecutor in impeachment trials, while the Senate serves as the court. Congress meets annually for sessions lasting between sixty and ninety days.

Suffrage is limited to male citizens who can read and write. Age requirements are twenty-one for single men and eighteen for married men. Additionally, voters must own real estate or have an annual income of 200 bolivianos (excluding income from service as a servant). This creates a small electoral body controlled by a political oligarchy.

## Finance and Economy
Bolivia's public debt is considered comparatively small. Following a war with Chile, an indemnity debt of 6,550,830 bolivianos was due to Chilean citizens; this was nearly liquidated by 1904 when Chile assumed the unpaid balance. In 1905, the internal debt was 6,243,270 bolivianos, which included 1,998,500 bolivianos in treasury bills. In 1903, the government authorized one million bolivianos in treasury notes for the National Territory and the department of Beni, with 10% of customs receipts from those districts set aside for redemption.

The currency system includes:
* **Paper Currency:** Bank-notes issued by four private banks (estimated at 9,144,254 bolivianos in circulation as of June 30, 1903).
* **Coinage:** Silver, nickel, and copper. Silver coins (1 boliviano/100 centavos, 50, 20, 10, and 5 centavos) are issued by the Potosi mint at approximately 1,500,000 bolivianos annually. Silver mining companies must send 20% of their product to the mint. Nickel coins are 5 and 10 centavos, and copper coins are 1 and 2 centavos.

Revenues are primarily derived from import duties and fees, excise taxes (sugar, tobacco, wines, and spirits), mining taxes, export duties on minerals (excluding silver), rubber, and coca, as well as stamp taxes, postal/telegraph revenues, and profits from stock companies. While sometimes called a "free-trade country," imports are subject to schedule rates of 10 to 40% ad valorem, plus consular fees, consumption taxes, and municipal/departmental levies.

## Education and Society
Bolivia maintains a free and compulsory school system, though progress has been limited and literacy rates remain low. The population is majority native, speaking Quichua, Aymara, and Guarani, while Spanish is the language of the dominant minority. Many natives and *cholos* (mestizos) are more familiar with native idioms than Spanish.

In 1901, there were 733 primary schools with 41,587 pupils and 938 teachers. The cost per pupil was estimated at 14.07 bolivianos. School enrollment was one in 43.7 of the population. Education is largely supervised by the Church, though schools are maintained by municipalities, the national government, and private means.

Higher education includes universities in La Paz, Sucre, Cochabamba, Tarija, Potosi, Santa Cruz, and Oruro. All provide law instruction; the first three provide medicine, and the first four provide theology. Only the universities at La Paz and Sucre (which dates from colonial times) are considered sufficiently equipped to merit the title of university. Specialized institutions include a military academy (La Paz), an agricultural school (Umala), a mining and civil engineering school (Oruro), and commercial schools (Trinidad and Sucre).

## Diplomatic and Territorial History
In 1866, a treaty between Chile and Bolivia established the 24th parallel as the boundary. Chile was granted a half share of customs and trading facilities between the 23rd and 24th parallels, as well as the right to mine and export from the district without tax. In 1870, Bolivia granted an Anglo-Chilean company the right to work nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel for $10,000.

Dissatisfaction with Chilean influence led Bolivia to enter a secret agreement with Peru on February 6, 1873, for mutual defense and the preservation of territorial integrity against potential Chilean encroachment.

Later, on May 18, 1895, a treaty was signed in Santiago. Chile pledged that if it acquired permanent sovereignty over Tacna and Arica, it would transfer them to Bolivia for an indemnity of $5,000,000 silver. If this failed, Chile bound itself to cede $5,000,000 silver and the roadstead (*caleta*) of Vitor or another analogous port to ensure Bolivia's free access to the sea.
