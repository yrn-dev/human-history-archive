# The Ashanti and the Fanti: Conflict and British Intervention

The history of the Ashanti is characterized by the rise of a powerful conquering nation in the dense forests of West Africa and its repeated conflicts with the Fanti people and the British Empire. Originating from bands of fugitives driven south by Moslem tribes in the 16th or 17th century, the Ashanti developed a national character of valor. They are of the pure negro type and are believed to be of the same race and language as the Fanti. Tradition suggests the two groups separated during a famine, with the Fanti moving south to live on herbs (*fan*) and the Ashanti remaining to subsist on Indian corn (*san*).

## Political Structure and Society
The Ashanti are composed of numerous tribes—including the Kumasi, Mampon, Bekwai, and others—each with its own king. Since the early 18th century, the king of Kumasi has been recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti, succeeding to the "golden stool," the symbol of sovereignty. The government was historically a blend of monarchy and military aristocracy, with the state organized into six great clans. The chiefs of these clans formed the King's Council, which often exercised more power than the king himself. 

Socially, the Ashanti practice polygamy. Land is held in common by tribes, while unallotted lands are designated as "stool lands" attached to the king or head chief. While some converts to Christianity and a few Mahommedan traders exist, the majority of the population are spirit-worshippers who generally show a repugnance to Islamic doctrines.

## Expansion and Early Conflict with the Fanti
Osai Tutu is regarded as the real founder of Ashanti power, having extended the empire through the conquest of Denkera, Banna, and Gaman. His successor, Osai Apoko, further expanded the empire toward the coast. In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina became king. In 1807, after the Fanti refused to surrender two defeated Assin chiefs, Osai Tutu invaded Fantiland. The Ashanti defeated the Fanti and reached the coast near Anamabo. Despite the presence of a British fort, the town was destroyed and 8,000 inhabitants were slaughtered.

Following this, Colonel G. Torrane, the governor of Cape Coast, concluded an agreement with the Ashanti. He acknowledged their conquest of Fantiland, surrendered some fugitives, and agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

## British Diplomatic and Military Interventions
In 1817, the British sent a mission to Kumasi consisting of Frederick James, T.E. Bowdich, and W. Hutchinson. Conflict arose when the king demanded payment of quit rents for the forts, which the Fanti had induced the British to pay to them instead of Kumasi. After Frederick James failed to provide a satisfactory explanation, the king reacted with rage. Bowdich and Hutchinson eventually concluded a treaty that satisfied the king's demands and recognized British control over natives in coast towns.

Later, in 1820, Consul Joseph Dupuis signed a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory. This was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and the British government in London. This tension led to a battle on January 21, 1824, at Essamako, where the Ashanti, numbering 10,000, defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy's force of 500. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was subsequently used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi.

The Ashanti's power over the coast waned after a signal defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826. By 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from administrator George Maclean for 600 oz. of gold and provide his son as a hostage.

## The Wars of the Late 19th Century
Hostilities resumed in the mid-19th century. Under Kwaka Dua I, the Ashanti tried to reassert authority over the Fanti in 1852, and a war broke out in 1863 over the refusal of the British to surrender a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief. Following the death of Kwaka Dua I in 1867 and a period of civil war, Kofi Karikari was enstooled, swearing that his business would be war.

The war of 1873-74 was triggered by two main events: the British transference of Elmina fort from the Dutch on April 2, 1872—which the Ashanti viewed as their territory—and the Ashanti captivity of four Europeans (including members of the Basel mission and a French trader). An Ashanti force invaded the British protectorate on January 22, 1873, defeating the Fanti. In response, the British dispatched Sir Garnet Wolseley with a force to march on Kumasi.

## Economy and Geography
The capital, Kumasi, serves as the distributing center for the hinterland. The region's economy is based on vegetable products, including the rubber tree, kola nuts, and cocoa (established since 1905), as well as tobacco and gum copal. Gold mining is a major industry, particularly in the western districts and the Obuassi mines. In 1905, gold output reached 68,259 oz. 

Revenue for the state is derived from liquor licenses, caravan taxes, government land rents, and contributions from gold-mining companies. The Ashanti are also noted for their skill in pottery, gold work, and weaving cotton.
