# The Influence of the French King on the Conspiracy of La Magione

## The Rise of Cesare Borgia
In October 1500, Cesare Borgia embarked on a campaign in the Romagna with an army of 10,000 men, supported by the friendship of Venice. During this campaign, the cities of Pesaro and Rimini opened their gates to him following the flight of Giovanni Sforza and Pandolfo Malatesta. The city of Faenza resisted due to the devotion of its people to their eighteen-year-old lord, Astorre Manfredi. Although Manfredi surrendered in April 1501 under a promise that his life would be spared, Cesare broke this word; Manfredi was sent to Rome as a prisoner, where he was put to death after being foully outraged.

By June, after the capture of Castel Bolognese, Cesare returned to Rome to engage in Spanish and French intrigues regarding the partition of Naples. Having acquired an extensive territory, he was created duke of Romagna by the pope. Cesare became a terror throughout Italy, characterized by an insatiable avidity, a total lack of scruple, and a morose, unsympathetic nature.

## The Conspiracy of La Magione
Despite his initial successes, including the conquest of Urbino and Camerino, Cesare's power was eventually shaken by a plot known as the conspiracy of La Magione, named for the castle near Perugia where the plotters convened. The conspiracy was formed by a coalition of those Cesare had deposed, the Orsinis, and several of his own captains. Among these captains were G.P. Baglioni, Oliverotto da Fermo, and Vitellozzo Vitelli, who had been granted estates but feared their loss.

This alliance led to risings in the Romagna and Urbino, resulting in the defeat of papal troops. At this juncture, Cesare found himself without allies, and it appeared that all of Italy was turning against the Borgia family.

## French Intervention and the Resolution
The tide of the conflict shifted when the French king promised help to Cesare. This promise was sufficient to frighten the confederates of the conspiracy into agreeing to terms. The archive notes that many of these conspirators lacked military or political skill and were prone to betraying one another.

Cesare, while maintaining a lack of trust in others, outmaneuvered the plotters. To demonstrate their repentance, several individuals—including Paolo Orsini, the duke of Gravina, Oliverotto, and Vitelli—seized Senigallia in Cesare's name, as the town still held for the duke of Urbino. Upon arriving at Senigallia, Cesare decoyed the unsuspecting *condottieri* into his house, where he had them arrested.
