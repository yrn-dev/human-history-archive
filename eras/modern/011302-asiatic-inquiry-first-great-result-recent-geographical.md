# Asiatic Inquiry and Geographical Research

Recent history has seen an unprecedented level of scientific enterprise directed toward the field of Asiatic inquiry. This period of research has focused heavily on geographical exploration, the refinement of cartography, and the classification of the diverse populations inhabiting the continent.

## Central Asian Orography and Cartography
One of the first significant results of recent geographical research has been the modification of previous ideas regarding the orography of the vast central region comprising Mongolia and Tibet. Researchers have now defined the great highland plateau with comparative geographical exactness; this region extends from the Himalaya northwards to Chinese Turkestan and from the frontier of Kashmir eastwards to China.

In modern map construction, the position of Sachu (or Saitu) in Mongolia serves as an obligatory point. While various values have been proposed—including those by Krishna (94 deg. 23'), Carey and Dalgleish (94 deg. 48'), Littledale (94 deg. 49'), and Kreitner (94 deg. 58')—the adopted longitude value is 94 deg. 54' E. of Greenwich, a revised value provided by Prjevalsky in the map accompanying his fourth exploration of central Asia. Prjevalsky's second and third explorations had previously suggested 94 deg. 26'.

Another obligatory point for cartography is Darchendo (or Tachienlu) on the extreme east. The Royal Geographical Society has adopted a value of 102 deg. 12", though other figures exist, such as those from Krishna (102 deg. 15"), Kreitner (102 deg. 5"), and Baber (102 deg. 18").

## Regional Surveys and Connectivity
Significant progress has been made in the geographical mapping of Western Asia. In Persia, steady scientific inquiry and the efforts of geographical explorers have resulted in a fairly complete geographical map. This includes detailed information on the routes and passes of the interior, the harbours and coast of the Persian Gulf, and the potential for commercial development via trade roads linking India, the Persian Gulf, the Karun, and the Caspian through Seistan.

In the context of Indian triangulation, minor extensions have pushed outwards through Baluchistan and Afghanistan to the eastern districts of Persia, and along the coast of Makran to Arabia. These surveys have also branched into Siam and western China, and have included the farther mountain peaks of Nepal. These efforts have raised the standard of accuracy in Asiatic surveys to levels considered unattainable fifty years prior.

Furthermore, a topographical connection has been established between Indian and Russian surveys. This proves that the deductive methods used by both nations to determine the co-ordinate values of fixed points are sufficiently aligned that no adjustment difficulties are expected for future Asiatic cartography. Additionally, the Madras observatory has been placed 2' 27" to the west of its previously adopted position based on latest determinations.

## Migration and Ethnography
Trans-border surveys to the west and north of India have established that central Asiatic immigration into the peninsula occurred through only two gateways: the west (Makran) and the north-west (the Kabul valley). The Kabul valley route, featuring passes across the Hindu Kush from the Kaoshan to the Khawak, served as the entry point for various central Asian hordes, including the Huns, Goths, Jats, Yue-chi, and Sacae. While some migrated from eastern Asia, none entered India via eastern passes, as the Himalayan barrier remained unoverflowed.

Ethnographic classification has also progressed regarding populations west of the Mongolian region:
*   **Tajiks:** Recognized as the Aryan population and descendants of the ancient Scyths or Sacae. Found in the central highlands, they form the underlying population of Afghanistan, Badakshan, Baluchistan, and Persia. Their language contains Aryan or Sanskrit words not found in Persian.
*   **Turks and Mixed Tribes:** The Turkoman is considered the purest form of the Turk element and tongue, compared to the more mixed dialect found at Constantinople. Some ethnographers have noted a connection between the Teutonic races of Europe and the central Asian Turkoman based on national customs.
*   **Kirghiz and Uzbegs:** These groups represent Turkish irruptions. The Kirghiz speak Turki and follow the religion of Mahomet. They are divided into the Kara Kirghiz (found in the Altai and Tian-shan valleys and identified as unmistakably Turkish) and the Kassak Kirghiz (more closely allied to the Mongol type).
*   **Kalmucks:** A Mongolian, Buddhist people who originated in Dzungaria, migrated to Siberia and the Lower Volga, and returned to Kulja under the Chinese in the late 18th century.

## Status of Knowledge
By the close of the 19th century, western Asia (excluding Arabia) was largely freed from geographical perplexity. However, enormous areas of eastern Siberia, Mongolia, and China remained in a state of "nebulous uncertainty" and primitive geographical knowledge.
