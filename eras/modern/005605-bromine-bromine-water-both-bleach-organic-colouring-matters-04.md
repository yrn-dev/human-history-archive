# Bromine

Bromine (symbol Br, atomic weight 79.96) is a chemical element belonging to the halogen group. Its name is derived from the Greek word *brômos*, meaning a stench, referring to its pungent and unpleasant smell. It was first isolated in 1826 by the French chemist Antoine Jerome Balard, who discovered the element in sea-water from the Mediterranean.

## Physical and Chemical Properties
At ordinary temperatures, bromine is a mobile liquid of a fine red colour, appearing almost black when in thick layers. It solidifies into a dark brown solid at -21° C. and boils at 59° C. (though measurements by Sir W. Ramsay and S. Young indicate boiling points of 57.65° C. when dried over sulphuric acid and 58.85° C. when dried over phosphorus pentoxide). Its vapour is deep red and possesses a directly poisonous and irritating effect on the respiratory organs.

Bromine has a specific gravity of 3.18828 (at 0/4°), a latent heat of fusion of 16.185 calories, and a latent heat of vaporization of 45.6 calories. It is readily soluble in ether, alcohol, and chloroform. It is also soluble in water (3.226 grammes per 100 grammes of solution at 15° C.), a solubility that is slightly increased by the presence of potassium bromide. This aqueous solution is orange-red and permanent in the dark, but decomposes into oxygen and hydrobromic acid when exposed to light. Cooling the solution can produce hyacinth-red octahedra of a crystalline hydrate (composition Br·4H₂O or Br₂·8H₂O).

Chemically, bromine's properties are generally intermediate between those of iodine and chlorine. It does not combine directly with carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen. Union with hydrogen requires a high temperature or a catalytic agent. Bromine reacts with other elements to form bromides, sometimes with explosive violence; for example, phosphorus inflames in its vapour and detonates in its liquid form. Potassium reacts energetically, while sodium must be heated to 200° C. to react.

## Compounds of Bromine
### Hydrobromic Acid and Bromides
Hydrobromic acid (HBr) is the only compound formed by bromine and hydrogen. It is a colourless gas at ordinary temperatures that fumes in moist air and has an acid taste. It can be condensed into a liquid (boiling point -64.9° C.) and further cooled into colourless crystals (melting point -88.5° C.). It is a strong acid and is readily soluble in water.

Bromides are formed when hydrobromic acid reacts with carbonates, hydroxides, and metallic oxides, or through the direct union of metals and bromine. Most metallic bromides are solids that volatilize and fuse upon heating. While most are water-soluble, lead bromide (soluble only in hot water), palladious bromide, mercurous bromide, and silver bromide are exceptions. Non-metallic bromides are typically liquids that decompose easily in water.

### Oxy-acids
While no oxides of bromine have been isolated, three oxy-acids are known:
*   **Hypobromous acid (HBrO):** An unstable compound obtained by distilling a mixture of bromine water and precipitated mercuric oxide. Its aqueous solution is light yellow and possesses strong bleaching properties.
*   **Bromous acid (HBrO₂):** Formed by adding bromine to a saturated solution of silver nitrate.
*   **Bromic acid (HBrO₃):** Known only as an unstable aqueous solution that decomposes into bromine, oxygen, and water when heated to 100° C. Its salts are called bromates.

## Manufacture and Occurrence
Bromine does not occur in nature in an uncombined state. It is found sparingly in combination with metals in river water, sea-water, mineral waters, and occasionally in animals and marine plants. The mineral bromargyrite is a combination of bromine and silver. The primary commercial sources are the brines of Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and West Virginia in the U.S.A., as well as salt deposits at Stassfurt in Prussian Saxony. Small amounts are also derived from kelp and the mother liquors of Chile saltpetre.

Industrial manufacture occurs primarily in the central district of Michigan and Stassfurt, utilizing the magnesium bromide found in "bittern." Two main processes are used:
1.  **Continuous Process:** Chlorine and steam are passed through a tower of broken bricks containing descending hot bittern, liberating bromine.
2.  **Periodic Process:** This involves the interaction of a bromide, sulphuric acid, and manganese dioxide (pyrolusite) in sandstone stills heated to 60° C.

## Applications
In analytical chemistry, the oxidizing action of bromine water is of chief use. Both bromine and bromine water are used to bleach organic colouring matters. In organic chemistry, bromine serves as an oxidizing and substituting agent and is used to prepare addition compounds. Sunlight can either accelerate, retard, or alter the products of bromine vapour's action on organic compounds.

Other applications include:
*   **Photography:** Extensive use of bromine salts, particularly silver bromide.
*   **Medicine:** Use of sodium, ammonium, and potassium bromides, as well as combinations with other substances and alkaloids.
*   **Antiseptics:** "Bromum solidificatum," consisting of bromine (about 75% by weight) impregnated into kieselguhr.
*   **Gold Extraction:** Proposed by R. Wagner and others, though high costs have limited its general use.
