# Bridgend and Barry

## Bridgend
Bridgend is a market town located in the southern parliamentary division of Glamorganshire, Wales. The town is situated on both sides of the river Ogwr, which gives the town its Welsh name, Penybont-ar-Ogwr. As of 1901, the population of the urban district was 6,062.

The town serves as an important center because it acts as the natural outlet for the mining valleys of the Garw, the Llynvi, and the two Ogwr rivers. These rivers converge approximately 3 miles north of the town and are linked to Bridgend via branch lines of the Great Western railway. Bridgend is also a railway junction, connecting the Great Western main line with the Barry Company's railway to Barry via Llantwit Major. Its station is located 165 miles from London on the South Wales trunk line of the Great Western railway.

Economically, Bridgend maintains a good market for agricultural produce. While the town lacks large manufacturing industries, it possesses a brewery, a tannery, a brass and iron foundry, and joinery works. Additionally, there are stone quarries and brick-works in the area, and lime is burnt extensively in the neighborhood. Public institutions located just outside the town at Parc Gwyllt and Angelton include the Glamorgan county lunatic asylums.

## Barry
Barry is a seaport and urban district in Glamorganshire, Wales, positioned on the Bristol Channel. It is located 8 miles southwest of Cardiff and 153 miles from London by rail. In 1901, the urban district had a population of 27,030, comprising the ecclesiastical parishes of Cadoxton, Barry, Merthyr-Dovan, and a portion of Sully. The latter includes Barry Island, which covers 194 acres and is now joined to the mainland. The growth of the area has been rapid; in 1881, the total population was approximately 500, with Barry village alone accounting for only 85 residents.

The town's name, as well as that of the island and the small brook (Barri) that flows into the sea, is believed to be derived from Baruch, a 7th-century Welsh saint who maintained a cell on the island. His chapel served as a place of pilgrimage during the middle ages and existed during the time of Leland. Giraldus noted that his own family took the name "de Barri" from the island, which they once owned. At the end of the 11th century, a follower of Fitzhamon settled in Barry and constructed a castle, of which only a gateway remains today.

Barry's religious landscape includes the rebuilt parish church of Barry and the old parish churches of Cadoxton and Merthyr-Dovan. There are four modern churches, one of which provides Welsh services, and approximately thirty nonconformist chapels, nearly a third of which hold services in Welsh. The town also features a Roman Catholic church and a church for Scandinavian and German seamen.

## Transportation and Infrastructure
Barry serves as a terminus on the Barry railway. This line begins at Hafod in the Rhondda Valley, where it joins the Taff Vale railway. The line has junctions at Cogan for Penarth and Cardiff, and at Treforest for Merthyr and Aberdare. It also connects with the Great Western main line at St Fagans and Peterstone. A branch from the main line at Tyn-y-caeau provides connections to the Brecon & Merthyr railway, the London & North-Western railway, and the Rhymney railway.

Furthermore, the Vale of Glamorgan railway, which is worked by the Barry company, has a junction with the Great Western railway at Bridgend. This provides a direct route from the Garw, Ogmore, and Llynvi coalfields to Barry.

## Llantwit Major
Llantwit Major (referred to as a corruption of Llanilltud or Bangor Illtud) is identified in the *Myvyrian Archaeology* as one of the principal *bangor* (college) institutions.
