# Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke

Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke (1678–1751), was an English writer and statesman. The son of Lady Mary Rich and Sir Henry St John, Bart., he was educated at Eton and traveled abroad in 1698 and 1699, where he gained an exceptional knowledge of the French language. In 1700, he married Frances, the daughter of Sir Henry Winchcombe, Bart.

## Diplomatic Missions to France
In August of an unspecified year, following his creation on July 7th as Baron St John of Lydiard Tregoze and Viscount Bolingbroke, St John traveled to France to conduct negotiations. During this mission, he signed an armistice between France and England on the 19th, which lasted for four months. These efforts eventually led to the signing of the Treaty of Utrecht on March 31, 1713, by all allies with the exception of the emperor. 

Bolingbroke is held primarily responsible for the manner of this peacemaking, which was characterized by a violation of solemn international assurances and a series of underhand conspiracies with the enemy rather than open conferences with allies. This process, along with the abandonment of the Catalans by the treaty, is noted as having left a lasting stain on national credit and honor. Additionally, a commercial treaty Bolingbroke established with France to create free trade was rejected in June.

## Political Career and Rivalries
Bolingbroke was a leading figure among the Tories, though his administration was marked by a dissolving friendship with Harley. In May, Harley was made lord treasurer and obtained the earldom of Oxford; St John felt greatly disappointed in July when he received only his viscountcy instead of an extinct family earldom and was passed over for the Garter.

As the health of the queen declined, Bolingbroke and Oxford both maintained secret communications with James, fearing their own downfall upon the accession of the elector of Hanover. Bolingbroke's political reputation was further complicated by his association with the actor Booth; Bolingbroke presented Booth with a purse of fifty guineas for "defending the cause of liberty against a perpetual dictator," referring to Marlborough. This occurred during a Whig-led demonstration of indignation against the peace.

## Exile and Later Life
Bolingbroke eventually retired to France in June, residing largely at the chateau of Argeville near Fontainebleau. During his time abroad, he wrote *The True Use of Retirement* and *Letters on the Study of History*. He also wrote *Reflexions upon Exile* and a 1717 letter to Sir W. Windham. While in France, he studied philosophy and criticized the chronology of the Bible, attracting the admiration of Voltaire.

In 1720, following the 1718 death of Lady Bolingbroke—whom he had treated with cruel neglect—he married Marie Claire Deschamps de Marcilly, the widow of the marquis de Villette. He resided at the estate of La Source near Orleans.

Bolingbroke sought a pardon from the English government and, in March 1716, declared his final abandonment of the Pretender. Although he refused to betray individuals or secrets, he received a pardon in 1723 through the duchess of Kendal, the king's mistress. He returned to London in June or July of that year.

## Final Years and Literary Contributions
Upon his return, Bolingbroke offered his services to Walpole, though they were received coldly. He later bought an estate at Dawley, near Uxbridge, where he renewed friendships with Voltaire, Swift, and Pope. He contributed the philosophy for the *Essay on Man* and attempted to organize a political opposition with Windham and Pulteney in 1726. He also wrote a series of letters to the *Craftsman* under the pseudonym "Occasional Writer" to attack the Walpoles.

In 1738, Bolingbroke visited England again as an adviser to Frederick, prince of Wales, writing *The Patriot King* for the occasion. After returning to France in 1739, he visited England again in 1742 and 1743. He finally settled at Battersea in 1744 with Hugh Hume, 3rd earl of Marchmont. In his final years, he assisted in negotiations for the "broad bottom" administration and recommended the tutor for the future George III.

Bolingbroke died on December 12, 1751. He and his wife were buried in the parish church at Battersea.
