# Agent to the Governor-General of India

In the administrative structure of the British Indian empire, the agent to the governor-general served as a high-level official overseeing specific provinces or agencies, often managing the complex relationship between the British paramount power and native states.

## Administrative Role and Authority
The Indian empire was divided for administrative purposes into four minor commissionerships and nine great provinces. Among the leaders of these nine great provinces was the agent to the governor-general, who specifically presided over the North-West Frontier Province. 

The supreme executive authority in India was vested in the viceroy in council, which consisted of the commander-in-chief and six ordinary members. For legislative purposes, the governor-general's council included fifteen additional members nominated by the crown. This council held the power, under certain restrictions, to create laws for British India, for native Indian subjects of the crown globally, and for British subjects residing within native states.

## The Baluchistan Agency
In Baluchistan, the agent to the governor-general exercised significant power. The region was divided into British Baluchistan—an official province consisting of the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali—and other territories that existed in varying degrees of independence but remained subject to British control. 

The Baluchistan Agency extended from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. Within this agency, the agent to the governor-general, supported by a staff of political assistants, practically exercised supreme control. The political status of the province as a whole was described as being almost precisely that of the native states found in the Indian peninsula.

The districts of British Baluchistan had originally been Afghan but were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, despite having been nominally handed over to Kalat forty years prior. Other areas within the agency included tribal areas held by the Bugti and Marri tribes, as well as native states such as Las Bela and Kalat (which included Kharan and Makran).

## The Bagelkhand Agency
In central India, the Bagelkhand agency operated under a different supervisory structure. It was placed under the political superintendence of the governor-general's agent for central India. Direct jurisdiction over the agency was held by a political agent who also served as the superintendent of the Rewa state and typically resided in Rewa or Sutna.

Established in March 1871, the Bagelkhand agency had previously been part of the Bundelkhand agency. It comprised the Rewa state and eleven estates and minor states, with Sohawal, Nagode, and Maihar being the most important. The region, occupied by the Baghelas (a branch of the Sisodhyia Rajputs who had migrated from Gujarat), covered a total area of 14,323 square miles.

## Political Administration of Native States
More broadly, the political administration of native states across India was managed through British agents stationed at principal native courts. The specific position and authority of these agents varied depending on the relationship between the individual principality and the paramount British power.
