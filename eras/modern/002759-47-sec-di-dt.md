# Mathematical and Physical Applications of Secant and Differential Equations

The provided archive records contain various mathematical equations and physical applications involving the secant function ($\sec i$) and differentials, specifically within the context of trajectory calculations and the determination of pseudo-velocity.

## Trajectory Equations and Integration
In the study of trajectories, equations are used to connect the variables $q$ and $i$. The archive details several differential relationships used to determine the values of time ($t$), position ($x, y$), and the angle ($i$). These include:

*   The relationship $\frac{dt}{dq} = \frac{-C \sec i}{f(q \sec i)}$
*   The relationship $\frac{dx}{dq} = \frac{-C q \sec i}{f(q \sec i)}$
*   The relationship $\frac{dy}{dq} = \frac{-C q \sec i \tan i}{f(q \sec i)}$
*   The differential $\frac{di}{dq} = \frac{Cg}{q \sec i \cdot f(q \sec i)}$
*   The differential $\frac{d \tan i}{dq} = \frac{C g \sec i}{q \cdot f(q \sec i)}$

The values for $t, x, y, i,$ and $\tan i$ are obtained through integration with respect to $q$, provided that $\sec i$ is given as a function of $q$. However, these integrations are described as "quite intractable," even when utilizing simple mathematical assumptions for the function $f(v)$, such as a cubic law ($f(v) = v^3/k$) or a quadratic law ($f(v) = v^2/k$).

## Siacci's Pseudo-Velocity
To resolve the difficulties associated with these intractable integrations, a method originally pointed out by Euler is employed. This method observes that in ordinary trajectories, the quantities $i, \cos i,$ and $\sec i$ vary slowly enough to be replaced by their mean values, denoted as $[\eta], \cos [\eta],$ and $\sec [\eta]$. This is particularly effective when a considerable trajectory is divided into arcs of small curvature, where curvature is defined as the angle between the normals or tangents at the ends of the arc.

By replacing the angle $i$ with the mean value $[\eta]$, Siacci's pseudo-velocity ($u$) is introduced and defined by the equation:
$u = q \sec [\eta]$

In this context, $u$ serves as a quasi-component parallel to the mean direction of the tangent, such as the direction of the arc's chord.

## High Angle Fire Calculations
When applying Siacci's method to high angle fire trajectories via successive arcs of small curvature, the following procedures are used:

1.  **Approximation of Mean Angle**: For an arc between angles $[\phi]$ and $[\theta]$, the mean angle $[\eta]$ is approximated as $[\eta] = \frac{1}{2}([\phi] + [\theta])$.
2.  **Pseudo-Velocity Calculation**: The initial pseudo-velocity $u_{[\phi]}$ is calculated using $u_{[\phi]} = v_{[\phi]} \cos [\phi] \sec [\eta]$.
3.  **Final Pseudo-Velocity**: The value of $u_{[\theta]}$ is determined using formulae such as $I(u_{[\theta]}) = I(u_{[\phi]}) - \frac{\tan [\phi] - \tan [\theta]}{C \sec [\eta]}$ or $D(u_{[\theta]}) = D(u_{[\phi]}) - \frac{[\phi]deg - [\theta]deg}{C \cos [\eta]}$.

Furthermore, the time and distance for the arc are calculated as:
*   Time: $[\phi]t_{[\theta]} = C[T(u_{[\phi]}) - T(u_{[\theta]})]$
*   Distance: $[\phi]x_{[\theta]} = C \cos [\eta] [S(u_{[\phi]}) - S(u_{[\theta]})]$
*   Position ratio: $[\phi](y/x)_{[\theta]} = \tan [\phi] - C \sec [\eta] [I(u_{[\phi]}) - \frac{\Delta A}{\Delta S}]$, where $\Delta$ represents a finite tabular difference of the function between the final and initial pseudo-velocity.
