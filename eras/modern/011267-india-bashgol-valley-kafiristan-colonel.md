# The Bashgol Valley and Kafiristan

The Bashgol valley is located within the region of Kafiristan, a territory characterized by rugged, snow-capped altitudes and hidden valleys that are largely isolated from one another. This region forms part of the southern edge of a great central upheaval or plateau, with the Hindu Kush representing its boundary. The topography of the northern hills and valleys of Kafiristan is considered analogous to the mountain districts of Badakshan.

## Geographical Context and Exploration
Kafiristan is situated in the highlands of the Hindu Kush, where the water-divide from the Dorah to the Khawak pass passes through the center of the region. To the west of the Kunar valley lie the mountains of Kafiristan, known as Kashmund, which reach altitudes of 14,000 feet. The region is bordered by the Kunar river system to the south and west, and is separated from Chitral and Bajour by the water-parting of the Hindu Kush.

Knowledge of this region was expanded through various missions and surveys. A mission led by Captain (later Sir William) Lockhart crossed the Hindu Kush into Wakhan and returned to India via the Bashgol valley of Kafiristan. This expedition provided Colonel Woodthorpe with the opportunity to determine the conformation of the Hindu Kush and verify previous explorations conducted by W.W. M'Nair. Additionally, scientific geographers such as Sir George Robertson and Cockerill contributed to the mapping of Kafiristan and the Hindu Kush.

## The Kafir Community and the Lower Bashgol
The Kafirs are described as remnants of old races who were driven into the higher mountains by Mahommedan invasions. In the lower Bashgol, a section of the Kafir community known as Kamdesh maintains a tradition of Greek origin, claiming the same ancestry as the Nysaeans. This group continues to chant hymns to a god who sprang from Gir Nysa (the mountain of Nysa) and asserts that they originally migrated to their current habitat in the lower Bashgol from the Swat country.

Historically, the Kafirs or Nysaeans maintained their culture in the lower Kunar valley and Bajour during a period when Buddhism had spread to Gilgit, Dir, Swat, and Chitral. In these specific areas, Buddhism apparently never prevailed. It is probable that the invader Baber fought the Kafirs in Bajour during the early 16th century while on his way to establish the Mogul dynasty in India.

## Regional Environment and Borders
The environment surrounding the Kafir hills is characterized by diverse flora. The Pechdara, a chief tributary from the Kafiristan hills, originates among forests of deodar and pine, as well as oaks, wild figs, olives, pomegranates, and thick tangles of ivy and wild vine. 

Politically and administratively, the region has seen shifts in control. The Kafirs of the wild valleys in the Kashmund mountains have been rapidly submitting to Afghan rule. The boundaries of the region are defined by the water-divide of the Hindu Kush, which separates Kafiristan from Chitral and Bajour. This demarcation helps maintain a belt of independent territory—including Bajour and Swat—between the boundaries of Afghanistan and British India.

## Linguistic Classification
The dialects spoken in the north-western frontier, including those of Kafiristan, Gilgit, Dardistan, and Chitral, have been studied by Dr. Grierson. His research suggests that these "Pisaca Languages" constitute a separate group that descended from Aryan but remains independent of both Iranian and Sanskrit influences.
