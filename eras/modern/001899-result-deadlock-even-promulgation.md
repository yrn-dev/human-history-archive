# Deadlock and Promulgation in Historical Contexts

The concepts of deadlock—a state of stalemate or impasse—and promulgation—the formal proclamation or reading of a law, doctrine, or text—appear across various historical, political, and religious records. These terms describe the friction of governance and the formalization of authority.

## Political and Diplomatic Deadlocks

In the context of early 20th-century international relations, deadlocks often arose from conflicting national interests and military laws. A complete deadlock was reached at an early stage of negotiations involving Germany, specifically regarding the German Navy Law. While there were efforts to placate Germany and create a friendly spirit through arrangements in the colonial sphere, the Navy Law was not to be withdrawn, only modified. Such a situation risked terminating the Entente and could have deprived certain powers of the ability to aid France in a war that Germany might allege was "forced" upon it due to a quarrel between Russia and Austria.

Within the British government, internal administrative deadlocks also occurred, such as the deadlock concerning the War Staff Policy involving Sir Arthur Wilson, the First Sea Lord, and Lord Fisher of Kilverstone.

In military strategy during the first world war, the Committee of Imperial Defence considered the possibility of a deadlock arising in both the Western and Eastern theatres during the winter. Such a stalemate might have allowed Germany to attempt a diversion by sending a regular invading army across the North Sea to England. However, the Admiralty viewed this as unlikely, noting that the Germans' absolute reliance on artillery would make maintaining an ammunition supply nearly impossible without allowing the British Fleet to intervene.

## Mechanisms for Resolving Deadlock

Ancient democracies developed specific institutions to break political impasses. In Athens and Miletus, the practice of ostracism was used during times of conflict. According to Gilbert Murray, this was a method for arriving at a decision when political feeling was so divided that it threatened a deadlock. Because these democracies lacked a regular government in office or a regular opposition, a strong leader could block popular policy. Ostracism allowed the community to make the least trusted or least popular chief leader retire for ten years without loss of property or honour. As an example, it is suggested that if the British Empire had faced such a deadlock over the 1914 question of Home Rule for Ireland, a Greek democracy might have ostracized Sir Edward Carson to proceed with the Home Rule Bill.

## Promulgation of Laws and Doctrines

Promulgation serves as the formal act of making a decree, law, or theory known. In the Grand-duchy of Baden, the issue of succession led to the issuance of a pragmatic sanction (_Hausgesetz_) in 1817 by Grand-duke Charles. This act promulgated that the counts of Hochberg—the result of a morganatic marriage between Grand-duke Charles Frederick and Luise Geyer von Geyersberg—were capable of succeeding to the crown. Later, in 1818, a liberal constitution was granted to the grand-duchy under article xiii. of the Act of Confederation, establishing two chambers whose assent was necessary for taxation and legislation.

In religious and ecclesiastical history, the assembly of Basel utilized a system of four "deputations" (committees) focusing on peace, faith, reform, and common concerns. Decisions made by three of these deputations were ratified in general congregation and led to decrees that were promulgated in session.

In the field of medicine, Samuel Hahnemann promulgated the "doctrine of similars" and a theory regarding the origin of chronic diseases. He asserted that such diseases derived from sycosis (fig-wart disease), syphilis (venereal disease), or psora (the itch). This hypothesis was later discarded by many of his followers and viewed as untenable by advancing science.

## The Qur'an on Promulgation

The Qur'an addresses the process of its own revelation and proclamation. The text teaches that it is the responsibility of God to collect and promulgate the revelation. It instructs the Prophet not to move his tongue in haste to recite the text, as the collecting and the reciting (promulgating) devolve upon God. Once the text has been promulgated, the instruction is to follow its recital. Furthermore, the text asserts that the explanation of the revelation also rests with God.

## Early Grecian Governance

In the legendary age of primitive Grecian communities, meetings were convoked as a means for kings to promulgate their intentions to the people. These gatherings, as described in the Homeric poems, functioned primarily as opportunities for the king to be advised rather than as restraints on his power. In the council, the resolutions of leaders like Agamemnôn or Hectôr typically prevailed. The council was a purely consultative body where members were subordinates to the presiding member, and it lacked the power to peremptorily arrest the resolves of the king.
