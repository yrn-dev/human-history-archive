# Fisher, Clarke, and the Unionist Administration

## The Triumvirate and Military Reform
In November 1903, a "triumvirate" consisting of Sir John Fisher, Sir George Clarke, and Lord Esher was appointed. This group produced a report that led to reforms of the highest importance under the administration of Mr. Balfour. These reforms included the institution of the new Army Council in 1904 and the establishment of the permanent Committee of Imperial Defence. 

Sir George Clarke had previously contributed to the literature on British imperialism, having authored the work *Imperial Defence* in 1897. Sir John Fisher later served as First Sea Lord and held positions at the Admiralty.

## The Unionist Administration under Mr. Balfour
The Unionist regime was characterized by Mr. Balfour's leadership, which was noted for his oratorical ability, intellectual *finesse*, a wide range of interests and knowledge, and a statesmanlike grasp of imperial problems. His tenure in foreign affairs was conspicuously successful, leaving the country in a position of strength abroad not seen since the Crimean War.

Despite these successes, the Unionist government eventually collapsed. The administration's ministers were described as having become "stale." Public dissatisfaction grew due to the retention of heavy taxation from the war years, which particularly disgusted income-tax payers. Additionally, the government faced challenges in the constituencies regarding labour questions, the Education Act, and the 1904 introduction of Chinese labour into South Africa.

## Internal Unionist Dissensions and Fiscal Conflict
The downfall of Mr. Balfour's administration was precipitated by internal dissensions over the fiscal question. During 1903, a struggle persisted within the Unionist party regarding the budget. Mr. Chamberlain advocated for a reorganization of the budget to increase taxes on imports, including food, with the goal of stimulating employment and improving the position of the working classes.

In opposition to Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Reform league, free-trade Unionists—represented primarily by Lord Goschen, the duke of Devonshire, Lord Hugh Cecil, and Lord James—formed a Free Food league. At a meeting on November 24, these free-trade Unionists rejected food taxes, discredited Chamberlain's statistics, and criticized Mr. Balfour's assumption that retaliation would be desirable. 

Mr. Balfour's own position was complicated by his "dialectical dexterity" in avoiding a definite expression of his fiscal opinions, a trait that drew contemptuous criticism from the Liberal press. While he did not fully support Mr. Chamberlain's "whole hog" approach, he also did not discourage the campaign. In December, a Tariff Commission was appointed by Mr. Chamberlain.

## Transition to Liberal Government
The Unionist regime's collapse resulted in an electoral defeat that weakened Mr. Balfour's public confidence. In late November 1905, Mr. Balfour tendered his resignation as Prime Minister to the King. He was succeeded by the government of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, which was formed in January.

This new Liberal government sought to unite the wings of the party that had been divided by the Boer War. The administration included Liberal Imperialists such as Sir Edward Grey (Foreign Office), Mr. Haldane (Secretary of State for War), and Mr. Asquith (Exchequer). It also included figures who had condemned the war in South Africa, such as Mr. John Morley (Secretary of State for India) and Sir Robert Reid (Lord Chancellor), as well as democratic politicians like Mr. John Burns and Mr. Lloyd George.
