# Arbitrary Constants and Constant Values in Technical Analysis

In various fields of mathematical, physical, and analytical study, the concept of a constant—specifically an arbitrary constant—serves as a fundamental tool for defining functions, solving differential equations, and calculating physical trajectories.

## Ballistics and Time Calculation
In the calculation of ballistic tables, the time $T(v)$ at any velocity $v$ is determined by the sum of preceding values of $\Delta T$ plus an arbitrary constant. This relationship is expressed as $T(v) = \sum(\Delta v)/gp + \text{a constant}$, or as the integral $\int dv/gp + \text{a constant}$, where $p$ is known as a function of $v$. 

Because the calculation of the difference between two tabular values—specifically for an initial velocity $V$ and a final velocity $v$—is the only requirement, the arbitrary constant may be any number. This allows for the determination of $T(V) - T(v) = \sum_{v:V} \Delta v/gp$ or $\int_{v:V} dv/gp$. For a shot with a ballistic coefficient $C$, the time is expressed as $t = C[T(V) - T(v)]$.

## Orbital Elements and Integration
In the analytic process of determining planetary orbits, specific data known as elements of the orbit appear as arbitrary constants introduced through integration. In instances involving two differential equations of the second order, four such constants (denoted as $a, b, c, d$) are present. 

The result of this integration allows the coordinates $x$ and $y$, as well as their derivatives relative to time (which express speed, direction of motion, and position), to be found as functions of time and these four constants. The general solutions are represented as $x = f_1(a, b, c, d, t)$ and $y = f_2(a, b, c, d, t)$. These arbitrary constants $a, b, c,$ and $d$ are either the elements of the orbit themselves or quantities from which those elements can be derived.

## Lubrication and Surface Geometry
Arbitrary constants are used to define the thickness of films in lubrication problems. When surfaces are plane, the general solution involves a single arbitrary constant: the least thickness ($h_0$). 

The number of constants increases based on the curvature of the surfaces:
*   **Two arbitrary constants** ($h_0$ and $\phi$, the angular position of $h_0$ relative to the direction of load) are required when both surfaces are curved in the plane of motion.
*   **Three arbitrary constants** ($h_0, \phi_0, z_0$) are required if both surfaces are curved in both the plane of motion and a plane perpendicular to the direction of motion.

## Mathematical Functions and Integration
In the general notion of functionality, a function is viewed as an indefinitely extended table where it remains arbitrary what values the function possesses at any point in the domain of the argument.

In the context of integration:
*   **Integraphs:** While an integrator determines a definite integral (a mere constant), an integraph provides the value of an indefinite integral, which is a function of $x$. Analytically, if $Y = \int y dx + \text{const}$, an arbitrary constant length $a$ is introduced as the unit to which the integraph draws the curve.
*   **Conformal Representation:** In mapping the interior of a region $R$ onto a circle, an arbitrary interior point $c$ of $R$ corresponds to the center of the circle. An arbitrary constant arising in the determination of the function $Q$ allows an arbitrary point of the boundary of $R$ to correspond to an arbitrary point on the circle's circumference.
*   **Algebraical Integrals:** In the study of algebraical integrals, rational functions can be constructed with poles of the first order at $p + 1$ arbitrary positions. Similarly, integrals of the second kind can be found with one pole of the first order at an arbitrary position, and integrals of the third kind can have two logarithmic infinities at arbitrary positions.

## Constant Values in Particle Observation
In observations of particles, a fairly constant number $C$ is obtained when the number of particles is multiplied by the limit of visibility. Data from May 1889 to May 1893 shows that this constant $C$ varies based on wet-bulb depression:
*   **2 deg. to 4 deg.:** Mean value of $C$ is 76,058.
*   **4 deg. to 7 deg.:** Mean value of $C$ is 105,545.
*   **7 deg. and over:** Mean value of $C$ is 141,148.
