# The Netherlands

The history of the Netherlands is characterized by long-standing religious conflicts, struggles for political autonomy from the Spanish Empire, and strategic diplomatic treaties aimed at securing its borders against foreign aggression.

## Religious and Political Conflict under Spanish Rule
During the sixteenth century, Protestant doctrines spread into the Netherlands under the reign of Charles V. Although Charles persecuted these movements with vigor, he handed the task of governance to his son, Philip II, in 1556. Under Philip II, tensions escalated due to both religious persecution and the king's spirited foreign policy. Because Philip was engaged in a war with France, he required supplies from the Netherlandish townsmen and noblemen, leading to a popular resistance.

This resistance was led by the Counts of Egmont and Horn, and William the Silent, Prince of Orange. While the great nobles were not initially Protestants, they adopted the faith as the struggle grew more bitter. Philip II, viewing himself as the absolute defender of Catholicism in Europe, determined to suppress all attempts at revolt. This policy ultimately resulted in the loss of the northern part of the Netherlands and the founding of the separate state of the United Provinces (Holland), which became an overwhelmingly Protestant, independent country.

In contrast, the southern region (Belgium) remained Catholic and subject to the Spanish branch of the Hapsburgs. The struggle left the land ruined; the population was reduced by at least 50 percent, trade and industry were largely destroyed, and the University of Louvain barely escaped ruin. In 1598, Philip II attempted to restore unity by erecting the Netherlands into an independent state under his daughter Isabella and her husband, Archduke Albert of Austria, though the region remained under significant Spanish influence.

## The Barrier Treaties
In the eighteenth century, the United Netherlands entered into a series of diplomatic agreements with Great Britain known as the Barrier Treaties. These treaties were designed to secure Holland against aggression from France.

The first Barrier Treaty, signed on October 29, 1709, established a mutual guarantee: the states-general of the United Netherlands guaranteed the Protestant succession in England in favor of the house of Hanover. In return, Great Britain undertook to procure an adequate "barrier" for the Dutch. This barrier consisted of several towns, including Lille, Tournai, Namur, Ypres, Menin, Charleroi, Valenciennes, Maubeuge, Conde, Halle, Damme, Dendermond, Furnes, Nieuport, and the citadel of Ghent.

A second Barrier Treaty was signed on January 29, 1713, which reduced the number of strong places in the barrier to the citadel of Ghent, Tournai, Menin, Ypres, Furnes, the fort of Knocke, Charleroi, Mons, and certain fortresses near Bruges. Great Britain agreed to obtain the right for the Dutch to garrison these locations from the future sovereign of the Spanish Netherlands. These terms were later included in the treaty of Rastatt on March 7, 1714. A third Barrier Treaty followed in November 1715.

## New Netherlands
In the seventeenth century, the Dutch established the New Netherlands (modern-day New York). The Dutch authorities provided guarantees of religious liberty to some settlers. For example, Lady Deborah Moody, an antipaedobaptist from Massachusetts, settled at Gravesend in 1643 under such a guarantee. However, religious tolerance was not absolute; in 1656, Francis Doughty, an English Baptist, was banished after baptizing converts. Similarly, William Wickenden was heavily fined and banished in the same year after administering ordinances at Flushing.

## Later Political Transitions
The political landscape of the region shifted again in the nineteenth century with the creation of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands in 1814. This arrangement lumped together the Protestant Dutch with the French-speaking Catholics of the old Spanish (Austrian) Netherlands. This union destroyed the previous Dutch Republic. During this era, the ruler of the Netherlands also served as the ruler of Luxembourg, which was included in the German confederation despite its ruler's nationality and the fact that many of its people spoke French.
