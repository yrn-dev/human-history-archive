# The Mekong River Boundary: Shan States and Tongking

The Mekong river serves as a critical geographical and political marker in Southeast Asia, specifically defining the limits of occupation between British and French interests. For a distance of approximately 200 miles, the channel of the river acts as the boundary between the Shan States, part of British India, and Tongking, a region associated with French interests.

## Geographical Demarcation
The eastern boundaries of Burma are defined by various natural landmarks. In the north, near the termination of the Burmese railway system at a point level in latitude with Mogaung, the boundary is marked by the eastern watershed of the Nmaikha, which is the eastern of the two great northern affluents of the Irrawaddy. From there, the boundary follows an irregular course southwards to a position south-east of Bhamo (latitude 24 deg.), where it defines the northern edge of the Shan States.

The boundary eventually strikes the Mekong river at approximately latitude 21 deg. 45'. From this point southwards, the river channel defines the limits of occupation between British and French subjects for some 200 miles. This boundary persists until approximately the parallel of 20 deg. N. lat., at which point the Burmese boundary leaves the Mekong to run westwards toward the Salween.

## The Shan States
The Shan States comprise the entire eastern portion of the province of Burma, covering an area of some 59,915 sq. m. They were declared part of British India by notification in 1886. The region is characterized by a mass of rugged hills and narrow gorges, though it contains some fertile and well-populated open plateaus.

### Administration and Law
Under the Shan States Act of 1888, administration—including civil, criminal, and revenue matters—is vested in the chief of the state, subject to the restrictions of a patent or *sanad*. The legal system is based on the customary law of each state, provided it aligns with justice, equity, and good conscience and does not oppose the spirit of the law in the rest of British India.

The chiefs are divided into three classes:
1. **Sawbwas**: There are fifteen sawbwas.
2. **Myosas**: There are sixteen myosas.
3. **Ngwegunhmus**: There are thirteen ngwegunhmus, found only in the Myelat (the border country between Burma and the southern Shan States).

General control over criminal justice is exercised by superintendents, who possess wide revisionary powers. Criminal jurisdiction is withdrawn from the chiefs and vested in superintendents or assistant superintendents in cases involving government servants, Europeans, Americans, British subjects who are not natives of a Shan State, or when such individuals are complainants or defendants. Superintendents and assistant superintendents do not have the power to try civil suits.

## Keng Tung
Keng Tung is the most extensive of the Shan States, with an area exceeding 12,000 sq. m. It is located in the charge of the southern Shan States and lies primarily east of the Salween river. Known to the Chinese as Mêng Kêng and to the Burmese as "the 32 cities of the Gôn" (Hkön), its classical name is Khemarata or Khemarata Tungkapuri.

### Geography and Hydrography
Keng Tung is bounded to the north by the states of Mang Lön, Möng Lem, and Keng Hung (the latter two under Chinese control). To the east, it is bounded by the Mekong river, beyond which lies French Lao territory. To the south are the Siamese Shan States, and to the west is generally the Salween river.

The state's terrain is dominated by mountainous country, with parallel hill ranges running north and south. Elevations are generally no lower than 5,000 ft., with some peaks exceeding 7,000 ft. The state is divided by a high, continuous watershed:
* **Mekong Basin**: Approximately 63% of the area. Chief tributaries include the Nam Lwe (navigable in the interior), Nam Nga, Nam Yawng, Nam Lin, Nam Hôk, and Nam Kôk.
* **Salween Basin**: Approximately 37% of the area. Chief tributaries include the Nam Hka and Nam Hsim, both of which are unnavigable due to rocks and rapids.

## Regional Context
The broader region is defined by high ranges extending south from the Tibetan mountains. Between these ranges flow the great rivers of the Indo-Chinese peninsula: the Mekong, the Menam, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy. These river valleys form the primary portions of Burma, Siam (including Laos), and Cochin-China (which includes Cambodia and Tongking). The people of Cochin-China are known as Anam.
