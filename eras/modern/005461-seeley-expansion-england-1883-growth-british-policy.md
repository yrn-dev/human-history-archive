# J.R. Seeley and the Literature of British Imperialism

## Overview of Imperial Literature
In recent years, the subject of British imperialism has inspired an expanding body of literature. This scholarly growth includes a variety of works focusing on different aspects of the empire, ranging from historical geography to the specifics of imperial defense and federation. Among the important works consulted on these topics are Sir C.P. Lucas's *Historical Geography of the British Colonies* (1888, et seq.), H.E. Egerton's *Short History of British Colonial Policy* (1897), and H.J. Mackinder's *Britain and the British Seas* (1902). Other notable contributions include Sir Charles Dilke's *Greater Britain* (1869), *Problems of Greater Britain* (1890), and *The British Empire* (1899), as well as works on imperial federation by G.R. Parkin (1892) and Sir John Colomb (1886).

## The Works of Sir J.R. Seeley
Sir J.R. Seeley is a significant figure in this literary movement. He authored two key texts that contribute to the understanding of the empire's development: *Expansion of England*, published in 1883, and *Growth of British Policy*, published in 1895. These works exist alongside other specialized texts of the era, such as Sir G.S. Clarke's *Imperial Defence* (1897) and J.L. Garvin's *Imperial Reciprocity* (1903). While much of the literature focuses on the expansion and maintenance of the empire, the archive also notes the existence of anti-imperialist perspectives, specifically J.A. Hobson's *Imperialism* (1902).

## Evolution of British Colonial Policy
The trajectory of British colonial policy was significantly influenced by foundational state papers. A primary example is Lord Durham's "Report on the Affairs of British North America," which was laid before parliament on January 31, 1839. This report is regarded as one of the greatest state papers in the English language and established principles that guided British colonial policy from that time forward.

The practical application of colonial growth was often tied to technological and economic shifts. Apart from India, significant European imperial expansion was largely dependent on the effective use of steamships and railways. In Canada, the railway allowed for westward expansion and the marketing of produce in Europe, while a new constitution in 1867 created a Federal Dominion to relieve internal strains between British and French inhabitants. Similarly, the Australian settlements gained new importance following the discovery of gold in 1851 and copper mines in 1842, aided by transport improvements that made wool more marketable. In the Pacific, New Zealand was added to the colonial possessions of the British Crown in 1840, following the earlier formation of a New Zealand Land Company.

## British Policy and International Relations
British policy during the late 19th and early 20th centuries was characterized by a period of "placid and tolerant detachment" from Continental European affairs. The British Empire viewed the colonial expansion of Germany with easy indifference, and the two nations maintained a strong commercial connection, serving as each other's best customers in Europe. 

Despite trade rivalries and temporary tensions—such as the 1896 telegram from the German Emperor to President Kruger regarding the Jameson Raid—Britain maintained sufficient naval superiority to avoid being disquieted by France or Germany. For a time, the British Foreign Office even proposed transforming the alliance between Britain and Japan into a Triple Alliance that included Germany in 1901.

However, this detachment shifted as Germany expanded its navy. While some in England initially debated whether the German menace was real, a general agreement eventually formed among leading figures across all political parties. This shift in opinion was reinforced by German naval construction at Kiel and Wilhelmshaven, which served to unite British opinion and strengthen the coalition of nations intended to resist Germany. By 1911, British policy remained focused on maintaining the supremacy of the sea and avoiding territorial expansion, a position communicated to the Turkish government to maintain old friendships and the existing status quo.
