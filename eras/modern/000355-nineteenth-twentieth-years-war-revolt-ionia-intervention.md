# Nineteenth and Twentieth Years of the War—Revolt of Ionia—Intervention

The nineteenth and twentieth years of the Peloponnesian War were characterized by strategic maneuvers in Ionia, the involvement of Persian emissaries, and the shifting alliances of various Greek city-states and islands.

## The Nineteenth Year and the Ionian Expedition
During the nineteenth year of the war, a competition emerged at Lacedaemon regarding the priority of military deployment. The central dispute was whether to send a fleet and army first to the Hellespont or to Chios and Ionia. The Lacedaemonians ultimately favored Tissaphernes and the Chians, a position supported by Alcibiades. Alcibiades held a personal connection to the ephors of that year, specifically Endius, whose family name was also Alcibiades.

To verify the claims of the Chians regarding the strength of their city and the size of their fleet, the Lacedaemonians dispatched Phrynis, a member of the Perioeci. Upon Phrynis confirming that the reports were truthful, Lacedaemon entered into an alliance with the Erythraeans and the Chians. The Lacedaemonians voted to provide forty ships to supplement the sixty ships already present on the island. Although the original plan was to send ten of these ships from Laconia under the admiral Melanchridas, an earthquake occurred, leading to the appointment of Chalcideus and a reduction of the Laconian contingent to five ships.

## Intrigues of Alcibiades
Alcibiades played a pivotal role in persuading Endius and the other ephors to proceed with the expedition. He argued that the voyage could be completed before the Chians became aware of any fleet misfortunes. Alcibiades claimed that once he arrived in Ionia, he could easily persuade the cities to revolt by emphasizing the zeal of Lacedaemon and the weakness of the Athenians. 

In private, Alcibiades appealed to the ambition of Endius, suggesting it would be more glorious for Endius to be the catalyst for the Ionian revolt and the alliance between the King and Lacedaemon than to leave such an honor to Agis, who was an enemy of Alcibiades. Consequently, Alcibiades set sail with Chalcideus and the five ships.

## Naval Engagements and the Revolt of Euboea
During this period, sixteen Peloponnesian ships returning from Sicily under Gylippus were intercepted off Leucadia by twenty-seven Athenian vessels commanded by Hippocles, son of Menippus. The Peloponnesian fleet was roughly handled and lost one ship before the remainder escaped to Corinth.

Simultaneously, the Boeotians utilized treachery to capture Oropus, which was then held by an Athenian garrison. This action was supported by Oropians and some Eretrians who were plotting a revolt in Euboea, as the Athenian presence at Oropus was a source of annoyance to Eretria. Following the capture of Oropus, the Eretrians traveled to Rhodes to invite the Peloponnesians into Euboea.

## The Twentieth Year and the Rhodian Intervention
As the twentieth year of the war drew to a close, the Peloponnesians focused on the relief of the Chians. They sailed from Rhodes and sighted the Athenian fleet near Triopium. The Athenian fleet was sailing from Chalce; however, neither side attacked the other. The Athenians arrived at Samos while the Peloponnesians arrived at Miletus, concluding that Chios could not be relieved without a battle.

The Peloponnesians subsequently determined to sail to Rhodes following an invitation from several principal men of the island. Their goal was to secure an island with significant land forces and seamen, which would allow them to maintain their fleet through their own confederacy rather than relying on financial subsidies from Tissaphernes. 

The Peloponnesians sailed from Cnidus with ninety-four ships and arrived at Camirus. The inhabitants, who were unaware of the intrigue, fled because the town was unfortified. The Lacedaemonians eventually assembled the residents of Camirus, Ialysus, and Lindus, persuading the Rhodians to revolt against the Athenians. As a result, the island shifted its allegiance to the Peloponnesians. The Athenians attempted to forestall this move by sailing from Samos, but they arrived too late. Subsequently, the Athenians waged war against Rhodes, operating from Samos, Cos, and Chalce.
