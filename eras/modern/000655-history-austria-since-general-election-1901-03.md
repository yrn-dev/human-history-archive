# History of Austria since the General Election of 1901

## Political Reform and Parliamentary Stability
Following the general election of 1901, the history of Austria was characterized by efforts toward franchise reform. This was viewed as a crowning attempt to restore the parliament to normal working conditions. During this period, the premier, Dr. von Koerber, sought to overcome political obstruction and hoped to establish a compromise between the Czechs and Germans.

To secure the sanction of the Chamber for the contingent of recruits, the estimates, and other "necessities of state" for 1901 and 1902, Dr. von Koerber promised the implementation of extensive public works projects that would serve the interests of both Germans and Czechs.

## Public Works and Infrastructure
The Koerber administration proposed several major canal projects to improve navigation and connectivity. These included:
* A canal connecting the Danube to the Oder.
* A ship canal from the Danube to the Moldau near Budweis, along with the canalization of the Moldau from Budweis to Prague.
* A ship canal from the projected Danube-Oder canal near Prerau to the Elbe near Pardubitz, including the canalization of the Elbe from Pardubitz to Melnik.
* A navigable connection linking the Danube-Oder Canal with the Dniester and the Vistula.

The construction of these four canals was estimated to take twenty years, funded by a 4% loan with an amortization period of ninety years.

Additionally, the cabinet proposed and the Chamber sanctioned a "second railway route to Trieste." This line was intended to shorten the distance between Salzburg, South Germany, and the Adriatic by passing under the southern and central Alpine ranges of Austria. The project involved boring chief tunnels through the Wochein, Karawanken, and Tauern hills. While the line is considered one of Europe's most interesting railway routes, the total cost greatly exceeded the original parliamentary estimate. Some contend that the adoption of the 1901-1902 Budget cost the state £100,000,000 for public works. These projects were often hastily begun or promised due to political expediency, though many were considered necessary or desirable.

## Political Shifts and the Dual Monarchy
The political landscape of the era saw significant shifts, most notably the disappearance of the Liberals in Vienna. In 1879, Liberals held 33 of the 37 seats returned in Lower Austria; however, by the later period, they were largely replaced by Socialists. This shift made it difficult for the government to maintain a strong party of moderate constitutionalists.

Further complications arose from the influence of Lueger, who was elected burgomaster after securing a two-thirds majority in the town council. Although the emperor initially refused to confirm the election and personally appealed to Lueger to withdraw, Badeni later advised that the election be confirmed following the 1897 election, which had increased Lueger's followers in the Reichsrath. The orderly conduct of debate in the Reichsrath was further hindered by the violent antipathy between German Nationalists and Christian Socialists.

Between 1903 and 1907, Austria-Hungary experienced a crisis that was temporarily settled but not definitively solved. This period, along with the introduction of universal suffrage in Austria, was considered as important to the development of the state as the years between 1866 and 1871. These events discredited the original interpretation of the dual system and raised questions regarding whether it remained the permanent form of the Austro-Hungarian polity.
