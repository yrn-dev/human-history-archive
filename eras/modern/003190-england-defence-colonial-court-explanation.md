# England as a Defence in a Colonial Court

In the legal framework governing the relationship between the United Kingdom and its colonies, specific provisions exist regarding the status of bankrupt individuals. An English bankrupt is permitted to use his discharge in England as a legal defence when appearing in a colonial court.

## Legal Basis for Discharge Recognition
The justification for this legal standing is rooted in the English act concerning bankruptcy. This act stipulates that all property belonging to the bankrupt—regardless of whether that property is located within the United Kingdom or within the colonies—is vested in his trustee in bankruptcy. Because the act effectively denudes the bankrupt of all his assets, it has been held that the bankrupt's discharge must consequently receive recognition within a colonial court.

## The Judicial System in the Colonies
The administration of justice in the colonies is closely linked to the English legal profession. The rank of barrister is a necessary qualification for nearly all offices of a judicial character. This restriction applies not only to the magistracies of minor rank, such as county court judgeships and recorderships, but also to the judgeships in the superior courts of law and equity located in both England and her colonies. This requirement ensures a harmony of interest and opinion between the judicial bench of all degrees and the bar as a profession.

## Imperial Defence and Colonial Administration
Beyond specific legal defences in court, the broader concept of "defence" in the colonies has evolved through various administrative and military phases.

### Land Defence and Funding
Historically, the first settlements of the American colonies were expected to provide for their own land defence. While they initially met this expectation—and occasionally served the empire at large, such as during the taking of Louisburg—the resources of the colonies eventually proved insufficient during the French wars. In 1755, Great Britain intervened by sending troops under General Braddock. Following this, the imperial exchequer bore the cost of North American colonial defence until the American War. To fund these expenses, the imperial parliament exercised the right to tax the colonies, and the Quartering Act of 1765 led to the stationing of 10,000 imperial troops in the colonies.

Following the loss of those colonies, the imperial authorities assumed the charge of land defences for the empire. Except for the Hudson Bay territories and India, where trading companies paid their own expenses, the taxpayers of the United Kingdom bore the full cost of imperial defence and the navy until the end of the Napoleonic Wars. By the mid-19th century, fiscal changes allowed self-governing colonies to command their own resources, leading to the expectation that colonial exchequers provide for their own governing expenses, including defence.

### Naval Defence and the Two-Power Standard
Because the empire's main thoroughfares are the world's oceans, a strong navy is considered fundamental to its existence. The "two-power standard" was adopted as a fundamental axiom, requiring the British navy to maintain a 10% margin of strength over the joint strength of the two next most powerful navies. 

Until the decade of 1890-1900, the colossal expense of this armament was borne exclusively by United Kingdom taxpayers. Later, self-governing colonies began contributing:
* **Australasian colonies:** Voted a subsidy of £126,000 per annum in 1895, which increased to £240,000 in 1902.
* **Cape Colony:** Offered £30,000 a year in 1897, increasing to £50,000 in 1902.
* **Natal:** Voted £35,000 a year.
* **Newfoundland:** Voted £3,000.

Despite these contributions and the local upkeep of fortifications, the primary cost of the imperial navy remained the responsibility of taxpayers in the British islands.

## Local Defence Examples
Defence structures varied by colony. In British Honduras, the Belize volunteer light infantry corps was raised in 1897 with approximately 200 officers and men, supplemented by a mounted section of about 40 created in 1904. The colony also maintained a police force of about 120 and a volunteer fire brigade consisting of 335 officers and men.
