# Swift's Drapier's Letters

The *Drapier's Letters* are a famous series of publications authored by Jonathan Swift. The creation of these letters was prompted by economic and political circumstances involving the English monarchy and the administration of coinage in Ireland.

## Origins and the Wood's Halfpence Controversy
The catalyst for the *Drapier's Letters* was the actions of Ehrengarde Melusina (1667–1743), the mistress of King George I. Created as the duchess of Kendal in 1719, the duchess was noted for her avarice and her practice of selling public offices, titles, and patent rights to obtain large sums of money.

One such patent right sold by the duchess of Kendal was the privilege of supplying Ireland with a new copper coinage. This right was purchased by William Wood (1671–1730), an iron merchant from Wolverhampton. Wood subsequently flooded Ireland with coins that became known as "Wood's halfpence," an event that gave occasion for Swift to publish the *Drapier's Letters*.

## Influence and Legacy
The form and impact of the *Drapier's Letters* extended beyond their immediate political context, influencing later Irish writers. For example, Henry Brooke (c. 1703–1783), an Irish author and son of William Brooke, utilized the style of Swift's work when composing his own writings. During the Jacobite rebellion of 1745, Brooke issued a series of works titled *Farmer's Six Letters to the Protestants of Ireland* (collected in 1746), the form of which was suggested by the *Drapier's Letters*.
