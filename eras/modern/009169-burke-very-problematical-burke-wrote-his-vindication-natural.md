# Edmund Burke

Edmund Burke was a prominent political figure and writer whose career was marked by a profound antipathy toward political rationalism and a commitment to social stability. Born most probably in Dublin on January 12, 1729, he was the son of a Protestant attorney and a Catholic mother from the Nagle family. Burke was educated at a school in Ballitore, County Kildare, run by Abraham Shackleton, a member of the Society of Friends. Burke regarded his time there as one of the most fortunate circumstances of his life, particularly his lifelong friendship with the instructor's son, Richard Shackleton.

## Early Life and Intellectual Development
Burke's early intellectual path was described as unsettled and desultory. He left Trinity in 1748 without a great stock of well-ordered knowledge or systematic intellectual discipline. After receiving his degree in Dublin, he moved to London in 1750 to keep terms at the Temple. The subsequent decade was characterized by obscure industry.

During this period, Burke produced several works that signaled a shift from abstract speculation toward the concrete conditions of social stability and the growth of nations. These included *Hints on the Drama*, an *Account of the European Settlements* in America, and a portion of an *Abridgment of the History of England* (reaching the reign of John), which contained a fragment on law. In 1759, he conceived the *Annual Register*, designed to group the chief movements of each year. This project, which he continued until 1788, strengthened his habits of observation and judgment.

## A Vindication of Natural Society
In 1756, Burke wrote *A Vindication of Natural Society*, a satire written in imitation of the style of Bolingbroke. The work purported to be a posthumous piece by Bolingbroke, presenting a view of the miseries and evils resulting from artificial society. While some critics, including Chesterfield and Warburton, viewed the work as serious, and it is suggested that Rousseau would have read it without suspecting irony, the text was actually a reduction to an absurdity.

Burke intended the piece to refute Bolingbroke's principles. He believed that civil institutions could not be safely measured by pure reason and feared that the rationalism used by the deistic school to challenge religion would similarly undermine political government. This early work serves as evidence of Burke's lifelong scorn for political rationalism.

## Political Career and the French Revolution
Burke's political philosophy was rooted in a conviction that there are permanent elements of human nature upon which the European order was built. He believed that destructive or merely rationalistic criticism applied to the complexities of social union was either futile or mischievous.

This perspective led to a severe rupture with his political associates during the agitation of 1791-1792. While many Whig leaders—including Fox, Sheridan, Grey, Windham, and Lord Fitzwilliam—as well as Pitt for a time, welcomed the French Revolution, Burke viewed parliamentary reform projects as "downright crimes." A public rupture between Burke and Charles Fox occurred in the House of Commons in May 1791. Burke's horror of Jacobinism outweighed his party associations.

Despite his opposition to the movement in France, Burke advocated for the admission of Irish Catholics to the franchise in his *Letters to Sir Hercules Langrishe*. While this appeared to contradict his stance on French movements and parliamentary reform, Burke maintained that his defense of Catholic relief was based on propositions specific to Ireland.

## Personal Life and Finances
Burke married the daughter of Dr. Nugent, a physician at Bath, toward the end of 1756. His wife was described as a mild and reasonable person who helped soothe his nervous and excitable temperament. Dr. Nugent later lived with the couple in London.

Burke's financial history was complex. In 1769, he purchased a 600-acre estate at Beaconsfield for £22,000. The purchase was funded through a small inheritance from his elder brother, a £6,000 advance from Lord Rockingham, and a mortgage for two-thirds of the cost, which remained unpaid throughout his life. Despite earning a salary of £700 as an agent for New York and receiving payments for the *Annual Register*, Burke consistently spent more than he earned and fell deeper into debt, receiving further assistance from Lord Rockingham.

## Family and Later Years
Burke was deeply devoted to his only son, Richard Burke, though others viewed Richard as presumptuous and empty-headed. In 1791, Burke sent his son to Coblenz to advise royalist exiles, and in 1794, Richard went to Ireland as an adviser to the Catholic Association. The latter mission was considered a failure due to Richard's arrogance and indiscretion. The loss of his son's reputation or the failures associated with him struck Burke heavily in the summer of 1794, a blow from which he never recovered.

Throughout his life, Burke was characterized by a resolute pride and an active self-confidence. He viewed himself as a power in Europe, a position he accepted seriously, driven by a desire to promote great causes.
