# Gases in Solution and Chemical Compositions

The study of gases, both in their free state and as constituents of solutions or complex compounds, reveals a wide array of chemical interactions across biological, geological, and industrial contexts. These include the behavior of oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon-based gases in various environments.

## Biological and Organic Compositions
In living bodies, a primary constituent is protoplasm, which is composed of a large proportion of water united with a complex compound of nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen, and carbon known as albumin or protein. Life is characterized by the universal disintegration and waste of this protoplasm through oxidation. Carbonic acid is believed to be a constant waste product of this process, while other waste products contain the remaining hydrogen, nitrogen, carbon, and other elements.

In the field of microbiology, symbiotic relationships between bacteria often involve the management of gases. For example, *Clostridium pasteurianum* is an anaerobic organism that can fix nitrogen only when aerobic species protect it from oxygen. Similarly, the interaction between *B. coli* and *B. denitrificans* allows for the reduction of nitrates, resulting in nitrogen passing off in a free state. Other bacterial fermentations produce various gases; *Bacillus chauvaei* ferments cane-sugar solutions to produce hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and inactive lactic acid.

## Volcanic and Natural Gas Emissions
Gases emitted from the earth provide insight into geological compositions. Fumaroles, or volcanic vents, discharge different gases based on their temperature:
* **Dry fumaroles** (at least 500°C) consist chiefly of anhydrous chlorides, such as sodium chloride.
* **Acid fumaroles** (300°C to 400°C) contain water vapour, sulphur dioxide, and hydrogen chloride.
* **Alkaline fumaroles** (above 100°C) evolve ammonium chloride.
* **Cold fumaroles** (below 100°C) discharge carbon dioxide, aqueous vapour, and potentially hydrogen sulphide.

Analysis of vapours from Mont Pele in 1901 showed a composition consisting chiefly of water vapour, along with nitrogen, oxygen, argon, hydrogen, methane, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, sulphur, and hydrogen chloride. These gases are noted to be practically the same as those obtained by heating certain rocks, such as granite.

Natural gas is primarily composed of methane (68.4% to 94.0% by volume). It also contains hydrogen (2.9% to 29.8%), and small amounts of ethane, ethylene, and carbon monoxide. Incombustible gases, including nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and oxygen, are also present, ranging from traces up to 5%.

## Industrial and Chemical Gas Processes
Various industrial processes manipulate the combination of carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen. "Producer gas" or "Siemens gas" is formed when nitrogen accompanying oxygen in atmospheric air remains mixed with carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and products of coal's destructive distillation. In contrast, "water gas" is produced by the reaction $\text{C} + \text{H}_2\text{O} = \text{CO} + \text{H}_2$. Because this process frees hydrogen and converts carbon to monoxide—both of which combine with oxygen—and because there is no atmospheric nitrogen involved, the resulting gas is almost entirely combustible. However, an unwelcome concomitant reaction ($\text{C} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} = \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2$) can convert carbon into incombustible $\text{CO}_2$.

In laboratory chemistry, bromine is used in various solutions, including water, hydrochloric acid, acetic acid, chloroform, and ether. Bromine water is specifically utilized for its oxidizing action and its ability to bleach organic colouring matters.

## Inorganic Compounds and Decomposition
The decomposition of various salts often liberates specific gases. Ammonium bichromate, when decomposed by heat, releases nitrogen and water. Barium nitride ($\text{Ba}_3\text{N}_2$) is decomposed by water, resulting in the evolution of hydrogen. Additionally, the heating of barium nitrate leads to decomposition used in pyrotechny for green fire.
