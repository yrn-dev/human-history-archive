# Ashanti

Ashanti is a British possession located in West Africa. The territory is shaped as an irregular oblong and features a triangular projection extending southward, which is the country of the Adansi. It covers an area of 23,000 square miles and had an estimated population of 500,000 in 1907.

## Geography and Boundaries
The territory is bounded to the west by the French colony of the Ivory Coast and to the north by the British Protectorate known as the Northern Territories of the Gold Coast. To the east, it is bordered by the river Volta, which serves as the boundary separating Ashanti from the German colony of Togoland. Its southern frontier is conterminous with the northern frontier of the British Gold Coast colony.

## Economy and Trade
The commercial vegetable products of Ashanti are similar to those of the Gold Coast. The rubber tree (*Funtumia elastica*) is the most important commercially, and the nut of the kola tree is in great demand. Since 1905, cocoa plantations have been established, particularly in the eastern districts, while tobacco is grown in the northern regions. The region also exports gum copal.

Gold is found in the western districts, with several companies operating mines between 1895 and 1901. The Obuassi mines provided most of the gold exported from the Gold Coast in 1902 and subsequent years. In 1905, the gold output from Ashanti was 68,259 oz., with a value of £254,790.

Kumasi serves as the distributing center for the hinterland and the entirety of Ashanti. While some trade was previously diverted to the French port of Assini due to wars between England and the Ashanti, measures were taken to improve trade between Cape Coast and Kumasi following the suppression of the 1900 revolt. Ashanti maintains considerable trade with its German and French neighbors, as well as with the Niger countries to the north. Revenue for the territory is derived primarily from liquor licenses, caravan taxes, contributions from gold-mining companies, and rents from government land. Its expenditure and revenue are included in those of the Gold Coast.

## Infrastructure and Communications
The primary means of communication within the region are tortuous forest paths, which are too narrow for wheeled vehicles. A wide road measuring 141 miles has been cut through the bush to connect Kumasi and Cape Coast. From Kumasi, ancient caravan routes lead to further inland trading centers, and ferries are maintained for crossing swamps and rivers. A common method of travel in the bush is the use of a palanquin carried on the heads of four carriers.

A railway connecting Sekondi to Kumasi was completed in 1903; this line passes through the auriferous region and was cut through one of the densest forest regions. Additionally, telegraph lines connect Kumasi with the towns in the Northern Territories and the coast towns, and the region possesses a well-organized postal service.

## History and British Relations
British settlement on the Gold Coast began around 1650, with the Northern Territories added in 1897. Early relations with the Ashanti kingdom involved attempts by the British government to cultivate intercourse with the powerful state. In January 1819, Joseph Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast as consul and reached Kumasi on February 28, 1820. Dupuis drew up a treaty that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over the territory of the Fanti and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies. This treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and the government in London assumed direct control of British settlements in 1821.

Conflict ensued, and on January 21, 1824, the Ashanti—numbering 10,000 men—defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy and his 500 men at Essamako. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. Following the death of Osai Tutu Quamina and the succession of Osai Okoto, a period of chronic warfare followed until the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826. By 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, by sending his son as a hostage to Cape Coast and paying 600 oz. of gold.

Following the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was named to the throne. Captain Donald W. Stewart was installed as a British resident at Kumasi. While other states in the confederacy kept their tribal systems and kings, the affairs of Kumasi were administered by chiefs under British guidance. A fort garrisoned by Gold Coast constabulary was established in the city.

In 1900, a serious rebellion broke out involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes, who commanded a force reported to be 40,000. Tensions escalated on March 28 when the governor of the Gold Coast, Sir F. Hodgson, demanded that Ashanti chiefs pay 4,000 oz. of gold annually and reproached them for hiding the golden stool since 1896. After an attack on a party of Hausa sent to find the stool, the Kumasi warriors threatened the fort. The fort was defended by 29 Europeans (including four women) and reinforcements including 100 Gold Coast constabulary, 250 Lagos constabulary, and 250 men under Major A. Morris from the British territory north of Ashanti.
