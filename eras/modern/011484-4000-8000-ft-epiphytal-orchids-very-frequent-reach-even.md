# Epiphytal Orchids and Related Vegetation

## Distribution in the Himalaya
In the Himalaya region, vegetation varies significantly based on elevation and geography. On the eastern slopes, where vegetation is most abundant and varied, forests extend with great luxuriance up to 12,000 ft. Within this region, epiphytal orchids are very frequent between the elevations of 4,000 and 8,000 ft., and they can be found reaching as high as 10,000 ft. 

The broader floral composition of these mountains includes a warm temperate, chiefly evergreen vegetation on the slopes. This comprises various groups such as Magnoliaceae, Ternstroemiaccae, subtropical Rosaceae, oak, *Ilex*, *Symplocos*, Lauraceae, and *Pinus longifolia*. Additionally, mountain forms of tropical orders are present, including palms, *Pandanus*, *Musa*, *Vitis*, and *Vernonia*. At lower levels, *Pandanus*, bamboos, plantains, tree-ferns, and several *Calami* are abundant.

## Regional Variations in Mountain Flora
The vegetation of the Himalaya differs between the east and west. In the west, the climate is drier, resulting in forests that are less dense and luxuriant. Below 8,000 ft., forests are not commonly dense, except in certain secluded valleys at low elevations; these warm slopes are covered with oaks, rhododendron, or *Pinus longifolia*. From 8,000 to 12,000 ft., a thick forest of deciduous trees is almost universal. 

Certain tropical forms found in the east, such as tree-ferns, do not extend west of Nepal. Furthermore, the deodar or cedar is hardly indigenous east of the sources of the Ganges, with western forms becoming more abundant as one advances toward Afghanistan. In Tibet, vegetation ascends to above 18,000 ft. on mountain slopes that are less snowy and drier.

## Other Epiphytic and Herbaceous Plants
Beyond the Himalaya, other epiphytal and herbaceous plants are noted in rainy regions. These include the Orchidaceae, Scitamineae, and Orontiaceae, along with Cyperaceae, Gramineae, ferns, and other Cryptogams. Some of these forms ascend into temperate zones on mountains, including various Cyrtandraceae, *Begonia*, *Osbeckia*, Scitamineae, and a few epiphytical orchids.

In other tropical regions, such as Central and South America (and occasionally Madagascar and tropical Africa), the genus *Rhipsalis* consists of about 50 species that are all epiphytal in habit. These are fleshy-stemmed plants with a woody axis and small, short-tubed flowers. Similarly, *Epiphyllum* refers to a few dwarf branching Brazilian epiphytal plants characterized by branches dilated into fleshy leaves and large magenta or crimson flowers.

## Epiphytism in Fungi
The term epiphytic is also applied to certain fungi. Obligate parasites may be epiphytic, such as *Erysipheae*, where the mycelium remains on the outside of the host plant, sending haustoria only into the epidermal cells. Not all epiphytic fungi are parasites; many saprophytes, such as moulds, develop a loose mycelium on living leaves. While these saprophytic epiphytes only destroy tissues after a leaf has fallen, they can cause harm by intercepting air and light from the leaf, as seen in *Fumago*.

## Related Flora in Other Regions
In the Khasi Hills, the landscape consists of steep ridges and table-lands. While the plateaus are often covered with grass and lack large timber, the highest ridges contain sacred groves of timber trees, including magnolias, chestnuts, and oaks. Beneath the shade of these trees, rhododendrons, wild cinnamon, and rare orchids grow.

In other mountainous areas, such as the northern plateau between the Arctic Ocean and the Seas of Okhotsk and Bering, vegetation is limited to mosses, lichens, and a few dwarf Siberian tree species. In the middle province of this region, rugged mountains between 4,000 and 7,000 ft. are clothed halfway up with thick forests of larch.
