# Arnold-Forster and the Balfour Ministry

## Appointment to the Ministry for War
Mr. H. O. Arnold-Forster was appointed as the minister for war during a period of reconstitution of the ministry. This appointment occurred during a critical fortnight in which the duke of Devonshire had apparently cooperated in the reorganization of the government and had acquiesced in a compromise proposed by Mr. Balfour. Other appointments made during this same period included Mr. Austen Chamberlain as chancellor of the exchequer, Mr. Alfred Lyttelton as colonial secretary, Mr. Brodrick as secretary for India, Lord Stanley as postmaster-general, and Mr. Graham Murray as secretary for Scotland. Additionally, Lord Londonderry became president of the council, Lord Lansdowne took the role of leader of the House of Lords, and Lord Salisbury (son of the late premier) joined the cabinet as lord privy seal.

In 1903, minor changes continued within the ministry; specifically, Mr. Brodrick moved to the India office, and Mr. Arnold-Forster assumed the position of minister for war.

## Army Reform and Reorganization
From 1903 onward, the government engaged in discussions regarding the question of army reform. The administration expressed a strong desire to settle these matters. However, the specific schemes for reorganization proposed by Mr. Arnold-Forster and Mr. Brodrick failed to obtain any general support. Despite these failures in reorganization, Mr. Balfour later instituted the new Army Council in 1904 and the permanent Committee of Imperial Defence. These reforms were based on a report from a "triumvirate" appointed in November 1903, consisting of Sir George Clarke, Sir John Fisher, and Lord Esher.

## Mr. Balfour's Personal Influence and Leadership
Despite the internal struggles of the Unionist party and the failures of the army reorganization schemes, Mr. Balfour's personal influence remained potent. This influence helped the government hold together, allowing for the successful passage of the Licensing Bill in 1904. 

Mr. Balfour's leadership was characterized by a "dialectical dexterity" used to avoid expressing his fiscal opinions beyond a certain point, a trait that drew contemptuous criticism from the Liberal press. He maintained that no definite action on fiscal matters could be taken until the next parliament. While he did not fully support Mr. Chamberlain's campaign—refusing to go "the whole hog"—he also did nothing to discourage it.

## Political Context and the Fiscal Crisis
The stability of the ministry was frequently threatened by disputes between free-trade Unionists and those favoring protectionism. On October 1st, Mr. Balfour spoke at Sheffield, declaring his intention to lead and expressing readiness to reverse traditional fiscal policy by removing the axiom that import duties should only be levied for revenue purposes. This stance caused friction with free-traders and led to the resignation of the duke of Devonshire.

The struggle intensified throughout 1903, with Mr. Chamberlain advocating for a reorganized budget with more taxes on imports, including food. In opposition, the Free Food league was started by the duke of Devonshire, Lord James, Lord Hugh Cecil, and Lord Goschen. By December, Mr. Chamberlain's Tariff Commission was appointed.

## Tenure and Downfall
The government's tenure was prolonged partly due to critical foreign affairs conducted by Lord Lansdowne, which made a change in government seem dangerous. These included the Russo-Japanese War and tensions with Russia over the right of search of British ships, highlighted by the stopping of the steamer "Malacca" on July 13, 1904, the sinking of the "Knight Commander" on July 24, and the firing on the English fishing-fleet off the Dogger Bank on October 23.

Ultimately, Mr. Balfour's administration was wrecked by dissensions over the fiscal question. He tendered his resignation as Prime Minister to the King in late November 1905.
