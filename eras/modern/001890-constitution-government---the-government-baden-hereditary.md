# Constitution and Government of Baden

## Political Structure and Executive Power
The government of Baden is an hereditary monarchy. Executive power is vested in the grand-duke, and the executive branch is organized into four state departments: finance; the interior; foreign affairs and the grand-ducal house; and justice, education, and ecclesiastical affairs. 

The state's revenue is derived from domains, railways, and both direct and indirect taxes. The railways are operated by the state, and the public debt, which totals approximately 22 million sterling, is attributed solely to the railways.

## Legislative Authority
Legislative power is shared between the grand-duke and a representative assembly known as the *Landtag*, which consists of two chambers that meet at least every two years.

The upper chamber is composed of several distinct groups:
* All adult princes of the reigning family.
* The chiefs of mediatized families (including the head of the family possessing a mediatized principality in the Upper Danube and Black Forest district).
* The president of the Protestant Evangelical church and the archbishop of Freiburg.
* A deputy from each technical high school and university.
* Eight members elected by the territorial nobility for four-year terms.
* Representatives from the chambers of agriculture (two), commerce (three), and trades (one).
* Municipal representatives, including two mayors, one burgomaster of lesser towns, and one district council member.
* Eight members nominated by the grand-duke, two of whom are legal functionaries.

The lower chamber consists of 73 popular representatives. Of these, 49 are elected by rural communities and 24 by the burgesses of specific towns. Members are elected for four years, with half of the chamber retiring every two years. Voting rights are granted to citizens aged 25 and older who are not paupers and have not been convicted. Elections are indirect; citizens nominate deputy electors (*Wahlmaenner*), who then elect the representatives.

## Judiciary and Military
The duchy's supreme courts of justice are located in Mannheim, Constance, Waldshut, Mosbach, Heidelberg, Offenburg, Freiburg, and Karlsruhe. Appeals from these courts are directed to the *Reichsgericht* (the supreme tribunal of the empire) in Leipzig.

Regarding military organization, the Baden army became a portion of the Prussian army following a convention in 1871.

## Constitutional History and Political Conflict
Baden's constitutional development has been marked by periods of instability and struggle. In 1818, Grand-duke Charles granted a liberal constitution with two chambers to secure popular support for the Hochberg heir during a succession dispute with Bavaria. However, the administration under Grand-duke Louis was viewed as inefficient and unpopular.

In 1849, tensions over a new constitution led to an insurrection. Following a military mutiny at Rastatt on May 11 and a mutiny at Karlsruhe on May 13, the grand-duke and his ministers fled. A provisional government was established on June 1, and a "constituent diet" of advanced politicians assembled on June 10. This revolution ended in June after the Prussians, led by Prince William, invaded. Following a defeat at Waghaeusel on June 20, Prince William entered Karlsruhe on June 25. The grand-duke returned on August 19, dissolved the diet, and passed reactionary laws to strengthen the government.

## Religion and Internal Politics
Internal politics in Baden have centered largely on religion. A 1859 concordat with the Holy See placed education under clergy oversight, sparking a constitutional struggle. This ended in 1863 with a victory for Liberal principles, making communes responsible for education, though priests retained a share in management.

Further conflicts between Liberalism and Clericalism occurred under the premiership of Julius von Jolly starting in 1867. Liberal changes included compulsory education, freedom of the press, and the responsibility of ministers. A law passed on September 6, 1867, required candidates for the priesthood to pass government examinations. This was resisted by the archbishop of Freiburg, and the see remained vacant from April 1868 until 1882. The introduction of civil marriage in 1869 and the 1870 proclamation of papal infallibility further intensified the "Kulturkampf," during which the government encouraged Old Catholic communities.

By the late 19th century, political tendencies shifted. In 1891, National Liberals held a majority of one in the diet; by 1893, they required Conservative aid to maintain power. In 1897, an opposition coalition of Socialists, Social-democrats, Radicals (*Freisinnige*), and Ultramontanes won a majority in the chamber.

## Education and Church Governance
Public education is managed entirely by the government. The state hosts a celebrated technical college at Karlsruhe and two universities: a Roman Catholic university at Freiburg-im-Breisgau and a Protestant university at Heidelberg.

The grand-duke is Protestant. The Evangelical Church is governed by a synod (comprising a "prelate," seven nominated members, and 48 elected lay and clerical members) and a nominated council. The Roman Catholic archbishop of Freiburg serves as the metropolitan of the Upper Rhine.
