# German Archaeological Expeditions in Babylonia

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, several German expeditions were conducted to explore and excavate ancient sites in the region of Babylonia. These missions contributed significantly to the understanding of ancient Mesopotamian burial customs and urban architecture.

## The Expedition to El Hibba
Between 1886 and 1887, a German expedition led by Dr. Koldewey explored the cemetery of El Hibba. This site is located immediately to the south of Tello. The work conducted by Dr. Koldewey's team provided the first acquaintance with the burial customs of ancient Babylonia.

## The Orientgesellschaft Expedition to Babylon
In 1899, the *Orientgesellschaft* despatched a large-scale German expedition with the specific objective of exploring the ruins of Babylon. This mission successfully laid bare the great processional road as well as the palace of Nebuchadrezzar.

## Excavations at Qal`at Sherqat
Following the work at Babylon, Dr. W. Andrae conducted further excavations at the site of Assur, located at Qal`at Sherqat.

## Context of Regional Exploration
The German efforts were part of a broader international interest in the region. Between 1877 and 1881, the French consul de Sarzec excavated at Tello (ancient Lagash), uncovering monuments from the pre-Semitic age. These included diorite statues of Gudea, whose stone inscriptions indicate the material was brought from the Sinaitic peninsula, known as Magan. De Sarzec's work pushed the history of the city back to at least 4000 B.C. and uncovered over 30,000 tablets that had been arranged on shelves during the time of Gudea (c. 2700 B.C.).

Other contemporary explorations included:
* **Turkish Government:** Dr. V. Scheil discovered tablets at the site of Sippara in 1897, which are now housed in the Museum at Constantinople.
* **American Expeditions:** E. J. Banks led excavations at Bismya (Udab) from 1903 to 1904. Additionally, the University of Pennsylvania began work at Niffer in 1889. At Niffer, J. H. Haynes uncovered the remains of the great temple of El-lil. These excavations revealed a platform of large bricks stamped with the names of Naram-Sin and Sargon of Akkad (3800 B.C.). Analysis of the debris—30 feet beneath the pavement and 34 feet above it (the topmost stratum being no later than the Parthian era)—suggests the lower debris represents a period of approximately 3000 years.
