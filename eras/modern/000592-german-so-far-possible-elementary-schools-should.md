# Germanization and Education in Austria

In the history of the Austrian monarchy, the role of education and language served as a primary battleground for political influence and national identity. Central to this conflict was the party of centralization, known as the Liberal German party (which later became the National German party), supported by some Ruthenes and Italians. This group sought to establish a common Austrian nationality through the implementation of a common parliament and the spread of the German language via the school system.

## The Germanization Strategy
The party of centralization maintained that the ascendancy of the Germans was the only way to preserve the unity of the monarchy. They argued that German was the sole language capable of facilitating communication between the different races of the empire and insisted it remain the language of the parliament, the civil service, and the army. Furthermore, they viewed the role of Austria as having a historic task to bring German culture to the "half-civilized races of the east."

To achieve these goals, the party demanded that all universities and higher schools remain German. They specifically advocated that elementary schools be Germanized as far as possible, viewing the German schoolmaster as an "apostle of German culture." Their ultimate vision was a centralized state where the national feelings of the Slavs would be obscured by a common Austrian nationality, leaving Slavonic idioms to survive only as local dialects of the peasantry.

## Resistance and Slavonic Education
The German population constituted less than one-third of the total population, meaning their rule required significant political ability. Opposition grew as Slav populations increased and moved westward into manufacturing districts and towns due to economic causes. While Germans hoped that education would lead these populations to abandon their native tongues, the opposite occurred; the movement of population spread Slavonic languages.

In the Slav districts, a law passed in 1867 required that elementary education be conducted in Slovenian or Czech, depending on the region. The Slavs further demanded—and were eventually granted by Minister of Education Prazak under Count Taaffe—the right to have their own schools provided even in towns where they were a small minority, so they would not be forced to attend German schools.

## Regional Conflicts and Institutional Shifts
The struggle over language and education was most vehement in Moravia and Bohemia, where the German and Czech populations were nearly equal. Germans feared that the victory of the Czech language would place nearly two million Germans in a position of subordination. Over a twenty-year period, Czech influence encroached upon German influence, aided by government actions:

*   **Administrative Changes:** An 1880 ordinance required government offices and law courts to deal with business in the language in which it was introduced. By 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid frequent translations.
*   **Educational Expansion:** A large number of Czech elementary schools were established, and middle schools were granted to the Czechs. Additionally, Czech classes were introduced into German schools.
*   **University Impact:** In 1882, the university of Prague began offering classes in Czech, an event the Germans viewed as a "desecration" of the oldest German university.

## The Concept of Kultur-sprache
The introduction of Slavonic languages into higher and middle schools was particularly sensitive for the Germans, who insisted that German was the *Kultur-sprache* (culture-language). This belief was exemplified by Count A. Auersperg (Anastasius Gruen), who once entered the diet of Carniola carrying the entirety of Slovenian literature under his arm to argue that the Slovenian language was an inadequate substitute for German as a medium for higher education.

## Other Educational Arrangements
Parallel to these struggles, other regional arrangements existed within the empire. In Galicia, Beust promised the Polish deputies that Polish would be the language of instruction in all secondary schools and the official language of public offices and law courts. Under these terms, Ruthenian was permitted only in elementary schools and subject to strict limitations.
