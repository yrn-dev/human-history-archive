# Bacterial Diseases in Higher Plants

The study of bacterial diseases in higher plants is a subject requiring careful treatment due to historical doubts regarding whether the described organisms are the actual causes of the diseases attributed to them. While a long list of plant diseases has recently been attributed to bacterial action, some cases remain doubtful.

## Theoretical Objections and Evidence
Historically, it was argued that plants were immune to bacterial diseases because of their acid contents. However, it is now understood that many bacteria are capable of flourishing in acid media. Another objection suggested that bacteria could not penetrate the cell-walls bounding intercellular spaces, even if they gained access through the stomata. This has been countered by the discovery of certain anaerobic forms that can ferment cellulose, as well as others capable of penetrating the cell-walls of living cells. An example of this was first described by Marshall Ward in 1887 regarding the bacteria of Leguminosae, which was later confirmed by Miss Dawson in 1898.

## Primary Agents vs. Saprophytic Forms
Distinctions are made between bacteria acting as primary agents of disease and those acting as saprophytes. In some instances, such as the slime fluxes of oaks and other trees or the Sereh disease of the sugar-cane, it is considered extremely improbable that bacteria are the primary agents. In these cases, bacteria are likely saprophytic forms that enter rotting tissues already injured by other agents. Saprophytic bacteria may enter through punctures made by insects or travel down the dead hypha of an invading fungus. For example, while Aphides were once credited with the bacterial infection of carnations, research by Woods concluded that Aphides alone are responsible for that specific disease.

Conversely, recent investigations have identified cases where bacteria are certainly the primary agents. These diseases are characterized principally by the wilting of shoots resulting from the stoppage of the vessels. When these shoots are cut in transverse sections, the vessels typically appear brown and are choked with a dark yellowish slime containing bacteria; this is observed in plants such as cucumbers, potatoes, and cabbages. Bacterial action is also the seat of disease in certain plants including tobacco and carnations.

## Mechanisms of Infection and Action
Bacterial infection is analyzed as a form of intoxication. The supply of toxins is regulated by and essential to the multiplication of living organisms within the tissues. Defense against such diseases depends on the ability to either kill the organisms producing the toxins or to neutralize the toxins themselves. Notably, there is no fixed relationship between the multiplication of bacteria in the body and the production of toxins, as some organisms that are highly active toxin producers have relatively little power to invade tissues.

## Symbiosis and Nitrogen Cycle
Beyond pathogenic action, bacteria play a critical role in the chemical changes within the soil and their relationship with higher plants. In ordinary arable soil, motile rod-like bacteria known as *Bacterium radicicola* enter the root-hairs of leguminous plants as a long, slimy thread (zoogloea) and penetrate the root tissues. This causes the tissues to become hypertrophied, forming a nodule. Within these nodules, the bacteria multiply and develop, absorbing nitrogen from the air and building it into the bacterial cell, which is then handed on to the host plant. This is a case of symbiosis where the green plant restricts bacterial development to the nodules and eventually absorbs the bacteria for its own use.

In the broader environment, bacteria facilitate the circulation of nitrogen. Some bacteria oxidize ammonia to nitrous acid and then to nitric acid, while others, as shown by Warington, reduce nitrates to nitrites. Further reduction by bacteria can result in the escape of ammonia or free nitrogen. This explains why putting nitrates and manure together on land is a wasteful process; fresh manure contains de-nitrifying bacteria that reduce nitrates to nitrites and set free nitrogen and ammonia, undoing the work of nitrifying bacteria. Ammonia may be oxidized to nitrites and nitrates, which are then taken up by higher plants and worked into proteids, eventually being broken down by bacterial action back into ammonia.
