# Dalmatia

## Early History and Roman Rule
The history of Dalmatia is traced back to 180 B.C., when a tribe of the same name established a republic after declaring independence from the Illyrian king Gentius. This republic, with its capital at Delminium, held territory extending from the Narenta northwards to the Cetina, and eventually to the Kerka, where it bordered Liburnia. Roman forces first attacked the Dalmatians in 156 B.C., forcing them to pay tribute. However, the region was not fully annexed until the reign of Augustus, following the defeat of several revolts by Tiberius in A.D. 9. This annexation led to the adoption of Latin civilization throughout Illyria.

Following the collapse of the Western Empire, Dalmatia was subject to the Gothic rulers Theodoric and Odoacer between 476 and 535, after which Justinian added the region to the Eastern Empire. In the first half of the 7th century, a significant migration of Slavs—including Bulgars, Croats, and Serbs—occurred. While these invaders absorbed or expelled native populations in other parts of the Balkan Peninsula, they were unable to do so in Dalmatia due to the presence of highly civilized maritime city-states. These cities relied on the support of kinsfolk in Italy. This resulted in a divided society where Slavs settled the country districts and the Italian or Latin population retreated to large towns such as Zara and Ragusa. This division was further deepened by the 1054 schism between Western and Eastern Christianity, as Italians remained attached to the Papacy while Slavs generally preferred the Bogomil or Orthodox creeds.

## Medieval Rivalries and Governance
Between 1102 and 1420, Dalmatian city-states were caught in a rivalry between Hungary and Venice. The cities were often split into two economic factions: seafaring communities favored Venice, the mistress of the Adriatic, while merchants and farmers favored Hungary, their powerful land neighbor. 

Under Hungarian rule, following the conquest by Coloman between 1102 and 1105, cities maintained municipal privileges. Subject to royal assent, they could elect their own judges, bishops, and chief magistrates, and their Roman law remained in effect. Some cities shared customs revenue between the municipality, bishop, chief magistrate, and king. Venice also granted analogous privileges, though both powers frequently infringed upon these rights through the appointment of bishops or the quartering of garrisons.

## Modern Political Transitions
After the fall of the Venetian republic in 1797, the treaty of Campo Formio transferred Dalmatia to Austria. The region then entered a period of instability during the Napoleonic wars. By the peace of Pressburg in 1805, the country was handed to France. During this time, the republics of Poglizza (1807) and Ragusa (1808) lost their independence. Between 1812 and 1815, a British naval force under Captain Hoste occupied the islands of Lagosta, Lesina, and Curzola, fostering overland trade through Germany, Austria, and Dalmatia. 

In 1814, allied Austrian and British forces expelled the French, and by 1815, Dalmatia was incorporated into the Austro-Hungarian empire. Its tenure under this empire was generally tranquil, interrupted only by ineffectual risings near Cattaro in 1869 and 1881-1882. In 1881, disturbances in Dalmatia also spread across the frontier into Herzegovina.

## Infrastructure and Economy
Dalmatia's economy is based on farming, fishing, and related trades such as oil pressing, wine production, shipbuilding, and the distillation of spirits, specifically *maraschino*. Other minor industries include soap manufacture and tile-burning. The region exports hides, wax, honey, oil, spirits, wine, bay salt, lignite, and asphalt, while importing foodstuffs and manufactured goods. A lucrative transit trade exists with Turkey, Montenegro, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and various Mediterranean and Adriatic ports.

Communications in the region have been described as defective, with some interior areas accessible only via rough mountain roads. The primary railway traverses the central districts, connecting Sinj, Sebenico, Spalato, and Knin. Southern lines of similar importance link Dalmatia to Herzegovina, terminating at Metkovic, Ragusa, and Castlenuovo on the Bocche di Cattaro. In 1897, the region had 98 telegraph offices and 151 post offices.

## Social and Cultural Conditions
Education in Dalmatia progressed slowly from 1860 to 1900. In 1890, over 80% of the population was illiterate. By 1893, the region's educational infrastructure included 337 elementary schools, 5 intermediate schools, 6 gymnasia, 6 theological seminaries, and approximately 40 technical and continuation schools.

Cultural tensions emerged in the early 20th century regarding the use of Slavonic liturgies written in the Glagolitic alphabet. While a privilege of Roman Catholics in Croatia and Dalmatia, the Vatican forbade its use at the festival of SS. Cyril and Methodius in 1904 to maintain Catholic unity. Some, including the Slavonic archbishop Rajcevic of Zara, viewed the Glagolitic movement as an innovation of Panslavism intended to distance the Catholic clergy from Latin Rome.

## Austro-Hungarian Administrative Agreements
In October 1907, Austria and Hungary reached a "customs and commercial treaty" following an economic crisis in Hungary. One of the key provisions of this agreement required Hungary to facilitate the establishment of direct railway communication between Dalmatia and Vienna. This communication was to be established by the end of 1911, with each state responsible for building the sections of the line located within its own territory.
