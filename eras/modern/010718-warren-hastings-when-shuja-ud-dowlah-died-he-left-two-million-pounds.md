# Warren Hastings

Warren Hastings was a prominent English administrator in India whose career was marked by significant political maneuvers, financial disputes, and a high-profile legal battle in England. His early experience in the region included a period of captivity in 1757 when he served as an assistant to the resident at Cossimbazar; both were taken as prisoners to Murshidabad following the town's capture by the nawab, Suraj-ud-dowlah.

## Administration in Oudh and the Begums
Hastings' tenure involved complex dealings with the leadership of Oudh. He worked to support the nawab, Asaf-ud-Dowlah (who served from 1775 to 1797), attempting to rescue him from his own incapacity. This period was characterized by financial negotiations involving the begums of Oudh—the mother and grandmother of Asaf-ud-Dowlah.

A central point of contention involved the estate of Shuja-ud-Dowlah, the previous nawab. Upon his death, Shuja-ud-Dowlah left two million pounds sterling buried within the vaults of the zenana. The mother and widow of the deceased prince claimed this entire treasure based on a will that was never produced. 

While pressing the nawab for the payment of debts owed to the Company, Hastings secured a loan of 26 lakhs of rupees from the begum, in exchange for which he granted her a *jagir* valued at four times the loan's amount. He later obtained an additional 30 lakhs of rupees; in return, he provided a full acquittal and the Company's recognition of her *jagirs* for her lifetime without interference. These *jagirs* were eventually confiscated due to documentary evidence showing the begum's complicity in the rising of Chai Singh.

## Conflict in Benares
Hastings' demands for resources also led to instability in other regions. In 1781, the Hindu kingdom of Benares experienced a rebellion led by Chait Singh. This uprising was triggered by Hastings' demands for troops and money to support the Mahratta War.

## Impeachment and Legacy
The administration of Warren Hastings became a subject of intense scrutiny in England. The spoliation of the begums of Oudh formed one of the primary charges in a legal action against him. In 1788, Hastings was impeached, a process that concluded with his acquittal in 1792. 

This impeachment reflected a broader struggle within the English Parliament, which found itself governing a London trading company that dominated an empire more populous and vast than all the domains of the British crown. Due to the remote and "romantically unreal" nature of India to the English public, effective supervision of the Company's proceedings remained difficult.

Hastings' likeness was preserved in art; the sculptor Thomas Banks created a bust of him which is held in the National Portrait Gallery.
