# Arundinaria

*Arundinaria* is a genus of bamboo consisting of species that are hardy under cultivation in the British Isles. These plants are primarily native to Japan and China, though the genus also includes a few species originating from the Himalayas.

## Species and Characteristics
Several species of *Arundinaria* are noted for their distinct physical attributes and growth patterns:
*   ***Arundinaria simoni***: A fine plant that has reached a height of 18 feet in the bamboo garden at Kew. This species is noted for flowering and fruiting in Britain not infrequently.
*   ***Arundinaria japonica***: A handsome and tall plant, which is generally grown in gardens under the name *Bambusa metake*.
*   ***Arundinaria nitida***: Described as remarkably hardy and the most attractive and daintiest species within its genus.

## Cultivation and Propagation
Plants of the *Arundinaria* genus are described as "hungry plants" that respond well to generous treatment. For optimal growth, they should be planted in a rich loam that is not too stiff. During the first two years of growth, the plants should be well mulched, provided with ample water during the growing period, and sheltered from winds. When transplanting these plants, it is necessary to disturb the roots as little as possible.

Propagation of *Arundinaria* can be achieved through three primary methods:
1.  **Seed**: While propagation by seed is possible, it is seldom applicable because fruit occurs rarely.
2.  **Division**: The plants may be propagated by dividing the plant.
3.  **Cuttings**: This is another viable method for propagation.

## Botanical Features and Uses
The fruit of *Arundinaria*, similar to that of the *Bambusa* genus and other related genera, resembles the grain typically characteristic of grasses.

In Oriental countries, the products and parts of the bamboo are used in nearly endless ways. The succulent, soft shoots are harvested as they begin to spring and served at the table similarly to asparagus. To extend their fitness for consumption, these shoots are earthed over. While they provide a continuous supply throughout the year, the supply is most abundant during the autumn. Additionally, these shoots may be candied and preserved in sugar, salted and eaten with rice, or prepared as pickles.

As the bamboo plant ages, a fluid is secreted within the hollow joints. This fluid gradually develops into a concrete, purely siliceous substance known as *tabascheer* or *tabaxir*. This substance possesses peculiar optical properties and was once highly valued in the East for medicinal qualities. However, as a medicinal agent, the bamboo is considered entirely inert and has not been accepted into the European materia medica.
