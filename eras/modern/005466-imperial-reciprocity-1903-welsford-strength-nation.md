## Commercial Reciprocity and Preferential Systems
The concept of reciprocity within the British Empire manifested through proposals for preferential trade systems. At a Colonial Conference in London in 1887, a delegate from South Africa formally proposed a preferential system that would impose a 2% duty on all foreign goods, with the resulting proceeds used to maintain the imperial navy. This proposal included the request to denounce certain treaties with foreign nations that restricted trade between different parts of the empire. By 1897, treaties with Belgium and Germany were denounced due to strong feelings in England against foreign engagements that hindered domestic trade arrangements between the mother-country and its colonies.

There was a growing recognition that Great Britain's foreign trade, primarily in manufactured goods, was linked to the enlargement of home markets for colonial raw materials. Consequently, it was understood that injury to Great Britain's foreign trade would necessarily impact the colonies. This perspective was expressed at the 1894 Colonial Conference at Ottawa.

Canada became the first major British colony to implement this sentiment commercially in 1897 by introducing an imperial tariff. This system granted a 25% deduction on customs duties for British goods without requiring a reciprocal advantage. This same advantage was extended to all British colonies trading with Canada on equal terms. Subsequently, New Zealand, Australia, and the South African states also granted preferential treatment to British goods.

## International Reciprocity and Treaties
Beyond the British Empire, the principle of reciprocal treatment has been applied to international relations and the resolution of conflicts. Following a war, the Imperial Governments of Japan and Russia agreed to a system of reciprocal treatment on the footing of the most favoured nation. This agreement covered:
* Import and export duties.
* Customs formalities.
* Tonnage and transit dues.
* The admission and treatment of vessels, subjects, and agents of one country within the territories of the other.

Additionally, the two governments agreed to the reciprocal restoration of all prisoners of war, with each government appointing a special Commissioner to manage the delivery and receipt of prisoners at convenient ports.

In other contexts, reciprocity has appeared in multilateral conventions. For example, Germany was required to make reciprocal agreements with new States regarding telegraphic and postal conventions as a condition of its readmission to several agreements.

## Reciprocity in Other Imperial Contexts
Reciprocity has also been a feature of regional alliances and internal imperial unions. In the Commercial Union of Austria and Hungary, the Customs Union was based on a reciprocal and binding treaty. While this treaty lapsed, both parties continued the practice until December 31, 1907, with negotiations for a renewal of the alliance intended to begin by 1901 and potentially renew from 1903, the year the commercial treaties were set to expire.

In ancient Greece, treaties between imperial states also utilized reciprocal obligations. A treaty involving the Athenians, Eleians, Mantineians, and Argeians established a peace and alliance for one hundred years. This agreement included reciprocal obligations for Athens to provide aid if Argos, Elis, or Mantineia were attacked, mirroring the requirement that the other three cities provide the most vigorous aid possible if Attica were invaded.
