# James Matthew Barrie

## Early Life and Education
James Matthew Barrie was born on May 9, 1860, in the small village of Kirriemuir, located in Forfarshire. He received his education at the Dumfries academy and later attended Edinburgh University. Barrie's interest in writing began early; in his quasi-autobiographical work *Margaret Ogilvy*, he noted that he wrote tales in the garret before he had even started school. While studying at Edinburgh, he wrote the majority of a three-volume novel. A publisher, believing the work was written by a clever lady, offered to publish it for £100, though Barrie did not accept the offer.

## Journalistic Career
Barrie entered the field of literature through journalism. Following a brief waiting period in Edinburgh, he became a leader-writer for the *Nottingham Journal* in February 1883. During his time with this publication, he wrote notes and special articles that helped train and develop his personal talent.

He eventually began submitting his work to editors in London. On November 17, 1884, Frederick Greenwood published Barrie's article "An Auld Licht Community" in the *St James's Gazette*. Under the encouragement of Greenwood, Barrie wrote further "Auld Licht Idylls," which led to his move to London in 1885. In London, he continued his contributions to the *St James's Gazette* and also wrote for *Home Chimes*, which was edited by F. W. Robinson.

Barrie's professional network expanded when he was enlisted by Alexander Riach for the *Edinburgh Evening Dispatch*. This connection subsequently led to him writing for Dr Robertson Nicoll's *British Weekly*, where he used the signature "Gavin Ogilvy." He later contributed to the *Speaker* upon its foundation in 1890, as well as the *Scots Observer* (which later became the *National Observer*), edited by W. E. Henley.

## Literary Works
In 1887, Barrie published his first book, *Better Dead*. This work was a *jeu d'esprit* and a specimen of his humorous journalism, which he had elaborated from his writings in the *St James's Gazette*. He followed this in 1888 with *Auld Licht Idylls*, a collection of sketches of a Scots village. 

These sketches portrayed the humours and life of his native village, which he idealized as "Thrums." The material for these stories was derived from his own early observations and the tales told to him by his mother. Barrie noted that his mother "told me everything," and as a result, his memories of their "little red town" were influenced by her own memories. The village of Kirriemuir itself was not entirely satisfied with the portrayal.
