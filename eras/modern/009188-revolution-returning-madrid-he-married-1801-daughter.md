# Revolution and Political Transition

## South American Independence and Simon Bolivar
Simon Bolivar (1783–1830) is recognized as the hero of South American independence. Born in Caracas, Venezuela, to noble parents, Juan Vicente Bolivar y Ponte and Maria Concepcion Palacios y Sojo, Bolivar pursued his studies in Europe and resided in Madrid for several years. During his time in Paris, he witnessed some of the final scenes of the Revolution. 

In 1801, after returning to Madrid, Bolivar married the daughter of Don N. Toro, who was the uncle of the marquis of Toro in Caracas. The couple traveled to Venezuela, where Bolivar intended to improve his large estate. However, his young wife died of yellow fever, prompting his return to Europe. Bolivar returned home in 1809 via the United States, where he observed the functioning of free institutions. Upon arriving in Venezuela, he joined the cause of independence that had been agitating Spanish colonies. In April 1810, he helped promote an insurrection at Caracas and was granted a colonel's commission by the revolutionary junta. He later participated in a mission to the court of Great Britain alongside Louis Lopez Mendez. While Venezuela declared its independence on July 5, 1811, subsequent war broke out with the advance of Spanish troops under Monteverde. Bolivar was given command of Puerto Cabello but was forced to evacuate the post due to a lack of support, and the Spaniards regained control of the country due to the inaction of Miranda.

## The French Revolution and Napoleonic Influence
The French Revolution and the subsequent rise of Napoleon Bonaparte significantly impacted European governance and personal trajectories. Lucien Bonaparte, a proponent of democracy, initially believed Napoleon possessed an ambition that surpassed his love for the general weal, suggesting Napoleon might "ride on the crest of events" in the event of a counter-revolution. Lucien assisted Napoleon in triumphing over parliamentary institutions in France, though their relationship became strained during the consulate (1799–1804). 

Napoleon appointed Lucien as ambassador to the court of Madrid in November 1800. During this tenure, Lucien and the Spanish minister Godoy signed peace preliminaries at Badajoz on June 6, 1801, which frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon. This led to Lucien's resignation and his return to Paris, where he opposed some of Napoleon's schemes.

Other figures were also touched by the French Revolution. Thérèse Cabarrus, the daughter of François Cabarrus, played a role in the later stages of the French Revolution under the name Madame Tallien. Her father, a French adventurer and financier in Madrid, had been a proponent of reform under King Charles III. However, under the reactionary government of Charles IV, Cabarrus was imprisoned on charges of embezzlement. Later, his French birth and knowledge of Spanish affairs led Napoleon to appoint him as minister of finance after Charles IV was forced to abdicate in favor of Joseph, Napoleon's brother.

## Other Revolutionary and Political Events
In Scotland, the concept of revolution is linked to the experiences of Lady Grizel Baillie (1665–1746). As a child, she carried letters to the patriot Robert Baillie of Jerviswood, who was imprisoned. Due to his friendship with Baillie, Lady Grizel's father, Sir Patrick Hume, became a suspected man, and the king's troops occupied their home, Redbraes Castle. Following the execution of Baillie in 1684, Hume fled to Holland, where his family eventually joined him. The family returned to Scotland at the Revolution.

In Spain, political instability continued into the 19th century. Agustin Duran (1789–1862), a scholar born in Madrid, was suspended from his post in the education department in 1823 due to his political opinions. Later, the revolution of 1840 resulted in his dismissal from his position as secretary of the board for the censorship of the press, although he was reinstated in 1843.
