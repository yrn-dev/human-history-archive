# Russian Explorations in Central Asia: Prjevalsky, Pevtsov, and Roborovski

During the 19th century, a series of Russian expeditions conducted on scientific principles significantly expanded geographical knowledge of the Asiatic continent, specifically regarding the topography of western Chinese Turkestan and the northern borders of Tibet. These efforts were led by several distinguished explorers, including Nicolai Prjevalsky, General Pevtsov, and V. L. Roborovski.

## The Expeditions of Nicolai Prjevalsky

Nicolai Prjevalsky conducted several major explorations into Central Asia. Between 1871 and 1873, he crossed the Gobi desert from the north to Kansu in western China. During this period, he mapped the hydrography and defined the geography of Tsaidam, a region from which the great rivers of Burma, Siam, and China emanate. He also penetrated southwards to within a month's march of Lhasa.

In 1876, Prjevalsky discovered the Altyn Tagh range and visited the Lop Nor. By 1879, he followed the Urangi river to the Altai Mountains, where he demonstrated the significant physical changes that had occurred in the heart of Asia since the time of Jenghiz Khan. During this journey, he traversed the Gobi from Hami to Sachu and named the Dzungarian extension of the Gobi desert. He also visited the sources of the Salween and the Hwang-ho (Yellow river).

Prjevalsky's fourth journey, occurring between 1883 and 1885, took him to the Chinese borderland trade center of Sining. From there, he crossed the Altyn Tagh to Lop Nor, traveled through northern Tibet, and used the Cherchen-Keriya trade route to reach Khotan, eventually following the Tarim to Aksu. His work provided critical data for modern map construction; the longitude of Sachu (or Saitu) in Mongolia was revised to 94 deg. 54' E. of Greenwich based on the map accompanying his fourth exploration.

## Pevtsov and Roborovski

Following the death of Prjevalsky, General Pevtsov led further scientific inquiries. In 1889–1890, and again in 1894, Pevtsov and the botanist V. L. Roborovski added greatly to the knowledge of the northern borders of Tibet and the topography of western Chinese Turkestan. 

During the 1889–1890 expedition, Pevtsov was accompanied by Roborovski and the zoologist P. K. Kozlov, and they were later joined by the geologist K. I. Bogdanovich. Their route included Yarkand, Khotan, Kiria, Nia, and Charchan. While in Yarkand, General Pevtsov recorded that the small oasis, covering 264 square miles, had a population of 150,000, resulting in a density of 567 inhabitants per square mile.

## Geographical Controversies and Findings

The identification of Lop Nor was a subject of significant scientific debate. Prjevalsky placed the lake one degree further south than Chinese topographers had and noted that its water was sweet. However, Baron von Richthofen challenged this, suggesting that Prjevalsky had followed a new road and new river courses to reach two sweet-water lakes, one of which corresponded to the ancient Khas-omo, rather than the true Lop-noor.

Later observations further complicated the matter. In 1889, Pierre Bonvalot noted that inhabitants of the village of Lob were awaiting the arrival of Pevtsov. Subsequently, Prince Henri d’Orléans and Father Dedeken visited Lop-nor and found it almost dry, noting that the water had receded since Prjevalsky's visit thirteen years prior. The Prince concluded that the lake he saw was not the one seen by Prjevalsky, nor was it the mass of water depicted on Chinese maps. Local tradition suggested that the name "Lob-nor" referred not to a single lake, but to the entire marshy region watered by the Tarim, extending from the village of Lob to the end of the river.

## Other Russian Contributors

Beyond Prjevalsky, Pevtsov, and Roborovski, other conspicuous Russian explorers of the 19th century included Potanin, P.K. Kozlov, Lessar, and Annentkov, the latter of whom bridged the Trans-Caspian deserts via a railway. Potanin's work, along with Prjevalsky's, touched upon Mongol legends, such as the "Edjen-joro" (a collection of felt tents) reputed to contain the bones of Jenghiz Khan, cared for by a caste of Darhats.
