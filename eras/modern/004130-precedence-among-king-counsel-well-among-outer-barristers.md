# Precedence and Hierarchy of the English Bar

In the English legal system, the profession of the barrister is structured by a distinct hierarchy of rank, privilege, and precedence. The grade of barrister encompasses several levels, including the attorney-general and solicitor-general—who serve as the heads of the profession and hold office at the will of the government—as well as king's counsel and ordinary practitioners, the latter of whom are sometimes technically referred to as "utter barristers."

## Precedence and Seniority
Within the bar, precedence is strictly regulated. For both king's counsel and outer barristers, precedence is determined by seniority. Historically, there existed an order of serjeants-at-law who ranked immediately after king's counsel, but this order is now extinct.

## King's Counsel
A king's counsel is appointed by the lord chancellor via letters patent to serve as "one of His Majesty's counsel learned in the law." While there is no fixed timeframe required between a barrister's "call" to the bar and their application for a seat within the bar (the "silk gown"), it is generally understood that an applicant should have at least ten years of standing.

The role of king's counsel differs significantly from that of junior barristers in several ways:
* **Professional Duties:** They lead in court and provide opinions on submitted cases. Unlike junior barristers, they do not admit pupils to their chambers, nor do they accept work involving pleading or conveyancing.
* **Restrictions:** Unless granted a special licence, a king's counsel may not take a brief against the crown. Such licences are typically granted unless the crown requires the counsel's services for the case.
* **Historical Context:** The first appointment was Sir Francis Bacon, named "queen's counsel extraordinary" by Queen Elizabeth. Following this, appointments were irregular until 1668, becoming a regular order from 1775 onward. Their numbers grew from only 20 in 1789 to over 250 by the beginning of the 20th century.

## Outer Barristers and Junior Practice
Outer barristers, or junior barristers, handle the foundational work of the legal process. This includes drafting pleadings—the counter statements of parties made before joining issue—and performing conveyancing. Formerly, these tasks were handled by a separate class of special pleaders and conveyancers who held renewable one-year licences, but these duties are now part of the ordinary work of a junior barrister.

## Professional Etiquette and Symbols
The hierarchy of the bar is reflected in the etiquette surrounding the "brief-bag," used to carry counsel's papers to and from court. 
* **Blue Bags:** These are provided by barristers when first called. According to professional etiquette, it is a breach to allow a blue bag to be visible in court.
* **Red Bags:** These are the only bags permitted to be placed on desks. By legal etiquette, a red bag is given by a leading counsel to a junior who has proven useful in an important case.

Historically, in the early 19th century, the possession of any brief-bag was strictly limited to those who had received one from a king's counsel. At that time, king's counsel were few in number, were considered officers of the court, and received a salary of £40 per year along with supplies of pens, paper, and purple bags, which they distributed to rising juniors. These perquisites were abolished in 1830, and the salary was abolished in 1831.

## Governance and Discipline
The "call to the bar," which converts a law student from an Inn of Court into a barrister, is the exclusive privilege of the Inns of Court. These bodies also hold disciplinary power. The benchers (the governing body) may reject applicants for membership, refuse to call a student to the bar, or "dis-bar" (expel) barristers or benchers for misconduct they deem unworthy of the rank.

To address concerns that the self-elected benchers were not sufficiently attentive to professional minutiae, a bar committee was formed in 1883. This was succeeded in 1894 by the general council of the bar. This council consists of a proportion of outer barristers and king's counsel elected by those listed in the *Law List* within the United Kingdom. While the council lacks disciplinary powers, it serves to alert the benchers to gross violations of professional etiquette.
