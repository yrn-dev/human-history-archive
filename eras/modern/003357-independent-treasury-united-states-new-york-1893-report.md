# The Independent Treasury of the United States

The Independent Treasury of the United States is a subject of historical and financial study, notably documented in the work *The Independent Treasury of the United States*, published in New York in 1893 by David Kinley. This text serves as one of the authorities for the study of American banking and financial history.

## Related Banking Institutions and Systems
The financial landscape of the United States has involved various entities and regulatory bodies. In New York, the Clearing-House Committee of the New York Clearing-House has historically exercised significant influence over banking conditions. This influence is manifested in its ability to deny emergency aid to banks deemed to be unwisely conducted. During the panic of 1907, this power was utilized to remove several speculative financial interests from the control of national banks. At that time, membership in the Clearing-House was limited to state banks, national banks, and the sub-Treasury.

The monitoring of the money-market and exchanges relied on weekly reports of condition from these members. However, this index was considered incomplete because trust companies did not provide regular weekly reports. To rectify this, the state superintendent of banking announced in early 1908 that he would use legal powers to require weekly reports from trust companies to provide a more complete mirror of New York's banking conditions.

## Financial Instability and the 1893 Panic
The period surrounding the 1893 publication of David Kinley's report was marked by significant financial volatility. Silver legislation enacted by Congress in 1890 and 1878 created unease among bankers, leading to discrimination against silver certificates and silver dollars in their cash holdings.

In 1893, the treasury experienced heavy losses of gold. In response, a combination of leading bankers from Chicago, Baltimore, Philadelphia, Boston, and New York turned over a large portion of their holdings to replenish government reserves. The resulting panic of 1893 led to the suspension of approximately 150 national banks, although 84 of these eventually resumed business.

The subsequent years of liquidation saw a continued decline in the banking system. Between June 1, 1893, and June 1, 1899, the number of banks decreased from 3,856 to 3,585. During this same interval, the aggregate capital fell from $698,454,665 to $610,028,895.
