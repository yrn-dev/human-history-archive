# The Presidency and Resignation of Simón Bolívar

## Formation of the Republic of Colombia
Following his return to Angostura, where he was hailed as the father and deliverer of his country, Simón Bolívar obtained the enactment of a fundamental law on December 17, 1819. This law united the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolívar serving as president. The seat of government was provisionally moved to Rosario de Cucuta. 

During this period, Bolívar led the republican army against Spanish forces under Morillo. After a six-month armistice concluded at Truxillo on November 25, 1820, hostilities resumed. Bolívar achieved a decisive victory at Carabobo, routing the Spaniards and forcing the remnants of their army to take refuge in Puerto Cabello. By the end of 1821, the Spaniards were driven from nearly every part of the province. To secure this independence through permanent political institutions, the constitution of Colombia was adopted on August 30, 1821, with Bolívar as president and Santander as vice-president.

## Campaigns in Peru and Bolivia
Bolívar expanded his efforts to liberate the neighboring provinces of Ecuador and Peru. After the routing of Spanish forces at Pichincha and the entry into Quito in June 1822, Bolívar entered Lima in triumph and was invested with absolute power as dictator. Despite temporary setbacks caused by republican factions in Peru, the liberating army was organized by June 1824. Bolívar defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin on August 6, while his colleague Sucre gained a decisive victory at Ayacucho, completing the dispersion of Spanish forces.

In June 1825, Bolívar visited Upper Peru, which had separated from the government of Buenos Aires to form a new state called Bolivia in his honor. The first congress of Bolivia, assembling in August 1825, declared Bolívar the perpetual protector and requested that he prepare a constitution for the new republic.

## The Bolivian Constitution and Political Tension
Bolívar presented his project for a constitution to the Bolivian congress on May 25, 1826. This code included a highly controversial provision: the executive authority was to be held by a president for life who would be without responsibility and possessed the power to nominate his own successor. This proposal alarmed republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires. In Peru, Bolívar faced accusations that he intended to unite Colombia, Bolivia, and Peru into a single state under his own rule as a perpetual dictator.

Despite these apprehensions, a committee's favorable report led the congress to approve the document as the constitution of the republic. General Sucre was chosen as president for life under this constitution, though Sucre only accepted the appointment for two years and on the condition that 2,000 Colombian troops remain with him.

## Domestic Unrest and Resignation
While Bolívar was abroad, the situation in Colombia deteriorated. Although Santander had administered the government ably, Paez—the commander in Venezuela—entered a state of open rebellion against the government. Paez had been accused of arbitrary conduct regarding the enrolment of citizens in the Caracas militia and refused to obey the senate, supported by a party in the northern departments seeking separation from the republic.

Bolívar departed Lima in September 1826, leaving the government to a council headed by Santa Cruz. He arrived in Bogota on November 14 and assumed extraordinary powers authorized by the constitution for cases of rebellion. He traveled to Venezuela to stop the effusion of blood, reaching Puerto Cabello on December 31 and issuing a general amnesty the following day. After a meeting with Paez, he established headquarters in Caracas to manage disturbances in the northern departments.

During this time, Bolívar and Santander were re-elected as president and vice-president, respectively. However, in February 1827, Bolívar formally resigned the presidency of the republic. In his resignation, he expressed a determination to retire into private life on his patrimonial estate to refute accusations of ambition.

## Return to Power
Santander opposed Bolívar's resignation, arguing that the country's agitations could only be appeased by the personal influence and authority of the liberator. This sentiment was confirmed by a resolution of congress, though the decision was not unanimous. Consequently, Bolívar decided to resume his functions and returned to Bogota to take the oaths of office. Prior to his arrival, he issued three simultaneous decrees: one establishing constitutional order throughout Colombia, one convoking a national convention at Ocana, and one granting a general amnesty.
