# English Book-Plates and Decorative Modes

The English book-plate, serving as a private token of book-ownership, reflects the prevailing tastes in decorative art from the late Elizabethan period through the late Victorian era. While the fashion for ornamental book-plates as marks of possession originated in Germany—where artists such as Albrecht Durer, Lucas Cranach, and Hans Holbein produced early examples—the English tradition developed its own distinct characteristics over several centuries.

## Early English Styles and the Restoration
The earliest known English example is the gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon. Another early instance is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585. Until the final quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number and characterized by a remarkable simplicity that lacked the elaborateness found in German styles. These early plates were generally plainly armorial, with decoration typically restricted to wreaths, palms, or a symmetrical arrangement of mantling.

Following the Restoration, book-plates became an established accessory for most well-ordered libraries. While they maintained the simplicity of previous heraldic arrangements, their "physiognomy" changed. They began to invariably use dots and tincture lines, a method devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta, author of *Tesserae Gentilitiae*, which was adopted across Europe. Additionally, the mantling surrounding the shield became more elaborate, resembling the periwigs of the period.

## French Influence and the French Revolution
The elaborate mantling style of the Restoration period was imported from France, though it developed its own character within England. From that point until the dawn of the French Revolution, English modes of decoration in book-plates—and most other chattels—followed the ruling French taste, typically trailing by some years.

## Classifications of Style and Composition
In 1880, the Hon. J. Leicester Warren (Lord de Tabley) published *A Guide to the Study of Book-Plates*, which established a general classification of styles:
*   **Early Armorial:** Plates produced prior to the Restoration, such as the Nicholas Bacon plate.
*   **Jacobean:** A term used to describe the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian, Queen Anne, and Restoration days (exemplified by the 1698 plate of Francis Gwyn of Lansanor).
*   **Chippendale:** This refers to the rococo style, represented by the 1762 plate of P.A. Convers.
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** A style belonging to the period associated with the urn.

Beyond these general styles, certain compositions were prevalent. Many older and modern plates were pictorial. Specific English genres include "book-piles" and "library interiors," such as the 1699 *ex-libris* of William Hewer. Other common forms included landscape-plates by the Bewick school of wood engravers, portrait-plates (notably those of John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys), and allegories engraved by artists such as George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth. In these pictorial styles, the armorial element usually played a secondary role.

## Modern Developments and Collecting
For much of its history, the design of book-plates was left to the routine skill of heraldic stationers. In more recent years, however, the practice has been recognized as a minor branch of higher art. Modern designs tend to emphasize symbolic or allegorical elements over purely heraldic ones. Notable modern English artists in this field include Warrington Hogg, John Leighton, John Vinycomb, Charles Ricketts, J. Forbes Nixon, Erat Harrison, J.D. Batten, Robert Anning Bell, G.W. Eve, and C.W. Sherborn.

The popularization of the book-plate, which was once restricted to important collections or ancestral libraries, has been aided by the development of process work, which made the reproduction of elaborate designs cheaper and easier. While most modern plates are reproduced by process, a few artists, such as G.W. Eve and C.W. Sherborn in England, continue to use the graver.

The academic study and collection of book-plates is a relatively modern pursuit, dating back to approximately 1875. This interest led to the formation of collectors' societies in the United States, France, Germany, and England. These societies produce various publications, including the *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* in Berlin, the *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* in Paris, and *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* in London.
