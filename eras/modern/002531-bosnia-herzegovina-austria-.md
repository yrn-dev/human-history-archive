# Bosnia and Herzegovina

Bosnia and Herzegovina are Yugo-Slav provinces located in the Balkan Peninsula. Historically part of the Turkish Empire, these territories underwent a transition from Turkish suzerainty to administration and eventual annexation by Austria-Hungary between 1878 and 1908.

## Administration and Occupation (1878–1908)
Under the Treaty of Berlin in 1878, the principalities of Bosnia and Herzegovina were placed under the administration of Austria-Hungary, though they continued to acknowledge the Sultan of Turkey as their suzerain. The occupation began on July 29, 1878, when 60,000 Austrian troops under General Philippovich crossed the frontier. 

The process of establishing control was arduous due to the mountainous terrain and strenuous resistance from Mahommedans. Austria eventually required 200,000 men to crush the resistance after months of fighting. Even after the capture of Serajevo in August, resistance persisted, and many insurgents were executed under military law. In 1879, the Porte recognized the occupation.

The provinces were not attached to either half of the Dual Monarchy but were instead placed under the control of the joint minister of finance. Early civil administration faced challenges: the Christian population remained dissatisfied as they were still required to pay rent to Mahommedan begs, and tensions existed between members of the Greek Church and Roman Catholics. Additionally, disturbances in Dalmatia spread into Herzegovina in 1881, necessitating another military expedition to restore order.

## Annexation Crisis of 1908
On October 5, 1908, Austria proclaimed the formal annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina without prior warning or parley. While Austria had already administered the provinces in fact, the formal declaration was intended to forestall a possible reassertion of Turkish sovereignty following the Young Turk Revolution of that summer. 

The annexation was supported by the German Emperor, but it triggered a significant European crisis. The act was viewed as a violent act and a public affront to Russia, specifically as a personal slight to the Russian negotiator, Monsieur Isvolsky, as the announcement interrupted ongoing negotiations before concessions to Russia had been finalized.

The international response was characterized by anger and protest:
* **England:** Refused to recognize the annexation, citing the principle from the 1871 London Conference that no power can modify treaty stipulations without the consent of all contracting parties.
* **Turkey:** Protested the "lawless act" and organized an effective boycott of Austrian merchandise.
* **Serbia:** Mobilized its army.
* **Italy:** The annexation served to estrange Italy from its ally, Germany.

The resulting bitter animosity between Russia and Austria is cited as a penultimate cause of the Great War. The annexation was eventually recognized by the Powers in 1909.

## Geography and Demographics
As of 1909, Bosnia and Herzegovina (under Austria-Hungary) covered an area of 19,696 square miles with a population of 1,568,092. Other records from 1895 list the area as 19,702 square miles and the population as 1,591,036. The region's population density in 1909 was 70.9 people per square mile.

## Post-War Status and Dismemberment
Following the conflicts of the World War, the dismemberment of Austria led to the transfer of territories. States arising from this dismemberment acquired property of the former Austrian Government within their borders. Property deemed to be of "predominant historic interest" to Bosnia and Herzegovina (along with other regions such as Croatia, Slavonia, and Dalmatia) could be transferred without payment.

In financial settlements regarding the unsecured bonded pre-war debt of the former empire, the Reparation Commission distributed debt based on the revenues of separated territories for the three years preceding the war; however, Bosnia and Herzegovina were specifically excluded from this particular calculation.
