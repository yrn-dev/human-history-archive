# Messianic Prophecy and Theological Criticism

The study of Messianic prophecy and the development of the Messianic idea has been a central focus of biblical and theological scholarship, particularly within the critical movements of the 19th century. This field involves the examination of prophetic writings, the identification of their authors, and the analysis of how these texts were compiled and edited over time.

## Critical Approaches to Prophetic Writings

Modern scholarship has introduced a radical examination of prophetic writings, developed by figures such as Wellhausen, Stade, Duhm, Cheyne, and Marti. A primary tenet of this newer criticism is the recognition that all pre-exilic prophecy has reached the modern reader through the work of post-exilic editors. Consequently, the critical focus has shifted from identifying which elements in books like Jeremiah or Isaiah are later additions to determining how pre-exilic elements can be extracted from these post-exilic collections.

This literary criticism is closely linked to the investigation of the origin and development of the Messianic idea. For example, in the study of the book of Jeremiah, scholars note that while the nucleus of the book was formed in 604 B.C. by the amanuensis Baruch, the work reached its final form through a succession of stages completed long after the return of the Israelites from Babylon. Certain sections, such as the prophecy announcing the fall of Babylon (l. 1-li. 58), are identified as not being written by Jeremiah, but rather shortly before 538 B.C.

## Theological Disputes and Heresy Trials

The application of critical methods to Messianic prophecy has led to significant theological conflict. Charles Augustus Briggs, an American Hebrew scholar and professor at the Union Theological Seminary, became a central figure in these disputes. In 1892, Briggs was tried for heresy by the presbytery of New York based on his inaugural address. Among the charges were his assertions that "the great body of Messianic prediction has not and cannot be fulfilled" and that "many of the Old Testament predictions have been reversed by history."

Briggs further challenged traditional views by stating that Moses was not the author of the Pentateuch and that Isaiah did not author half of the book bearing his name. He also argued that the processes of redemption extend to the world to come, criticizing Protestant theology for limiting redemption to this world. Although acquitted by the presbytery, the general assembly suspended him in 1893, influenced in part by the "dogmatic and irritating" tone of his address.

## Perspectives on the Messianic Kingdom

Different texts and scholars present varying visions of the Messianic kingdom and the destiny of the Israelites. Some writings exhibit a vigorous optimism regarding the ultimate well-being of Israel on earth, depicting the blessedness of the chosen people in the Messianic kingdom in sensuous and glowing colors. 

In contrast, other passages are characterized by hopeless pessimism. In these views, the world is seen as a scene of irremediable corruption whose end is near, and the destiny of Israel on earth is described only in terms of "woe," "mourning," and "lamentation," with the advent of a new spiritual world being the only hope.

## Comparative Religious and Literary Analysis

The presence or absence of Messianic hope varies across different historical texts. For instance, the work of 1 Maccabees, while written in the style of Old Testament historical books, is noted for a virtual absence of Messianic hope and a religious reticence that avoids the use of the divine name. The text emphasizes the observance of the law and deplores the cessation of prophecy, offering rewards for the dying that are limited to this life, with no mention of the resurrection of the dead or the immortality of the soul.

Other theologians have focused on the polemical aspects of prophecy. Abraham Kuenen, a Dutch Protestant theologian and supporter of modern theology at Leiden, published a study on Hebrew prophecy in 1875. His work was largely polemical and specifically targeted those who attempted to base theological dogmas on the fulfillment of prophecy.
