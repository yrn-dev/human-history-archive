# Europe: Education and Systems

## Historical Social and Educational Conditions
The social and educational landscape of Europe has undergone significant transformations across different eras. In the eighth century, Western Europe was characterized as a shattered civilization. While not entirely barbaric, it existed in a state of confusion and universal insecurity, lacking law and administration. During this period, roads were destroyed and education was disorganized. This environment of brigandage and unpunished crime led to the development of the feudal system, as individuals sought protection by linking themselves to powerful leaders, such as barbaric chiefs, bishops, landowners, or claimants to Roman official positions. Traces of this system remain in the social structure of every European community west of Russia.

## Legal Education in England
The systematic education of legal professionals in England evolved through distinct paths for solicitors and barristers. For solicitors, professional education was largely absent until the establishment of the Incorporated Law Society in 1825. Prior to this, a 1729 statute required applicants to undergo an examination by a judge for a fee of one shilling and complete five years of preliminary service under articles, though the examination often became a mere formality. The Law Society introduced lectures in 1833 and regular examinations in 1836. By 1860, a system of preliminary, intermediate, and final examinations was implemented, with the latter two focusing on professional law and practical acquaintance with modern English law.

For barristers, the corporate life of the Inns of Court in London declined during the 16th and 17th centuries, and the Inns of Chancery ceased to be used for student education. Systematic teaching did not revive until the middle of the 19th century with the 1852 establishment of the council of legal education. This body, consisting of twenty judges and barristers, controls the education of students preparing for the bar. The central feature of this system is a rigorous examination covering subjects such as Roman law, Equity, Common law, Evidence, Procedure and Criminal law, Real and Personal Property, and Constitutional law and legal history.

## European Systems and International Influence
European systems of organization and education have served as models for other nations. In Japan, the departments of State were reorganized on European lines in 1885 under Premier Ito Hirobumi. As part of these reforms, a legion of students was sent westward to complete their education.

Additionally, European systems of measurement and science have been adopted internationally. Russia's geodetic surveys in its European half utilize European systems of triangulation to connect St Petersburg with Greenwich, and Russia adopts the Greenwich meridian as the zero for all longitude values.

## Imperialism and Internal State
By the closing years of the nineteenth century, European powers operated under the assumption that the entire world should fall under European dominion. This drive for "loot and lordship," described by Rudyard Kipling as "the White Man’s Burthen," led European foreign offices to carve up populous countries in Asia, including China, Japan, Persia, and the disintegrating Ottoman Empire. 

However, this outward imperialism contrasted with internal instability. The European Powers engaged in this global competition while facing several internal crises:
*   **Education and Science:** Populations at home were often illiterate or half-educated, and scientific research was conducted by only a few thousand men.
*   **Politics and Economics:** Internal political systems were in states of convulsive change or tension, and economic systems were described as creaking and provisional.
*   **Religion:** The religions of these powers were considered to be far gone in decay.

## Academic Study of European Education
The study of European educational systems has also been a subject of specific academic and professional inquiry. Between 1836 and 1838, Alexander Dallas Bache, an American physicist and professor at the University of Pennsylvania, spent time in Europe specifically to examine European systems of education. This research was conducted on behalf of the trustees of what would become Girard College in 1848. Upon his return to the United States, Bache published a report on his findings which was regarded as very valuable.
