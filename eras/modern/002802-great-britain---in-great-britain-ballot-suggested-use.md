# Great Britain

## History of the Ballot and Parliamentary Voting
The use of the ballot in Great Britain was first suggested in a political tract during the time of Charles II. In 1662, the Scots parliament utilized the ballot to proceed on the Billeting Act, a measure proposed by Middleton to secure the ostracism of political opponents, including Lauderdale, who were declared incapable of public office via secret vote. The process involved members writing the names of twelve suspected persons in a disguised hand on pieces of paper. These billets were placed in a sealed bag held by the registrar, then opened in the exchequer chamber where the names were concealed on oath and the billets burned.

Following the king's repudiation of the Billeting Act, the ballot was not mentioned again until 1705. At that time, Fletcher of Saltoun proposed secret voting to protect members from court influence in a measure for a provisional government of Scotland by annual parliaments in the event of Queen Anne's death. However, as the British parliament became emancipated from the power of the crown and adopted a strictly representative system of election, open voting became essential, and the reasons for deliberative secret voting disappeared.

Serious demands for the ballot in parliamentary elections resurfaced during the parliamentary reform agitations of the early 19th century. The Benthamites advocated for the system in 1817, and banners calling for the ballot appeared at the Peterloo Massacre in 1819. In 1830, O'Connell introduced a bill on the subject. The original draft of Lord John Russell's Reform Bill also provided for its introduction, likely suggested by Lords Duncannon and Durham. Later, the historian Grote became the primary supporter of the ballot in the House of Commons. Between 1833 and 1839, despite ridicule from Sydney Smith—who referred to the system as a "mouse-trap" and Grote's proposal as a "dagger-box" for stabbing a candidate's card—the minority supporting the ballot grew from 106. Eventually, Great Britain adopted the Australian ballot, a system that was subsequently introduced in Canada.

## International Relations and Global Conflict
Throughout the 18th century, Great Britain was engaged in significant global struggles, most notably with France. Between 1755 and 1763, the two nations struggled for control of India and America. This period included the Seven Years' War (1756–1763), during which France was allied with Russia and Austria against Britain and Prussia. In 1759, the British general Wolfe took Quebec, and the 1763 Peace of Paris resulted in Canada being ceded to Britain and the British becoming dominant in India.

Relations with the United States were marked by the American revolutionary drama, leading to the 1776 Declaration of Independence and a subsequent Treaty of Peace in 1783. In the 19th century, some American leaders, such as a prominent Democratic leader in Congress, viewed Great Britain as the foremost and natural rival of the United States, arguing that their interests were always opposed. This tension manifested in disputes over the Oregon territory—where the U.S. refused to "yield up one inch" to Great Britain—and conflicts over the Clayton-Bulwer Treaty and the British reassertion of the right of search on the high seas.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the Bismarckian system in Germany included the principle of maintaining good relations with Great Britain. This was partly because Italy would not commit to any action that brought it into war with Britain, a fact specified in the secret text of the Triple Alliance. While Germany initially maintained these relations, it also encouraged France to develop colonial possessions to create friction and rivalry with Great Britain.

In the early 20th century, the British government, under the influence of Mr. Lloyd George and the Chancellor of the Exchequer, emphasized the necessity of Britain maintaining its prestige and place among the Great Powers. The Chancellor stated that Britain's influence had historically been invaluable to human liberty and had redeemed continental nations from national extinction or overwhelming disaster.

## Trade and Agriculture
Great Britain maintains significant commercial ties globally. For example, it is the primary export market for Denmark, receiving 60% of Denmark's total exports. In return, Great Britain accounts for approximately 20% of Denmark's principal import trade. Additionally, a large portion of Denmark's commercial fleet is used for the carrying trade between foreign ports, particularly British ones.

Domestically, Great Britain has seen a continuous expansion in its fruit industry. Board of Agriculture returns for orchard areas showed growth from 199,178 acres in 1888 to 234,660 acres in 1901. While there was a decline in 1892, this was attributed to a change in data collection: after 1891, returns were only collected from holdings of more than one acre, whereas they previously included holdings of a quarter of an acre or more. Because many small holdings and suburban gardens are not included in official returns, the actual land devoted to fruit culture is higher than official figures suggest. By June 1908, official returns stated that 308,000 acres were devoted to all kinds of fruit cultivation. This expansion was mainly concentrated in England, with slight decreases in Scotland and Wales. The largest extent of orchard land is found in the dozen counties located in the west or south of the island.
