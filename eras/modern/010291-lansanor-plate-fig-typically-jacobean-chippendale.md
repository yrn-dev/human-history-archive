# Book-Plates and Decorative Styles

The study and collection of book-plates—private tokens of book-ownership—is a comparatively modern pursuit, with the taste for collecting them hardly dating further back than 1875. Significant impetus for the field was provided in 1880 by Lord de Tabley (then the Hon. Leicester Warren) through the publication of the *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates*. This work established a general classification of styles that remains accepted.

## Classification of Book-Plate Styles

The classification of book-plates is primarily based on the main styles of decoration, which serve as the criteria for dating old examples when other data is absent. The established styles include:

*   **Early Armorial:** This style refers to plates created previous to the Restoration, such as the Nicholas Bacon plate.
*   **Jacobean:** Though described as a somewhat misleading term, it is understood to include the heavy decorative manner associated with the early Georgian days, Queen Anne, and the Restoration. An example of this style is the 1698 book-plate of Francis Gwyn of Lansanor.
*   **Chippendale:** This style is identified with the rococo; the French plate of Convers (dated 1762) is a representative example.
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** This style belongs to the period characterized by the urn.

## Composition and Genres

Beyond general stylistic classifications, certain compositions were prevalent during specific periods. While many older and modern plates are essentially pictorial, several distinct English genera have been identified:

*   **Library Interiors and Book-Piles:** These are pictorial representations, exemplified by the 1699 *ex-libris* of William Hewer, who served as secretary to Samuel Pepys.
*   **Portrait-Plates:** Notable examples include those of the architect John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys.
*   **Allegories:** These were engraved by artists such as George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.
*   **Landscape-Plates:** These were produced by wood engravers of the Bewick school.

In these pictorial genres, the armorial element typically plays a secondary role.

## Evolution of the English Book-Plate

The English book-plate reflects the prevailing decorative tastes of different epochs from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian period. The gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon is the oldest discovered English example, though a hand-painted armorial device belonging to Henry VIII in the British Museum's King's library is not considered a book-plate in the modern sense. Another early example is the plate of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585.

Until the last quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number and characterized by a simplicity that lacked German elaborateness. These were generally plainly armorial, featuring symmetrical mantling and occasional wreaths or palms. 

Following the Restoration, book-plates became established accessories for well-ordered libraries. This period introduced distinctive characteristics, such as the use of tincture lines and dots—a method devised by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae*—and more elaborate mantling that resembled the periwigs of the era. From this point until the French Revolution, English book-plate decoration generally followed the ruling French taste, albeit with a delay of several years.

## Related Decorative Arts

The term "Chippendale" also appears in the context of furniture. In the second half of the 18th century, carved mahogany chairs were produced in the style of Chippendale, sometimes featuring ribbon patterns. William Ince, an 18th-century cabinetmaker and furniture designer, was a successful imitator of Chippendale. While Ince's work was lighter and served as a bridge between the florid, massive Chippendale style and the boudoir-like forms of Hepplewhite, his chairs are considered inferior to those of Chippendale. Ince, who partnered with Thomas Mayhew in London, published a folio of ninety-five plates titled *The Universal System of Household Furniture* (likely around 1762).

## Professional Societies and Literature

As interest in book-plates grew, collectors founded societies in the United States, France, Germany, and England. These societies produced various archives and journals, including the quarterly *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* in Berlin and the monthly publications *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* in Paris and *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* in London.
