# European Health and Fever in Various Regions

The experience of Europeans in different global territories has been significantly shaped by the prevalence of various fevers and the influence of local climates on their health. While some regions have proven favorable to European existence, others have been characterized by severe illness and high death rates.

## Tropical and Equatorial Regions

In certain tropical areas, the environment is often unhealthy for Europeans. In some rich and fertile lands, the primary health threats include dysentery and black-water fever, alongside pneumonia, bronchitis, and ordinary malarial fever and malarial ulcers. In these regions, the climate is generally agreeable and not unbearably hot, except in low-lying districts, while frost can occur on high mountain plateaus during the dry season.

In British East Africa, health outcomes vary by zone. The coast region is generally more healthy than the coast lands of other tropical countries due to the dryness of the soil and a constant breeze from the Indian Ocean. The highlands, defined as districts over 5,000 ft. high, are considered very healthy, and fever is almost unknown there. However, the region bordering Victoria Nyanza is typically tropical with annual rainfall exceeding 60 inches, making it quite unsuitable for Europeans.

In Bangkok, the death rate is high, particularly among children. This is attributed to the prevalence of fevers, smallpox, and cholera during dry weather. To combat these issues, sanitation has been improved through the boring of numerous artesian wells to provide good water.

## Arid and Upland Territories

In British Baluchistan, the climate is described as bracing, exhilarating, and dry. While this environment is generally favorable to European existence, the extremes of temperature in the region lead to the development of fever in very severe forms.

## Infectious Fevers and Dengue

Dengue is an infectious fever found in warm climates, characterized by a sudden attack of fever, severe headache, erythema, and rheumatic pains in the muscles and joints. After a few days, a crisis is reached, followed by a slighter return of pain and fever and an eruption resembling measles. The disease is rarely fatal, except in cases of infancy, old age, or extreme weakness caused by other illnesses. 

The virus is likely communicated by an intermediary culex. Dengue is nearly always epidemic, though it can be pandemic or endemic. It ranges generally between 32° 47' N. and 23° 23' S., with the West Indies and equatorial East Africa being areas where it is most nearly endemic. Historically, epidemics occurred in Egypt and the East Indies (1779-1780), India and the West Indies (1824-1826), and across tropical portions of the East reaching China (1870-1875). A major outbreak also occurred in Asia Minor and along the shores of the Aegean in 1888 and 1889. The name "dengue" is identified with the Spanish word for prim or stiff behavior, referring to the cramped movements of sufferers; it was also called "dandy-fever" by negroes. Some sources suggest both terms are corruptions of the Swahili word *dinga* or *denga*, meaning a sudden attack of cramp.

## Fever in Historical and Military Contexts

Fever has appeared in various historical accounts of individuals and military forces. In the case of Alexander, his final illness was marked by recurring fever. His diary notes that he was feverish on the eighteenth day of Daisius and suffered from fever during the night of the nineteenth. By the twenty-first, his fever grew much worse, and it remained with him until he became speechless on the twenty-fifth. Alexander died on the twenty-eighth day of the month.

In the context of the conflict between the British and the Ashanti, fever played a role in the military outcome. When Sir Garnet Wolseley landed at Cape Coast on October 2, he found that the Ashanti force had been decimated by fever and smallpox, which led them to prepare for a return home.
