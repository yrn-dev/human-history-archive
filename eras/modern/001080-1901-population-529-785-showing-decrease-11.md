# Population Trends and Regional Data (1901)

The archival records from 1901 provide a detailed snapshot of population statistics, land area, and economic conditions across various districts and states, particularly within India and the Danish colony of Greenland. A recurring theme across these records is a notable decrease in population during the decade leading up to 1901, frequently attributed to famine and disease.

## Population Decreases in Indian States and Districts

Several regions reported significant population declines in 1901. In the native state of Idar, part of the Mahi Kantha agency in the Gujarat division of Bombay, the population was 168,557, representing a 44% decrease over the decade due to famine. Similarly, the native state of Bundi in the Rajputana agency saw its population fall to 171,227, a 42% decrease also attributed to famine.

In Kotah, an offshoot of the Bundi state, the 1901 population was 544,879, showing a 24% decrease resulting from famine. The state had experienced drought in 1896-1897 and more severely in 1899-1900. In the native state of Baroda, located within the Gujarat province of Bombay, the population was 1,952,692, a 19% decrease in the decade. This decline was attributed to the famines of 1896-1897 and 1900-1901, accompanying epidemics of fever and cholera, and the plague.

Other districts also showed declines:
*   **Azamgarh:** Population of 1,529,785 (11% decrease).
*   **Buldana:** Population of 423,616 (12% decrease due to famine).
*   **Bilaspur:** Population of 1,012,972 (13% decrease).
*   **Bijapur:** Population of 735,435 (8% decrease).
*   **Benares District:** Population of 882,084 (4% decrease due to famine).
*   **Benares Division:** Population of 5,069,020 (6% decrease).

## Geography and Economy of Selected Districts

The districts varied significantly in their land use and resources. Azamgarh, covering 2207 sq. m., is bounded by the river Gogra to the north and the Ganges to the east. Its fertile soil supports crops of indigo, sugar-cane, and rice, with several indigo factories in operation. Benares District, covering 1008 sq. m., is characterized by a level surface with deep ravines and fertile soil (except in saline Usar tracts), producing opium, sugar-cane, rice, barley, wheat, and other food-grains.

In the district of Bijapur, which occupies a barren plain of 5669 sq. m., the principal crops are cotton, oil-seeds, pulse, wheat, and millet. The region is known for its factories for pressing and ginning cotton, as well as the manufacture of blankets and silk and cotton goods. Buldana, in Berar, is rich in agricultural produce, particularly in the valley of the Purna, and exports oil-seeds, wheat, and cotton.

## Administrative and Political History

Some regions possess distinct political histories. Azamgarh was ceded to the Company in 1801 by the wazirs of Lucknow and later became a center of mutiny in 1857. On June 3, 1857, the 17th Regiment of Native Infantry mutinied there, murdering officers and taking government treasure to Fyzabad. The area remained a site of conflict between rebels and Gurkhas until Colonel Kelly cleared it in October 1858.

Kotah was established when the emperor Shah Jahan bestowed it upon a younger son of the Bundi raja for services rendered during Shah Jahan's rebellion against his father, Jahangir. In 1897, Kotah regained a portion of its area that had been used to form Jhalawar in 1838.

## Infrastructure and Urban Centers

Railway development was a key feature of these regions. The Bengal & North-Western railway opened a branch to Azamgarh town in 1898. Benares is served by the Oudh & Rohilkhand railway, the East Indian railway, and a branch of the Bengal & North-Western railway. Bilaspur is traversed by lines of the Bengal-Nagpur railway in three directions.

## Danish Greenland

Outside of India, the Danish colony had an estimated area of 45,000 sq. m. and a population of 11,893 in 1901, including approximately 300 Europeans. The Eskimo population on the west coast had decreased since the mid-18th century, possibly due to smallpox and other infectious diseases. While the native population saw a slight increase during the latter half of the 19th century, the east coast population continued to decrease. In the north, near Scoresby Fjord and Franz Josef Fjord, ruins of settlements exist where Eskimos have either wandered south or died out. A small group known as the "Smith Sound Eskimo" or "Arctic Highlanders" lived near Cape York, numbering about 240.
