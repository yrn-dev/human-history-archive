# German-Magyar Domination and the Bohemian National Movement

The period following the death of Francis I on March 2, 1835, was marked by increasing political instability within the Austrian Empire. Under the succession of Ferdinand I, who was incapacitated by epilepsy, a veiled regency governed the state. This era of weak central administration and divided counsels allowed popular discontent to grow, particularly among Slav populations who felt oppressed under German-Magyar domination.

## Early Resistance and the Czech Literary Movement
In Bohemia, the Czech literary movement evolved into an organized resistance against the established order. Nationalists in Bohemia adopted "Repeal" as their watchword, mirroring the rhetoric used by nationalists in Ireland, and framed their opposition to the existing system as a criticism of the English administration in Ireland. Simultaneously, other Slav groups expressed similar unrest; in the south, the "Illyrian" movement, led by Ljudevit Gaj through the *Illyrian National Gazette* from 1836, initially sought a Pan-Slav union before shifting its focus toward the revival of a "Triune Kingdom" (comprising Dalmatia, Croatia, and Slavonia) independent of the Hungarian crown.

## The Struggle for Bohemian Autonomy
Bohemia, the richest part of the Austrian dominions, became a primary center of racial and political conflict. By 1900, the population reached 6,318,280, consisting of approximately 65% Czechs and 35% Germans. The Czechs predominantly occupied the south, south-east, and middle of the country, while Germans were concentrated in the large towns and near the northern and western borders.

A significant party led by Rieger and Palacky advocated for the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy in its fullest extent, including Silesia and Moravia. They demanded that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia in Prague and that the region be granted a position in the monarchy similar to that of Hungary. This movement was supported not only by Czechs but also by certain German-descent nobles—such as Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg, Count Leo Thun, and Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz—who sought to preserve their power against German Liberals.

In 1868, Czech leaders presented a "declaration" to the diet at Prague. This document asserted that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia, that the diet was not qualified to elect representatives for Vienna, and that a separate settlement must be reached with Bohemia. This declaration was signed by eighty-one members, including bishops and feudal nobles, leading the German majority to declare those seats forfeited and trigger new elections and subsequent riots.

## Linguistic and Educational Conflict
The contest between Czechs and Germans was fought with great vehemence over language and education. Germans viewed German as the *Kultur-sprache* and resisted the introduction of Slavonic languages into higher education. However, several government actions shifted the balance:
*   **1880 Ordinance:** Required that business brought before law courts or government offices be handled in the language in which it was introduced.
*   **1886 Order:** Mandated that business introduced in Czech be handled in Czech within the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid translations.
*   **Education:** Czech elementary and middle schools were founded, and Czech classes were introduced in German schools. Most controversially for the Germans, Czech classes began at the university of Prague in 1882.

While educated Czechs were generally bilingual, German parents in Bohemia stopped teaching their children Czech after 1867. Consequently, Germans began to view the requirement for Czech language skills in government posts as a means of excluding them from employment.

## Parliamentary Obstruction and Administrative Failure
The tension between the two races frequently paralyzed the government. In the early 20th century, Dr. von Koerber attempted to resolve the conflict by proposing that Bohemia be divided into ten districts (five Czech, three German, and two mixed). The Czechs rejected this, demanding that Czech and German be placed on equal footing for all official purposes throughout Bohemia.

Because the Germans refused to recognize Czech as a language of internal service, the Czechs blocked parliamentary business using "urgency motions" and noisy obstruction, including one sitting that lasted 57 hours. This aggressiveness led German parties—including the Christian Socialists, German Populists, German Progressists, and Constitutional Landed Proprietors—to form a supreme committee to protect German racial interests.

Ultimately, the inability of the parliament to vote on budgets for 1903, 1904, and 1905 forced the government to sanction estimates by imperial ordinance under paragraph 14 of the constitution.
