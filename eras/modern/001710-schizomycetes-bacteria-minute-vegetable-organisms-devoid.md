# Schizomycetes

The schizomycetes, commonly known as bacteria, are minute vegetable organisms characterized by a lack of chlorophyll and an ordinary nucleus. They multiply through repeated bipartitions and consist of single cells that may be cylindrical, oblong, or spherical in shape, or may form other aggregates of cells or filamentous structures. They are further distinguished by the absence of ordinary sexual reproduction.

## Classification and Terminology
While the term "bacteria" is the most common designation, it is often used alongside other terms such as "microbes," "micro-organisms," "microphytes," "bacilli," and "micrococci." However, these are often considered unsatisfactory or too vague for scientific use. Botanists generally prefer the term "schizomycetes," proposed by Naegeli in 1857.

Due to their manner of division and the absence of a nucleus and sexual reproduction, schizomycetes resemble the Schizophyceae (blue-green algae or Cyanophyceae). Consequently, these two groups are usually united in the class Schizophyta, reflecting the view that most typical bacteria derived from Cyanophyceae. Other relationships have been suggested: some forms, such as "Sarcina," resemble the Palmellaceae among green algae, while the Myxobacteriaceae group suggests a link to the Myxomycetes. Additionally, the formation of endospores and the existence of ciliated micrococci recall flagellate Protozoa, such as *Chromulina* and *Monas*.

## Distribution and Habitat
Schizomycetes are ubiquitous saprophytes. They are found in the sea, running streams, rivers, and still ponds or ditches. They are especially prevalent in soil, bogs, refuse heaps, and drains, as well as in any organic infusions left to stand. Any solid food-stuff (such as vegetables or meat preserves) or liquid containing organic matter (such as milk, beer, blood, or urine) will soon swarm with bacteria if moisture is present and temperatures are not abnormal.

Their distribution in the air is uneven. While they exist worldwide in the air and on exposed bodies, they are not equally distributed. In open country and at high altitudes, particularly in the north, few or no schizomycetes may be detected. In towns, they may exist in minute clouds with empty interspaces, though laboratories and closed spaces where cultivation is promoted may have air considerably laden with them. This distribution is easily influenced by wind, rain, temperature changes, and movement.

## Life History and Morphology
Historically, F. J. Cohn's classification (published in 1872 and extended in 1875) dominated the study of these organisms. Cohn based his system on the assumption that bacterial forms—such as the rod-like bacillus or spherical micrococcus—were practically constant. However, this was challenged in 1873 by Lankester, who noted that *Bacterium rubescens* (later *Beggiatoa roseo-persicina*) passes through various phases that could be mistaken for separate species or genera. This led to discussions on pleomorphism (mutability of shape). It is now generally agreed that while some forms show different cell types during their life history, the majority are uniform.

A characteristic method of reproduction is the formation of spores, which serve as a resting stage. There are two types:
*   **Arthrospores:** Not true spores, but ordinary vegetative cells that separate and enter a state of rest.
*   **Endospores:** True spores that begin as a minute granule in the protoplasm of a vegetative cell. This granule enlarges, consumes the protoplasm, and secretes a resistive envelope to become a ripe ovoid spore.

Some simple forms, such as *Micrococcus*, are provisionally called "asporous" because no definite spores have been discovered in them; instead, any vegetative cell may begin a new series of growth and division.

## Biological and Medical Impact
The vast majority of schizomycetes are not pathogenic; many are harmless or indispensable to natural operations. They are involved in numerous fermentation processes, including the preparation of tobacco, indigo, hay, and ensilage, as well as the soaking of skins for tanning and rotting. Some species are capable of fixing free nitrogen or carbon-assimilation.

As parasites, certain schizomycetes prey upon the organs of animals and humans. They exist in every part of the animal alimentary canal, except perhaps where acid secretions prevail. While some may be incidentally harmful—such as those that destroy teeth—others are directly pathogenic. The "germ theory of disease," which gained acceptance from 1870 onwards, established that contagious and infectious diseases are caused by bacteria (or occasionally fungi or protozoa). Examples include tuberculosis, septicaemia, fowl-cholera, glanders, and relapsing fever. Specifically, the anthrax bacilli were proven by Davaine and Koch to cause splenic fever, and a *Micrococcus* was found to be intimately concerned in producing diphtheria.

Plants are generally less subject to bacterial attacks, possibly because the acid fluids of higher vegetable organisms are less suited for schizomycete development, though some parasitic forms do exist.

## History of Study
Knowledge of these organisms was limited before 1860. Early observations were made by A. van Leeuwenhoek in the 17th century, O. F. Mueller in 1773, and Ehrenberg in 1830. Modern scientific understanding grew from the researches of F. J. Cohn (1853–1872) and Louis Pasteur. In 1857, Pasteur demonstrated that lactic fermentation depends on an organism, and in 1862, he showed that the ammoniacal fermentation of urea is due to a schizomycete. These findings, along with work by Schwann and Helmholtz, helped disprove the theory of "spontaneous generation," proving that no putrefaction occurs without bacteria or other living organisms.
