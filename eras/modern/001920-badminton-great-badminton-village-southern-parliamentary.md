# BADMINTON

## The Village of Great Badminton
Badminton, also known as Great Badminton, is a village situated in England within the southern parliamentary division of Gloucestershire. It is located 100 miles west of London and is accessible via the Great Western railway's direct line to south Wales.

The village is home to Badminton House, the seat of the dukes of Beaufort, which is set within a park approximately 10 miles in circumference. The manor of Badminton was held for several centuries by the family of Nicolas Boteler before being acquired in 1608 by Thomas, Viscount Somerset (who died in 1650 or 1651). Thomas was the third son of Edward, the 4th earl of Worcester. The manor was later given by Thomas's daughter and heiress, Elizabeth, to Henry Somerset (1629–1699), the 1st duke of Beaufort and 3rd marquess of Worcester.

The current mansion was built in 1682 by the 1st duke of Beaufort on the site where the old manor house once stood. This Palladian-style stone building is noted for its fine wood-carving and a collection of splendid paintings. Located close to the mansion is the parish church of St. Michael. Constructed in 1785 in the Grecian style, the church features an inlaid altar-pavement and a richly ornamented ceiling. It serves as the burial place for Field Marshal Lord Raglan, the youngest son of the 5th duke of Beaufort, and contains various memorials to former dukes featuring fine sculpture.

Additionally, the earls and the 1st marquess of Worcester previously held their seat at Raglan Castle near Monmouth. This site is now a beautiful ruin after being besieged and dismantled by Parliamentarians in 1646 following its capitulation.

## The Game of Badminton
The sport of badminton is a fast-paced game played with rackets and shuttlecocks, deriving its name from the duke of Beaufort's seat in Gloucestershire. While the game appears to have first been played in England around 1873, it was played in India prior to that time and remains very popular there.

The Badminton Association in England was established in 1895. Its laws were based on the original Poona rules of 1876 and a code of rules drawn up in 1887 for the Bath Badminton Club. In England, the game is typically played in covered courts.

### Gameplay and Equipment
Badminton consists entirely of volleying and is considered to require more staying power than a single at lawn tennis. It can be played using artificial light or daylight, and in two formats:
*   **Two-handed (single game):** One player on each side.
*   **Four-handed (double game):** Two players on each side.

The game requires skill and judgment, particularly in "smashing" (a hard downward stroke) and "dropping" (hitting the shuttle gently just over the net). A shuttle is considered "in play" from the moment it is struck by the server's racket until it touches the ground, touches the net without crossing over, or a "fault" is committed.

The equipment consists of:
*   **The Racket:** Generally weighing about 6 oz and strung with strong fine gut; there are no specified requirements for its weight, shape, or size.
*   **The Shuttlecock:** Weighing between 73 and 85 grains, featuring 16 feathers that are 2-1/2 to 2-3/4 inches long.

### Court Specifications
A Badminton hall must be at least 18 feet high. The net is 30 inches deep and ranges from 17 to 24 feet in length, depending on the placement of the posts. The top of the net is edged with 3-inch wide white tape and stands 5 feet from the ground at the center and 5 feet 1 inch at the posts.

In the four-handed game, the distance between net posts is 20 to 24 feet. In the two-handed game, the distance is 17 to 21 feet, and the width of the court is reduced to 17 feet. In the two-handed version, long service lines are dispensed with in favor of the back boundary lines, and the lines dividing the half courts are extended to meet those back boundary lines.

## Competitive History and Administration
The All England championships were instituted in 1899 for mixed, ladies', and gentlemen's doubles, followed by singles championships for ladies and gentlemen in 1900. The first championship between Ireland and England took place in 1904.

The official laws of the game are published annually in London as the *Laws of Badminton and the Rules of the Badminton Association*. Regarding official publications, *Lawn Tennis and Badminton* served as the official organ of the Badminton Association until October 1907, after which the *Badminton Gazette* took over the role in November 1907. Other related literature includes articles by S. M. Massey in the *Badminton Magazine* (February 1907) and the *Badminton Gazette* (November 1907).
