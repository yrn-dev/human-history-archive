# Barn Elms and the History of Duelling

## Barn Elms and the Kit-Cat Club
Barn Elms served as the residence of Jacob Tonson, a publisher and the secretary of the Kit-Cat Club. Founded in London around 1703, the Kit-Cat Club was composed of Whig politicians, painters, men of letters, and wits. While the club initially met in a pie-house in Shire Lane kept by Christopher Cat, and later at the Fountain tavern in the Strand, a room was eventually built specifically for the club's purposes at Barn Elms.

The membership of the club grew from thirty-nine to forty-eight individuals, including figures such as Sir Robert Walpole, the duke of Marlborough, Lords Somers and Halifax, as well as Steele, Addison, Congreve, and Vanbrugh. Sir Godfrey Kneller, who was himself a member, painted portraits of many of the members. These canvases, measuring 36 by 28 inches, were designed to fit the height of the dining room at Barn Elms. This specific format, which allowed for a portrait longer than a half-length to include a hand, became known as the "kit-cat size." The club was dissolved approximately 1720.

## The Evolution of the Duel in France
The practice of judicial combat spread from Germany to France, where it was prevalent between the 10th and 12th centuries. French kings viewed it as a means to limit the judicial powers of their half-independent vassals. The trial was open to all freemen, and under Louis VI, the privilege was extended to serfs. Even the church used judicial combat to resolve disputes over property; in 858, Pope Nicholas described it as a "legitimate and just combat," despite the council of Valence having condemned the practice three years prior by refusing last rites to the vanquished.

The decline of the judicial duel began when the results were perceived as preposterous. In 1385, a duel was fought before Charles VI between Jacques Legris and the husband of Jean Carrouge's wife. Legris was defeated and hanged, but a later confession by another criminal revealed Legris was innocent. This led the parlement to annul the institution. Consequently, duelling in France shifted from being an "appeal to Heaven" to a means of satisfying wounded honour. The final duel authorized by magistrates and conducted according to law took place on July 10, 1547, between Guy Chabot de Jarnac and Francois de Vivonne de la Chataignerie at the chateau of St Germain-en-Laye.

## Duelling in England
Duelling appeared in England roughly a century after it arose in France, with private duels being rare before the reign of James I. Following the Restoration, there was a return to the loose manners and gallantry of French society, leading to a surge of duels depicted in the theatre of the era. During the reign of Queen Anne, the subject was frequently discussed in publications such as the *Spectator* and the *Tatler*.

Historical examples of English duels include the "doubly fatal" encounter between Lord Mohun and the duke of Hamilton. By the 19th century, the legal consequences for duelling became more severe. In 1808, Major Campbell was executed for killing Captain Boyd. In 1813, the survivors and seconds of a duel that killed Lieutenant Blundell were convicted of murder and sentenced to death, though they received a royal pardon and were subsequently cashiered.

## Suppression of Duelling
By the 1840s, public attention turned toward the abolition of the practice. In 1843, a duel between Lieutenant Monro and his brother-in-law, Colonel Fawcett, highlighted the pressure of the prevailing military code. In 1843, an association for the suppression of duelling was formed in London, consisting of distinguished officers and members of both houses of parliament. 

On March 15, 1844, Sir H. Hardinge, the secretary of war, announced in the House of Commons that Queen Victoria desired an expedient to discourage the "barbarous practice." During this debate, Mr. Turner estimated that 172 duels were fought during the reign of George III, 91 of which resulted in death.
