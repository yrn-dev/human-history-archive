# The Vienna Cabinet and the 1848 Revolutions

## The Declaration of Hungarian Liberalism
In February 1848, the fall of Louis Philippe in France acted as a catalyst for revolutionary fires that had been smouldering across Europe, including Austria. On March 3, Kossuth delivered a famous speech within the diet at Pressburg, which served as a formal declaration of war by Hungarian Liberalism against the existing Austrian system. During this address, Kossuth used vivid imagery to describe the oppressive nature of the government, exclaiming that a "pestilential air" breathed upon them from the "charnel-house of the Vienna cabinet," an atmosphere he claimed paralyzed the spirit and dulled the nerves.

## Visions of Fraternization and National Conflict
Kossuth's vision for a liberated Hungary was that it would serve as the center of freedom for all races under the Austrian crown. This goal was framed as a new "fraternization of the Austrian peoples." However, this enthusiasm overlooked the critical issue of how conflicting nationalities would be positioned within such a fraternal union.

Historically, the Austrian system had relied on "Germanism" as its basis. This was not utilized as a national ideal, but rather as a common, unnational mediating element intended to balance various clamorous and contradictory racial tendencies. This dynamic shifted with the growth of the idea of German unity. A new ideal of Germanism emerged, centered outside the boundaries of the Austrian monarchy, which became antagonistic to the aspirations of other races. Ultimately, the doctrine of fraternization among Austrian races conflicted with the traditional German ascendancy and the new sentiment of a united Germany. This conflict is cited as the "rock" upon which the revolution suffered shipwreck in both Germany and Austria.

## Other Vienna Cabinets and Institutions
The term "cabinet" in Vienna also referred to specialized collections and academic institutions. The Hofmineralien Cabinet at Vienna employed Gustav Adolph Kenngott (1818–1897), a German mineralogist who later became a professor of mineralogy at the university of Zürich and published extensively on crystallography and petrology. Additionally, Vienna housed a Cabinet of Medals, managed by M. Eckhel, a Professor of Antiquities. Eckhel authored the eight-volume work *Doctrina veterum Nummorum*, published in Vindobona in 1797, which provided a series of the Augustan History derived from inscriptions and medals.
