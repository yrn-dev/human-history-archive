# Gujarat

Gujarat is a northern division of the Bombay Presidency in British India. The region is characterized by a complex administrative structure consisting of both British districts and various native states.

## Administrative Structure
The Gujarat division includes several British districts, specifically Ahmedabad, Broach, Kaira, Panch Mahals, and Surat. Additionally, the region contains a significant number of native states organized into agencies. The chief groups of states in North Gujarat include:
* Cutch
* Kathiawar agency
* Palanpur agency
* Mahi Kantha agency
* Rewa Kantha agency
* Cambay

The native state of Baroda also falls within the Gujarat province of Bombay, though it maintains direct relations with the governor-general. Baroda consists of four isolated divisions: Kadi, Baroda, and Nausari in Gujarat proper, and Amreli with Okhamandal in the Kathiawar peninsula.

## Geography and Geology
The western boundary of the province of Gujarat is formed by the Runn or Lake of Cutch. This feature is a salt marsh, an inland lake, or an arm of the sea depending on the season, covering an area of 8,000 sq. m. During the rains, the Runn floods and unites the Gulfs of Cutch and Cambay, which effectively converts the territory of Cutch into an island. The peninsula of Kathiawar is situated between these two gulfs.

Geologically, the Kathiawar peninsula and Cutch are largely covered by horizontal lava flows of the Deccan Trap series. In these areas, the lava is underlaid by Neocomian beds (consisting of shales and sandstones with plant remains) and marine Jurassic beds containing numerous Ammonites. Tertiary shales, sandstones, and limestones overlie the Deccan Trap in Cutch. The eastern portion of Gujarat, along with the plain of Sind, is covered by wind-blown sand and alluvium.

## Hydrography
Several key rivers drain the Gujarat region:
* **The Tapti:** Flowing through the district of Khandesh, the native state of Rewa Kantha, and the district of Surat, it falls into the Gulf of Cambay. It is considered the most useful of the Gujarat rivers from a commercial perspective.
* **The Nerbudda:** This river falls into the Gulf of Cambay, creating the alluvial plain of Broach, which is one of the richest districts in the presidency.
* **The Sabarmati and Mahi:** Both rise in the Mahi Kantha hills, flow southwards through the districts of Northern Gujarat, and enter the sea near the head of the Gulf of Cambay.
* **The Banas and Saraswati:** These streams rise in the Aravalli hills, flow eastward through the native state of Palanpur, and empty into the Runn of Cutch.

## History
The region has seen various dynasties and powers. Around 500, a chief named Bhatarka established a dynasty at Valabhi in Kathiawar, which lasted until 770 when it was overthrown by Arab invaders from Sind. The Gurjaras, believed to be ancestors of the Gujar caste, established power over Gujarat around 600 and overran north-eastern Kathiawar, making the raja of Valabhi a tributary and establishing a branch at Broach between 585 and 740. Later, the Rashtrakutas succeeded to the bulk of the dominions of the Chalukyas, including Gujarat.

Under British administration, the Gujarat districts were taken over by the Bombay government in 1805 and enlarged in 1818. Measures for the settlement of Mahi Kantha and Kathiawar occurred between 1807 and 1820. During the mid-19th century, the region experienced agricultural prosperity due to the American Civil War, which created an enormous demand for Indian cotton in Europe.

## Notable States and Local Events
The state of Idar, part of the Mahi Kantha agency, covers 1,669 sq. m. Its present capital is Ahmednagar, though the town of Idar was formerly the capital. The state's economy is based on sugar-cane, oil-seeds, and grain. In 1901, Idar's population of 168,557 reflected a 44% decrease over the previous decade due to famine.

The region has been heavily impacted by health and environmental crises. In 1899, the monsoon failed in Gujarat, leading to famine. The native states of Palanpur and Cutch, as well as the state of Baroda, suffered heavy mortality from the bubonic plague. In Baroda, the population of 1,952,692 in 1901 showed a 19% decrease over the decade, attributed to the plague, epidemics of fever and cholera, and the famines of 1896-1897 and 1900-1901.
