# Modjeska, Fedora, Sweethearts, and Lords and Commons

The theatrical productions of *Odette*, *Fedora*, *Sweethearts*, and *Lords and Commons* were part of a significant era in the development of the English stage during the late 19th century, specifically associated with the management of Sir Squire Bancroft and Effie Marie Wilton.

## The Bancroft Management
Sir Squire Bancroft (born May 14, 1841) began his stage career in 1861 in Birmingham before making his London debut in 1865 in *A Winning Hazard* at the Prince of Wales's theatre. This theatre was managed by Effie Marie Wilton (born 1840), whom Bancroft married in 1868. Together, the couple produced all of the Robertson comedies, including *Society* (1865), *Ours* (1866), *Caste* (1867), *Play* (1868), *School* (1869), and *M.P.* (1870). 

The Bancroft management is credited with reviving London's interest in modern drama and establishing a new era for the English stage. Their early premieres at the Prince of Wales's theatre included Lytton's *Money* (1872), Boucicault's *London Assurance* (1877), and *Diplomacy*, which was an adaptation of Sardou's *Dora*.

## Productions at the Haymarket
In 1879, the Bancrofts moved their operations to the Haymarket. During this period, they staged several notable productions:
*   **Odette**: A work by Sardou for which the management engaged Madame Modjeska.
*   **Fedora**: Another production by Sardou.
*   **Sweethearts**: A play by W. S. Gilbert.
*   **Lords and Commons**: A play by A. W. Pinero.

These works, along with revivals of their previous successes, were central to their tenure at the Haymarket. The Bancrofts retired in 1885 after accumulating a considerable fortune. Sir Squire Bancroft, who was knighted in 1897, later returned to the stage in 1889 to play the abbe Latour in a revival of Watts Phillips's *Dead Heart* alongside Sir Henry Irving.

## Context of Late 19th-Century Drama
The broader landscape of English drama in the late 1880s was characterized by a shift toward representing reality and truth. While Tom Robertson's plays in the 1870s were seen as timid recognitions of these claims, the English consciousness gradually became aware of the superiority of French drama, specifically the works of Sardou, Dumas *fils*, and Augier.

This evolution was further influenced by the arrival of Ibsen in the 1880s, noted for his intrepid stage-craft and daring handling of actuality. A. W. Pinero and Oscar Wilde were among the first playwrights to adapt the spirit of these new discoveries for the English stage. Additionally, Gilbert Murray introduced new flexibility to English dramatic ideas through his versions of Euripides, while Bernard Shaw and Granville Barker introduced novel subjects and wit to the theatre.
