# Codification of the Law of Nations at the Hague

The effort to codify the Law of Nations through conferences held at the Hague has manifested in several distinct contexts over three centuries, ranging from the regulation of maritime commerce and the settlement of territorial disputes to the establishment of international laws governing warfare and peace.

## Maritime Commerce and the York-Antwerp Rules
In the 19th century, the need for uniformity in maritime commerce became apparent, specifically regarding the principle of general average. This principle holds that any loss incurred for the benefit of all coadventurers should be compensated by all parties in equitable proportion to their stakes. Because the application of this principle varied across different countries and periods, technical rules were required to ensure consistency.

Initial efforts toward uniformity began at a congress in Glasgow in May 1860, presided over by Lord Brougham and assisted by Lord Neaves. Subsequent congresses took place in London (1862) and York (1864), resulting in the "York Rules." This work was later adopted by the "Association for the Reform and Codification of the Law of Nations," which held a series of conferences at the Hague in 1875, followed by conferences in Bremen (1876) and Antwerp (1877). The Association introduced changes to the York Rules, and the resulting body of regulations was adopted at the Antwerp conference as the "York and Antwerp (or York-Antwerp) Rules." These rules were quickly put into practical use, though they were later found to be unsatisfactory on certain points, leading to a further conference in Liverpool in the autumn of 1890.

## The Hague Conferences on Peace and War
At the end of the 19th century, Tsar Nicholas II of Russia issued a rescript in 1898 inviting the Great Powers to a conference of states to seek the triumph of universal peace over discord. This initiative led to two major conferences held at the Hague in Holland, the first in 1899 and the second in 1907. By the second conference, nearly all the sovereign states of the world were represented.

These gatherings were conducted diplomatically, and the deliberations remained largely unknown to the common man. Rather than pursuing the abolition of war—which was viewed by many as a chimæra—the assembled representatives focused on haggling over points of international law affecting war. The Great Powers demonstrated a general unwillingness to merge sovereign powers or develop a world commonweal. Instead, their practical goals were to "cheapen" war, which had become too costly, by economizing the wastage of minor conflicts and establishing international laws that could embarrass formidable opponents during wartime without hindering their own actions.

## The Hague Convention and International Law
The outcomes of these gatherings included the Hague Convention. One specific application of this convention concerns the right of asylum in international law—the right of an independent state to allow fugitives from another country to enter or stay on its territory. The neutral right to grant asylum to belligerent forces is governed by articles 59, 58, and 57 of the regulations annexed to the Hague Convention of July 29, 1899, which relate to the Laws and Customs of War on Land.

## Other Hague Agreements and Conferences
The Hague has served as a site for various other legal and political settlements:
*   **Territorial Settlements:** The Treaty of the Hague (1790) settled the question of the Belgian republic. Following a conference at Reichenbach involving England, Prussia, Holland, and Austria, it was decided to restore Austrian rule in Belgium with an amnesty for the past and guarantees for ancient institutions.
*   **Religious Disputes:** In 1610, a conference was held at the Hague where Arminians addressed a remonstrance to the states-general. This document contained five articles known as the five points of Arminianism, which challenged the doctrine of predestination and the irresistibility of grace.
*   **Opium Regulation:** A special protocol was opened at the Hague in accordance with resolutions from a third opium conference in 1914, linked to an opium convention dated January 23, 1912.
