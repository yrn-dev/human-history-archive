# The Siege and Relief of Kumasi Fort

## Overview of Kumasi
Kumasi is the capital of Ashanti in British West Africa, situated on a low rocky eminence and extending across a valley. The town is located in a clearing of dense forest, with the small river Dah—a headstream of the Prah—to the north-west. The name Kum-asi (or Kum-ase) is derived from the okum trees found in its streets. By 1906, the population was 6,280, exceeding 12,000 when including the suburbs. The town's most imposing structure is the fort, built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner and can house a garrison of several hundred men.

## Origins of the 1900 Rebellion
Following the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was appointed to the Ashanti throne. Instead, the affairs of Kumasi were administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart. Although the Kumasi chiefs appeared submissive, they were not reconciled to British rule. 

Tensions escalated on March 28, 1900, during a public palaver where the governor of the Gold Coast, Sir F. Hodgson, announced that Ashanti chiefs were required to pay the British government 4,000 oz. of gold annually. Sir F. Hodgson further reproached the chiefs for hiding the golden stool since 1896. Three days later, Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa who had been sent to discover the stool. This sparked a serious rebellion involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes, who commanded a force reported to number 40,000.

## The Siege of the Fort
The rebels threatened the Kumasi fort, leading Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, as well as Basel missionaries including Mr. and Mrs. Ramseyer, to take refuge inside. Reinforcements arrived in stages: 100 Gold Coast constabulary on April 18, 250 Lagos constabulary on April 29, and 250 men under Major A. Morris on May 15. This brought the garrison to 700 men, including 29 Europeans and four women.

As the situation deteriorated, a small force consisting of 100 Hausa and three white men—Dr. Hay, Mr. Ralph, and Captain Bishop—were left behind in the Kumasi fort. They were provided with rations intended to last for only three weeks.

## The Relief Expedition
Colonel James Willcocks organized a relief expedition at Cape Coast, arriving from Nigeria on May 26. He faced severe obstacles, including the height of the rainy season, impenetrable bush, roads that were deep mire, a lack of local food supplies, and difficulty obtaining carriers. The enemy fought from concealed stockades and were described as both cunning and brave.

Colonel Willcocks reached Fumsu on July 2 and Bekwai on July 9. To divert the enemy, he spread a report that he would attack Kokofu on July 13. This maneuver drew several thousand enemy troops away from Kumasi. After a feint toward Kokofu, Willcocks marched west. Following smart fighting on July 14 and a march through roads in "indescribably bad condition," the main rebel stockade was encountered at 4:30 P.M. on July 15. The stockade was carried at the point of the bayonet by Yoruba troops.

The relieving force entered Kumasi that evening. This column consisted of 1,000 West African fighting men, 60 white officers and non-commissioned officers, six Maxims, four seven-pounder guns, and two 75-millimetre guns. Upon their arrival, a bugler from the war-worn garrison sounded the "general salute." Most of the defenders were too weak to stand, and the area outside the fort was filled with putrid bodies and burnt-down houses.

## Aftermath and Peace
Following the relief of the fort, Colonel Willcocks's force was augmented by Sikhs and Yaos from Central Africa, bringing the total to 3,368 natives, 134 British officers, and 35 British non-commissioned officers, supplemented by Ashanti levies. The Kumasi forces were completely defeated at Obassa on September 30. The remaining two rebel chiefs in the field were captured by December 28, and peace was restored by the start of 1901.

The campaign resulted in 1,007 total casualties, including those who died of disease. Among the British, nine officers were killed in action, six died of disease, and 43 were wounded. Colonel Willcocks was promoted and created a K.C.M.G. for his efforts.
