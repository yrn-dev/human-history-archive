# THE STUDY OF BACTERIA

## Nomenclature and Classification
The minute organisms commonly referred to as "bacteria" are known by various popular designations, including "microbes," "micro-organisms," "microphytes," "bacilli," and "micrococci." However, these terms are often considered unsatisfactory; "bacterium," "bacillus," and "micrococcus" possess narrow technical meanings, while other terms are viewed as too vague for scientific use. In 1857, Naegeli proposed the term "schizomycetes," which is the designation usually preferred by botanists. Despite this, the term "bacteriology" remains the common name for the science.

Historically, little was known of these organisms before 1860. A. van Leeuwenhoek provided figures of bacteria in the 17th century, and O. F. Mueller identified several important forms in 1773. In 1830, Ehrenberg began the scientific grouping of bacteria, proposing sixteen species across four genera by 1838. Modern knowledge was significantly advanced by F. J. Cohn, whose researches between 1853 and 1872 dominated the field. Cohn based his classification on the assumption that bacterial forms are practically constant, noting that rod-like bacilli and spherical micrococci regularly produce similar offspring. 

This doctrine of constancy was challenged in 1873 by Lankester, who observed that *Bacterium rubescens* (later named *Beggiatoa roseo-persicina*) passes through various conditions that could be mistaken for separate species or genera. Similarly, Lister proposed related ideas, and in 1874, Billroth suggested the extravagant view that all bacteria were different states of a single organism called *Cocco-bacteria septica*. While the debate over pleomorphism (mutability of shape) continued, it is now generally agreed that while some forms show different cell types during their life-history, the majority are uniform. Additionally, Cohn is credited with discovering that schizomycetes produce spores in 1857, a finding confirmed by others including Koch, Brefeld, and de Bary.

## General Biological Processes
Bacteria are involved in various chemical changes and fermentations. In 1857, Pasteur demonstrated that lactic fermentation depends on the presence of an organism. Earlier researches by Schwann (1837) and Helmholtz (1843) had already linked fermentation and putrefaction to organisms derived from the air. Pasteur further showed in 1862 that the ammoniacal fermentation of urea is caused by a minute schizomycete.

The production of vinegar is another bacterial process involving the oxidization of alcohol in beer or wine to acetic acid. This occurs in two stages: first, alcohol is oxidized to water and aldehyde, and then the aldehyde is oxidized to acetic acid. This process may proceed further, oxidizing acetic acid to $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{OH}_2$. Four bacteria—*Bacterium aceti*, *B. pasteurianum*, *B. kutzingianum*, and *B. xylinum*—have been thoroughly studied in this context by A. Brown and Hansen. These organisms form the zoogloea of the "mother of vinegar." It is believed that alcohol is absorbed and used as a respirable substance by the organism. This is linked to the presence of "oxydases," enzymes capable of carrying oxygen, which are also found in fungi and the living cells of higher plants.

Furthermore, studies by Beyerinck, Lankester, Engelmann, and Ewart have explored chromogenic bacteria. Certain sulphur bacteria contain a red colouring matter called *Bacteriopurpurin*, which absorbs solar energy. Engelmann demonstrated that these red-purple bacteria collect in bands corresponding to the absorption spectrum of the colouring matter (primarily ultra-red, and to a lesser extent, orange and green). Like the chlorophyll in green plants, these bacteria use trapped radiant energy to perform work, such as the reduction of highly oxidized compounds, and set free oxygen.

## Pathological Importance and Methodology
Bacteriology is divided into general scientific aspects and medical aspects. The action of bacteria as pathogenic agents is largely an extension of their general role as producers of chemical change. The study of the relationship between bacteria and disease generally follows three steps:
1. Discovery of a bacterium in affected tissues using a microscope.
2. Obtaining the bacterium in a pure culture.
3. Producing the disease through inoculation with that pure culture.

Microscopic examination requires fine lenses and staining methods, typically using basic aniline dyes due to their affinity for bacterial protoplasm. To enhance staining, weak alkali or heat may be applied, or mordants used to fix the stain. Decolorizing agents like alcohol or acids are then used to remove excess stain. Gram's method is a specific technique involving treatment with a solution of iodine before decolorization.

## Toxins and Disease Mechanisms
It is an accepted fact that the primary effects of bacteria in tissues are caused by poisonous bodies or toxins rather than mechanical means, such as plugging capillaries, or the consumption of blood oxygen. Toxins are categorized as:
* **Extracellular toxins:** Soluble toxins set free in the culture medium, as seen with diphtheria and tetanus bacilli. These can be isolated by filtering a culture through a germ-free porcelain filter.
* **Intracellular toxins:** Toxins contained within the bacterial bodies, as seen in cholera, typhoid, and tubercle organisms. These are released upon the disintegration of the bacteria.

To study intracellular toxins, researchers have developed methods to break up bacterial bodies. Koch used an agate mortar and distilled water to grind tubercle bacilli, while Rowland and Macfadyen used liquid air to make the bodies brittle for easier breakage. Because toxins are often mixed with other organic substances, they are frequently referred to as "dry toxins" after being precipitated, purified, and desiccated.

## Bacterial Origin of Dental Caries
The mouth serves as a primary portal of entrance for many bacteria that cause specific diseases. Professor W. D. Miller demonstrated in 1884 that dental caries are of bacterial origin. A group of bacteria in the mouth grows on the debris of carbohydrate or starchy food, producing fermentation that results in lactic acid. This acid dissolves the calcium phosphate (the mineral constituent of the tooth), leaving the organic matrix exposed.
