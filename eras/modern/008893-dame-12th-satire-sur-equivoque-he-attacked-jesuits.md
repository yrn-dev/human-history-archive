# Boileau and the 12th Satire

## Career and Royal Appointment
Boileau served as the historiographer to the king starting in 1677. Following this appointment, the volume of his literary production decreased. During this period of his life, he authored several notable works, including the ode *Sur la prise de Namur*, the epistles *Sur l'amour de Dieu* and *A mes vers*, as well as the satires *Sur l'homme* and *Sur les femmes*.

## Controversies and the 10th Satire
Boileau's satires earned him a significant number of enemies. His 10th satire, which focused on women, prompted Charles Perrault to write an *Apologie des femmes*. The work also drew the attention of Bossuet, the bishop, who declared that the 10th satire was subversive of morality and asserted that all satire was incompatible with the spirit of Christianity. While Antoine Arnauld wrote a letter in defense of Boileau during the year of his death, Arnauld's friends argued that it was inconsistent for a churchman to write on a subject as trivial as poetry. Boileau later provided a vindication of the dignity of his art through his epistle *Sur l'amour de Dieu*.

## The 12th Satire and the Jesuits
Around 1705, Boileau wrote his 12th satire, titled *Sur l'equivoque*. In this work, he attacked the Jesuits in verses that Sainte-Beuve described as a recapitulation of Pascal's *Lettres provinciales*. 

Following the composition of this satire, Boileau focused on arranging a definitive and complete edition of his works. However, the Jesuit fathers successfully petitioned Louis XIV to withdraw the publication privilege that had already been granted. Furthermore, the Jesuits demanded that the 12th satire be suppressed. These annoyances are said to have hastened Boileau's death, which occurred on March 13, 1711.

## Later Life and Residence
Boileau was admitted to the Academy on April 15, 1684, an appointment made at the wish of the king. In 1687, he purchased a country-house at Auteuil; the poet Racine referred to this residence as his *hotellerie d'Auteuil* due to the large number of guests it hosted. In 1705, Boileau sold the house and returned to Paris. He spent his final years living with his confessor within the cloisters of Notre Dame.
