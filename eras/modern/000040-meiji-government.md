# THE MEIJI GOVERNMENT

## Leadership and Origins
The transition to the Meiji Government was the result of a revolution in 1867 that led to the fall of the Tokugawa. While feudatories are often mentioned in these events, the feudal chiefs themselves had very little to do with the completion of this change. The revolution was primarily conceived and achieved by fifty-five samurai of inferior grade. Of this group, only thirteen were aristocrats, consisting of eight court nobles and five feudal barons. The average age of these fifty-five individuals did not exceed thirty years.

During the first decade of the Meiji era, the men in power were characterized by their far-seeing statesmanship and intellectual superiority. Because of a traditional Japanese habit of looking to officialdom for direction and example, the Government took on the role of the nation's teacher. This was particularly necessary as the Government sought to equip the people with the products of Western civilization, an effort to assimilate a foreign civilization that had been an object of national repugnance for nearly three centuries. Consequently, the Government had to provide both the models for these reforms and the inspiration for the movement.

## Financial Administration and National Debt
One of the primary challenges facing the Meiji Government was the stabilization of the national currency. The Government initially issued notes that fell to a fifty per cent discount. To ensure these notes circulated at par, the Government employed various devices that were more or less despotic. While the Government's credit improved over the first five years—resulting in sixty million yen of inconvertible notes passing freely—the need for funds for official enterprises and wholesale reforms increased. By 1881 (the fourteenth year after the Restoration), the face value of notes in circulation reached 180 million yen and stood at a heavy discount.

To resolve this depreciation, the Government reduced administrative expenditures, taxed alcohol, and bought exporters' bills to accumulate a specie reserve. By 1885, the volume of fiduciary notes was reduced to 119 million yen, and the treasury held forty-five million in precious metals, allowing for the resumption of specie payments.

The national debt of Japan did not exceed 305 million yen by the close of the twenty-eighth year of the new era. This debt originated largely from the commutation of samurai pensions and the incomes of feudatories, which were primarily paid via public loan-bonds. Other contributing factors included the debts of the fiefs taken over by the Central Government, two small foreign loans, costs associated with public works and naval construction, and the expenses of quelling the Satsuma rebellion.

## Military and Naval Development
The Meiji Government's military and naval capabilities built upon earlier studies of foreign military science that began during the thirteenth shogun. This earlier period saw the establishment of a college at Nagasaki with instructors from Holland and a naval college (Gunkan Kyojujo) at Tsukiji in Yedo. An iron-foundry was also erected at Akunoura in Nagasaki, where the first attempts at shipbuilding on foreign lines occurred; this site later became the Mitsubishi Company's premier private dockyard. Katsu Rintaro, a graduate of the Nagasaki college, later served as the minister of Marine in the Meiji Government.

## Internal Conflict: The Satsuma Insurrection
The Central Government's resources were severely taxed by an insurrection in the south led by Saigo Takamori. The insurgents consisted of approximately forty thousand samurai who were equipped with field-guns and rifles and were fully trained in Occidental tactics. Their stated goal was to ensure that the military class regained its old position and held all posts within the navy and army. While it has been claimed that Saigo Takamori took command to restrain his followers rather than to overthrow the Meiji Government, he ultimately appeared to be swept away by their enthusiasm.

## Territorial Administration
Early in the Meiji era, the Government addressed the problem of territories that had lacked effective Japanese administration during the feudal period, specifically the Kurile Islands, Saghalien, and Yezo. In the case of Yezo, the feudatory of Matsumae maintained a castle in the extreme south, but his jurisdiction was only nominal. 

Regarding Saghalien, Japanese vassals of the Matsumae feudatory had landed on the island as early as 1620. While some European theories suggest that Captain Nevelskoy discovered the island's insular character in 1849, the fact had been known in Japan since 1806, when the traveler Mamiya Rinzo voyaged the Amur and discovered the narrow strait separating Saghalien from the continent.
