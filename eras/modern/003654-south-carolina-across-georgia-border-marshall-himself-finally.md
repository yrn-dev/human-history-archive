# The Spread of "Separate" Baptist Influence in the Southern Colonies

The expansion of Baptist principles throughout the southern colonies was driven significantly by the efforts of "New Light" evangelists who transitioned into the Baptist faith, most notably Shubael Stearns and his brother-in-law, Daniel Marshall (d. 1784). Their movement, known as the "Separate" Baptists, played a pivotal role in the growth of the denomination across North Carolina, South Carolina, and Georgia.

## The Sandy Creek Movement
Shubael Stearns, described as an evangelist of great power, settled at Sandy Creek, North Carolina, accompanied by Marshall and approximately twelve fellow believers. Within a few years, Stearns established a church with a membership exceeding six hundred people. Following this success, Daniel Marshall organized and ministered to another church at Abbott's Creek, located about 30 miles away.

These two locations served as primary centers from which "Separate" Baptist influence radiated. From North Carolina, this influence permeated Virginia and extended further west into Tennessee and Kentucky. The resulting Sandy Creek Association eventually included churches across several colonies. Stearns, seeking to maintain the harmonious operation of the churches under his leadership, strongly resisted any proposals to form additional associations.

## Expansion into South Carolina and Georgia
The influence of the "Separate" Baptists spread from the Sandy Creek centers throughout North Carolina and South Carolina, eventually crossing the border into Georgia. Daniel Marshall himself eventually settled in Georgia, where he formed a church at Kiokee.

In South Carolina, the Baptist faith existed in two distinct forms. One was represented by the Charleston Association, formed in 1751 on the model of the Philadelphia Association. This body was highly cultivated and progressive, raising funds for the education of ministers (starting in 1756) and domestic missionary work (starting in 1755). Notable leaders of this group included Oliver Hart (1723-1795), who led the First Church, Charleston, to prosperity starting in 1749, and Richard Furman (1755-1825), who served as pastor of the First Church during the four decades following the War of Independence.

However, the numerical growth of the denomination in South Carolina was driven primarily by the "Separate" Baptist movement. Between 1787 and 1812, Baptist membership in the state grew from 1,620 to 11,325, a surge attributed far more to the work of Stearns and Marshall than to the Charleston Association.

## Social Reach and Regional Impact
Both the "Separate" and the more cultivated Baptist traditions permeated Georgia. The influence of the Charleston Association was felt primarily in Augusta, Savannah, and other cultivated communities. In contrast, the "Separate" Baptists focused on evangelizing the masses.

This religious expansion included many negro slaves in Georgia, the Carolinas, and Virginia. While most became members of white Baptist churches, some were encouraged to establish their own churches in towns such as Savannah and Richmond.

## Comparison with Other Baptist Efforts
The "Separate" Baptists differed from other Baptist groups in their relationship with state authority. While the Virginia colonial government provided limited toleration to some Baptists, the "Separate" Baptists were viewed as too enthusiastic and too opposed to state control in religious matters to accept licenses for their meetings, leading to sharp conflicts with authorities.

This contrasted with the earlier efforts to strengthen the Baptist cause in North Carolina and Virginia through missionaries from the Philadelphia Association, such as John Gano, Benjamin Griffith, John Thomas, Samuel Eaton, Benjamin Miller, David Thomas, and John Garrard. This group of churches eventually formed the Ketockton Association in 1776.
