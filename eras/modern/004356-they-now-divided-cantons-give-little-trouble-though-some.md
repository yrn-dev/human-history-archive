# Cantonal Systems and Divisions

The term "canton" appears across various historical and geographical contexts, referring to administrative divisions, native tribal groupings, or political subdivisions in different regions of the world.

## The Bashkirs
The Bashkirs were a strong and independent people until the middle of the 13th century, when the Mongolians arrived. During their period of independence, they were considered troublesome to the Petchenegs and the Bulgarians. Early European mentions of the Bashkirs come from William of Rubruquis (1220-1293) and Joannes de Plano Carpini (c. 1200-1260), who encountered them in the upper reaches of the river Ural and referred to them as "Pascatir," noting that they spoke the same language as the Hungarians.

Following the downfall of the Kazan kingdom, the Bashkirs entered a weak state and voluntarily recognized Russian supremacy in 1556. This led to the founding of the city of Ufa to protect them from the Kirghiz and the imposition of a fur-tax. The Bashkirs rebelled against Russian officials in 1676 under a leader named Seit, and again in 1707 under Kusyom and Aldar due to ill-treatment. A third insurrection occurred in 1735 during the foundation of Orenburg, lasting six years. By 1786, they were freed from taxes, and an irregular army was formed from among them in 1798. Currently, they are divided into cantons and generally cause little trouble, although land questions have caused some differences with the government.

The Bashkirs are divided by mode of life into settled and nomadic groups. Settled Bashkirs engage in bee-keeping, cattle-rearing, and agriculture, living without want. Nomadic Bashkirs are further divided into those of the steppes and those of the mountains. Their primary occupation is rearing cattle, which they do negligently, often failing to collect enough winter fodder and allowing part of their herds to perish. Consequently, they are usually very poor, surviving the winter on a cheese called *skurt* and a gruel known as *yuryu*. They are described as hospitable but suspicious, lazy, and apt to plunder. Physically, they are characterized as strong and muscular with swarthy skin, black hair, small foreheads, narrow and flat eyes, and ears that always stick out.

## Swiss Cantons
In Switzerland, cantons serve as primary political divisions. Basel is one of the most northerly cantons and, along with Schaffhausen, is one of the only cantons with territory north of the Rhine. It is watered by the Ergolz and Birs rivers and traversed by the Jura chain. Between 1803 and 1814, Basel was one of six "Directorial" cantons of the Confederation. Since 1833, it has been split into two half cantons with independent constitutions:

*   **Basel Stadt (Bale Ville):** This half canton includes the city of Basel and the rural districts of Bettingen, Riehen, and Klein Hueningen. It covers 13.7 sq. m., with 11 sq. m. classed as productive and 1.5 sq. m. of forests. As of 1900, its population was 112,227, consisting of 73,063 Protestants, 37,101 Romanists (including Old Catholics), and 1,897 Jews. Its 1889 constitution establishes an executive of seven members and a legislature (*Grossrat*) of 130 members, both elected by direct popular vote every three years. Since 1875, 1,000 citizens can exercise the right of initiative or claim a popular vote (*facultative Referendum*) on bills.
*   **Basel Landschaft (Bale Campagne):** With Liestal as its capital, this half canton comprises 74 communes and four administrative districts. It covers 165 sq. m., of which all but 5 sq. m. is productive (including 55.9 sq. m. of forests). In 1900, its population was 68,497, including 52,763 Protestants, 15,564 Romanists, and 130 Jews.

Other Swiss cantonal data includes the canton of Grisons, which had a population of 104,520 in 1900. This population included 55,155 Protestants (concentrated in the Schanfigg valley, the Prattigau, and near Davos and Coire) and 49,142 Romanists (concentrated in the Oberhalbstein, the Val Mesocco, and the Bundner Oberland), as well as 114 Jews, 81 of whom lived in Davos.

Another Swiss canton, ranking third in the confederation after Bern and Zürich, was formerly in the diocese of Constance. It contains 107 communes and five administrative districts. Its legislature (*Grossrath*) is elected from 55 electoral circles at a rate of one member per 1,000 souls. Proportional representation was adopted for these elections on April 4, 1909. The executive of seven members is elected by popular vote every four years. Citizens have the right of "initiative" (requiring 4,000 citizens) and a facultative referendum (requiring 5,000 citizens) regarding financial decrees, legislative projects, or constitutional revisions.

## Other Historical Cantonal Systems
In Roman Britain, the administration of the province was divided. While Roman municipalities ruled themselves and the Imperial Domains were managed by agents of the emperor, the majority of the country was divided among native tribes or cantons. There were approximately ten to twelve of these cantons, each centered around a country town where its council (*ordo*) met. This system was a Roman recasting of an old native element, similar to the system found in Gaul.

In ancient Attica, the population maintained a deep-seated "cantonal feeling." Even until the Peloponnesian War, residents lived in their several cantons, where temples and ancient festivals remained as relics of previous autonomy. They viewed their country residences as their real homes, visiting the city only for political or religious purposes. This attachment to their cantons persisted even after the Persian invasion forced them into temporary exile.
