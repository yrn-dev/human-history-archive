# Flügelhorn

The Flügelhorn is a treble brass wind instrument characterized by a conical bore and a cup-shaped mouthpiece. As its name denotes, it served as the signal instrument for the infantry in Germany, a role similar to that of the bugle in England. In the military bands of Austro-Hungary, the Flügelhorn continues to hold its own alongside the keyed bugle.

## Classification and Characteristics
The Flügelhorn is categorized among the bugles and bugle-horns, which include various forms such as the keyed bugle, the Kent bugle (or Regent's bugle), and the signal horn. These instruments are defined by a conical bore of rapidly increasing diameter, a characteristic preserved from the original bugle, which was a bull's horn. In different languages, these instruments are referred to as the *Bugle* or *Clairon* in French, the *Signalhorn* or *Klappenhorn* in German, and the *Corna cromatica* in Italian.

## Development of Military Signal Instruments
The use of the bugle as a military duty and signal instrument in England is closely linked to the creation of the light infantry. During the 17th century, the bugle began to supersede the drum as the primary signal instrument for these units. This transition occurred gradually as improvements in firearms led the infantry to abandon armor. For example, Prince Albert's 1st Somerset Light Infantry, formed in 1685, still employed a drummer at that time. By the end of the 18th century, the bugle was the recognized signal instrument for the light infantry, while the trumpet remained the instrument of the cavalry. A general order dated December 28, 1814, introduced the bugle as a minor badge for the light infantry.

## Evolution into Brass Bands
The bugle served as the nucleus for brass bands across many countries during much of the 19th century. This development occurred through two primary paths: the use of keys (resulting in the ophicleide and the Royal Kent bugle) and the use of valves (resulting in tubas and saxhorns).

The Royal Kent bugle reached its peak popularity in the army by 1856. During this period, a bugle-band was established in the Royal Artillery to replace the drum and fife band. Under the leadership of Trumpet-major James Lawson, a skilled cornet player, the band achieved a high standard of excellence. Because Lawson found the scale of the service bugle too restricted, he obtained permission to add a valve attachment. This modification transformed the bugle into a chromatic instrument similar to the cornet and, effectively, a saxhorn. This led to the addition of other copper instruments, including tenor horns in B flat, euphoniums, horns in E flat, and bass tubas. Consequently, the "bugle band" was renamed the R.A. Brass Band in 1869, before merging into the Mounted Band in 1877.
