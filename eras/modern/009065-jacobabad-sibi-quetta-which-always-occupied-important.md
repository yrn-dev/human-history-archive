# Quetta, Sibi, and the Bolan Pass

The region encompassing Quetta, Sibi, and the Bolan Pass constitutes a strategically vital area on the Baluch frontier. This territory serves as a critical link between the plains of the south and the high plateaus of Baluchistan, playing a significant role in British administrative and military history.

## Geography and Climate
Quetta is situated on an open plain 5,500 feet above sea level, formed by high-level tributaries of the Lora. It is surrounded by some of the most imposing peaks and precipices in Baluchistan, including Takatu to the north, Chahiltan (Chiltan) to the south-west, Khalifat to the east, and the square-headed Murdar to the south, with heights ranging from 10,500 to 11,500 feet.

To the south-east lies Sibi, located at the foot of the hills where the Bolan and Nari basins drain into the Kach Gandava. The Kach Gandava is a flat alluvial desert extending 130 meters from the Indus to Sibi. This area is characterized by a harsh climate that prevents European occupation; it is a land of poisonous winds and dust-storms where summer temperatures never drop below 100°F and winter temperatures fall below the freezing point. In contrast, the climate of British Baluchistan is generally dry, bracing, and favorable for Europeans, though extreme temperature fluctuations can lead to severe forms of fever.

## The Bolan Pass and Transport
The Bolan Pass is an essential route connecting Jacobabad and Sibi with Quetta. It has historically been a key location for British campaigns in Afghanistan. Following the Treaty of Gandamak in 1879, the Bolan route came under direct British control and was chosen for the initial alignment of the Sind-Pishin railway.

The railway from Sibi originally ran south-west through Rindli, following the Bolan stream. However, due to destructive flooding, this alignment was abandoned in favor of the Mashkaf valley, which leads to a junction with the Bolan at Mach. An alternative, longer route for ordinary traffic was established through the Harnai valley, starting northeast of Sibi and entering the hills at Nari. The Bolan loop is now reserved for emergencies.

The terrain of the Bolan route is rugged; at the Khundilani gorge, conglomerate cliffs rise to 800 feet, and at Sir-i-Bolan, the limestone rocks are so narrow that they barely allow three persons to ride abreast. The pass experiences extreme weather, with very high summer temperatures and destructive, ice-cold winds in winter near its head.

## Administration and Strategic Importance
Quetta serves as a central dominant position that safeguards the security of Kalat and bars potential advances toward India via Kandahar. The Quetta agency was founded in 1877, which helped secure the pass from plundering bands of Baluch marauders, specifically the Marris.

British Baluchistan is an irregularly shaped block of upland territory leased from Kalat. It includes the basins of the Nari and Bolan as far as Sibi, the Toba plateau, and the Thal Chotiali valley. On November 1, 1887, British Baluchistan was incorporated into British India and divided into two districts: Thai Chotiali and Quetta-Pishin. The administration is typically composed of military officers, and the level of independence in various districts generally varies based on their distance from Quetta.

In terms of military organization, under the 1904 re-arrangement by Lord Kitchener, Quetta became the headquarters of the 4th division of the Western army corps, overseeing troops in the Sind and Quetta districts.

## Tribal Populations
The region is inhabited by various tribes. The Marri and Bugti sections of the Rind confederation, who claim Arabic origin, occupy the twisted flexures of the Suliman system. The Bugti tribe, numbering approximately 15,500, occupies the hills between Jacobabad and Sibi. Described as fine horsemen and swordsmen, the Bugtis were historically hereditary robbers. While an expedition against them was organized by Sir C. Napier in 1845, they were not brought under control until the rule of Sir Robert Sandeman. The construction of the railway, which outflanks their country, has since rendered them fairly orderly.

To the north of the Marris and Bugtis are the Bozdars, another Rind clan. These Rind tribes are the exception to the general Pathan occupation of northern Baluchistan. Prominent Pathan divisions in the area include the Mando Khel of Zhob and the Dumars and Kakars of Pishin.
