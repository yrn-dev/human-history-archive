# The Accession of George I and the Events at Golden Square

## Accession and Court Transitions
In October 1714, George I ascended to the British throne. He was accompanied to London by his son, George Augustus (the electoral prince of Hanover), and his daughter-in-law, Caroline. Caroline, the daughter of John Frederick, margrave of Brandenburg-Ansbach, had maintained a keen interest in the Hanoverian dynasty's approach to the throne and had been on friendly terms with the old electress Sophia. Upon her arrival in London, Caroline served as princess of Wales; she was noted for her popularity and her tact in managing her high-ranking position at court.

## Celebrations and Political Fallout at Golden Square
The accession of George I was marked by public displays of celebration. At the house of Lord Bolingbroke, located in Golden Square, the bonfires and illuminations held to mark the occasion were described as "particularly fine and remarkable." Despite these celebrations, Bolingbroke's political standing collapsed immediately following the accession, as he was dismissed from office.

## The Exile and Activities of Lord Bolingbroke
Following his dismissal, Bolingbroke retired to Bucklebury. During this period, he is said to have written a response to the *Secret History of the White Staff*, a work that had accused him of Jacobitism. In March 1715, Bolingbroke attempted to defend the previous ministry within the new parliament, but these efforts were unsuccessful. 

On March 28, 1715, upon the announcement that Walpole intended to attack the authors of the treaty of Utrecht, Bolingbroke fled to Paris in disguise. Before leaving, he sent a letter from Dover to Lord Lansdowne, in which he protested his innocence and challenged his enemies to provide any evidence of "criminal correspondence."

While in Paris, Bolingbroke was well received and, by July, fully aligned himself with the interests of the Pretender, eventually becoming the Pretender's secretary. However, his influence was limited, as his counsel was ignored in favor of Irish priests and "ignorant refugees." Although Bolingbroke drew up James's declaration, the priests cancelled the assurances he had included regarding the security of the Church of England. An expedition in 1715 was launched despite Bolingbroke's advice against it.

Bolingbroke's tenure with the Pretender ended when he was dismissed from office due to jealousies and petty intrigues upon James's return. Following this dismissal, Bolingbroke renounced all further efforts on behalf of the Pretender. In a reply to Mary of Modena, who had sent a message regarding his ill-will, Bolingbroke expressed a wish that his arm might rot off if he ever again used a sword or pen in their service. He was attainted on September 10.

## The Nature of Bonfires
The celebrations at Golden Square utilized bonfires, a practice with diverse historical meanings. In a general sense, bonfires have been used as funeral piles, fires for immolation (such as for proscribed books or heretics), or as signals of alarm, such as those used to warn England of the Armada's approach. They are also used as large open-air fires for national rejoicing.

Historically, the custom of lighting bonfires and dancing or leaping over them is traced back to the middle ages and heathen Europe. Early Christian synods in the 7th and 8th centuries attempted to suppress these practices as pagan. For example, the third council of Constantinople (A.D. 680) commanded an end to fires lit on new moons before houses and shops. Similarly, the Synodus Francica under Pope Zachary in A.D. 742 forbade "sacrilegious fires" known as *Nedfri* (or bonefires). 

The practice of leaping over fires was also recorded by Ovid in *Fasti* during the Palilia (the feast of the goddess Pales), where shepherds jumped over burning heaps of straw. In Cornwall, bonfires are traditionally lit on the eve of St. Peter's day and St. John the Baptist; the Cornish term for midsummer, *Goluan*, signifies both "festivity" and "light."
