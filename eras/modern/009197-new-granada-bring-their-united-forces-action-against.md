# New Granada

New Granada was a region in South America that became a central theater of military and political activity during the early 19th-century struggle for independence from Spanish rule. Its history during this period is closely linked to the campaigns of Simón Bolívar and the efforts to establish republican governance.

## Early Revolutionary Activity
In September 1812, Simón Bolívar traveled to Cartagena after hearing of significant movements occurring within New Granada. Upon his arrival, he was granted a commission to conduct operations against Spanish troops located on the Magdalena river. Bolívar's expedition in this area was highly successful; he drove Spanish forces from post to post before advancing toward the province of Venezuela.

Following a period of conflict in Venezuela—which included the "war to the death" decree and the temporary liberation of the country by 1814—Bolívar suffered a defeat by Boves near Cura in the plains of La Puerta. After the royalists retook Caracas in July 1814 and established undisputed mastery over Venezuela by the end of that year, Bolívar retreated first to Cartagena and then to Tunja. In Tunja, the revolutionary congress of New Granada was in session. Despite the presence of personal enemies and his recent military misfortunes, Bolívar was treated with great consideration by the congress, which appointed him to lead an expedition against Santa Fe de Bogota.

## The Campaign for Independence
Bolívar's path to securing New Granada involved a series of strategic maneuvers and alliances. After a period of reorganization and military successes in other regions—including the defeat of royalist forces under Morillo in February 1817 and the establishment of headquarters at Angostura on the Orinoco—Bolívar addressed a congress on February 15, 1819. While he initially surrendered his authority to the congress, he was required to resume power until independence was fully established.

To achieve this, Bolívar executed a bold and original design: he reorganized his troops and crossed the Cordilleras. His objective was to effect a junction with General Santander, who was the commander of the republican forces in New Granada. By uniting these forces, Bolívar intended to bring them into action against their common enemy.

This strategy resulted in complete success. In July 1819, following a sharp action on the adjoining heights, Bolívar entered Tunja. This was followed on August 7, 1819, by the victory of Boyaca. This decisive victory granted Bolívar immediate possession of both Bogota and the entirety of New Granada.

## International Relations and Treaties
Following the period of independence, New Granada engaged in diplomatic efforts to settle territorial and legal matters with other powers. In 1825, drafts of treaties were created between New Granada and Great Britain. These diplomatic efforts occurred in a broader context of British sovereignty claims in Central America, specifically regarding the settlement of Belize. Great Britain sought to secure its position in the region by incorporating previous treaty provisions into new agreements with various entities, including Mexico (1826), the United States of Central America (1831), and New Granada.
