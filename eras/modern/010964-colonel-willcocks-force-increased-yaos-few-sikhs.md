# Colonel Willcocks and the Kumasi Campaign

## The Relief of Kumasi
Colonel James Willcocks arrived at Cape Coast from Nigeria on May 26 to organize a relief expedition for the Kumasi fort. The situation at the fort was dire; a force of 100 Hausa and three white men—Dr. Hay, Mr. Ralph, and Captain Bishop—had been left behind with only three weeks of rations. 

Willcocks faced severe environmental and tactical challenges. The rainy season was at its peak, leaving roads as "deep mire" and the bush nearly impenetrable. Local food supplies were non-existent, and carriers were difficult to obtain. Additionally, the enemy was described as cunning and brave, utilizing concealed stockades for their defense.

Colonel Willcocks began his advance to Fumsu on July 2. Upon learning of the governor's escape and the distress of the Kumasi garrison, he pushed forward, reaching Bekwai on July 9, where the local king remained loyal. To divert enemy forces, Willcocks spread a report that he would attack Kokofu, east of Bekwai, on July 13. This maneuver successfully drew several thousand enemy troops away from Kumasi. After a feint toward Kokofu, Willcocks marched west. Following "smart fighting" on July 14 and a march through roads in "indescribably bad condition," his forces encountered the main rebel stockade. The position was carried by Yoruba troops using the bayonet, who were noted as being fully equal to the Hausa in their *elan*. Kumasi was entered that evening on July 15. The relieving force consisted of 60 white non-commissioned officers and officers, 1,000 West African fighting men, six Maxims, four seven-pounder guns, and two 75-millimetre guns.

## Suppression of the Rebellion
Following the relief of Kumasi, the focus shifted to crushing the remaining rebellion. To achieve this, Colonel Willcocks's force was increased with a few Sikhs and Yaos from Central Africa. This brought the total number of natives in his force to 3,368, supported by 35 British non-commissioned officers and 134 British officers. The force also included Ashanti levies.

The rebellion was effectively broken on September 30, when the Kumasi were completely defeated at Obassa. This victory led many rebel chiefs to surrender. The final two chiefs remaining in the field were captured on December 28. Peace was restored by the opening of 1901.

## Campaign Casualties and Aftermath
The total number of casualties for the campaign, which included deaths from disease, was 1,007. Among the British officers, nine were killed in action, six died of disease, and forty-three were wounded. Following the campaign, Colonel Willcocks was created a K.C.M.G. and received a promotion.
