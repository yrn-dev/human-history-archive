# Operations in Italy (1745-1747)

The military campaigns in Italy between 1745 and 1747 were characterized by significant troop movements and shifting alliances. In March 1745, a secret treaty was established, aligning the republic of Genoa with Naples, Spain, and France.

## Allied Concentration and Early Victories
The initial movements of the allies were favored by a change in the command of the Austrian forces. De Gages moved from Modena toward Lucca, while Marshal Maillebois led French and Spanish forces through the Riviera from the Alps to the Tanaro. By the middle of July, these two armies concentrated between the Tanaro and the Scrivia, reaching an unusually large total of 80,000 men.

This concentration of forces allowed for a swift march on Piacenza, which drew the Austrian commander away. In the Austrian commander's absence, the allies attacked the Sardinians at Bassignano on September 27, achieving a complete victory. This success was followed by the capture of Casale, Valenza, and Alessandria.

## Austrian Counter-Offensives
Despite the victory at Bassignano, the complicated politics of Italy prevented Marshal Maillebois from fully utilizing the win. In early 1746, Austrian troops—having been freed by a peace agreement with Frederick—entered Italy via Tirol. These forces launched brusque attacks on the Franco-Spanish winter quarters, resulting in the capitulation of a French garrison of 6,000 men at Asti.

Simultaneously, Count Browne led an Austrian corps to strike the allies on the lower Po. This maneuver successfully cut off the allies' communication with their main body located in Piedmont. These series of minor actions effectively destroyed the previously great concentration of allied forces.

## The Spanish Advance and the Battle of Piacenza
Following the destruction of their concentration, the allies separated. While Maillebois covered Liguria, the Spaniards marched against Count Browne. However, Browne was promptly and heavily reinforced. Consequently, the Spaniards were only able to entrench themselves at Piacenza.

In response to this critical situation, the Spanish Infant, serving as supreme commander, called upon Maillebois for aid. The French forces, marching rapidly and under skillful conduction, joined with the Spaniards once more. Despite the reunion, the allied situation remained critical; the army of the king of Sardinia was pursuing them only two marches behind, while the principal Austrian army lay before them.

This culminated in the pitched battle of Piacenza on June 16. The engagement was hard fought, and Marshal Maillebois had nearly achieved victory before orders were issued.
