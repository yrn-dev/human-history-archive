# Riesengebirge (Kroknose)

The Riesengebirge, known in Czech as the *Kroknose*, are a mountain range located in central Europe. After the Alps, they are among the highest mountains in the region, reaching a maximum altitude of 5,264 ft. at the Schneekoppe.

## Geographic Context and Classification
The Riesengebirge are part of the Sudetes, a mountain system that forms the north-east frontier of Bohemia. The Sudetes comprise several distinct groups, including the Jeschkengebirge (3,322 ft.), the Lausitzergebirge (2,500 ft.), and the Isergebirge, the latter of which contains the Tafelfichte peak at 3,683 ft. Other groups within the Sudetes in Bohemia include the Adlergebirge (3,664 ft.) and the Heuscheuergebirge (2,532 ft.).

From a broader orographic perspective, Bohemia is shaped like an irregular rhomb and is characterized as a separate massif. It is bordered on three sides by mountain ranges: the Bohmerwald (Bohemian Forest) to the south-west, the Erzgebirge (Ore Mountains) to the north-west, and the Sudetes—including the Riesengebirge—to the north-east.

## Regional Topography
The surrounding landscape of Bohemia includes various other elevations and landmarks:
* **The Erzgebirge:** These mountains form the north-west frontier with an average altitude of 2,600 ft. and a highest point at the Keilberg (4,080 ft.). The village of Gottesgab, situated at 3,345 ft., is the highest place in central Germany and Bohemia.
* **The Bohmerwald:** This range runs from south-east to north-west and is divided by the pass of Neumark. Its southern part, known as *Sumava*, is the most picturesque and highest section, featuring the Plockenstein (4,513 ft.) and the Osser (4,053 ft.), though its highest peak, the Arber (4,872 ft.), is located in Bavaria.
* **The Bohemian-Moravian Hills:** Forming the fourth side of the rhomb, this area consists of a broad series of low hills or a plateau composed of primitive rocks, reaching altitudes of 2,500 ft. in some areas.

## Comparative European Mountain Systems
While the Riesengebirge are prominent in central Europe, they exist within a larger system of European mountains. To the east of the Silesian and Moravian chains lie the Carpathian Mountains, the eastern wing of Europe's great central mountain system. The Carpathians are the most extensive mountain system in Europe after the Alps, covering 72,600 sq. m. and stretching over 800 m. in length. 

Unlike the Alps, the Carpathians lack extensive snow-fields, large glaciers, and perpetual snow, recalling instead the middle region of the Alps in appearance and flora. The Alps themselves contain significantly higher peaks, such as the Marmolata in the Dolomites (10,972 ft.) and the Aiguille de Chambeyron in the Basses-Alpes (11,155 ft.).
