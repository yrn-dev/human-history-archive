# Lombardy

Lombardy is a region characterized by its geography, specifically its "capricious and irregular abundance of waters." This environmental trait is noted by travelers and historians; for instance, the Polcevera, a small torrent, is capable of transforming rapidly into a "terribil gigante," a fact that surprised Austrian forces who had encamped in its dry bed before Genoa.

## Ancient and Classical History
In antiquity, the region was the site of significant military engagements. The Trebia, described as a small river of Lombardy that flows into the Po slightly above Placentia, was the location of the Battle of the Trebia. During this conflict, the Roman consul Sempronius, having returned from Sicily to Ariminum, joined his forces with those of Scipio. The Roman army and the forces of Hannibal were separated only by the Trebia, leading to frequent skirmishes. In one such encounter, Sempronius led a body of horse to a trifling advantage over a party of Carthaginians.

In later Roman history, Lombardy was involved in dealings with the Goths. Settlements were offered to the Goths in the region, and they advanced from the Po toward the Alps to take possession of these lands. However, this was a "treacherous stratagem" by Stilicho, who surprised the Goths while they were relying on the faith of the treaty.

## Military Campaigns of the 18th Century
Lombardy was a focal point of strategic planning and conflict during the mid-18th century, particularly during the Second Silesian War and subsequent operations in Italy.

### The Campaign of 1744
In April 1744, France declared war on Sardinia and Austria. As part of a broader strategic plan, the prince de Conti was tasked with leading a fresh army to assist the Spaniards in Lombardy and Piedmont. However, this plan was quickly dislocated due to the maneuvers of Prince Charles. Assisted by the veteran Traun, Charles moved his army over the Rhine near Philipsburg on July 1, captured the lines of Weissenburg, and cut off the French marshal from Alsace.

### Operations from 1745 to 1747
The Italian campaign of 1745 was characterized by a secret treaty aligning the Genoese republic with Spain, France, and Naples. Allied forces, including French and Spanish troops under Marshal Maillebois, advanced through the Riviera to the Tanaro. By mid-July, two armies totaling 80,000 men were concentrated between the Tanaro and the Scrivia. 

The allies achieved a significant victory at Bassignano on September 27, where they completely defeated the Sardinians. This success led to the capture of Casale, Valenza, and Alessandria. Jomini described the concentration of forces that enabled this victory as "le plus remarquable de toute la guerre."

However, the allies were unable to maintain this advantage due to the complex politics of Italy. In early 1746, Austrian troops entered Italy via Tirol following a peace agreement with Frederick. They attacked the Franco-Spanish winter quarters, forcing a French garrison of 6,000 men at Asti to capitulate. Simultaneously, Count Browne led an Austrian corps on the lower Po, severing the allies' communications with their main body in Piedmont. 

The resulting fragmentation saw the allies separate, with Maillebois covering Liguria and the Spaniards marching against Browne. The Spaniards were eventually forced to entrench themselves at Piacenza. Although Maillebois and the Spanish forces eventually reunited, they faced a critical situation, pursued by the army of the king of Sardinia and opposed by the principal Austrian army. This culminated in the hard-fought pitched battle of Piacenza on June 16.

## Comparative Infrastructure
In the mid-19th century, the irrigation systems of Lombardy served as a point of comparison for imperial engineering projects. Specifically, the Ganges canal in India was noted to have exceeded all the irrigation lines of Egypt and Lombardy combined.
