# Administration and Political Divisions

The organization of political divisions and administrative structures varies significantly across different regions and eras, ranging from imperial protectorates and colonial circles to provincial divisions and specialized military departments.

## Colonial and Protectorate Administrations

In the Nyasaland Protectorate, which encompasses the Shiré province and districts surrounding Lake Nyasa, administration is handled directly by the imperial government. A governor, acting under the orders of the colonial office, leads the region with the assistance of an executive council and a nominated legislative council consisting of at least three members. To the west, the provinces of North Western and North Eastern Rhodesia are governed by two administrators from the British South Africa Chartered Company, who consult with the colonial office and the governor of Nyasaland.

In other colonial systems, such as those in French West Africa, the colony is administered by a lieutenant-governor and a council of official and unofficial members. For administrative purposes, the colony is divided into provinces or "circles," each led by an administrator with extensive powers. These colonies are further categorized into territories that are protected, annexed, or "territories of political action." In protected territories, native laws and customs are generally retained under French supervision, though individuals may choose to place themselves under French law, becoming known as "Assimiles." Revenue in these regions is primarily sourced from customs and a capitation tax.

## Regional and Provincial Systems

In Siam, the provincial division of Nakawn Racha Sema (the "frontier country") has its capital at Korat. This area serves as the headquarters for an army division and a high commissioner. Since 1896, the region has been integrated into the Siamese rural administration system, ending its previous tributary status. Korat also houses the government sericultural department, established in 1904 with Japanese assistance to improve silk quality.

In Persia, the province of Astarabad is bounded by the Elburz Mountains to the south, the Caspian Sea and Russian Transcaspian to the north, Khorasan to the east, and Mazandaran to the west. The province is divided into several administrative divisions known as *buluk*, which include Astarabad town, Astarabad rustak (villages), Sadan rustak, Anazan, Katul, Shahkuh Savar, and Findarisk (including Nodeh and Kuhsar).

Historically, the Turkish government has utilized a system where a Defterdar is nominated as a finance administrator in each province. This official derives authority directly from the government and is professedly independent of the Pacha.

## Specialized Administrative Controls

Different strategies are employed based on the nature of the territory. In the northern Karen hills, the British have applied a principle of political control rather than administrative control, leaving tribes undisturbed provided they maintain peace. Conversely, the Kachin hills—specifically the country in the Myitkyina and Bhamo districts—are divided into forty tracts. In these tracts, government is regulated by the Kachin hills regulation; petty chiefs are given appointment orders, the population is disarmed, and a fixed rate of tribute per household is established.

In other regions, such as the division that in 1891 comprised the Tarai, Garhwal, and Kumaon districts, administration has shifted over time. Kumaon and the Tarai were later renamed after their headquarters, Almora and Naini Tal. Following the Gurkha war of 1815, this area was governed for seventy years under a non-regulation system by administrators including Sir Henry Ramsay, Mr. J. H. Batten, and Mr. Traill.

## Military and Theoretical Perspectives

Administrative functions are sometimes separated from operational or strategic duties. In the naval system, it was proposed that the Mobilisation Division be renamed the "Manning Department" and separated from the War Staff. This is because the division's duties—primarily the day-to-day provision of complements for commissioning ships—are almost entirely administrative, a sphere considered foreign to the War Staff's reflective and organizing duties.

From a theoretical standpoint, some argue that a "natural political map" exists which provides the best geographical divisions for human administration. Proponents of this view suggest that any political division that deviates from this natural map is a "misfit" that creates stresses of hostility and insurrection. This perspective suggests that many European conflicts following the Napoleonic wars resulted from governments that were not socially representative and were further complicated by differences in race, language, and religion between the rulers and the ruled.
