# Bolingbroke's Political and Literary Works

## Political Tracts and Writings
Bolingbroke produced a significant body of political work, characterized by a style that was spirited, dignified, and splendidly eloquent, though often described as exceedingly rhetorical. Among his primary publications was *A Collection of Political Tracts*, which was published in 1748. 

Other notable political efforts include *The State of Parties at the Accession of George I* and an essay titled *The Spirit of Patriotism*. In 1738, while serving as an adviser and friend to Frederick, Prince of Wales, Bolingbroke wrote *The Patriot King*. This work was initially entrusted to Pope and remained unpublished until Pope secretly printed 1,500 copies. Upon discovering this, Bolingbroke published a correct version of *The Patriot King* in 1749. This publication led to a bitter altercation with Warburton, who defended Pope; Bolingbroke responded by publishing a *Familiar Epistle to the most Impudent Man Living*. Around 1749, he also wrote an unfinished pamphlet titled *Present State of the Nation*.

In 1727, Bolingbroke began a celebrated series of letters to the *Craftsman* attacking the Walpoles, writing under the pseudonym "Occasional Writer."

## Philosophical Essays and Other Works
Bolingbroke's philosophical writings were often composed as amusements during his retirement and were intended to display his eloquence. His collected works, which include his chief political writings and several philosophical essays, were first published in a 1754 edition by Mallet—an edition denounced by Johnson as "the blunderbuss charged against religion and morality." This collection was subsequently published in 1778, 1809, and 1841.

His philosophical essays include:
* *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge*
* *On the Folly and Presumption of Philosophers*
* *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*
* *On Authority in Matters of Religion*

Additionally, during his time in France, he wrote *Reflexions upon Exile* and *Letters on the Study of History* (printed privately before his death and published in 1752), as well as the *True Use of Retirement*. He also attempted to write verse, as noted in Walpole's *Royal and Noble Authors*.

## Correspondence and Letters
Bolingbroke's letters are preserved across various archives and publications. His *Letters* were published by G. Parke in 1798, and Grimoard published *Lettres historiques, politiques, philosophiques, &c.* in 1808. A letter written in 1717 to Sir W. Windham explaining his position is regarded as one of his finest compositions, though it was not published until 1753.

Further collections of his correspondence can be found in:
* The *Correspondence* of Pope and Swift.
* W. Coxe's *Walpole*.
* Phillimore's *Life of Lyttelton*.
* *Hardwicke State Papers*, vol. ii.
* *Marchmont Papers* (edited by Sir G.H. Rose, 1831).
* *Add. MSS. Brit. Museum* (Letters to Lord Chancellor Hardwicke), mostly transcribed by W. Sichel.
* *Hist. MSS. Comm., MSS. of Marquis of Bath, Duke of Portland at Welbeck*.
* A collection in the British Museum relating to the treaty of Utrecht.

## Critical Reception and Legacy
While Bolingbroke was highly admired by contemporaries for his wit, social qualities, and unrivalled eloquence, his lasting impression on posterity is considered weaker. Critics have suggested his genius was superficial and focused on ephemeral objects rather than universal ideas. 

His influence is a subject of debate. While George III and Bute derived political ideas from *The Patriot King*, his influence on Gibbon, Voltaire, and Burke is viewed as problematical. Burke wrote *Vindication of Natural Society* in imitation of Bolingbroke's style, but specifically to refute his principles. Burke later questioned who still read Bolingbroke in full and denied that the author's words left a permanent impression on his mind. His philosophical works are viewed as lacking originality and solidity, presenting a "feeble deism" and attacks on theology and metaphysics.
