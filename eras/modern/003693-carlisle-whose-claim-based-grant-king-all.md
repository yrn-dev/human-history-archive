# The Earl of Carlisle and Barbados

## Colonial Acquisition and the Grant of 1624
The colonization of Barbados was a process of peaceful acquisition without conquest. The island was first touched in 1605 by the British ship "Olive Blossom," and the crew took possession in the name of James I after finding the island uninhabited. The first actual settlement occurred in 1625 under the direction of Sir William Courteen, acting under a patent held by Lord Leigh (later the earl of Marlborough), to whom the king had granted the island.

In 1627, a compromise was reached with Lord Marlborough, and a grant of the island was obtained by the earl of Carlisle. The earl's claim to the island was based on a grant he had received from the king in 1624, which encompassed all the Caribbean islands.

## Early Governance and Settlement
Following the establishment of the earl of Carlisle's claim, Charles Wolferstone, a native of Bermuda, was appointed governor in 1628. That same year, the present capital was founded when sixty-four settlers arrived at Carlisle Bay.

During the English Civil War, Barbados became a place of refuge for many Royalists. Under the leadership of Lord Willoughby, who had leased the island from the earl of Carlisle, these Royalists offered stout resistance against the forces of the Commonwealth. This period of resistance ended when Willoughby was defeated and exiled.

## Transition to Crown Government
After the Restoration, the crown took over the government of the island. This move was intended to appease the planters, who were uncertain about the titles under which they held their estates, which had been developed into valuable properties. To resolve this, the proprietary or patent interest was abolished. To satisfy the claims of the patentees, a duty of 4-1/2% was imposed on all exports. By 1684, during the governorship of Sir Richard Dutton, a census recorded the island's population at 20,000.

## Other Historical References to Carlisle
Beyond the colonial history of Barbados, the name Carlisle appears in other historical contexts within the archive:

*   **Ecclesiastical Holdings:** In 1230, Walter Mauclerc, the bishop of Carlisle, purchased the manor of Horncastle from Ralph de Rhodes. The see of Carlisle retained this manor until the reign of Edward VI, when it was granted to Edward, Lord Clinton, though it was recovered in the following reign. During the middle ages, Horncastle frequently served as a residence for the bishops of Carlisle. In 1230, Henry III directed the men of Horncastle to provide reasonable aid to the bishop, who was granted the right to hold a court leet, try felons, and have free warren. Additionally, the bishop was granted a great horse fair for the octave of St Lawrence, as well as a fair on the feast of St Barnabas.
*   **Palatinate Possessions:** At the time of the Conquest, the possessions of the bishop (associated with the post-conquest palatinate) included the city of Carlisle and part of Teviotdale, although Henry I later deprived the bishopric of these areas, compensating the see with the vills of Carlton, Aycliffe, and Burdon.
*   **Transportation:** In later history, the Glasgow & South-Western railway and the North British railway (via Riddings Junction in Cumberland) provided rail access to Carlisle.
