# A-SHE-HO

A-SHE-HO (also known as Alchuku) is a town located in Manchuria, China. It is situated 125 miles northeast of Kirin and 30 miles south of the Sungari. The town has a population of approximately 60,000 people and is governed by a mandarin of the second class.

## Regional Context: Manchuria
A-SHE-HO is located within Manchuria, a region described as being larger than the United Kingdom and France combined. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, this territory became a focal point of international conflict and diplomatic scrutiny. The region consists of three eastern provinces and possesses a littoral on the Yellow Sea and the Gulf of Pehchili.

During this period, Manchuria was the site of a major war between Japan and Russia. The conflict was driven largely by Russia's desire for an ice-free port in the Far East and a more efficient communication line than the Amur waterway. To achieve this, Russia sought to divert its trans-Asian railway from the northern bank of the Amur—which required a long, semicircular detour—to a straighter, more economical line passing through Chinese territory in Manchuria.

## Russian Occupation and Expansion
Russia obtained permission in Peking to construct this railway through Manchuria as a recompense for assistance nominally rendered to China. Following the seizure of Shantung by Germany in response to the murder of two missionaries, Russia obtained a lease of the Liaotung peninsula and extorted permission from China to build a railway branch from north to south, running from Harbin through Mukden to Talien and Port Arthur.

The Boxer rising of 1900 provided Russia with a plausible excuse to garrison the region, leading to the practical occupation of the whole of Manchuria. Russia subsequently attempted to secure a conventional title to the territory, proposing a convention that would have recognized its title to Manchuria.

## International Diplomacy and the Open Door
The Russian presence in Manchuria caused profound anxiety for Japan, which feared that Russian control of the region would make the absorption of Korea inevitable. Japan, supported by the United States and Great Britain, advocated for the "open door" policy and the maintenance of China's territorial integrity.

The United States, Great Britain, and Japan worked to strengthen China's position so that Russia would be induced to withdraw its troops. While Russia initially signed a treaty pledging to evacuate its troops in three installments by fixed dates, it later behaved as though no such promise had been made, proposing new conditions to strengthen its grasp on the region.

Following the war between Japan and Russia, the Portsmouth treaty and subsequent ententes with France and Russia reinforced the principles of China's integrity and the open door. It was agreed that portions of Manchuria under Japanese or Russian control would be restored to China's exclusive administration, with the exception of leased territory. The Russian government was required to disavow any exclusive concessions that impaired Chinese sovereignty.

## Chinese Sovereignty and Rights Recovery
By the early part of 1909, relations between China and Japan became strained. While China nominally pursued a course of earnest reforms and industrious developments to recover concessions granted to foreign powers, the population entered a mood of impatience. This manifested as a "rights-recovery campaign," in which the Chinese people protested vehemently against any conditions that impaired their sovereignty, particularly regarding the situation in Manchuria.
