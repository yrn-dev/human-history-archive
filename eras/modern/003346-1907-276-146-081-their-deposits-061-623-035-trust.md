# Trust Companies and the Panic of 1907

## Growth of Trust Companies
At the end of the 19th century, trust companies represented a relatively small component of New York finance. In 1897, the total capitalization of all trust companies across the United States was $106,968,253, with individual deposits totaling $566,922,205. By 1907, these figures had grown significantly; the capital of these companies rose to $276,146,081, and their deposits increased to $2,061,623,035.

## The Panic of 1907
The position of trust companies, particularly those located in New York City, became a disturbing element during the financial panic of 1907. The crisis was precipitated by over-speculation conducted by a group of national banks. This was followed on October 22 by the suspension of the Knickerbocker Trust Company, which held deposits of $48,000,000. 

This event triggered runs on other companies. In the week of November 2, New York banks faced a deficit in required reserves amounting to $38,838,825. To combat the crisis, arrangements were made to import foreign gold in amounts that soon approached $100,000,000. Additionally, $72,000,000 was transferred from the treasury to the banks, and national bank circulation increased by approximately $77,000,000 during the autumn. In total, the amount of currency that either disappeared or was added to the circulation during the few weeks of the panic exceeded $275,000,000, representing nearly one-tenth of the country's usual circulation volume. By December 28, 1907, the total bank-note circulation had reached an abnormal high of $687,340,835, though this was reduced during the spring of 1908.

## Reserve Inadequacies and Speculation
The vulnerability of the trust companies was linked to state law, which required New York trust companies to maintain only 5% of their demand deposits in cash within their vaults. While many of these companies held large deposits in national banks, these reserves were inadequate to support the vast mass of credit built upon them.

Beyond the absolute amount of reserves, the nature of the business conducted by less conservative companies contributed to the instability. Rather than maintaining liquid assets through loans on first-class negotiable securities or the purchase of commercial paper, some companies engaged in speculative underwritings. This resulted in funds being locked up in enterprises that required a long time to reach consummation.

## Impact on New York Resources
The distrust surrounding the Knickerbocker Trust Company led to runs on various institutions in late October and early November. These events caused a sharp decline in the total resources of the forty-eight trust companies in Greater New York. On August 22, 1907, their total resources were $1,205,019,700; by December 19, 1907, this figure had fallen to $858,674,000. During the same period, individual deposits subject to cheque dropped from $692,744,900 to $437,733,400.

## Legislative Response
The rapid reduction of resources within a few weeks was nearly unprecedented in banking history. While the ability of stronger companies to meet demands suggested that some criticisms were exaggerated, the crisis highlighted a critical need for greater safeguards against speculative operations and stronger reserves. Consequently, the New York legislature enacted several restrictive measures during its 1908 session to prevent similar abuses in the future.
