# Fischer's Classification of Bactrinium and Bactrillum

Within the broader taxonomic study of bacteria, Fischer developed a classification system that distinguishes between various genera based on morphological characteristics, specifically the presence and arrangement of motile organs. This system is situated within the order Haplobacterinae, which comprises organisms with unicellular vegetative bodies that may be cylindrical, spheroidal, or spirally twisted.

## The Family Bacillaceae
Fischer's classifications of *Bactrinium* and *Bactrillum* fall under the family Bacillaceae. The vegetative cells of this family are straight and may be ovoid, ellipsoid, or cylindric (referred to as rodlets). A defining characteristic of this family is that division planes are always perpendicular to the long axis of the cell.

Within the sub-family Bacilleae, which consists of sporogenous rodlets that do not undergo alterations in shape, Fischer differentiates between motile and non-motile forms. While the genus *Bacillus* (Cohn) is non-motile, Fischer identifies three distinct motile genera based on their cilia:

*   **Bactrinium**: Defined as motile organisms possessing a single polar flagellum, a condition known as monotrichous.
*   **Bactrillum**: Defined as motile organisms possessing a terminal tuft of cilia, a condition known as lophotrichous.
*   **Bactridium**: Defined as motile organisms with cilia distributed over the entire surface, a condition known as peritrichous.

## Objections to the Classification System
Despite the structured nature of Fischer's system, several principal objections have been raised regarding its practical application.

One primary difficulty is the challenge of obtaining satisfactory preparations that clearly show the cilia. It has been discovered that these motile organs are not formed on all substrata. Furthermore, cilia may only develop during short periods of activity while the organism is young and vigorous, rendering this character almost nugatory. For example, *B. subtilis* and *B. megatherium* transition from a motile stage with peritrichous cilia to a stage of filamentous growth—preceded by the casting of the cilia—within a few hours after growth begins.

Another objection concerns the distribution of species. The vast majority of described species (over 1,000) are concentrated in only three genera: *Micrococcus* (approximately 400), *Bacillus* (approximately 200), and *Bactridium* (approximately 150). This means only about a quarter of the forms are selected out by the other genera in the system.

Finally, the monotrichous and lophotrichous conditions are not always constant, even during the motile stage. An example is *Pseudomonas rosea* (Mig.), which may possess one, two, or three cilia at either end. Consequently, depending on which state is observed, *Pseudomonas rosea* would be distributed between *Bactrinium* and *Bactrillum* under Fischer's classification.

## Context of Bacterial Classification
Fischer's work exists within a long history of attempts to classify bacteria, beginning with Cohn's system in 1872 and its extension in 1875. Other attempts have included Zopf's 1885 scheme based on pleomorphism, as well as systems based on the formation of endospores and arthrospores. 

Medical bacteriologists have proposed schemes based on physiological properties, such as the ability to secrete colored pigments, liquefy gelatine, or ferment media with the evolution of gases. However, these have generally failed to maintain their position because physiological properties are liable to great fluctuations based on the organism's age, nutrition, vitality, and external growth factors. Because of these variables, such properties are often too unstable to aid in the discrimination of genera and species.
