# Effects Produced by Bacteria

The study of bacteria from a pathological standpoint focuses on their role as pathogenic agents, which is largely an instance of their general ability to produce chemical changes. While bacteria may occasionally produce mechanical effects, such as plugging the capillaries of the kidneys or brain, it is an accepted fact that all important results within the tissues are due to toxins or poisonous bodies formed by the bacteria.

## Classification of Bacterial Effects
The effects produced by bacteria are categorized under three primary heads:
1. **Tissue changes in the vicinity of the bacteria:** These occur at either primary or secondary foci. These changes are viewed as a combination of the direct action of toxins on living cells and the reaction of the tissues. Many of these changes are grouped as "inflammation," which can be acute, subacute, or chronic. Fundamental changes include the death and degeneration of cells, leucocyte emigration, haemorrhages, proliferation of other cells and connective tissue, and serous and fibrinous exudations. Complex resulting processes include granulation-tissue formation, suppuration, and various types of acute inflammation.
2. **Tissue changes produced at a distance:** These result from the absorption and distribution of toxins. Examples of these widespread effects include fatty degeneration, cloudy swelling, capillary haemorrhages, serous effusions, and various degenerations of muscle. In certain chronic diseases, waxy degeneration may occur, and small blood-vessels may undergo hyaline degeneration.
3. **Symptoms:** The general clinical manifestations resulting from bacterial activity.

## Nature and Action of Toxins
Toxins are the primary drivers of bacterial pathology. These poisonous substances are classified based on their location relative to the bacterium:
*   **Extracellular Toxins:** These are soluble toxins set free into the culture medium. Their presence can be demonstrated by filtering a bouillon culture through a porcelain filter; the resulting filtrate can reproduce the characteristic features of the disease when injected into an animal. This is seen in the case of diphtheria and tetanus bacilli.
*   **Intracellular Toxins:** These are contained within the bodies of the bacteria and are released upon disintegration. This is observed in organisms such as cholera, typhoid, and tubercle. The action of these toxins is often non-characteristic, generally interfering with vital processes and producing fever, which may lead to fatal results. In these instances, the features of the disease are largely regulated by the distribution and vital properties of the bacteria.

Some toxins exhibit a special affinity for specific tissues. For example, tetanus causes minute alterations in the protoplasm and nucleus of nerve cells, while diphtheria produces changes in both nerve fibres and nerve cells.

## Patterns of Bacterial Invasion
Bacteria behave differently upon entering the body, leading to different classes of disease:
*   **Septicaemia:** In extreme instances, such as pneumococcus septicaemia in rabbits or anthrax in guinea-pigs, bacteria enter the blood stream and multiply freely, causing general poisoning. This is indicated by lesions such as capillary haemorrhages and cloudy swelling in internal organs. In humans, often due to streptococci, organisms are primarily found in the capillaries of internal organs and may not be detectable in peripheral circulation during life.
*   **Secondary Extension:** Some organisms create a local lesion first, then spread via the blood or lymph stream to create secondary nodules, tubercle, or abscesses. In typhoid fever, this involves the secondary invasion of the mesenteric glands and the presence of bacilli clumps in internal organs, particularly the spleen.
*   **Localized Infection:** In some diseases, bacteria remain restricted to one area, and symptoms are caused by absorbed toxins. Examples include tetanus (restricted to a wound), diphtheria (restricted to the region of the false membrane), and cholera (practically confined to the intestine).

## Other Bacterial Actions
Beyond pathology, bacteria produce significant chemical effects in the environment. In soil and rivers, some bacteria oxidize ammonia to nitrous acid and then to nitric acid. Conversely, de-nitrifying bacteria can reduce nitrates to nitrites, and some may further reduce them so that ammonia or free nitrogen escapes. This process explains why applying nitrates and fresh manure together is wasteful, as the de-nitrifying bacteria in manure undo the work of nitrifying bacteria.

In agriculture, certain bacteria specialize in leguminous plants, forming nodules. Research by Professor Bottomley in 1907 suggested that this specialization could be broken, allowing bacteria from leguminous nodules to infect cereals, roses, and tomatoes to improve growth.
