# Government and Administrative Divisions

The organization of government and territorial administration varies significantly across different regions and historical periods, ranging from the subdivision of island archipelagos to the complex provincial systems of continental nations.

## The Azores
The Azores, an archipelago belonging to the kingdom of Portugal, are organized into three administrative districts. These districts are named after their respective chief towns:
*   **Ponta Delgada**: The capital of St Michael's, this district includes the islands of St Michael's and St Mary.
*   **Angra** (or Angra do Heroismo): The capital of Terceira, this district encompasses Terceira, St George, and Graciosa.
*   **Horta**: The capital of Fayal, this district includes Pico, Fayal, Flores, and Corvo.

In terms of political representation, Ponta Delgada returns four members to the parliament in Lisbon, while the districts of Angra and Horta each return two members. Military administration is structured differently, with the islands forming two commands headquartered at Ponta Delgada and Angra.

## French Administrative Structure
The territorial division of France has evolved since the French Revolution. Previously, the local unit was the province, but these were replaced due to administrative mismanagement associated with the old régime. Following a suggestion by Mirabeau, the thirty-four provinces were broken into eighty-three departments. These were designed to be roughly equal in population and size, often taking their names from mountains, rivers, or other geographical features.

The number of departments fluctuated over time: Napoleon increased the count to one hundred and thirty, which was then reduced to eighty-six in 1815. In 1860, three departments were added from Nice and Savoy. Following the German war in 1871, three departments (Moselle, Haut-Rhin, and Bas-Rhin) were lost. The remaining fragments of Moselle were incorporated into Meurthe (renamed Meurthe-et-Moselle), and the remains of Haut-Rhin became the territory of Belfort, resulting in a total of eighty-seven departments.

Each department is led by a government-appointed officer called a prefect, who is assisted by a prefectorial council (*conseil de préfecture*). The hierarchy of subdivision continues as follows:
*   **Departments** are divided into **arrondissements**, each managed by a sub-prefect.
*   **Arrondissements** are divided into **cantons**.
*   **Cantons** are further divided into **communes**, which serve as the administrative unit with self-government in local matters.

## Other Provincial and Regional Systems
Different regions employ various terms and structures for provincial governance:

**Greece**
In modern Greece, the country is divided into nomarchies. These are subdivided into eparchies, which are further subdivided into demarchies. Historically, in Roman Greece, the governor of a province was known as an eparch (from the Greek *eparchos*), a title equivalent to the Roman *praefectus*. The area under his administration was called an eparchy.

**Nyasaland and Rhodesia**
In the Nyasaland Protectorate, the Shiré province and districts surrounding Lake Nyasa are administered by a governor under the colonial office, assisted by a nominated legislative council (of at least three members) and an executive council. To the west, the provinces of North Western and North Eastern Rhodesia are governed by two administrators from the British South Africa Chartered Company, who consult with the colonial office and the governor of Nyasaland.

**Other Systems**
Certain kingdoms utilize a system where the kingdom is divided into provinces, which are further subdivided into cantons and communes. In such systems, the king nominates a governor for each province. The canton serves as a judicial division for the jurisdiction of the *juge de paix*. In communes with 5,000 or more inhabitants, a burgomaster is appointed by a communal council. These councils are chosen by electors who must meet a three-year residence requirement.
