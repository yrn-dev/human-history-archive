# Ballistics and Mathematical Surfaces

## Direct Fire and Trajectory Calculations
In the study of ballistics, "direct fire" is officially defined as fire from guns utilizing a full charge at an elevation that does not exceed 15 degrees. Under these specific conditions, the vertical component of air resistance is considered insensible and may be ignored. Consequently, the horizontal component of velocity—or the component parallel to the line of sight—is indistinguishable from the actual velocity.

The equations of motion for this type of fire, with coordinates $x$ and $y$ measured in feet, are expressed as:
*   $d^2x/dt^2 = -r = -gp/C$
*   $d^2y/dt^2 = -g$

From these equations, the time ($t$) and distance ($x$) are derived as $t = C\{T(V) - T(v)\}$ and $x = C\{S(V) - S(v)\}$. Integration of the motion equations further establishes that $dy/dt = \text{constant} - gt = g(1/2T - t)$, where $T$ represents the total time of flight from point O to point B, the point where the trajectory intersects the line of sight. The time to reach the vertex A, where the shot flies parallel to OB, is $1/2T$.

The vertical position is calculated using the formula $y = g(1/2Tt - 1/2t^2) = 1/2gt(T - t)$. By denoting $T - t$ as $t'$ and using the value $g = 32\text{f/s}^2$, the formula becomes $y = 16tt'$. This is known as Colonel Sladen's formula, which is utilized for plotting the ordinates of a trajectory. At the vertex A, where $y = H$, the time $t = t' = 1/2T$. This results in the formula $H = 1/8gT^2$, which for practical purposes (taking $g = 32$) is replaced by $H = 4T^2$ or $(2T)^2$.

## Rational Functions and Surface Integrals
In the mathematical analysis of surfaces, $R_i(x, y)$ represents a rational function of $y$ and $x_1, \dots, x_p$. An original surface possesses $p$ integrals of the form $\int(R dx + S dy)$, where $R$ and $S$ are rational in $x, y, z$ and remain finite everywhere. It has been shown that no other such integrals exist for the surface.

The value $p = p_g - p_a$ for a surface is considered analogous to the deficiency of a plane curve. This analogy is further illustrated by comparing specific theorems: in a plane curve with zero deficiency, there is no algebraic series of sets of points that does not consist of sets belonging to a linear series. Similarly, for a surface where $p_g - p_a = 0$, there is no algebraic system of curves that is not contained within a linear system.
