# Military and Imperial Defence Literature

The study of military affairs and imperial defence in the British context has produced a significant body of literature, ranging from historical surveys of architecture to strategic analyses of empire and the personal accounts of those involved in military campaigns.

## Imperial Defence and Strategy
In recent years, a growing body of literature has emerged concerning British imperialism. Key works addressing the strategic and organizational aspects of the empire include Sir G.S. Clarke's *Imperial Defence* (1897) and Sir John Colomb's *Imperial Federation, Naval and Military* (1886). Other relevant texts on the growth and policy of the empire include Sir J.R. Seeley's *Expansion of England* (1883) and *Growth of British Policy* (1895), as well as Sir Charles Dilke's *Greater Britain* (1869), *Problems of Greater Britain* (1890), and *The British Empire* (1899).

The evolution of land defence has been closely tied to the internal growth of the empire. Initially, early American colonies were expected to provide for their own land defence, a principle also practiced by the East India Company in India. However, the costs of the French wars eventually exceeded local resources in America, leading Great Britain to intervene in 1755 with troops under General Braddock. To fund this, the imperial parliament exercised the right to tax the colonies, passing a Quartering Act in 1765 to station 10,000 imperial troops there. Following the American War, the imperial authorities assumed the charge of land defences for the empire, with the cost borne by United Kingdom taxpayers, except in the Hudson Bay territories and India, where trading companies continued to pay their own expenses. This arrangement persisted until the end of the Napoleonic Wars.

## Military Architecture and History
The study of medieval military architecture is anchored by G.T. Clark's two-volume *Medieval Military Architecture in England*, which serves as a standard work despite some inaccuracies superseded by later research. Professor Oman's *Art of War in the Middle Ages* provides a wide survey of the subject, though it shares some of Clark's errors. Other valuable contributions to the field include Mackenzie's *The Castles of England* (1897), which is noted for its illustrations, and Viollet-le-Duc's *Essay on the Military Architecture of the Middle Ages*, translated by M. Macdermott in 1860.

More recent specialized studies include works by J.H. Round on Geoffrey de Mandeville (1891), and research on early Norman castles by Mrs. Armitage. Additionally, G. Neilson has written on motes in Norman Scotland, and G.H. Orpen has documented motes and Norman castles in Ireland.

## Military Figures and Campaigns
Historical records highlight various individuals' intersections with military service. Philip Sidney took part in a campaign in the Netherlands and was killed on October 17, 1586. His contemporary, Fulke Greville, 1st Baron Brooke, had intended to sail with Sir Francis Drake in 1585 against the Spanish West Indies, but was forbidden by Queen Elizabeth. Greville later served briefly in Normandy under Henry of Navarre around 1591, which marked his final experience of war.

In the early 20th century, Winston Spencer Churchill was involved in several military and naval matters. He attended the Committee of Imperial Defence and authored a piece titled "Military Aspects of the Continental Problem" in 1911. His activities included studying the military position in Europe, proposing a Naval War Staff, and sending "warning telegrams" to Commanders-in-Chief.
