# British Central Africa: Native Population and Demographics

## Population Statistics
According to archive records, the total native population of British Central Africa is approximately 2,000,000. This figure is part of a broader summary of native populations across the empire, which includes other regions such as South Africa (5,211,329), East Africa (7,425,000), and West Africa (18,863,000).

## Ethnic and Racial Composition
The indigenous native population of British Central Africa is represented by a single native type: the Negro. This general designation is used to encompass the Bantu, Congo Pygmy, Bushman, and Hottentot. In practice, the population is characterized as being of a black or chocolate-coloured Negro type.

While the population is predominantly Negro, there are specific nuances regarding racial mixture:
*   **Hamitic Influence:** No trace of Hamitic intermixture is found, with the possible exception of the native Awankonde at the north end of Lake Nyasa, whose physique is reminiscent of the Nilotic negro.
*   **Arab Influence:** Arabs from Zanzibar settled in the country, though records indicate this did not occur earlier than the beginning of the 19th century.
*   **Other Intermixtures:** In certain areas, Bantu races show signs of intermixture with Bushmen or Hottentots. There are also legends in mountain districts, specifically Mount Mlanje, regarding the former existence of unmixed Bushman tribes.

## Linguistic Characteristics
The population of British Central Africa belongs exclusively to the Bantu family linguistically. The region features several interesting forms of Bantu speech, particularly in the areas between the river Luapula, the south end of Lake Tanganyika, and the north end of Lake Nyasa.

In the plateau country within these geographical limits, the Bantu dialects are considered to be of an archaic type. One such dialect, Kiwemba (or Kibemba), is noted for being close to the original form of the Bantu mother-language, though the Subiya of southern Barotseland is similarly interesting. These languages, found in northern Nyasaland, North Eastern Rhodesia, and the Kafukwe basin, are connected to the Bantu languages of Uganda via dialects spoken to the north and west of Tanganyika. Additionally, they bear a slight resemblance to Zulu-Kaffir.

## Regional Context and History
The British interest in this part of South Central Africa was centered by the explorations of David Livingstone, who discovered Lake Nyasa in 1859 and journeyed to Lake Mweru and Lake Bangweulu. Following Livingstone, missionary societies entered the region for evangelization, and Scottish settlers established themselves. These groups opposed Portuguese attempts to extend control from the Zambezi and Moçambique provinces.

The region's political structure evolved from a struggle between Britons and Arabs for possession of the country, a conflict that lasted from approximately 1885 until 1896. This period involved the African Lakes Trading Corporation and volunteers such as Mr. Alfred Sharpe and Captain (later Sir F.D.) Lugard. In 1889, Mr. H.H. Johnston was dispatched to arrange a dispute between the Arabs and the African Lakes Corporation and to protect friendly native chiefs from Portuguese aggression. These efforts resulted in the creation of a British sphere of influence and protectorate north of the Zambezi.
