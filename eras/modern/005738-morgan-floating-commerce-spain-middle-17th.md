# Henry Morgan

Henry Morgan was a Welshman and a prominent leader among the buccaneers during the 17th century. Described as both intrepid and unscrupulous, Morgan rose to prominence during a period when the floating commerce of Spain had become utterly insignificant by the middle of the century, although Spanish settlements persisted.

## Early Career and Rise to Command
Morgan first joined Mansfield, another influential buccaneer leader, in 1664. Together, they pursued a scheme to establish a permanent settlement on New Providence, a small island in the Bahamas. This plan was the only rational settlement scheme to animate the wild community of buccaneers, but it was ended prematurely by the death of Mansfield. Following Mansfield's death, Morgan was elected commander.

From his headquarters in Jamaica, Morgan led triumphant expeditions to the mainland and Cuba, sweeping across the Caribbean. Among his notable achievements during this period was the surprise and plundering of Porto Bello, which was recognized as one of the best-fortified ports in the West Indies.

## The Expedition to Panama
Morgan later received commissions from Thomas Modyford, the governor of Jamaica (1620–1679), who maintained a close alliance with the buccaneers—officially titled "privateers." Modyford commissioned Morgan to lead a major attack on the Isthmus of Panama. This location was strategic as it served as the route for transporting bullion from South American mines to Porto Bello for shipment to Spain.

In 1671, Morgan led a force of 2,000 buccaneers. The expedition began with the seizure of Chagres, followed by a difficult journey on foot and in canoes toward the city of Panama. On the morning of the tenth day of their approach, the buccaneers engaged the defenders of the town, resulting in a rout. The city was taken and subsequently burnt, an event accompanied by great barbarities. However, the Spaniards had managed to remove the treasure before the city fell.

## Legal Troubles and Later Life
The aftermath of the Panama expedition was marked by conflict between Morgan and his men. Morgan was accused of defrauding his followers during the division of the booty. The resulting shares per man were small, and many buccaneers died of starvation during the return trip to Jamaica.

As a result of these events, Governor Modyford was recalled, and in 1672, Morgan was called back to England and imprisoned in the Tower. He was eventually allowed to return to Jamaica in 1674, serving as lieutenant-governor alongside Lord Vaughan. His return was met with hostility; he became so unpopular that he was threatened in the streets by the relatives of those who had died during the 1671 expedition. In his final years, Morgan's activities shifted toward the suppression of the buccaneers, who had developed inconvenient claims against him.
