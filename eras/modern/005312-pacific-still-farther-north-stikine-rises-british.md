# Geography and Hydrography of British Columbia

British Columbia is characterized as an essentially mountainous country. Its landscape is defined by the Cordillera, or Cordilleran belt of North America, which comprises the entire width of the province. This region is dominated by two primary mountain systems: the Coast Range on the south-west or Pacific side, and the Rocky Mountains proper on the north-east side. Between these two systems lie the "Interior Plateau"—an elevated tract of hilly country with summits of accordant altitude—and various other subordinate ranges.

The mountain ranges were produced by successive foldings of the earth's crust in a direction parallel to the Pacific Ocean border, resulting in a general trend that runs south-east and north-west. The province is described as a series of parallel mountain ranges separated by long, narrow valleys. Additionally, the Queen Charlotte Islands and Vancouver Island are remnants of another mountain range that runs parallel to the coast, though it is now almost entirely submerged beneath the Pacific waters.

## River Systems and Waterways

Along the south-western side of the Rocky Mountains exists a remarkable valley of considerable geological antiquity, measuring between 1 and 6 miles in width and extending for at least 800 miles. This valley serves as the upper course for approximately seven of the great rivers of the Pacific slope, including the Finlay, Fraser, Columbia, and Kootenay.

The Fraser River rises in the Rocky Mountains and flows north-west for a long distance before turning south. It eventually crosses the Coast Range through a deep, canton-like valley and empties into the Strait of Georgia, a few miles south of Vancouver. The Columbia River rises further south in the same range and flows north for about 150 miles. It crosses the Canadian Pacific railway at Donald, bends abruptly, and flows south, recrossing the railway at Revelstoke. It then passes through the Arrow Lakes in the Kootenay country into the United States, where it empties into the Pacific Ocean at Astoria, Oregon.

In the north-western part of the province, the Skeena flows south-west into the Pacific. Further north, the Stikine rises in British Columbia and crosses the coast strip of Alaska before entering the Pacific. Other significant waterways include the Liard, which rises in the same district as the Stikine but flows east to fall into the Mackenzie, eventually emptying into the Arctic Ocean. The headwaters of the Yukon are also located in the northern part of the province.

Most of these rivers are swift and frequently interrupted by rapids, rendering them of indifferent value for commercial communication. Large lakes in southern British Columbia, including the Arrow Lakes, remain open throughout the winter.

## Transportation and Infrastructure

The importance of rivers as means of communication diminishes wherever railway lines are constructed. The Pacific division of the Canadian Pacific railway enters British Columbia through the Rocky Mountains in the east and travels approximately 500 miles across the province to its terminus at Vancouver. 

Another branch of the Canadian Pacific railway leaves the main line at Medicine Hat, traveling south-west through the Crow's Nest Pass to enter British Columbia just north of the United States boundary. This line runs approximately parallel to the boundary as far as Midway in the Boundary district, crossing a productive mining district and opening extensive coal fields.

On Vancouver Island, two railways operate: the Victoria & Sydney railway (about 16 miles) and the Esquimalt & Nanaimo railway (78 miles), the latter of which connects southern ports with coal fields. Additionally, the Great Northern railway maintains several short lines in the southern portion of the province that connect with its system in the United States. As of 1905, the province contained 1,627 miles of railway, with 1,187 miles owned or controlled by the Canadian Pacific railway.

## Historical Administration

British Columbia and Vancouver Island were settled under the Hudson's Bay Company in 1821. The province later entered the Canadian confederation in 1871.
