# The 1905 Award of the King of Italy regarding Barotseland

## Overview of the Anglo-Portuguese Barotse Boundary Dispute
The 1905 award by the king of Italy served as the resolution to a boundary dispute between Great Britain and Portugal concerning the limits of Barotseland. This arbitration followed a period of establishing British suzerainty in the region. In October 1889, the British South Africa Company was granted a charter allowing it to establish rule in regions north of the Middle Zambezi that were not included in Portuguese dominions. Subsequently, a treaty signed on June 11, 1891, between Portugal and Great Britain declared that the Barotse kingdom fell within the British sphere of influence.

Despite this agreement, the two contracting powers remained in dispute over the specific western limits of Barotseland. To resolve the matter, the dispute was referred to the king of Italy for arbitration.

## The Terms of the 1905 Award
On May 30, 1905, the king of Italy issued his award, which established a formal frontier. The boundary was fixed at the Kwando river as far north as 22deg E. From that point, the frontier followed that meridian up to 13deg S., then followed that parallel eastward to 24deg E., and finally followed that meridian to the frontier of the Belgian Congo.

The publication of this award introduced a second meaning to the term "Barotseland." The ruling declared that the northern and western sections of the territory (as previously described) were outside the dominion of the paramount chief. Consequently, these areas were determined to be outside the British sphere of influence. While the award altered political boundaries and complicated tribal boundaries through the introduction of latitudinal and longitudinal frontiers, the ethnological nature of Barotseland remained largely unchanged.

## Administration and British Protection
Following the establishment of British influence, the area of the country under British protection was approximately 182,000 sq. m. The British South Africa Company maintained friendly relations with Lewanika, the paramount chief of the Barotse. To manage the region, the company appointed an administrator to reside in the country and raised a native police force commanded by a British officer. Additionally, district commissioners and magistrates were appointed.

The company's approach to governance was characterized by non-interference in the internal affairs of the Barotse, and relations between the British and the Barotse were described as uniformly friendly.

## Role of Missionaries and Early Influence
The agents of the Chartered Company were not the primary pioneers of Western civilization in the region; rather, this role was filled by missionaries. F. S. Arnot, an Englishman, spent two years in the country between 1882 and 1884. In 1884, the Societe des Missions Evangeliques de Paris established a mission. Its first agent, Francois Coillard (1834-1904), had previously worked in Basutoland and dedicated the remainder of his life to the Barotse.

Coillard was an admirer of British institutions and desired that the country eventually fall under British jurisdiction. However, for the sake of his mission, he initially sought to delay the arrival of white men in the country. It was contrary to Coillard's advice that Lewanika petitioned the "Great White Queen" to assume a protectorate over his dominions.
