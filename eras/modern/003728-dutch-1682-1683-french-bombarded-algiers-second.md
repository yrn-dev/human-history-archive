# Dutch Naval and Global Activity

## Naval Conflicts and Trade Rivalries
During the 17th century, Holland was a dominant naval power and the "ruler of the sea." This position led to significant friction with England, a trade rival. The English fleet engaged in a series of obstinate sea fights against the Dutch, eventually driving them from British seas and establishing England as the ascendant naval power. Following this shift, Dutch ships, along with French ships, were required to dip their flags to the English.

Further naval engagements occurred in the Mediterranean. During the revolt of Messina from Spain, the French naval officer Marquis d'Arraham Duquesne was sent to support the insurgents. In this theater, Duquesne encountered the combined fleets of Holland and Spain, which were under the command of Admiral de Ruyter. Additionally, in 1673, the island of St Helena was captured from the Dutch.

## Relations with Barbary Piracy
The Barbary pirates, particularly those based in Algiers, operated extensively during the first half of the 17th century. They captured traders from nations that did not pay "blackmail"—disguised as ransoms or presents—to secure immunity. While some European powers tolerated these insults or made payments to avoid conflict, the existence of this piracy was often fueled by European political rivalries. France encouraged the pirates during its rivalry with Spain; subsequently, when France no longer found them useful, the pirates were supported against France by Holland and Great Britain.

By the 18th century, some British public figures viewed Barbary piracy as a beneficial check on the carrying trade competition of weaker Mediterranean nations. However, by 1816, there was a universal feeling that the suppression of African piracy was a necessity. On August 27, 1816, a Dutch squadron under Admiral Van de Capellen combined with a British force under Lord Exmouth to deliver a "smashing bombardment" to Algiers. This action terrified the pirates of Algiers and Tunis, resulting in the release of over 3,000 prisoners.

## Activity in Asia
Dutch influence extended into Asia, notably in the Dutch East Indies. Bantam, the westernmost residency of the island of Java, served as a significant region. Bounded by the Java sea to the north, the Indian Ocean to the south, and the Strait of Sunda to the west, Bantam included several islands such as Princes Island and Dwars-in-den-weg. 

The region's economy was based on the cultivation of coffee, rice, and pulse (_kachang_), while government culture previously focused on indigo, sugar, and pepper. The residency was divided into five native regencies: Lebak, Charingin, Pandeglang, Anjer, and Serang. The population in 1897 included 302 Europeans, 1,959 Chinese, and 89 Arabs and other Asiatic foreigners, alongside native Javanese and Sundanese inhabitants.

In the mid-19th century, the Dutch participated in the opening of Japan. In 1863, a fleet comprising warships from the United States, France, Britain, and Holland bombarded the batteries of a Japanese nobleman who had fired on foreign vessels at the Straits of Shimonoseki. This action, followed by an allied squadron in 1865, helped impose the ratification of treaties that opened Japan to the world.
