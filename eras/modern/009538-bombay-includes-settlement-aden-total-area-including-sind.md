# Bombay Presidency

The Bombay Presidency was a province of British India administered by a governor. Its territory extended from 13 deg. 53' to 28 deg. 45' N. and from 66 deg. 40' to 76 deg. 30' E. The presidency was bounded by the Arabian Sea to the west, Madras and Mysore to the south, Indore, Hyderabad, and the Central Provinces to the east, and Rajputana, the Punjab, and Baluchistan to the north. While the Portuguese settlements of Goa, Damaun, and Diu, as well as the native state of Baroda, lay within these limits, the presidency politically included the settlement of Aden, along with its dependencies of Sokotra Island and Perim.

## Territorial Composition and Administration
The total area of the presidency, including Sind but excluding Aden, was 188,745 sq. m. Of this total, 65,761 sq. m. were under native rule and 122,984 sq. m. were under British administration. The province was organized into twenty-six districts and four commissionerships: Sind, the southern (Carnatic), the central (Deccan), and the northern (Gujarat).

The twenty-six districts comprised:
*   **Gujarat and surrounding areas:** Bombay City, Ahmedabad, Broach, Kaira, Panch Mahals, Surat, Thana.
*   **Deccan and Southern regions:** Ahmednagar, Khandesh (which was partitioned into two districts in 1906), Nasik, Poona, Satara, Sholapur, Belgaum, Bijapur, Dharwar, Kanara, Kolaba, Ratnagiri.
*   **Sind:** Karachi, Hyderabad, Shikarpur, Thar and Parkar, and the Upper Sind Frontier.

Additionally, the presidency included 353 separate native state units. These were managed by collectors of the districts or political agents. Major groups of states included South Gujarat (Sachin, Bansda, and Dharampur), North Gujarat (Cambay, Palanpur agency, Mahi Kantha agency, Rewa Kantha agency, Kathiawar agency, and Cutch), North Konkan and Khandesh (Jawhar, Surgana, and the Khandesh political agency), South Konkan and Dharwar (Savanur, Sawantwari, and Janjira), the Deccan Satara Jagirs (Daphlapur, Jath, Phaltan, Aundh, Bhor, and Akalkot), the southern Mahratta states (including Kolhapur), and Khairpur in Sind.

## History and Expansion
In 1803, the presidency was limited to Bankot, Surat, Salsette, and the islands of the harbour. Between 1803 and 1827, it expanded significantly. The Gujarat districts were taken over in 1805 and enlarged in 1818, while the settlement of Mahi Kantha and Kathiawar began between 1807 and 1820. Following the defeat and capture of Baji Rao, the last of the peshwas, in 1817-1818, large portions of his dominions—including Dharwar, Belgaum, Sholapur, Nasik, Ahmednagar, and Poona—were added. Governor Mountstuart Elphinstone (1819–1827) oversaw the settlement of these areas, establishing schools, courts, and codified laws while ruling largely on native lines.

Further expansions occurred through the addition of Aden in 1839, Sind in 1843, and the 1853 lease of the Panch Mahals from Sindhia. During the Mutiny, the presidency remained largely stable under Lord Elphinstone (1853-1860), though local rebellions occurred in the southern Mahratta country, Gujarat (among the Bhils), and among troops in Ahmedabad, Kolhapur, and Karachi. Agricultural prosperity peaked between 1862 and 1867 under Sir Bartle Frere due to the American Civil War, which created a massive European demand for Indian cotton.

## Geography and Natural Resources
The geology of the region varies by zone. South of Gujarat, the area is largely covered by the Deccan Trap series of horizontal lava flows, which also extend into Cutch and the Kathiawar peninsula. In Cutch and Kathiawar, these are underlaid by Neocomian and Jurassic beds. The plains of eastern Gujarat and Sind are characterized by wind-blown sand and alluvium.

Notable water features include the Manchar Lake on the right bank of the Indus, which can reach 20 m. in length during inundations. The Runn or Lake of Cutch, covering 8000 sq. m., acts as a salt marsh, inland lake, or arm of the sea depending on the season; when flooded, it turns Cutch into an island.

Forests are divided into two classes: alluvial plains and hills. Alluvial forests in Sind, located near the Indus, cover 550 sq. m. Hill forests are found between 14 deg. and 23 deg. N. lat., primarily in the Western Ghats (Sahyadri hills). Key timber trees include teak and various species of Acacia, Dalbergia, and Terminalia. The region also produces valuable fruit trees such as the jack and mango.

## Infrastructure and Military
The presidency's railway network largely concentrates at Bombay City, with the exception of the North-Western line, which terminates at Karachi after entering Sind from the Punjab. Other major systems include the Indian Midland, Great Indian Peninsula, Southern Mahratta, and Bombay, Baroda & Central India, as well as the Rajputana-Malwa. By 1905, the Bombay government had 7980 m. of railway open for traffic, excluding the system in Sind. Irrigation works were limited except in Sind.

Military organization was restructured in 1904 by Lord Kitchener, replacing the Bombay command with the Western army corps under a lieutenant-general. This corps consisted of three divisions:
*   **4th Division (Quetta):** Troops in the Sind and Quetta districts.
*   **5th Division (Mhow):** Includes the Bombay district north of the Tapti and the districts of Bundelkhand, Nerbudda, Nagpur, Deesa, and Mhow.
*   **6th Division (Poona):** Includes the Bombay district south of the Tapti, the Poona district, the Aurungabad and Dharwar districts, and the Belgaum district north of the Tungabhadra. This division also includes brigades at Aden, Ahmednagar, and Bombay.

## Demographics
As of the 1901 census, the total population was 25,468,209. Of these, 18,515,587 resided in British territory and 6,908,648 lived in native states.
