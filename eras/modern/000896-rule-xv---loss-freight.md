# RULE XV.--LOSS OF FREIGHT

## Overview of Average in Maritime Commerce
In modern maritime law, the term "average" refers to expenses or damages resulting from accidents of navigation. This is categorized into two types: particular average and general average. Particular average involves partial loss or damage to freight, goods, or the ship caused by an unavoidable or fortuitous accident; such losses are borne by the insurers or the parties whose property was damaged. 

General average (G.A.) occurs when expenditures are incurred or sacrifices are made to preserve the freight, cargo, and ship from the effects of a peril of the sea. This necessitates a rateable contribution from all concerned parties, based on the values of their respective interests, to compensate for the loss.

## Rule XV and the Loss of Freight
Under Rule XV of the York-Antwerp Rules, the loss of freight is treated as general average in two specific scenarios:
1. When the loss of freight arises from damage to or loss of cargo and is caused by a general average act.
2. When the damage to or loss of the cargo itself is made good as general average.

The York-Antwerp Rules were established to provide uniformity, certainty, and definiteness to the principles used in adjusting general average losses, replacing various customary rules that differed by location.

## Related Rules on Cargo and Ship Damage
The framework surrounding the loss of freight is supported by other rules regarding sacrifice and contribution:
* **Cargo Sacrifice (Rule XVI):** When goods are sacrificed, the amount made good as G.A. is the loss sustained by the owner, determined by market values at the termination of the adventure or the date the vessel arrives.
* **Contributory Values (Rule XVII):** Contributions are based on the actual values of property at the termination of the adventure, plus any amount made good as G.A. for sacrificed property. Certain deductions are made from the shipowner's passage-money and freight at risk, specifically for crew's wages and port charges that would not have been incurred had the cargo and ship been totally lost at the time of the sacrifice or G.A. act. Notably, personal effects and passengers' luggage not shipped under a bill of lading do not contribute to G.A.
* **Intentional Running Ashore:** If a ship is intentionally run on shore for common safety, the resulting damage to freight, cargo, and the ship is allowed as G.A., unless the ship would have inevitably sunk or driven onto rocks regardless of that course.
* **Press of Sail (Rule VI):** While damage to spars or sails caused by forcing a ship off the ground for common safety is made good as G.A., no loss to freight, cargo, or the ship is allowed as G.A. if the ship is afloat and the damage is caused by carrying a press of sail.

## Liability and Contribution
The primary liability for paying G.A. contributions rests with the owners of the freight, goods, or ship. However, in practice, these are typically paid by insurers. While standard insurance policies may not explicitly state this obligation, they often include clauses recognizing it, such as agreements that G.A. be payable according to the York-Antwerp Rules.

The possibility of contribution is central to the insurer's liability. In the case of the *Brigella* (1893), a claim was disallowed because the shipowner was the only party interested in the voyage, meaning there were no contributories. Conversely, in *Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co.* (1901), cargo underwriters were held liable for a contribution to ship damage (cutting away masts for safety) even though the ship, cargo, and freight belonged to one person, as the loss was theoretically spread over all interests at risk.
