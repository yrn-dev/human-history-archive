# Ausgleich (Compact with Hungary)

The *Ausgleich*, or "Compromise," was a constitutional settlement concluded on February 8, 1867, that regulated the relations between Austria and Hungary. This agreement established the framework for the Dual Monarchy, creating two separate states—Austria and Hungary—that remained completely independent of one another, each possessing its own government and parliament.

## Constitutional Structure and Unity
The unity of the Dual Monarchy was maintained through a common head of state, who held the titles of Emperor of Austria and Apostolic King of Hungary. Beyond the sovereign, the two states shared a common administration for specific affairs that affected both halves of the monarchy:
1. **Foreign Affairs**: This included consular and diplomatic representation abroad.
2. **The Army**: This encompassed the navy, though it excluded the special army of each state and the annual voting of recruits.
3. **Finance**: Joint administration was limited to matters concerning joint expenditure.

The broader constitutional basis of the monarchy rested upon the Pragmatic Sanction of Emperor Charles VI (1713), which settled the succession in the dynasty of Habsburg-Lorraine via primogeniture, and the diploma of Emperor Francis Joseph I (October 20, 1860), which introduced the constitutional form of government.

## Political Implementation and Conflict
The process of ratifying the *Ausgleich* was marked by significant political instability and shifts in policy. The Hungarian requirement that the settlement be ratified by a parliament necessitated the procurement of a body where Germans held a majority, as the system of dualism opposed the ambitions of Federalists and Slavs.

In January 1867, Belcredi, a Federalist who had come into power in 1865, suspended the constitution of 1861 and ordered new elections for the diets to elect deputies to an extraordinary Reichsrath to consider the *Ausgleich*. The wording of Belcredi's decree implied that the February constitution no longer existed as law. This prompted protests from Liberals and Germans, who argued that the February constitution remained in force and that changes required a regular Reichsrath. Because Federalists opposed the *Ausgleich*, it was likely Belcredi intended for the Reichsrath to refuse ratification.

To prevent the endangerment of the empire, Beust intervened. Belcredi was dismissed, and Beust became minister-president on February 7, 1867. A new edict was issued stating the constitution was completely valid. However, diets with Federalist majorities—specifically those of Tirol, Carinthia, Moravia, and Bohemia—refused to elect representatives on terms acceptable to the government.

## Economic and Financial Settlements
The *Ausgleich* also functioned as a general settlement and bargain regarding economic and financial reforms, which were renegotiated every ten years. These periodic settlements included the determination of the "Quota" (the share of common expenses).

Negotiations were often difficult due to "race hatred" and "national jealousy" within the two parliaments. Each measure had to be weighed against a general balance of advantage; a concession in one area was often offset by a gain in another. For example, in 1877, Austria granted Hungary a share in the management of the bank, while Austria benefited from the arrangement for paying the bonus on exported sugar. Conversely, because increased duties on petroleum and coffee fell more heavily on Austria, the Austrians sought to persuade Hungary to pay a larger quota of common expenses. There was also a dispute regarding Hungary's responsibility for a debt of 80 million gulden to the bank.

The first such settlement took two years to complete, beginning in 1876 and concluding in June 1878. This required the two ministries to agree on drafts, followed by negotiations between committees from both parliaments and subsequent debates.

## Later Developments and Decay
By the late 19th century, the hegemony of the Liberal party—the mainstay of dualism since 1867—began to decay as extreme nationalist Magyar spirit grew. This was evident in the 1889 Army bills, which increased the peace footing of the joint army but faced demands for the "Magyarization" of the force. Count Julius Andrassy the Elder warned the country of the dangers of nationalist Chauvinism and reminded it of its obligations under the 1867 Compact.

In 1902, the Hungarian premier Szell and Austrian premier Koerber concluded the "Szell-Koerber Compact," a new customs and trade alliance that established a joint Austro-Hungarian tariff. This was intended to facilitate commercial treaties with Italy, Germany, and other states. Although initially greeted as an end to economic strife, the arrangement was never fully ratified by either parliament and was eventually discarded.
