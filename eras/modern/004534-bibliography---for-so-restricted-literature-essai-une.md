# Bibliography

## Definition and Evolution
The term bibliography derives from the Greek word *bibliographia*. In post-classical Greek, it was used to describe the writing of books. This meaning persisted into the 18th century; for instance, Fenning's *English Dictionary* (1761) defined a bibliographer as someone who copies or writes books. 

A transition occurred in France during the 18th century, shifting the meaning from "a writing of books" to "a writing about books," as evidenced by the 1763 publication of de Bure's *Bibliographie instructive*. In England, this updated meaning was popularized in the early 19th century by the Rev. Thomas Frognall Dibdin. During this period, Southey used the alternative term *bibliology*, though that form is now rarely used.

## Scope and Application
Modern custom generally restricts the province of bibliography to printed books, distinguishing them from manuscripts. However, the scope is broad enough to include various interests of the book-loving antiquary, such as book-collecting, book-illustration, book-binding, and the history of printing. In a technical sense, bibliography is the art of describing, collating, and examining books, as well as their arrangement and enumeration in lists for informational purposes. The study of the literature of this subject is referred to as the bibliography of bibliography.

## Specialized Bibliographies and Authorities
Bibliographies serve as essential tools across various academic and scientific disciplines to organize restricted or extensive literatures.

### Linguistics and Regional Studies
In the study of the Basque language, Julien Vinson's *Essai d'une bibliographie de la langue basque* (1891), including its 1898 volume of additions and corrections, is considered practically exhaustive. Other significant linguistic works include A. Oihenart's *Notitia utriusque Vasconiae* (1638 and 1656), Fl. Lecluse's *Manuel de la langue basque* (1826), and W. J. Van Eys's *Grammaire comparee des dialectes basques* (1879). For general readers, Vinson's *Les Basques et le pays basque* (1882) provides a sufficient survey.

### Science and Medicine
In fields where literature is vast, such as bacteriology, it is often impossible to list every original article. Instead, scholars rely on comprehensive authorities. Key works include Kolle and Wassermann's *Handbuch der pathogenen Mikroorganismen* (1904), which is cited as the most important work on the subject. Other essential texts include Abbott's *Principles of Bacteriology* (1905) and the *System of Medicine* edited by Clifford Allbutt (1907). For ongoing references, researchers consult the *Index Medicus* and *Centralbl. fuer Bakter. u. Parasitenk*.

### Literature and Humanities
Bibliographies are frequently used to track criticism and translations of specific authors. For example:
* **Lucian of Samosata:** Engelmann's *Scriptores Graeci* (1880) provides an exhaustive bibliography of earlier literature on Lucian.
* **Lord Macaulay:** G. Körting's *Grd. der engl.Literatur* (1905) contains a bibliography of German criticism regarding Macaulay.
* **Alphonse Daudet:** A bibliography of Daudet's work was provided by J. Brivois in 1895.
* **Ballads:** Professor Child's *English and Scottish Popular Ballads* (1897-1898) includes a bibliography of ballad collections containing approximately four hundred titles.

## General Literary Histories
Broad bibliographies also categorize general histories of literature. Examples include Hallam's *Introduction to the Lit. of Europe* (1838-1839), W.J. Courthorpe's *History of English Poetry* (1895-1905), and J.J. Jusserand's *Histoire litteraire du peuple anglais* (1904). These works often categorize literature by era, such as the Augustan Ages or the Elizabethan period, and may include specialized bibliographies on foreign influences, such as Mary A. Scott's bibliography of Elizabethan translations from the Italian (1895).
