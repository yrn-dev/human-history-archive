# Defending the Cause of Liberty Against a Perpetual Dictator

The concept of defending liberty against the imposition of perpetual dictatorial power appears across various historical contexts, ranging from the political struggles of early modern Europe and the Americas to the governance of antiquity. These instances often involve the tension between centralized executive authority and the desires of republicans or citizens for personal and civic freedom.

## Political Opposition in Early Modern England
In the early 18th century, the struggle against perceived dictatorial power manifested in the political climate of England. Following the signing of the Treaty of Utrecht on March 31, 1713, the Whigs used the first production of Addison's play *Cato* as a means of demonstrating indignation against the peace. During this period, Bolingbroke presented the actor Booth with a purse of fifty guineas. This gesture was specifically intended to honor Booth for "defending the cause of liberty against a perpetual dictator," with the dictator in this instance being Marlborough.

## Dictatorial Power in the Americas
In the 19th century, Simón Bolívar's administration of freed provinces led to significant conflict regarding the nature of executive power. While a constituent congress in February 1825 had previously invested Bolívar with dictatorial power for one year due to the unsettled state of the country, his later proposals for the government of Bolivia caused alarm. 

On May 25, 1826, Bolívar presented a project for a constitution that proposed lodging executive authority in a president for life. This president would be without responsibility and possess the power to nominate his own successor. This proposal excited lively apprehensions among republicans in Chile and Buenos Aires, who viewed it as a threat to liberty. Furthermore, in Peru, Bolívar faced accusations that he intended to unite Peru, Colombia, and Bolivia into a single state to render himself the perpetual dictator of that confederacy.

## Liberty and Governance in Antiquity
The archives record various forms of perpetual authority and the defense of liberty in the ancient world. In Rome, Caius Cæsar was declared perpetual dictator, a position from which he spoke regarding the rights and privileges of Hyrcanus, the high priest and ethnarch of the Jews.

In Greece, the Boeotians asserted their role in defending the liberty of Hellas against the Mede. They highlighted their participation in the action at Artemisium and their cooperation with Pausanias and the Lacedaemonians, noting that they had contributed to these Hellenic exploits in a proportion that exceeded their own strength.

## Civic Duty and Popular Liberty in France
In the 19th century, the defense of liberty also occurred within the legislative bodies of France. Daru, who had served as secretary of state and handled the complex affairs of the French empire under Napoleon, later became a member of the Chamber of Peers following the second Restoration. In this capacity, Daru utilized his position to defend the cause of popular liberty against attacks launched by the ultra-royalists.
