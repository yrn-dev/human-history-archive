# Bombay and the East India Company

The development of Bombay as a primary center of English power in India was a gradual process involving transitions between the British crown and the East India Company, as well as various territorial and administrative shifts during the 17th and 18th centuries.

## Early Acquisition and Transfer
English presence in the Bombay presidency began in 1618 with the establishment of a factory at Surat by the East India Company, protected by a charter from Emperor Jahangir. While the English and Dutch made an unsuccessful attempt to seize the island of Bombay in 1626, the British crown eventually acquired the island in 1661. It was ceded by Portugal as part of the dower of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. Despite the acquisition, the British did not actually occupy the island until 1665, facing significant opposition from the Portuguese and religious orders.

Due to the poor administration of crown officers and a lack of esteem for the acquisition in England, the crown transferred Bombay to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10. At this time, the Company was granted powers for the administration of justice and the island's defense.

## Administrative Rise and the Year 1687
Initially, the East India Company placed Bombay under the factory of Surat. However, the settlement's strategic importance grew. In 1687, Bombay was placed at the head of all the Company's possessions in India, and the presidency was removed from Surat. The Company viewed Bombay as an independent settlement and the seat of English trade and power in the East Indies.

This prominence lasted until 1753, when the government of Bombay became subordinate to that of Calcutta. Later, in 1773, an act of parliament established that the presidency of Bengal (with its seat at Calcutta) would exercise control over all other Company possessions, including Bombay.

## Development under Gerald Aungier
The modern foundation of the city is attributed to Gerald Aungier, who succeeded Sir George Oxenden as president of Surat in 1669 and moved his headquarters to Bombay in 1672. Aungier faced numerous threats, including the Dutch, the Mahrattas, and Malabar pirates. He also had to contend with the Portuguese, who occupied the island of Salsette and maintained a customs-barrier in the channel.

Aungier fortified the town and shore and raised a force of regulars, primarily Germans. He implemented enlightened administrative and fiscal reforms, including:
* Commuting the burdensome land tax for a fixed money payment with the consent of a general assembly of the people's chief representatives.
* Protecting all castes in their religious ceremonies.
* Forbidding the forced labor of natives.

These policies led to rapid population growth, increasing from 10,000 to 60,000 within eight years. The city became a haven for Armenians, Parsees, and the Hindu capitalist class (banya).

## Internal Conflict and External Threats
Following Aungier's death on June 30, 1677, the settlement's position became precarious. Under the weak administration of Rolt (1677–1682), Bombay's waters became a battleground between the navies of the Mahrattas and the Siddi admirals of the Moguls.

The Company also faced internal instability due to the "niggardly policy" of the board of directors, which prioritized dividends over military rule. This led to a mutiny in 1674 and a more significant rebellion in 1683 led by Richard Keigwin. Keigwin, a naval officer, was elected governor by popular vote and declared the government to be under the immediate authority of the crown, citing the "oppressions, exactions, and intolerable extortions" of the Company. Keigwin reformed taxation and increased trade by admitting "interlopers," but he eventually surrendered the fort to Sir John Child after receiving a royal order.

## Later Expansion and Naval Importance
The English long recognized Bombay's value as a naval base. This importance was furthered by the construction of a dock in 1750 and a second in 1762. The city's fortifications were strengthened during the French wars of 1744–1748 and 1756–1763.

Territorial growth continued through treaties and conflict. In 1782, the treaty of Salbai resulted in the cession of Salsette to the British. By 1800, Surat had been annexed, and following the second Mahratta war ending in 1803, the East India Company received the districts of Kaira and Broach.

The city's population fluctuated due to disease, such as plague and cholera caused by the silting of creeks, but grew steadily through administrative reforms and migration. By 1744, the population reached 70,000, and by 1780, it had grown to 113,000. This growth was further accelerated by the famine of 1803, which drove people from the Deccan and Konkan to the city.
