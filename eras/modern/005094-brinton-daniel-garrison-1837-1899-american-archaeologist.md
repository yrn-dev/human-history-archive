# BRINTON, DANIEL GARRISON (1837-1899)

Daniel Garrison Brinton was an American ethnologist and archaeologist born on May 13, 1837, in Thornbury, Pennsylvania. His career spanned medicine, linguistics, and the scientific study of indigenous peoples of the Americas.

## Education and Early Career
Brinton graduated from Yale in 1858. Following his graduation, he spent two years studying at the Jefferson Medical College. He then traveled in Europe for one year, continuing his academic pursuits in Heidelberg and Paris. 

During the American Civil War, from 1862 to 1865, Brinton served as a surgeon in the Union army. Between 1864 and 1865, he held the position of surgeon in charge of the U.S. Army general hospital located at Quincy, Illinois. In the years following the war, he practiced medicine in Westchester, Pennsylvania.

## Professional Appointments and Leadership
Brinton transitioned into editorial and academic roles in the latter half of the 19th century. From 1874 to 1887, he served as the editor of the *Medical and Surgical Reporter*, a weekly periodical based in Philadelphia. 

His academic career included two major professorships in Philadelphia:
* In 1884, he became a professor of archaeology and ethnology at the Academy of Natural Sciences.
* In 1886, he was appointed professor of archaeology and American linguistics at the University of Pennsylvania, a position he held until his death on July 31, 1899.

Brinton was active in several learned societies across Europe and the United States. He served as president of the American Association for the Advancement of Science, the American Folk-Lore Society, and the Numismatic and Antiquarian Society of Philadelphia.

## Literary and Scientific Contributions
Starting with his first book in 1859, Brinton authored a score of books, along with numerous brochures, magazine articles, pamphlets, and addresses. He is noted for editing and publishing the *Library of American Aboriginal Literature* (8 vols. 1882-1890), a work considered a valuable contribution to American anthropology; Brinton edited six of the eight volumes.

His principal published works include:
* *The Myths of the New World* (1868): This work represented the first attempt to correlate and analyze the mythology of American Indians using true scientific principles.
* *The Religious Sentiment: Its Sources and Aim: A Contribution to the Science and Philosophy of Religion* (1876).
* *American Hero Myths* (1882).
* *Essays of an Americanist* (1890).
* *Races and Peoples* (1890).
* *The American Race* (1891).
* *The Pursuit of Happiness* (1893).
* *Religions of Primitive People* (1897).

Additionally, Brinton authored the *Annals of the Cakchiquels*, a study regarding a tribe of Mayan stock in Guatemala.

## Theoretical Perspectives
In his research on mythology, Brinton addressed specific figures such as Michabo. He argued that "Michabo" should be translated as "the Great White One," referring to the light of the dawn, and maintained that the term had nothing to do with "Great Hare," despite the beliefs of the Algonkins and the theriomorphic nature of the myth's earth-maker.
