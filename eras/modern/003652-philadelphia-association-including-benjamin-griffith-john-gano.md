# Philadelphia Association

The Philadelphia Association was a delegated body of Baptist churches formed in 1707. Its primary purpose was to consult on matters that were lacking within the churches and to set them in order. From its beginning, the association was highly influential in several key areas, including the promotion of Baptist co-operation in educational and missionary work, the maintenance of sound doctrine, and efforts to supply churches with suitable ministers while silencing those deemed unworthy.

## Origins and Early Development
The foundation of the association was preceded by the arrival of Baptists in the Quaker colonies, who were attracted by the region's large measure of religious liberty. These arrivals came from England, Wales, and New England. Early church foundations included a Baptist church at Cold Spring in Bucks county, Pennsylvania, established around 1684 through the efforts of Thomas Dungan, an Irish Baptist minister. In 1688, Elias Keach—son of the English evangelist Benjamin Keach—helped form the Pennepek church. 

While services were held in Philadelphia under the auspices of the Pennepek church starting in 1687, the city did not achieve independent organization until 1698. This church eventually became the owner of the Keithian meeting-house after several Keithian Quakers united with the congregation. General meetings among the churches of these colonies had occurred almost from the start, leading to the formal creation of the Philadelphia Association in 1707. Additionally, Sabbatarianism appeared within the association's bounds early on, resulting in the formation of Seventh-day Baptist churches from 1705 onward.

## Missionary Influence and Expansion
The Philadelphia Association played a significant role in strengthening the Baptist cause in the southern colonies, particularly in North Carolina and Virginia, where the cause had previously been feeble. This was achieved through the work of missionaries from the churches of the Philadelphia Association. Notable individuals involved in these efforts included:
* Benjamin Griffith
* John Gano (1727–1804)
* John Thomas
* Benjamin Miller
* Samuel Eaton
* John Garrard
* David Thomas

Under the influence of these missionaries, several churches were either formed or reformed and subsequently united with the association. This group of churches later formed the Ketockton Association in 1776.

## Relation to "Separate" and "Regular" Baptists
The Baptist movement in the south was characterized by a distinction between "Regular" Baptists of the Philadelphia type and "Separate" Baptists. The "Separate" Baptists, including evangelists such as Shubael Stearns and Daniel Marshall, were characterized by great enthusiasm and a strong opposition to state control in religious matters. Because of this, they were often unwilling to obtain licenses for their meetings, leading to sharp conflicts with the Virginia colonial government.

Despite these differences, the two groups eventually sought harmony. The "Separate" Baptists adopted the Philadelphia Confession of Faith—a Calvinistic document with provisions against overly rigid construction—as a means of preserving harmony and moving toward alignment with the "Regular" Baptists. By 1780, the "Regular" Baptists were providing hearty co-operation to the "Separate" Baptists in their struggle for religious equality and the entire separation of church and state.
