# Finance and Administration of Crown and Imperial Colonies

The administration of the colonial empire is organized into over fifty distinct governments, categorized into three primary classes of colonies and various dependencies. These entities may sometimes be associated into federated groups for administrative purposes. The classification of these colonies determines the level of control the imperial government exerts over legislation, public officers, and finance.

## Classification of Colonies and Governance
The three classes of colonies are defined by their level of autonomy and the nature of their government:

*   **Crown Colonies:** In these territories, the crown maintains entire control over legislation. Public officers are under the control of the home government. Legislation is typically conducted by the governor with the concurrence of a council nominated by the crown. However, in Gibraltar and St Helena, the governor alone may make laws. These councils are categorized by their origin: in colonies acquired by settlement, the council is created under imperial or local laws (such as in Jamaica), whereas in those acquired by cession or conquest, the council's authority rests wholly on the crown (such as in Ceylon).
*   **Representative Colonies:** These colonies possess representative institutions but lack responsible government. The crown holds a veto on legislation, and the home government retains control of public officers. Laws are made by the governor with the concurrence of legislative bodies; in cases where a second chamber exists, at least one body must have a preponderance of elected representatives. Examples include the Bahamas, Barbados, and Bermuda, which have both elected and nominated bodies, and Malta and the Leeward Islands, which have a single partly elected and partly nominated body.
*   **Responsible Colonies:** These colonies possess both representative institutions and responsible government. Legislation is carried out via parliamentary means similar to the home government, featuring a cabinet responsible to parliament. The crown retains a veto on legislation, exercised at the governor's discretion for certain bills. The home government has no control over public officers except for the governor.

## Financial Control and Responsibility
The financial organization of the empire varies strictly according to the colony's classification:

In crown colonies, finance is under the direct control of the imperial government. In representative colonies, while not under direct control, finances are usually influenced by the opinion of the imperial government during important departures. In responsible colonies, finance is entirely under local control. Consequently, the imperial government is dissociated from any material or moral responsibility regarding the debts of responsible colonies.

## Imperial Defence and Fiscal Evolution
Land defence has historically been linked to the internal growth of the empire. Initially, early American colonies were expected to provide for their own land defence. However, the costs of the French wars eventually exceeded local resources. In 1755, Great Britain intervened with troops, and the imperial exchequer bore the cost of North American defence until the American War. To fund this, the imperial parliament claimed the right to tax the American colonies, including the 1765 Quartering Act which placed 10,000 imperial troops in the colonies.

Following the American War, the imperial authorities assumed charge of land defences for the empire, with the exception of the Hudson Bay territories and India, where trading companies paid their own expenses. This arrangement, where the taxpayers of the United Kingdom bore the cost of the navy and imperial defence, lasted until the end of the Napoleonic Wars. By the middle of the 19th century, fiscal changes granted self-governing colonies command of their own resources, leading to the expectation that colonial exchequers provide for their own governing expenses, including defence.

## Trade and Preferential Systems
At the 1887 Colonial Conference in London, a proposal was made to establish a preferential system that would impose a 2% duty on all foreign goods to maintain the imperial navy. This required the denunciation of certain foreign treaties, such as those with Belgium and Germany, which were eventually denounced in 1897.

There grew a perception that increasing Great Britain's foreign trade in manufactured goods would enlarge home markets for colonial raw materials. This sentiment was expressed at the 1894 Ottawa Colonial Conference. Canada led this movement in 1897 by introducing an imperial tariff that granted a 25% deduction on customs duties for British goods, an advantage also offered to other British colonies. Later, New Zealand, Australia, and the South African states also granted preferential treatment to British goods.
