# Kumasi

Kumasi, also known as Coomasie, is the capital of Ashanti in British West Africa. It is situated on a low rocky eminence that extends across a valley to an opposite hill, located in a clearing within the dense forest that covers much of Ashanti. The town occupies an area approximately 1½ miles in length and over 3 miles in circumference. Its name, more correctly Kum-ase, means "under the okum tree," referring to the number of such trees found in its streets.

## Geography and Climate
The town is located at 6° 34´ 50´´ N., 2° 12´ W. It is positioned 120 miles north-north-west of Cape Coast by road and 168 miles north of Sekondi by rail. The small river Dah, a headstream of the Prah, flows to the north-west, and the land immediately surrounding the town was formerly marshy before being drained. Approximately 25 miles south-east of the city lies Lake Busumchwi, the sacred lake of the Ashanti, which is nearly circular with a maximum diameter of 6 miles and surrounded by forest-clad hills reaching 800 feet.

The climate of Kumasi is less unhealthy than that of the West African coast towns, though it is considered unsuited for the prolonged residence of Europeans. The mean annual rainfall is 40 inches, with a rainy season lasting from late May until October, and the mean temperature is 76 degrees F.

## The Conflict of 1873-1874
War broke out between the British and the Ashanti due to two primary causes. The principal cause was the transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti considered the Elmina their subjects and the fort to be Ashanti territory; the king of Ashanti had received an annual payment (a "custom-note") from the Dutch, which the Dutch viewed as a trade present but the Ashanti viewed as ground rent. A secondary cause was the captivity of four Europeans—Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife, his infant son (who died shortly after capture), Mr. J. Kuhne, and a French trader, Monsieur M.J. Bonnat—who had been taken to Kumasi.

On January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force invaded the British protectorate, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition led by Sir Garnet Wolseley. Wolseley landed at Cape Coast on October 2, finding the Ashanti forces decimated by fever and smallpox. While Captain John Glover cooperated from the east with native levies, Wolseley marched to Kumasi.

Upon reaching the city in 1874, the British found a town that bore marks of a high state of culture. The city featured broad, regular streets, with a main avenue 70 yards wide, a large market-place to the south-east, and a "Spirit House" used for executions. The king's palace, built of red sandstone and believed to be modeled on Dutch buildings at Elmina, contained valuable goldsmiths' work and beaten gold masks showing Moorish influence. However, on February 6, 1874, Wolseley's forces blew up the palace.

## Subsequent History and Administration
Following the 1874 war, the Ashanti deposed King Kofi Karikari and installed his brother, Mensa, on the "golden stool," the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's rule was characterized by tyranny and human sacrifice. After a revolution in 1883 and a subsequent civil war, Prince Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III) became king on March 26, 1888.

Tensions returned in 1893 when Prempeh closed roads to traders. After Prempeh declined to accept a British protectorate or a resident at Kumasi, he was deported. A British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart, was installed, and the town was administered by chiefs under British guidance. A fort was built in Kumasi to house the Gold Coast constabulary.

In 1900, a serious rebellion broke out involving the Kumasi, Adansi, and Kokofu tribes. The conflict was sparked in part by Governor Sir F. Hodgson's demand that the chiefs pay 4,000 oz. of gold yearly and his reproaches regarding the hidden golden stool. The rebels attacked the fort, where Sir F. and Lady Hodgson and Basel missionaries took refuge. The garrison was eventually reinforced by Lagos constabulary and forces under Major A. Morris, reaching a total of 700 men.

## Infrastructure and Economy
Historically, Kintampo served as the commercial capital and a meeting place for caravan routes, which limited Kumasi's early population growth (estimated not to have exceeded 20,000 in the first quarter of the 19th century). However, the arrival of the railway from the coast in September 1903, passing through the Obuassi and Tarkwa gold-fields, led to a rapid expansion. By 1906, Kumasi became the distributing centre for all of Ashanti, with a population of 6,280 (over 12,000 including suburbs).

Modern communications include a 141-mile wide road from Cape Coast, telegraph lines, and a well-organized postal service. The most imposing modern structure is the fort built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner. The town also hosts European and native hospitals and stations for the Wesleyan and Basel missions. While native houses are built of red clay, some principal Ashanti chiefs own European-style houses which they lease to strangers.
