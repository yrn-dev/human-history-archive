## Political Upheaval in Austria (1848)
In March 1848, events in Austria progressed rapidly. On March 11, "young Czechs" in Prague drafted a petition for liberal and nationalist demands. Simultaneously, the diet of Lower Austria petitioned the crown to summon delegates to organize Austrian finances, a proposal the government accepted the following day.

The situation in Vienna escalated quickly. A mob of students and workmen invaded the hall of the diet, adopting the proposals of Kossuth's speech as a popular programme. The members of the diet were forced into a tumultuous procession to the Hofburg to demand government assent to a petition based on the "catch-words of the Revolution." While authorities initially temporized, riots broke out around the diet hall, leading to military intervention and bloodshed. As the middle classes joined the rebels, the riots evolved into a revolution. Consequently, on the evening of March 13, Metternich escaped the Hofburg and went into exile in England.

Later conflict returned to Vienna, pitting the radical populace against the government and the Slav majority of the Reichsrath. Although German democrats sought aid from the Hungarian government, the Magyar focus on constitutional legality caused delays. By October 7, Emperor Ferdinand fled from Schoenbrunn to the Slav district of Olmuetz, issuing a proclamation for those who loved "freedom" and "Austria" to rally to the throne. On October 11, Windischgraetz announced his march against Vienna, and by October 16, he was appointed commander-in-chief of all Austrian armies except those in Italy. By October 20, the Slav majority and members of the Right had left Vienna for a meeting of the diet at Bruenn, leaving only a rump of German radicals, students, and the proletariat in the capital. Despite a defense organized by Bern, an ex-officer of Napoleon, the city fell to Windischgraetz on November 1 after his attack began on October 28.

## Military Campaigns and Conflicts
### Ancient Greece
In another historical account, Pelopidas marched into Thessaly against the despot Alexander, who held the Phthiot Achæans, the Magnetes, and a large portion of Thessaly in dependence, with Athens as an ally. Despite an eclipse of the sun and warnings from prophets that intimidated his fellow-citizens, Pelopidas proceeded to Pharsalus. 

At the hills called Kynos Kephalæ (the Dog’s Heads), Pelopidas's cavalry first beat the enemy cavalry. However, this left the hills open to the enemy's superior infantry, who repulsed Pelopidas's own infantry. Pelopidas then ordered his cavalry to charge the infantry on the hill in flank while he personally led a third, successful attack on the summit. This forced the enemy army to retire in disorder, though Alexander personally attempted to rally them on the right wing.

In a separate conflict, the Athenians invested Potidæa. After raising works against the wall on the isthmus, Athens sent sixteen hundred heavy infantry under Phormio, son of Asopius, to Pallene. Phormio fixed headquarters at Aphytis and ravaged the country. Once the investment was complete, Aristeus advised most residents to sail out to preserve provisions. Aristeus himself eluded Athenian guardships to continue the war among the Chalcidians, where he cut off many Sermylians in an ambuscade and sought help from the Peloponnese. Phormio subsequently used his forces to take some towns and ravage Bottica and Chalcidice.

### 19th Century Burma and World War I
In February 1825, Sir Archibald Campbell advanced on Prome. He moved in two divisions on February 13, with General Willoughby Cotton leading a flotilla toward Danubyu. Following the failure of the attack on Danubyu, Campbell began a retrograde march on March 11. By April 2, English forces entered the entrenchments at Danubyu without resistance after Bandula was killed by a bomb explosion. Campbell entered Prome on April 25. During that summer, General Joseph Morrison conquered Arakan, the Burmese were expelled from Assam, and the British progressed in Cachar.

In a later era, during a German Aisne offensive starting May 27, forces advanced rapidly toward Paris and the River Marne. Marshal Foch utilized the Third Division, whose motorized machine-gun battalion held the bridgehead opposite Château-Thierry. The Second Division was sent by motor trucks to check the enemy's progress, retaking the railroad station and town of Bouresches. This was followed by the battle of Belleau Wood and the capture of the village of Vaux on July 1.

## Geographical Exploration and Urban Growth
### The Far East
Geographical research into the route between China and Burma progressed rapidly after 1874. Following the death of Augustus Margary, several experts explored the region for commercial development. Captain William Gill traveled across China to Burma and eastern Tibet, while E.C. Baber explored western provinces between 1877 and 1878. Archibald Colquhoun wandered from Talifu to Peking in 1881, and Mark Bell illustrated ancient trade routes in 1887. 

The acquisition of Burma further extended surveys. Prince Henri d'Orleans explored the sources of the Mekong, Salween, and Irrawaddy. James M'Carthy mapped Siam, while Warrington Smyth and Curzon studied its coast. Between 1900 and 1904, C.C. Manifold mapped the upper basin of the Yang-tsze river.

### Greenock
The Scottish village of Greenock evolved from a 17th-century fishing village of thatched cottages into a prosperous burgh. After being erected as a burgh of barony by Charles I in 1635, its growth was accelerated by the Treaty of Union (1707), which developed trade with the West Indies and America. Despite a brief suspension during the American War of Independence, shipping trebled between 1783 and 1790. By 1832, Greenock became a parliamentary burgh.

## Literary Developments in England
In England, a more flexible prose developed to serve multiplying magazines, journals, and critical reviews. These publications exploited a "masculine interest" in natural philosophy, topography, the picturesque, and history. Simultaneously, circulating libraries grew to meet a "feminine passion" for fiction, memoirs, and poetry. Notable figures in this era of authorship included Fanny Burney, daughter of Dr. Burney, and James Boswell, described as a biographical genius.
