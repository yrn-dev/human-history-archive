# Willie's Lady

In the study of early popular poetry and ballads, the depiction of luxury and material splendor often manifests through the use of precious metals. In the ballad *Willie's Lady*, this is exemplified by the description of the lady's steed, which is described as being "splendidly accoutred." The horse is specifically noted as being "Silver shod before, And gowden shod behind."

## Comparative Luxury in Early Epics and Ballads
The use of gold and silver in *Willie's Lady* reflects a broader pattern of "primitive luxury" found in early epics and folk songs. Similar motifs appear in the *Chanson de Roland* and the works of Homer, the latter of which may reflect the actual radiance of "golden Mycenae." 

Other examples of this thematic element in popular verse include:
*   A song from Poitou featuring a rustic with a *faucille d'or* (golden sickle).
*   A variant of Hugh of Lincoln that introduces tables and chairs made of gold.
*   A ballad shared by Scotland and France in which a lover uses "ciseaux d'or fin" (fine gold scissors) to cut a winding-sheet from a living bride.
*   Romaic ballads, which describe the horses of the Klephts as being gold shod.

## Context of Border Ballads
*Willie's Lady* exists within a tradition of poetry that includes Border songs and ballads. This genre includes works such as "Kinmont Willie," "Jamie Telfer," "Jock o' the Side," and "The Bonny Earl o' Murray." 

While Gaston Paris suggests that early popular poetry is contemporary with the facts it describes and is improvised, other analysis indicates that historical Border ballads—particularly those from 1595-1600—often confuse or distort facts. These works may have been composed in later generations based on erroneous oral traditions or defaced by the interpolations of reciters. It is probable that the existing form of most Border ballads dates no earlier than the generation of 1603-1633, following the union of the crowns. Some reciters may have also been influenced by printed broadsheets or "stall copies."

## Related Motifs in Folk Tradition
Beyond the depiction of luxury, early popular poetry frequently employs specific recurring motifs. One such motif is the presence of talking birds, which cause no surprise to human listeners. Examples include:
*   The "omniscient popinjay" found in Border minstrelsy.
*   The *tria poulakia* (three little birds) that introduce stories in Romaic ballads.
*   The wise birds whose speech is understood by exceptionally gifted Zulus.
*   A wicked dove in French folk-songs that whispers temptation.
*   The bird in *Water o' Wearies Well* that emerges from a bush to dine on water.

Additionally, popular verse in "happier climes," such as southern Italy and France, often focuses on themes of nature, including apple blossom, rose trees, and the nightingale.
