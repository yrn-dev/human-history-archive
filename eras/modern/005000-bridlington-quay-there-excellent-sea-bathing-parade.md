# Bridlington

Bridlington is a seaside resort, municipal borough, and market town located in the East Riding of Yorkshire, England. It is situated within the Buckrose parliamentary division, approximately 31 miles north-northeast of Hull, and is accessible via a branch of the North Eastern railway. As of 1891, the population was 8,919, increasing to 12,482 by 1901. The municipal borough covers an area of 2,751 acres and is governed by a mayor, 18 councillors, and 6 aldermen.

## Geography and Layout
The town is divided into two distinct sections. The ancient market town is located about one mile from the coast. In contrast, the modern houses of Bridlington Quay, which serves as the watering-place, fringe the shore of Bridlington Bay. The coastline to the south is low, while the coast to the north is steep and very fine, featuring the eastward projection of the great spur of Flamborough Head.

## Bridlington Quay and Tourism
Bridlington Quay is noted for providing excellent sea-bathing. The area features ornamental gardens and a parade that offer pleasant promenades for visitors. Extensive works have been carried out along the sea front to improve the area. Additionally, the town possesses a chalybeate spring. The harbor is protected by two stone piers, and the bay provides good anchorage.

## History and Governance
The Domesday survey mentions four burgesses at Bridlington (referred to as Brellington or Burlington), indicating it was a borough prior to the Norman Conquest. During the reign of Edward the Confessor, the town was part of the possessions of Earl Morcar and had a value of £32; however, following the ravages of the Normans, its value decreased to 8s. by the time of the Domesday survey, at which point it was held by the king due to the forfeiture of Earl Morcar.

William II granted the town to Gilbert de Gaunt. Gilbert's son and heir, Walter de Gaunt, founded the priory around 1114 and endowed it with other lands and the manor of Bridlington. The town's importance grew steadily from this period, with several kings confirming the gift. King Stephen further granted the right to have a port. In 1446, Henry VI granted the prior three new annual fairs. Previously, in 1200, King John had granted a weekly Saturday market and an annual fair on the vigil, feast, and morrow of the Assumption of the Virgin Mary.

In 1546, Henry IV granted the convent and prior exemption from subsidies, tenths, and fifteenths in exchange for prayers for himself and his queen at every mass sung at the high altar. Following the Dissolution, the manor remained with the crown until 1624, when Charles I granted it to Sir John Ramsey. In 1633, Sir George Ramsey (brother and heir to John) sold the manor to thirteen inhabitants of the town on behalf of all manor tenants. These thirteen lords were assisted by twelve other inhabitants chosen by freeholders. When the number of lords eventually fell to six, seven others were chosen from the assistants, and a chief lord was elected annually. This system of governance persists. While this arrangement suggests a form of incorporation in the 17th century, the official incorporation charter was not granted until 1899, when it became a municipal borough.

## Religious and Civic Architecture
The old town contains the church of St Mary and St Nicholas. This structure consists of the priory church of an Augustinian foundation from the time of Henry I, featuring an Early English portion and a fine Perpendicular and Decorated nave. The priory flourished until 1537, ending when the last prior was executed for his involvement in the Pilgrimage of Grace. A Perpendicular gateway remains and serves as the town-hall. Additionally, a Congregational society was established in 1662; its old church, built in 1702, remained until 1906.
