# Bampton Lectures and Related Theological Works

## The Bampton Lectures
The Bampton Lectures are a series of annual lectures delivered at the University Church in Oxford. The series was established by the will of John Bampton (c. 1690–1751), an English divine and former canon of Salisbury who graduated M.A. from Trinity College, Oxford, in 1712. Bampton died on June 2, 1751, at the age of 61.

The lectures are delivered on Sunday mornings during full term, specifically between the commencement of the last month in Lent term and the end of the third week in Act term. According to the directions in Bampton's will, the lectures must address specific subjects, including:
* The confirmation and establishment of the Christian faith and the confutation of schismatics and heretics.
* The divine authority of the Holy Scriptures.
* The authority of the writings of the primitive fathers regarding the faith and practice of the primitive Church.
* The divinity of the Holy Ghost and the divinity of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ.
* The articles of the Christian faith as found in the Nicene and Apostles' Creeds.

## Administration and Requirements
The lecturer must hold at least a Master of Arts degree from either Cambridge or Oxford. Historically, the lecturer was chosen yearly by the heads of colleges on the fourth Tuesday in Easter term. A rule exists stating that no individual can be chosen to deliver the lectures a second time.

The series began in 1780. While the lectures continued, elections shifted to alternate years starting in 1895 due to a depreciation of the fund's revenue. The endowment provides £120 for each lecturer, and there is a requirement that the lectures be published within two months of their delivery.

## Related Theological Figures and Works
William Sanday, who delivered the 1893 Bampton Lecture on *Inspiration*, is further identified as the Lady Margaret Professor of Divinity and Canon of Christ Church, Oxford. He is also a Fellow of the British Academy, an Hon. Fellow of Exeter College, Oxford, and a Chaplain in Ordinary to the King. His other works include a *Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans*.

Other theological writings mentioned in the archive include those by Athanasius (293–373), the bishop of Alexandria and saint. Before the Arian controversy, Athanasius wrote two essays: *Against the Gentiles* and *On the Incarnation*.

Additionally, the archive notes the existence of other theological texts, such as the *Kompendium der Dogmatik* (9th ed. 1893) and *Geschichte der christlichen Ethik* (1888–1893).

## Historical Theological Context
Theological history has been marked by significant disputes, particularly following the extinction of paganism. After the disputes regarding the Trinity, the church faced conflicts concerning the Incarnation. These disputes involved the human and divine nature of Christ and led to enmity between the Patriarchs of Constantinople and Alexandria. 

Key events and figures in these conflicts include St. Cyril, Nestorius, and the Fourth General Council of Chalcedon and Third General Council of Ephesus. These tensions resulted in the emergence of various Oriental sects, including the Jacobites, the Nestorians, the Copts and Abyssinians, the Maronites, and the Armenians.
