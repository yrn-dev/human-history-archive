# Nasir Khan and the Khans of Kalat

The history of the Kalat state is marked by the leadership of several figures named Nasir Khan, whose reigns involved complex diplomatic relations with the Persian, Afghan, and British empires, as well as internal struggles for sovereignty.

## Nasir Khan I
Nasir Khan, the second son of Abdulla Khan, rose to power in 1739. He had previously accompanied Nadir Shah of Persia to Delhi, where he earned the monarch's confidence. Upon returning to Kalat, he found his brother and predecessor, Haji Mahommed Khan, ruling as a tyrant who alienated the population through licentious behavior and oppressive taxation. After attempts to reason with his brother failed, Nasir Khan stabbed Haji Mahommed Khan to death.

Following this act, Nasir Khan was hailed as a deliverer by the population and ascended the *musnud*. He informed Nadir Shah of the events, and the shah responded by issuing a *firman* appointing Nasir Khan as the *beglar begi* (prince of princes) of all Baluchistan.

## Governance and Conflict with Kabul
Described as a warlike, politic, and active prince, Nasir Khan I focused on fortifying the city of Kalat and re-establishing internal government across his provinces. Following the death of Nadir Shah in 1747, he acknowledged Ahmad Shah (Durani), the king of Kabul. However, in 1758, Nasir Khan declared his total independence.

This declaration led Ahmad Shah to dispatch a minister with a military force, which Nasir Khan's army totally routed. In response, the king marched with strong reinforcements and defeated Nasir Khan in a pitched battle. Nasir Khan retired to Kalat, where he mounted a vigorous defense against the royal army's attempts to take the city by storm or surprise.

The conflict ended in a treaty of peace. The terms stipulated that the king would marry Nasir Khan's cousin and that the khan would pay no tribute. Instead, Nasir Khan agreed to provide troops to assist the king's armies upon request, for which he received a cash allowance equal to half of the troops' pay. Nasir Khan later distinguished himself in the wars of Kabul, and the king rewarded him with several districts in entire and perpetual sovereignty.

## Succession and Decline
Nasir Khan I died in June 1795 at an extreme old age, having previously quelled a dangerous rebellion led by his cousin, Behram Khan. He left behind five daughters and three sons. He was succeeded by his eldest son, Mahmud Khan, who was approximately fourteen years old at the time. Mahmud Khan is described as an indolent and humane man. Under his reign, the dominions of the khans of Kalat diminished significantly as governors of various districts withdrew their allegiance and the country was plagued by sanguinary broils.

## Nasir Khan II and British Relations
In 1839, the British army advanced through the Bolan Pass toward Afghanistan. The ruler of Baluchistan at the time, Mehrab Khan, was accused of treachery, leading General Willshire to assault Kalat with 1,050 men. The town and citadel were stormed, resulting in the death of Mehrab Khan and over 400 Baluches, with 2,000 prisoners taken. Subsequent inquiries revealed that the treachery—specifically the plundering of British convoys in the Bolan Pass and Kach Gandava—was actually the work of the vizier, Mahommed Hussein, and certain chiefs who sought to ruin Mehrab Khan to seize power.

In 1841, the British recognized Nasir Khan II, the youthful son of Mehrab Khan, as the leader. After a period of limited diplomatic intercourse, General John Jacob was deputed by the governor-general, the marquess of Dalhousie, to arrange a treaty. This treaty, executed on May 14, 1854, annulled a previous 1841 agreement. Under the new terms, Nasir Khan II and his successors bound themselves to:
* Oppose all enemies of the British government.
* Act in subordinate co-operation with the British government.
* Refrain from negotiating with other states without British consent.
* Allow British troops to occupy positions within Kalat territory if deemed necessary.
* Prevent their subjects from plundering within or near British territory.

Nasir Khan II performed these provisions loyally until his death in 1856.

## Later Succession and Anarchy
Nasir Khan II was succeeded by his twelve-year-old brother, Mir Khodadud Khan. Khodadad Khan's early reign was marked by a rebellion of turbulent chiefs who attempted to depose him in favor of his cousin, Sher dil Khan. In 1857, the British government appointed Major Henry Green as a political agent to assist the Khan in maintaining control.

In 1863, during the absence of political agent Major Malcolm Green, Sher dil Khan attacked and severely wounded Khodadad Khan, who fled to the British border. Sher dil Khan was proclaimed Khan, but his rule was short; he was murdered in the Gandava Pass in early 1864. Khodadad Khan was then re-elected as chief. However, unable to govern his unruly subjects without British assistance—which the British were unwilling to provide—his country fell into anarchy. Because some provisions of the 1854 treaty were broken, diplomatic relations between the British and the Kalat state were discontinued after 1874.
