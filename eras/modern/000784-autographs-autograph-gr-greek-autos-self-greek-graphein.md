# AUTOGRAPHS

The term autograph is derived from the Greek words *autos* (self) and *graphein* (to write). In common usage, it refers to an independent signature, a document signed by the person from whom it emanates, or a document written entirely by the hand of that person. The latter is more technically described as a *holograph*, from the Greek *holos* (entire) and *graphein* (to write). In a restricted sense, the term is often used to imply signatures or documents written by persons of notoriety or eminence across various professions and ranks of life.

## Historical Development and Early Examples
The existence of autographs has been coeval with the invention of letters. While documents in the handwriting of their composers may possibly exist among early examples of writing in the East, such as the clay tablets of Babylonia and Assyria or early Egyptian papyri, the practice of using professional scribes and seals for authentication made autographs rare. For example, when King Darius "signed" a decree or when Jezebel wrote letters in Ahab's name, these were written by professional scribes and authenticated with the impression of a seal, which served as the equivalent of a western signature.

Certainty regarding autographs is found in the Roman and Ptolemaic periods of Egyptian history. Due to Egypt's dry atmosphere, an abundance of Greek papyrus documents from the 3rd century B.C. and later have been preserved, including original letters and personal documents from both lettered and unlettered individuals. In Italy and Greece, most ancient writings perished, though some waxen tablets and charred papyrus rolls were recovered from Pompeii and Herculaneum. These tablets are particularly valuable as they contain autograph signatures of witnesses and principals to legal deeds, as required by Roman law.

## Medieval and Early Modern Autographs
Autographs of famous men from the early middle ages are difficult to identify or rarely exist, with the exception of sovereigns. For instance, while a charter at Canterbury claims to be written by Dunstan, a duplicate in the British Museum makes it probable that both are copies. However, some autograph manuscripts do exist, including the chronicles of Sigebert of Gembloux, Robert de Monte, and Ordericus Vitalis, as well as the writings of Matthew of Paris (the English chronicler of Henry III's reign). Other examples include documents in the hand of William of Wykeham in the British Museum and the writings of the historian Joinville in French archives. By the 15th century, the Paston Letters—correspondence of the Norfolk family—contain numerous autographs of historical figures.

From the 16th century onward, autograph documents became more plentiful. Despite this, some notable figures left very little behind. William Shakespeare is the most famous example; the only vestiges of his handwriting are two signatures on property conveyances in Blackfriars and three signatures on the sheets of his will. Other purported signatures in books are dismissed as imitations, such as the forgeries produced by W. H. Ireland.

## The Practice of Autograph Collecting
The taste for collecting autographs as specimens of handwriting did not exist in England during Shakespeare's time. Early collections, such as those in the library of Sir Robert Cotton (now in the British Museum) or the collection of Philippe de Bethune and his son during the reign of Louis XIV, were gathered for their historical or literary interest rather than for the sake of the handwriting itself.

The practice of autograph collecting appears to have originated in the Low Countries and Germany, primarily among university students. They collected signatures and inscriptions from friends in *alba amicorum* (friendship albums), which were small, oblong pocket volumes. The British Museum holds a collection of these, the earliest beginning in 1554 (Egerton MS. 1178).

Once established, the hobby expanded from living persons to those of the past. While many famous collections were eventually dispersed, the collection of Alfred Morrison remains intact and is documented in a sumptuous catalogue containing many facsimiles.

## Published Collections and Facsimiles
Many collections of autograph facsimiles have been published, particularly in England. Notable works include:
* *British Autography* by J. Thane (1788-1793).
* *Autographs of Royal, Noble, Learned and Remarkable Personages in English History* by J. G. Nichols (1829).
* *Autographs of the Kings and Queens and Eminent Men of Great Britain* by J. Netherclift (1835).
* *The Handbook of Autographs* by F. G. Netherclift (1862).
* *The Autograph Album* by L. B. Phillips (1866).
* *Facsimiles of Autographs* published by the British Museum (1896-1900).

Official publications also include facsimiles of national manuscripts from Ireland (1874-1884), Scotland (1867-1871), and those from William the Conqueror to Queen Anne (1865-1868).
