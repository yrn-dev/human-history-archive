# Bacterial Behavior and Pathogenesis

The behavior of bacteria upon gaining entrance to a body, the effects they produce, and the circumstances that modify resulting diseases vary significantly depending on the organism and the host. While some bacteria cause widespread systemic poisoning, others remain localized or act upon specific tissues.

## Mechanisms of Infection in Animals and Humans
Bacterial invasion can manifest in several distinct patterns. In extreme instances, such as pneumococcus septicaemia in rabbits or anthrax septicaemia in guinea-pigs, bacteria introduced into subcutaneous tissue rapidly enter the blood stream. Once there, they multiply freely and release toxins that cause general poisoning. This widespread toxic action is evidenced by capillary haemorrhages and cloudy swelling in internal organs, which may progress to fatty degeneration. In humans, septicaemia is often caused by streptococci; while the process is similar, the organisms are primarily found in the capillaries of internal organs and may not be detectable in peripheral circulation during the subject's life.

Other diseases follow a pattern of secondary extension. Bacteria first create a well-marked local lesion, then spread via the blood or lymph stream to other parts of the body to form corresponding lesions, such as secondary nodules, tubercle, or abscesses. In typhoid fever, this results in the secondary invasion of mesenteric glands and the presence of bacilli clumps in internal organs, particularly the spleen, often with minimal surrounding tissue change. These cases demonstrate a selective distribution, as certain organs are more susceptible to infection than others.

A third class of diseases involves bacteria restricted to a specific area, where symptoms arise from absorbed toxins. Examples include tetanus (restricted to a wound), diphtheria (restricted to the region of the false membrane), and cholera (practically confined to the intestine). In the case of tetanus, the bacillus cannot multiply in healthy tissues and depends on the presence of other bacteria for local multiplication.

## Toxin Production and Tissue Effects
It is an accepted fact that the primary results of bacteria in tissues are due to toxins rather than mechanical actions, such as plugging capillaries in the kidneys or brain, or the consumption of blood oxygen. Toxins are categorized as either extracellular or intracellular. Extracellular toxins, such as those from diphtheria and tetanus bacilli, can be demonstrated by filtering a bouillon culture through a porcelain filter; the resulting filtrate can reproduce the disease in animals. Intracellular toxins are contained within the bacteria and are released upon their disintegration, as seen with cholera, typhoid, and tubercle organisms. These often produce non-specific results, such as fever and interference with general vital processes.

The effects of bacteria are categorized into three areas:
1. **Local tissue changes:** These occur at primary or secondary foci and include inflammation (acute, subacute, or chronic), degeneration and death of cells, leucocyte emigration, serous and fibrinous exudations, haemorrhages, and the proliferation of cells and connective tissue.
2. **Distant tissue changes:** These result from the absorption of toxins and include hyaline degeneration of small blood vessels, waxy degeneration in chronic diseases, serous effusions, and fatty degeneration. Some toxins have specific affinities; for example, tetanus causes minute alterations in the protoplasm and nucleus of nerve cells, while diphtheria affects nerve fibres and cells.

## Bacterial Action in Plants
The study of bacterial diseases in higher plants has faced scrutiny regarding whether bacteria are the primary cause of disease. While it was once thought that plant acidity provided immunity, it is now known that many bacteria flourish in acid media. Furthermore, while cell-walls were thought to be impenetrable, certain anaerobic forms can ferment cellulose, and others, such as those in Leguminosae described by Marshall Ward (1887) and Miss Dawson (1898), can penetrate the cell-walls of living cells.

Some attributed plant diseases, such as the slime fluxes of oaks or Sereh disease of sugar-cane, are considered doubtful; in these cases, bacteria are likely saprophytic forms entering rotting tissues already injured by fungi or insects. However, bacteria are certainly the primary agents in other cases, characterized by the wilting of shoots due to the stoppage of vessels. Transverse sections of these shoots (e.g., in potatoes, cucumbers, and cabbages) typically show vessels that are brown and choked with a dark yellowish slime containing bacteria.

## Study Methods and Biological Properties
The relationship between bacteria and disease is studied through three chief steps: discovering the bacterium in affected tissues via microscopy, obtaining a pure culture, and producing the disease through inoculation with that pure culture. Microscopic examination utilizes basic aniline dyes, which have an affinity for bacterial protoplasm. To enhance results, weak alkali or heat may be applied, and mordants may be used to fix the stain. Excess stain is removed using alcohol or acids. Gram's method specifically employs a solution of iodine before decolorizing.

The high activity of bacteria—including rapid growth and the resistance of spores to heat and desiccation—is attributed to their small size and isolation. This maximizes the surface area of the protoplasm in contact with the environment compared to higher organisms. This isolation facilitates the rapid exchange of gases, liquids, food materials, and the expulsion of metabolism products and enzymes.

## Bacteria in Food Production
Bacteria are present in all milk due to the external orifices of milk-ducts. Normal fluids contain bacteria that cause "turning" or souring, as well as those responsible for fermentations in cheese and butter. In American dairies, a specific bacillus is used in pure cultures to produce a fine butter flavour, and Denmark uses pure cultures to suppress rancidity. In cheese production, bacteria are involved in ripening processes, such as in Edam cheese. Additionally, the Japanese produce cheeses through the bacterial fermentation of boiled Soja beans.
