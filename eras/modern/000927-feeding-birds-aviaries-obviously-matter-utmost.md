# Aviaries and Aviculture

An aviary (from the Latin *avis*, meaning bird) is a structure used to keep birds in captivity. While the practice of keeping birds in cages is ancient, structures specifically termed aviaries were likely first utilized by the ancient Romans. During the time of Varro (116-127 B.C.), these structures, known as "ornithones," served two primary purposes: some were constructed for pleasure to house song-birds like nightingales, while others were used by poulterers to fatten birds, such as thrushes, for market or the tables of their owners. Varro maintained an aviary exclusively for song-birds, whereas Lucullus utilized a combined facility for both pleasure and delicacies.

## History and Scientific Importance
The modern practice of using aviaries for pleasure and the study of avian habits is of a more recent date, beginning with 15th-century geographical research. Explorers brought back beautiful bird forms, leading to the custom of erecting aviaries to study them at home. By the 16th century, aviaries were common in the gardens of the wealthy; Bacon mentioned them in his 1597 essay on gardening, and Elizabeth of Bohemia had an outdoor aviary at Coombe Abbey featuring a roof and back made of natural rock.

Beyond being a pastime, aviculture is of significant scientific importance. Many bird species are too shy, rare, or inhabit remote native haunts to be studied in the wild. In large, suitable aviaries, researchers can accurately observe and record courtship, display, incubation, moult, and nesting habits. To reveal these natural secrets, aviaries must be of sufficient size and design; birds in small aviaries or cages will not exhibit these behaviors.

## Design and Construction
Aviaries vary greatly in size, ranging from the "Crystal Palace aviary" (named for its shape) to structures as large as a church. Some are simply rooms in a house with wire-netting over the windows, but most are outdoor structures made of wire-netting on iron or wooden frameworks. While hardy birds only require netting and natural shelters like rock-work or shrubs, tropical species require a wooden or brick house that can be artificially warmed during inclement months.

Ideal construction includes a southern or southeastern aspect, with protection from north, north-east, and north-west winds provided by banks, high walls, or belts of fir-trees. The outer area should be turfed, planted with deciduous and evergreen shrubs, and include a gravel path. A highly useful design involves several compartments with a rear passage, allowing the aviculturist to visit one section without disturbing birds in others. For example, a four-compartment plan may feature inner houses of 10 feet square with outer flights of double that area. Water should be supplied via underground pipes to shallow basins connected to an overflow pipe and drain.

## Avian Nutrition
Feeding is a matter of utmost importance in aviculture. The aviculturist should research the wild life of a species—or a closely related species if the bird is rare—to provide food that resembles its natural diet.

### Parrots and Finches
The majority of parrots eat fruit, seed, and various nuts. Larger parrots are fed on hemp, oats, maize, canary-seed, dari, sunflower-seed, nuts, and ripe fruits. Grass-parrakeets thrive on green food (such as groundsel, chickweed, and grass in flower) and canary-seed. However, the *Loriidae* (brush-tongued parrots) naturally subsist on nectar and pollen, specifically from *Eucalyptus*. In captivity, they live well on fruit and "sweetened milk-sop" (boiling milk poured over biscuit or crumbled bread). While they may learn to eat seed, an exclusive seed diet combined with a lack of exercise can lead to fatal fits.

Finches are popular foreign aviary birds. African weaver-birds are generally hardy and eat mostly canary-seed. Australasian grass-finches and African waxbills (such as the "cordon-bleu") eat millet and canary seeds, and find wholesome food in flowering grasses.

### Insectivorous and Specialized Diets
Many birds are partially or exclusively insectivorous. Purely insectivorous species can be accustomed to "insectile" food, a composition of dried flies, dried ants' cocoons, preserved egg yolk, dried powdered meat, and bread or biscuit crumbs, moistened with water or mashed boiled potato. This staple is often supplemented with grubs, spiders, caterpillars, and mealworms.

Other specialized diets include:
* **Fruit-eaters:** Tanagers and New World sugar-birds require abundant ripe fruit alongside a staple insectile diet.
* **Waders:** These require a diet similar to insectivorous birds, supplemented with mealworms, insects, and chopped raw meat.
* **Earth-worm eaters:** Shredded raw meat is added to their diet.
* **Birds of prey:** These require a meat diet consisting of small, freshly killed birds and mammals; the feathers and fur should remain to aid digestion.

## Breeding and Nesting
Most wild birds will breed in aviaries if provided with appropriate nesting sites that imitate their natural surroundings. Species that build in holes or crevices will use small baskets or boxes fastened to the roof or walls. Others will use large bundles of brushwood in sheltered spots or build within evergreen shrubs. Parrots, which naturally scrape hollows in rocks or hollow trees without building a nest, require boxes or hollow logs with small entrance holes near the top.
