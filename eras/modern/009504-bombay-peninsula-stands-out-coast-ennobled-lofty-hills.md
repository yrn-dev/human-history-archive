# Bombay Peninsula

The Bombay Peninsula, also referred to as Bombay Island, is a landmass that extends from a coast characterized by lofty hills. Its harbor is defined by precipices and rocky islands with peaks that reach great heights. The approach to the peninsula from the sea is described as one of the finest panoramas in the world, with the Bay of Naples serving as the only European analogy.

## Physical Geography
The peninsula consists of a plain approximately 3 miles broad and 11 miles long, which is flanked by two parallel lines of low hills. A neck of land extending toward the south-west protects the harbor on its eastern side from the force of the open sea, enclosing a body of water between 5 and 7 miles wide. 

At the south-west of the island is Back Bay, a shallow basin roughly 2 miles wide that runs inland for about 3 miles between the extreme points of the two hill ranges. A slightly raised strip of land located between the harbor and the head of Back Bay serves as the site for the fort, which is the nucleus of the city of Bombay. From this point, the land slopes westward toward a low-lying central plain. Prior to the construction of the Hornby Vellard embankment, this central tract was submerged by the sea during high tide.

Additionally, the spur of the island running south is known as Kolaba Point. This point protects the entrance to the harbor and houses an observatory, a lunatic asylum, barracks for a European regiment, and the terminus of the Bombay & Baroda railway.

## The City of Bombay
The city is noted for having one of the finest water-fronts in the world. Along the esplanade facing Back Bay is a line of public offices built in a mixture of Saracenic and Gothic styles, which provide a cumulative effect of great dignity. Other prominent structures include the Victoria terminus of the Great Indian Peninsula railway and the Taj Mahal hotel. The latter, built by the Tata family in 1904, is considered the most modern and palatial hotel in India.

The town's layout includes spacious streets dedicated to European commerce and handsome native bazaars. In these bazaars, houses reach three or four storeys in height and feature front work and pillars that are elaborately carved. Some commercial buildings and European hotels are built on an American scale, possessing no rivals in any other Indian city.

European residents typically live in private houses located apart from the mercantile and native quarters. These residences are generally set within large compounds or gardens; while their architecture is less imposing than the stately homes in Calcutta, it is noted for its comfort, beauty, and suitability to the climate. Malabar hill, a high ridge running into the sea, is a favorite suburb. It is terraced with handsome houses that offer some of the finest views of their kind. Near the northern end of Malabar hill are the Parsee Towers of Silence, where the Parsees expose their dead to be devoured by vultures before casting the bones into a well to crumble into dust. In 1905, the prince of Wales laid the foundation-stone for a museum.

## Regional Context and Infrastructure
Bombay is part of the Bombay Presidency, a long strip of land along the Indian Ocean extending from the north of Mysore to the south of the Punjab. The coastline is generally regular and rock-bound, making access difficult. Along with Karwar, Marmagoa, and Karachi-in-Sind, Bombay is one of the few locations with a harbor land-locked enough to protect shipping during the south-west monsoon.

The region is well-supplied with railways, most of which concentrate at Bombay City. These include the Indian Midland, the Bombay, Baroda & Central India, the Southern Mahratta, the Rajputana-Malwa, and the Great Indian Peninsula systems. In 1905, the total length of railway open for traffic under the Bombay government (excluding Sind) was 7,980 miles.

The peninsula is also linked to the Kolaba district (a southern division of Bombay) via a steam ferry. Kolaba is a hilly district lying between the sea and the Western Ghats, known for rice cultivation, salt manufacture, and forests of black wood and teak. The value of these forests is increased by their proximity to Bombay.
