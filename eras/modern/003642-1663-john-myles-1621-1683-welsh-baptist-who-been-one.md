# John Myles and the Early Baptists in North America

## John Myles and the Massachusetts Settlement
John Myles (1621–1683) was a Welsh Baptist who had previously served as one of Cromwell's Tryers. In 1663, seeking refuge from the intolerance of the government of Charles II, Myles and his congregation migrated to Massachusetts. The group was permitted to settle in Rehoboth. Even after the authorities discovered that the settlers were Baptists, they were allowed to remain, provided that their meeting-place was established at a considerable distance from that of the standing order. Myles played a significant role in promoting the growth of the Baptist Church within Massachusetts and provided service to the denomination in Boston and other areas.

## Baptist Development in Boston
The growth of Baptist views in the Boston area involved several individuals and groups. As early as 1654, Thomas Gould of Charlestown appeared to share antipaedobaptist views and maintained close contact with President Dunster. By 1665, several English Baptists had settled near Boston, and others had adopted Baptist beliefs. In May 1665, these individuals, along with Gould, were baptized and joined those who had already been baptized in England through a church covenant.

Despite their organization, the Boston church faced severe persecution. Members were frequently fined and imprisoned, and they were denied the use of a building they had constructed to serve as a meeting-house. Even after the Act of Toleration (1689) became fully effective in England, the Boston Baptists were unable to secure the privileges granted by the act. Recognition of their right to exist was only achieved after earnest efforts by other dissenters and English Baptists. A mandate issued by Charles II in July 1679 had previously rebuked Massachusetts authorities for denying others the liberty to secure, noting that the authorities themselves had once gone into exile, but this mandate had little effect.

## Baptist Activity in the New Netherlands and Other Regions
Antipaedobaptists began settling in the New Netherlands (New York) from 1643 onward. Lady Deborah Moody left Massachusetts for the New Netherlands in 1643 due to her antipaedobaptist views; during her journey, she stopped at New Haven and won Mrs. Eaton, the wife of Governor Theophilus Eaton, to her principles. Moody settled at Gravesend (now part of Brooklyn) after receiving a guarantee of religious liberty from Dutch authorities.

Other Baptist figures active in the region included Francis Doughty, an English Baptist who had spent time in Rhode Island. In 1656, Doughty labored in the region and baptized several converts, an action that led to his banishment. Later that same year, William Wickenden of Providence administered ordinances and evangelized at Flushing, resulting in a heavy fine and his banishment. 

In other areas, Baptists settled on Block Island around 1663. By 1711, Valentine Wightman (1681–1747) of Connecticut, a General Baptist, began making occasional missionary visits to New York at the invitation of Nicolas Eyres. In 1714, Wightman baptized Eyres and others, helping them organize a church. This church was nearly wrecked in 1730 due to debts incurred from building a meeting-house. Additionally, a Baptist church, likely Arminian, was formed at Oyster Bay before 1724.

## Origins of the Particular Baptist Church
In England, the rise of the first Calvinistic or Particular Baptist Church occurred with the Jacob church in Southwark. This church was originally Independent before becoming Baptist. Its membership included William Kiffin (1616–1701), Henry Jessey (1601–1663), Hanserd Knollys (ca. 1599–1691), Praise-God Barbon (ca. 1596–1679), and John Lothropp or Lathrop (d. 1653). Five of the six churches that later sprang from the Jacob church were Baptist. However, before the Jacob church itself became Baptist, it dismissed a group of members in 1633 because the church had grown beyond proper limits; this group became the first Particular Baptist Church.
