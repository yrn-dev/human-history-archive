# Base-Ball

Base-ball is the national summer sport of the United States, though it is also popular in Japan and throughout Canada. The sport is named for the ball and bases used during play.

## Origins and Early Development
The origins of base-ball are obscure. Some authorities suggest it is derived from rounders, an old English game with several variations played in America during the colonial period. Other authorities claim the resemblance to rounders is coincidental and that the game originated in the United States, specifically in Cooperstown, New York, in 1839, where Abner Doubleday (later a U.S. army general) is said to have devised the scheme for playing.

At the start of the 19th century, a game called "One Old Cat" became popular among schoolboys in the North Atlantic states. This version involved three boys who took turns batting and fielding; a run was scored if the batsman ran to a single base and back without being put out. This game was later modified into "Two Old Cat" (four players), "Three Old Cat" (six players), and "Four Old Cat" (eight players). A further development of these games was known as town-ball, and the Olympic Town-Ball Club of Philadelphia was organized in 1833.

Organized club matches first appeared in the neighborhood of New York, including the founding of the Washington Baseball Club in 1843. In 1845, the Knickerbocker Baseball Club drew up the first regular code of rules, which were used in matches against the Empire and Gotham Eagle clubs of New York, as well as the Atlantic, Putnam, Eckford, and Excelsior clubs of Brooklyn.

## Organization and Evolution
The first National Association was organized in 1858. While its laws were similar to the modern code, the ball used was "livelier" and larger, and pitchers were required to deliver the ball with a full toss, as no approach to a throw was permitted. As the game's popularity grew, famous clubs were established, including the Maple Leaf of Guelph, the Red Stockings of Cincinnati, the Forest City of Cleveland, and the Lowell and Beacon of Boston. Sharp rivalry between top teams eventually led to the introduction of semi-professionalism.

Amateur base-ball is primarily played by athletic associations and school or university clubs. The first college league was formed in 1879, consisting of Brown, Amherst, Princeton, Harvard, and Dartmouth, with Yale joining a year later. In 1887, the Eastern College League was formed with Yale, Princeton, Harvard, and Columbia, though it was later dissolved. While leagues still exist in the south, west, and New England, the most important universities in the eastern states currently belong to no league.

## Gameplay and Rules
The game involves a batsman who stands within a "box" (a parallelogram 4 ft. wide and 6 ft. long) facing the pitcher. The batsman's goal is to reach first-base without being put out. This can be achieved through a "safe-hit" (a fair ball that cannot be caught or fielded in time), being struck unavoidably by a pitched ball, or if the pitcher throws four "bad balls" (balls not over the base, or not between the batsman's knee and shoulder) that the batsman does not strike. A batsman may also reach first-base via a fielder's error or if the catcher fails to catch a third strike and the batsman reaches the base before the first-baseman can touch the base with the ball.

Balls hit outside the foul-lines are "fouls." These are counted as strikes until two strikes have been called, after which they are not counted. A batsman is declared out if he oversteps the batting lines, fails to take his position within one minute of being summoned by the umpire, fails to bat in the published order, or if a fair ball is caught before hitting the ground.

## Pitching and Fielding
The pitcher is considered the most important member of the team. The pitcher must keep one foot on a pitcher's plate (6 in. wide and 24 in. long) located 60.5 ft. from the back of home-base. While pitchers originally used a full toss, a disguised underhand throw was legalized around 1875, followed by the overhand throw. Pitchers use wrist movements to create curves, such as the incurve, the drop, and the out-curve (which swerves away from a right-handed batsman).

Fielding positions include:
*   **First-baseman:** Usually a tall man who must be a sure catch of balls thrown to head off runners.
*   **Second-baseman and Short-stop:** Positioned to the right and left of the diamond respectively, they field ground balls and cover second-base.
*   **Third-baseman:** Must be an exceptionally good thrower due to the long distance to first-base and must be ready to field "bunts" (balls rebounded from a loosely held bat).
*   **Catcher:** Fields balls that drop in front of home-plate.
*   **Outfielders:** Three players who catch "flies" (high balls that have not touched the ground) and return ground balls.

## Team Strategy and Scoring
Team batting involves cooperation between the batsman and base-runner. Examples include the "hit and run," where a runner starts for second-base as the pitcher delivers the ball, and the "sacrifice-hit," where the batsman is retired to advance a runner to the next base (often via a bunt or a long fly to the outfield).

Scoring is recorded in vertical columns tracking:
*   Times at bat (excluding sacrifice-hits and bases taken on balls).
*   Runs scored and base-hits made.
*   Sacrifice-hits.
*   Opponents put out and "assists."
*   Errors (though balks, wild pitches, and passed balls are not counted as errors).

A "two-base-hit" allows two bases, a "three-base-hit" allows three, and a "home-run" allows four. A player's batting record is calculated by dividing hits by times at bat, while the fielding record is the number of accepted chances divided by total chances.
