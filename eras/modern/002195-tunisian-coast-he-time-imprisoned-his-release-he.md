# Tunisian Coast and the Barbary States

The Tunisian coast and the surrounding Mediterranean region were historically characterized by the activities of the Barbary pirates, the pursuit of coral fisheries, and complex diplomatic and military interactions between North African states and European powers.

## Barbary Piracy and Captivity
During the first half of the 17th century, the Barbary pirates experienced a period of significant growth. In Algiers alone, it is said that more than 20,000 captives were imprisoned. The fate of these captives depended largely on their financial status; while the wealthy were permitted to redeem themselves, the poor were condemned to slavery. In many instances, masters refused to grant freedom even if the captives professed Mahommedanism.

Captives included individuals of high social standing from England, Germany, Italy, and Spain, though the primary sufferers were the inhabitants of the coasts of Spain, Naples, and Sicily. Any trader from a nation that did not pay "blackmail"—disguised as ransoms or presents—to secure immunity was liable to be captured at sea. Despite these payments, the "faithless barbarians" did not always guarantee safety.

The continued existence of this piracy was viewed as a disgrace to Europe, fueled by the jealousies of European powers. France encouraged the pirates during its rivalry with Spain, while Great Britain and Holland later supported them against France. Some British public figures in the 18th century even viewed Barbary piracy as a useful mechanism to check the carrying trade competition of weaker Mediterranean nations.

## Military and Diplomatic Engagements
Various European and American naval forces engaged with the Barbary states, including Tunis, Algiers, Tripoli, and Salli, to protect trade and subjects. In 1655, British Admiral Robert Blake delivered a "severe beating" to the Tunisians. Later, during the reign of Charles II, the British fleet undertook a long series of expeditions.

In 1661, the Dutch naval officer Michael Adrianzoon de Ruyter grounded a vessel belonging to Tunis. Following this action, he released forty Christian slaves and established a treaty with the Tunisians.

The United States also dealt with these states. Initially, the U.S. paid tribute to the dey of Algiers to secure the exemption of its merchant ships from capture. When bribery failed to stop the outrages, the U.S. employed force. Commodore William Bainbridge served against both Tunis and Algiers. During these operations, Bainbridge's ship, the *Philadelphia*, ran aground on the Tunisian coast, leading to his temporary imprisonment. Following his release, he briefly returned to the merchant service to recover pecuniary losses resulting from his capture.

## Coral Fisheries
The African coasts were sites of intense rivalry among Mediterranean European communities seeking to secure coral fisheries. Prior to the 16th century, the Italian republics controlled these fisheries. Spain, under Charles V, secured the Tunisian fisheries for a short period, but the monopoly was subsequently taken by the French. France held this right until 1793, when the Revolutionary government opened the trade. The British government controlled the fisheries briefly around 1806, after which they returned to French authority.

While the trade originally centered in Marseilles, it later became a predominantly Italian industry based in Genoa, Rome, and Naples. In the Algerian coast, boats not flying the French flag were required to pay heavy dues. The industry utilized two types of vessels: large boats (12 to 14 tons) that dredged from March to October, collecting 650 to 850 lbs of coral, and small boats (3 to 4 tons) that worked year-round, collecting 390 to 500 lbs. To ensure proper growth, Algerian reefs were divided into ten portions, with only one fished annually.

## Regional Terminology
The term "Rum" (and the people "Rumis" or "Rumas") was used by Berbers, Arabs, and Saracens to describe countries and peoples that had been part of the Roman Empire. This label did not always signify "Christian" or "Christendom." For example, Tunisians and Algerians used the term "Rumas" to refer to the Spanish Moors and their descendants.
