# Population and Regional Geography of India

The population of India is very large, with certain districts ranked among the most densely peopled in the world. The general climate of the region allows the population to secure the necessaries of life without severe labour. Due to extremes of drought and heat that render the land unsuitable for pasture, the people typically live in towns or settled villages, subsisting through commerce or the cultivation of the soil.

## Regional Population Centers and Demographics

Population density varies significantly across the subcontinent. The old province stretching from the frontier of Nepal to the hills of Chota Nagpur across the Ganges valley is the most densely populated tract in India. This region, corresponding to the administrative divisions of Bhagalpur and Patna, had a population of 24,241,305 over an area of 44,197 sq. m. Because of this density, the area is always liable to famine, though railways now provide protection almost everywhere.

In other districts, population figures as of 1901 include:
* **Bankura (Bengal):** 1,116,411
* **Kheri (United Provinces):** 905,138
* **Bijapur:** 735,435
* **Cachar (Eastern Bengal and Assam):** 455,593
* **Buldana (Berar):** 423,616 (which increased to 613,756 when the district area was enlarged in 1905)
* **Darrang (Eastern Bengal and Assam):** 337,313
* **Basim (Berar):** 353,410

Socially, the Kunbis are a great agricultural caste of Western India, corresponding to the Kapus in the Telugu country and the Kurmis in the north. In some Deccan districts, the Kunbis form an actual majority of the population. In 1901, the combined total of Mahrattas and Kunbis in all India was returned at nearly 8¾ million.

## Environmental Impacts on Population

Famine has historically caused significant fluctuations in regional populations. In the district of Buldana, the 1901 population showed a 12% decrease over the preceding decade due to famine. Similarly, the district of Basim saw an 11% decrease in the same period due to the famine of 1899-1900. In Bijapur, population changes reveal the severe effects of famines in 1899-1900 and 1876-1878; the population in 1901 showed an 8% decrease, following a 27% increase in the previous decade and a 21% decrease between 1872 and 1881. Bankura also suffered from famines in 1866, 1896-1897, and 1874-1875.

Health and geography also dictate settlement patterns. In the Kheri district, the area north of the river Ul is considered very unhealthy due to stagnant waters and endemic fevers caused by dense vegetation and shifting river courses. Consequently, people reside near the low ground where the soil is more fertile and less expensive to cultivate than the forest-covered uplands.

## Land Use and Economy

India is generally well cleared, with forests typically found along mountain flanks where rain is most abundant. The more open parts are highly cultivated and contain many large cities.

* **Agricultural Production:** In the valley of the Purna (Buldana), cultivation covers the land in a "many-coloured sheet" during seasonable years. In Darrang, rice is the principal grain-crop, and tea cultivation has brought prosperity to the Tezpur subdivision. In Cachar, the alluvial plain bears heavy crops of rice and tea.
* **Industry:** Cotton is a major economic driver. Buldana and Basim both produce cotton, and Malkapur is an important trade center with factories for pressing and ginning cotton. Bijapur also has factories for ginning and pressing cotton, as well as manufactures of silk goods, blankets, and cotton. In Bankura, industries include coal-mines, indigo factories, and the carving of soap-stone.
* **Infrastructure:** Communication is facilitated by various means. The Assam-Bengal railway serves Cachar, while the Nagpur line of the Great Indian Peninsula railway runs through north Buldana. In Darrang, the principal means of communication is the river, though a 20-mile steam tramway exists from Tezpur to Balipara.

## Geography of the Northern and Peripheral Regions

The great rivers of northern India—the Indus, the Brahmaputra, and the Ganges—derive their waters from the Tibetan mountain mass, with the northern water-parting lying north of the Himalaya in central Tibet. 

The island of Ceylon is distinguished from British India primarily by its separate administration and the Buddhistic religion of its population. Its highest point reaches approximately 9,000 ft. above the sea. While its mountain slopes are densely forested, its lower levels are similar in cultivation and climate to regions of the Malay peninsula at the same latitude.
