# Colonies Possessing Representative Institutions Without Responsible Government

Within the structure of the British colonial empire, which comprises over fifty distinct governments, colonies are categorized into three primary classes and dependencies. One specific class consists of colonies possessing representative institutions but lacking responsible government. These are characterized as partially self-governing communities.

## Legislative Process and Authority
In colonies with representative institutions but no responsible government, the crown generally cannot legislate by order in council. Instead, laws are enacted by the governor with the concurrence of the legislative body or bodies. In instances where a second chamber exists and possesses a preponderance of elected representatives, the concurrence of at least one of these bodies is required.

While the home government retains control over public officers, the crown holds a veto on legislation. This differs from crown colonies, where the crown has entire control of legislation, and responsible colonies, where the crown also retains a veto but the home government has no control over any public officer except the governor.

## Administrative Structure and Examples
The administration of these colonies involves a mix of elected and nominated officials. For example, the Bahamas, Barbados, and Bermuda each possess two legislative bodies: one that is elected and one that is nominated by the crown. In contrast, Malta and the Leeward Islands have only one legislative body, which is partly elected and partly nominated.

In the specific case of Barbados, the colony is administered by a governor who is assisted by an executive council and a legislative council consisting of nine nominated members. Additionally, there is a house of assembly composed of 24 members who are elected via a limited franchise. In such representative colonies, the home government appoints public officials, though Barbados is noted as an exception regarding the appointment of the treasurer.

## Financial Control
The financial management of representative colonies occupies a middle ground between crown colonies and responsible colonies. Unlike crown colonies, where finance is under the direct control of the imperial government, the finance of representative colonies is not directly controlled. However, the imperial government usually influences important departures in financial policy through its expressed opinion. This contrasts with responsible colonies, where finance is entirely under local control and the imperial government is dissociated from material or moral responsibility for debts.

## Evolution and the Demand for Responsible Government
The development of representative institutions was a step in the evolution of colonial governance. In 1791, Pitt granted representative institutions to Canada, though the home government maintained responsible control. Similar institutions were later granted to South Africa and Australia.

As these regions grew into important communities during the early 19th century, representative institutions controlled by the home government were deemed insufficient. This led to a reassertion of claims for the liberty to manage their own affairs. In Canada, the adoption of responsible government was specifically advocated as a remedy for grievances by Reformers, such as Mackenzie, who presented the *Seventh Report on Grievances*.

Under a system of responsible government, legislation is conducted via parliamentary means identical to those used in the home country, featuring a cabinet responsible to parliament. In such a system, the crown reserves only a right of veto, exercised at the governor's discretion for certain bills.

## Colonial Governance and the Crown
Colonial governors represent the crown, and their specific titles—such as governors-general, governors, lieut.-governors, administrators, high commissioners, or commissioners—vary based on the status of the colony, dependency, or group of colonies they preside over. Their powers vary according to the position they occupy.

The crown receives advice on colonial administration from the secretaries of state for India and the colonies. Historically, this replaced the "Council of Trade and Plantations," which was suppressed in 1782. While the theory of the direct and exclusive dependence of colonial possessions on the crown is recognized as far as possible, it is understood that the principal sections of the empire enjoy equal rights under the crown and none is subordinate to another.
