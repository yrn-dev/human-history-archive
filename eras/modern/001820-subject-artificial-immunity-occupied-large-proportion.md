# Immunity

The term immunity, derived from the Latin *immunis* (meaning not subject to a *munus* or public service), is a general term for exemption from liability. In a legal and international context, it refers to exemption from the jurisdiction of a state. In pathology, it refers to non-susceptibility to a specific disease or to experimental inoculation with a given toxin or bacterium.

## Legal and Diplomatic Immunity
In international law, immunity implies that a person is exempt from the jurisdiction of the state where the immunity arises. This status is generally granted to sovereigns, chiefs of states, and their direct representatives in the states to which they are accredited.

Public ministers in foreign countries, along with their families and officially attached functionaries, are exempt from the civil and criminal jurisdiction of the state to which they are accredited. They remain subject to the penal laws and jurisdiction of their own country. This immunity applies to acts connected with their official function; for acts not connected with the function, immunity is only claimed for the duration of the function.

However, immunity from jurisdiction cannot be invoked in the following instances:
*   Proceedings resulting from engagements entered into in the exercise of a profession carried on in the country concurrently with diplomatic functions (excluding official or private capacities).
*   Real actions, including possessory actions, regarding movable or immovable property in the country.
*   Cases where persons of the nationality of the country to which they are accredited attempt to claim the privilege.

## Biological Immunity: General Concepts
In bacteriology, immunity is viewed as a relative term, as an animal's susceptibility can be influenced by the conditions present. For instance, an animal might be susceptible during experimental inoculation but rarely suffer from a disease naturally if the necessary conditions for infection are absent in nature. Susceptibility can be increased by general or local diminished vitality.

Natural immunity against toxins may depend on the insensitiveness of a cell to a toxic body or the absence of chemical affinity between the toxic molecule and the living molecules of the tissues. For example, the nervous system of the fowl has little combining affinity for tetanus toxin and possesses immunity against it.

Natural powers to destroy bacteria involve several mechanisms:
*   **Phagocytosis and Chemiotaxis:** Leucocytes are guided by chemiotaxis (sensitiveness to chemical substances) to ingest bacteria. This process involves ingestion and subsequent intracellular digestion. Metchnikoff demonstrated that phagocytosis is present in immune animals and deficient or absent in susceptible ones.
*   **Serum Action:** Blood serum can have a direct bactericidal action, though this varies by animal. While the serum of a white rat kills the anthrax bacillus (making the rat immune), the serum of a guinea-pig does not, leaving the guinea-pig susceptible. However, this is not a general law.

## Artificial Immunity
Artificial immunity is divided into two chief varieties based on the mode of production: active and passive.

### Active Immunity
Active immunity involves producing a reaction or series of reactions in the body, typically through injections of bacteria or their products. This is based on the observation that recovery from certain infective diseases provides protection against subsequent attacks. Immunity often results from substances introduced during the disease rather than the disease itself; properly adjusted doses of poison can produce immunity without symptoms.

Methods for producing active immunity include:
*   **Attenuated Viruses:** Inoculation with bacteria whose virulence has been diminished. Attenuation can be achieved by growing organisms outside the body under unsuitable conditions (such as higher temperatures or in the presence of weak antiseptics), growing them on artificial media, passing air over the growth, or passing the bacterium through a different animal species.
*   **Vaccines:** Immunity can be increased gradually using vaccines of increasing virulence, potentially reaching a high degree through "supervirulent" cultures attained by the method of passage.
*   **Dead Bacteria:** Injecting the bacterium in a dead condition to produce immunity against the living organism.

When a high degree of immunity is achieved using a large dose of toxin or a very virulent bacterium, the immunity is "specific" to that toxin or bacterium. Conversely, "non-specific" immunity or increased tissue resistance can be produced locally (e.g., in the peritoneum) using nucleic acid, peptone, or non-pathogenic organisms.

### Passive Immunity
Passive immunity is produced by transferring the serum of an actively immunized animal to a fresh animal. It is termed "passive" because it does not cause an active change in the tissues of the recipient animal. While active immunity is applied in preventive inoculation, passive immunity is the basis for serum therapeutics.

## Historical Development
Systematic research into immunity against bacterial diseases began around 1880. This progress was stimulated by Pasteur's demonstration that bacteria produce definite diseases and by Koch's 1881 introduction of the "plate-method" and solid culture media, which simplified the separation and cultivation of bacteria. By 1876, Koch had obtained the anthrax bacillus in pure culture.

Between 1880 and 1890, organisms for diseases such as cholera, typhoid fever, diphtheria, glanders, tetanus, and tubercle were identified. Later discoveries included the bacilli of influenza (1892), plague (1894), and dysentery (1898). Pioneers in producing active immunity via attenuated viruses included Pasteur, Toussaint, and Chauveau in France, and Duguid, J. S. Burdon-Sanderson, and W. S. Greenfield in Great Britain. Metchnikoff's work from 1884 on the theory of phagocytosis further advanced the field.
