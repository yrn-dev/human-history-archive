# BALEARIC ISLANDS

The Balearic Islands (_Baleares_) are an archipelago located in the Mediterranean Sea off the east coast of Spain. The archipelago forms a province of Spain and covers an area of 1,935 square miles. As of 1900, the total population was 311,649. Geographically, the islands are situated between 38° 40' and 40° 5' N. latitude and between 1° and 5° E. longitude.

## Geography and Composition
The archipelago is divided into two distinct groups. The eastern and larger group, which corresponds to the ancient _Insulae Baleares_, consists of the two principal islands, Majorca (Spanish: _Mallorca_) and Minorca (Spanish: _Menorca_), along with seven islets: Aire, Aucanada, Botafoch, Cabrera, Dragonera, Pinto, and El Rey. 

The western group, known anciently as the Pityusae or Pine Islands, comprises two relatively large islands, Iviza (Spanish: _Ibiza_ or formerly _Ivica_) and Formentera, as well as the islets of Ahorcados, Conejera, Pou, and Espalmador. While all eleven islets were inhabited as of 1900, their combined population was only 171. Most of these islets are of little importance, with the exception of Cabrera, which is characterized by numerous caverns and was previously utilized as a place of banishment. In 1808, Spanish captors landed a large group of Frenchmen on Cabrera, where they were left to nearly perish from starvation.

## Geology, Climate, and Nature
The geological strata of the islands are split into two divisions. An older series, ranging from the Devonian to the Cretaceous, is folded and faulted, forming the higher hills. A newer Tertiary series lies nearly horizontal upon these older beds. The folds of the older series form an arc convex toward the south-east, running south-west to north-east in Majorca, west to east in Iviza, and south to north in Minorca. The Devonian system is only visible in Minorca; in the other islands, the Trias is the oldest system. The Eocene beds are nummulitic, and the Miocene includes limestone with _Clypeaster_. Pliocene beds are also present. A lacustrine group, once thought to be Lower Eocene, is proven to be Oligocene in part by the discovery of _Anthracotherium magnum_ in interbedded lignites.

The climate is generally healthy, mild, and favorable for plant life, though it varies based on shelter from wind and altitude. Both the flora and fauna of the archipelago resemble those found on the Mediterranean coasts of France and Spain.

## History and Etymology
The origin of the name _Baleares_ is a matter of conjecture, though the Greeks used the names _Baleares_ and _Gymnasiæ_ because the inhabitants exercised in slinging stones from a very early age. Mothers would place a child's breakfast on a high tree bough, and children were not permitted to eat until they brought the bread down using a sling. This practice produced the most expert slingers in the world, who served the Carthaginians in sieges and battles. These slingers used leaden bullets or stones weighing over a pound, which were thrown with enough violence to pierce shields, cuirasses, and strong helmets.

The Carthaginians seized the Balearic isles, including Majorca and Minorca. In Minorca, the Carthaginian general Mago—likely the brother of Hannibal—fortified and made use of Port Mahon (_Portus Magonis_), which remains one of the most significant harbors in the Mediterranean. Other historical names include _Ebusus_ (of Carthaginian origin) for Iviza, and _Ophiusa_ (Greek) or _Colubraria_ (Roman) for Formentera. The names Majorca and Minorca are derived from the Latin _Major_ and _Minor_ via Byzantine forms. Early inhabitants also utilized cave residences, as noted in the works of Diodorus.

## Administration and Culture
The administration of the Balearic Islands is identical to that of other Spanish provinces on the mainland. The province is divided into five judicial districts (_partidos judiciales_), named after the chief towns of Palma, Manacor, Inca, Iviza, and Port Mahon.

Culturally, the islands maintain local traditions such as the _copla_ (verses sung to traditional or local melodies). An example of a _copla_ heard in Polenza, formed on the rhythm of the _Malaguena_, describes a star that disappears from the sky to shine upon a person's forehead.
