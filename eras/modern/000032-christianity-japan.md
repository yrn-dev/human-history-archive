# Christianity in Japan

## Arrival and Early Expansion
Japan's first contact with Europe occurred in the sixteenth century. In 1542, Portuguese explorers arrived in a Chinese junk, and by 1549, the Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier began teaching in Japan. For a period, the Japanese welcomed European intercourse, and Christian missionaries successfully gained a significant number of converts. 

By the close of 1581, thirty-two years after Xavier's landing, there were approximately 150,000 converts. The majority of these—roughly 125,000—were located in Kyushu, with the remainder situated in Kyoto, Yamaguchi, and the surrounding areas. While the number of Jesuits grew to seventy-five by this time, they had numbered no more than nine until 1563. This rapid growth is described as a "harvest" that was largely artificial, driven by the insistence of feudal chiefs who sought to attract foreign trade and held absolute power over their vassals.

## Missionary Methods and Religious Conflict
Early Christian propagandism in Japan employed various striking methods to attract attention. In 1557, fathers in Hirado organized activities similar to the later Salvation Army, including parades with bells and litanies led by brothers and bands of boys. Some converts and children engaged in self-flagellation at a model of Mount Calvary, and missionaries claimed to heal the sick using scourges or a booklet of prayers and litanies written by Xavier.

These activities often led to violent conflict with Buddhism. Some converted sub-feudatories expressed their zeal by destroying Buddhist idols and temples. In one instance, a campaign of violence was inaugurated by a convert who sought to exterminate Buddhist priests from his states. Consequently, Buddhist priests experienced a form of persecution based solely on creed, a phenomenon previously unknown in Japan. Conversely, the Jesuits were noted for treating Buddhists with great acrimony, persecuting and insulting them.

## Political Tensions and Decline
The stability of the Christian missions was undermined by quarrels between different European factions, including the Portuguese Jesuits, Spanish Dominicans, and English and Dutch Protestants. Each group warned the Japanese government about the political designs of their rivals. Concerns grew that Catholic Christianity served as a cloak for the political ambitions of the Spanish monarchy—which already held the Philippine Islands—and the Pope.

Reports from figures such as William Adams and the pilot of the *San Felipe* suggested that Spain and Portugal viewed the annexation of non-Christian countries as justifiable. The Japanese government became wary of "greate shipps" from New Spain carrying soldiers and treasure, fearing they would coordinate with Christian converts to revolt against the Yedo Government. This was particularly concerning in Kyushu, the center of Japanese Christianity, which did not maintain the same level of allegiance to the Tokugawa shoguns as it had to Hideyoshi.

## Suppression and Persecution
The Tokugawa shogunate eventually moved to eradicate the faith. While Tokugawa Ieyasu initially interviewed a Franciscan priest named Jerome de Jesus—who had secretly returned to Japan after escaping crucifixion in Manila—he later issued prohibitions against Christianity in September 1612 and April 1613. These early decrees were warnings rather than punitive measures.

On January 27, 1614, a strict edict ordered the removal of all foreign priests to Nagasaki, the destruction of all churches, and the forced abjuration of the faith by all converts. At the time, there were 156 ministers in Japan, including 122 Jesuits, 14 Franciscans, 9 Dominicans, 4 Augustinians, and 7 secular priests. Many missionaries defied the order; forty-seven evaded the edict by hiding or returning in disguise to continue their work.

Persecution intensified under Iemitsu, who ascended to the shogunate in 1623. During that year, approximately five hundred Christians were martyred within Tokugawa domains. Governors of Nagasaki, such as Mizuno and Takenaka, and the feudal chief of Shimabara, Matsukura, were particularly active in the eradication of the faith. Tortures included the "fosse"—where victims were suspended by their feet head-downward in a pit until death—and throwing converts into solfataras at Unzen.

By 1638, Japan was absolutely closed to Europeans. This isolation lasted for over 200 years, during which time no European could enter, no Japanese could go abroad, and the construction of ships larger than coasting boats was forbidden.

## Role in Modern Japan
Following the period of isolation, some writers have argued that Christian missionaries contributed significantly to the creation of "New Japan." However, other perspectives suggest that while missionaries have contributed to education and moral education, their effect on the character of modern Japan has been indirect. The primary impetus for the statesmen who led the Restoration and national rejuvenation—such as Sakuma, Saigo, Okubo, and Kido—is attributed instead to Bushido, the strict and punctilious code of honor associated with samuraihood.
