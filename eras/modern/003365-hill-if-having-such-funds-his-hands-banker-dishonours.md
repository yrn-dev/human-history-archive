# Banking Relations and the Law of Cheques

The relationship between a banker and a customer is characterized by a debtor-creditor dynamic, supplemented by specific obligations regarding the handling of funds, the honoring of cheques, and the management of accounts.

## Obligations Regarding Cheques
A banker is under an obligation to honor a customer's cheques, provided there is a sufficient and available balance in the banker's hands for that purpose (_Foley v. Hill_). If a banker dishonors a cheque despite having sufficient funds, they are liable to the customer for substantial damages without the need to prove actual injury (_Rolin v. Steward_). In cases where multiple cheques are presented simultaneously and funds are insufficient to cover all of them, the banker is not required to discriminate between cheques and should pay as many as the available funds allow.

It is considered a legitimate condition that a cheque be drawn in an ordinary recognized form; banks may be justified in refusing cheques that raise doubts about their validity or effect, such as those drawn to "petty cash or order" or "wages or order." Additionally, money paid into an account so shortly before a cheque's presentation that it has not yet passed through the bank's books is not treated as available for drawing.

## Rights and Liabilities in Payment and Collection
In England, a cheque is not an assignment of funds (_Bills of Exchange Act 1882, sec. 53_). Consequently, if payment is refused, the holder of the cheque has no direct claim against the banker, but must seek remedy against the drawer or endorser. This rule applies even if the banker marked the cheque as "good" for the amount before issue, as such marking is not an acceptance. However, between two bankers, such marking may be a binding representation that the cheque will be paid.

Regarding the collection of cheques, a banker acting as an agent is bound to give notice of dishonour to either the customer or the parties liable on the bill. The common practice of returning the dishonoured cheque to the customer is validated by sec. 49, subs. 6 of the Bills of Exchange Act.

A banker who pays or collects a cheque for a person with defective or no title is _prima facie_ liable to the true owner for money had and received or conversion, regardless of good faith. However, payment of an open cheque payable to bearer (originally or by endorsement) is always considered a good payment and discharge (_Charles v. Blackwell_).

## Account Management and Secrecy
Bankers are bound to observe secrecy regarding a customer's account, an obligation that continues even if the account becomes overdrawn (_Hardy v. Veasey_). 

Regarding account records, entries in a pass-book serve as _prima facie_ evidence against the banker. While the return of a pass-book without comment is sometimes disputed as a settlement of account, credit entries cannot be disputed if the customer relied upon them and altered their position. In cases of forgery, a customer may still object to being debited regardless of their dealings with the pass-book (_Kepitigalla Rubber Co. v. National Bank of India_).

## Overdrafts and Interest
A banker is not obligated to allow a customer to overdraw unless there is an express or implied agreement based on the course of business. Drawing a cheque without sufficient funds is viewed as an implied request for an overdraft. Interest is chargeable on overdrafts based on the universal custom of bankers. Compound interest or interest with periodical rests may be supported if the system was brought to the customer's attention via the pass-book and not objected to.

## The Banker's Lien and Set-Off
The banker's lien, part of the law merchant, allows a banker to retain and apply any of the customer's securities that come into the banker's possession in their capacity as banker to discharge the customer's liability. This includes cheques and bills paid in for collection (_Currie v. Misa_). This lien acts as an implied pledge.

Certain items are exempt from this lien, specifically moneys paid in to meet particular bills or goods deposited for safe custody. Furthermore, the banker's strict legal rights may be curtailed by the "course of business" rule; if dealings have proceeded on a recognized footing for a reasonable time, the banker cannot suddenly assert strict rights to the customer's detriment. For example, a banker may be precluded from asserting a lien for an overdraft on one account against another account that has been operated separately.

## Trust Accounts and Fiduciary Capacity
While theoretically supposed to refuse trust accounts, bankers often open accounts with headings indicating fiduciary capacity. The obligation to honor the customer's cheque remains the predominant factor; a banker is not required to question the object of a cheque unless there is clear evidence of impending fraud (_Gray v. Johnston_). When dealing with known trust moneys, a banker is entitled to insist on the authority of the entire body of direct trustees.
