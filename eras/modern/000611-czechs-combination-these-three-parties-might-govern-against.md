# Czechs

The Czechs constitute a significant nationality within the Austrian dominions, particularly in Bohemia, the richest part of the empire, where they comprise over three-fifths of the population. Their political history is characterized by a struggle for national recognition, linguistic rights, and the restoration of the Bohemian kingdom.

## National Aspirations and the Bohemian Kingdom
The central objective of the Czech national movement has been the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom. Led by figures such as Palacky and Rieger, a great party demanded the full restoration of the Bohemian monarchy, including Silesia and Moravia. This movement insisted that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia at Prague, following the precedent of his predecessors, and sought a position within the monarchy similar to that obtained by Hungary. 

This cause was supported not only by the Czechs but also by several great nobles of German descent—including Count Leo Thun, Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz, and Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg—who hoped a self-governing kingdom would preserve their power against the German Liberals. In 1868, a "declaration" was presented to the diet at Prague, signed by eighty-one members, including bishops and feudal nobles. This document asserted that no foreign assembly was qualified to impose taxes in Bohemia and that the diet was not qualified to elect representatives to Vienna.

## Internal Political Divisions
The Czech political landscape has been marked by significant internal conflict, primarily between the "Old Czechs" and the "Young Czechs."

*   **Old Czechs:** Led by Rieger, this group maintained an alliance with the clergy and feudalists, pursuing a policy of passive opposition.
*   **Young Czechs:** Also known as Radicals, this party arose from a dislike of the alliance with the aristocracy and clergy. They recalled Hussite traditions, advocated for universal suffrage, and desired to join Germans in political reform.

Tensions between these factions peaked when twenty-seven members led by Stadkowsky and Gregr resigned from the Czech Club after being outvoted. While the Young Czechs were initially defeated in subsequent elections, they eventually became the dominant force. By 1891, the Young Czechs were completely victorious in a struggle against the Old Czechs.

## Parliamentary Influence and the "Iron Ring"
The Czechs have played a pivotal role in the shifting power dynamics of the Reichsrath. Under the ministry of Taaffe, who sought "unpolitical politics," a combination of Czechs, Poles, and the Right (under Hohenwart) formed an "iron ring" that opposed the German Liberals. This alliance allowed Taaffe to maintain his position for fourteen years, as the Right supported him to prevent a return to a Liberal government. During this period, Smolka was elected president of the Lower House of the Reichsrath.

However, the Czechs' relationship with the government remained complex. While they acquiesced to certain arrangements, they continued to press for the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom. In 1890, Taaffe attempted to reconcile opposing parties through conferences in Vienna. An agreement was reached regarding a revision of the electoral law and a separation of German and Czech districts. This failed because the Young Czechs were excluded from the conference and used obstruction to oppose the measures, arguing they threatened the unity of the Bohemian country.

## Language Disputes and Obstruction
A recurring point of contention has been the use of the Czech language in official capacities. In 1897, Minister Badeni published ordinances to secure the support of the Young Czechs. These ordinances mandated that:
1. All correspondence and documents brought before government officials in Bohemia be conducted in the language in which they were first introduced.
2. After 1903, no one could be appointed to a government post in Bohemia without passing an examination in Czech.

These measures led to violent opposition and obstruction from German Nationalists and Radicals. Later, under Dr. von Koerber, attempts at compromise were made, including a proposal to divide Bohemia into ten districts (five Czech, three German, and two mixed). The Czechs rejected this, demanding that Czech and German be placed on equal footing throughout Bohemia for all official purposes.

When these demands were refused, the Czechs frequently resorted to parliamentary obstruction, using "urgency motions" and noise. In one instance, a sitting lasted 57 hours without interruption. This aggressiveness prompted various German parties to form a joint executive committee to protect German racial interests.
