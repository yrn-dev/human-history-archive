# BALLISTICS

Ballistics, derived from the Greek word *ballein* (to throw), is the science concerned with the throwing of projectiles or warlike missiles. The field is divided into two primary branches: interior ballistics and exterior ballistics.

## Interior Ballistics
Interior ballistics focuses on the investigation of the dynamics of a shot as it moves up the bore of a gun. This branch of science analyzes the relations between the temperature, volume, and pressure of the powder-gas inside the bore, as well as the work realized by the expansion of that powder. It further examines the stress placed upon the material of the gun. 

In this context, a gun is viewed as a simple heat-engine or thermo-dynamic machine. Unlike ordinary gas or steam engines that operate in periodic cycles, a gun performs its work in a single stroke. The goal of interior ballistics is to determine the necessary charge of powder to achieve the projectile's initial velocity without placing undue strain on the gun.

## Exterior Ballistics
Exterior ballistics considers the motion of a projectile after it has received its initial impulse and is moving freely. In this state, the projectile is influenced by the resistance of the air and gravity. The objective of this study is to determine the circumstances necessary to hit and either perforate or destroy a specific object.

### Factors of Accuracy
Despite careful pointing and laying according to theory and practice, absolute certainty in hitting the same spot repeatedly is unattainable. This is due to non-eliminable errors, including variations in muzzle-velocity, variations in the air, and the steadiness of the shot during flight. To estimate a gun's accuracy, practitioners use the laws of probability to calculate 50% zones as shown in range tables.

### The Ballistic Table and Coefficients
The construction of a ballistic table begins with experimental values of $p$ for a standard projectile fired under standard conditions in air of standard density. To determine the time ($t$) required for a shot of weight $w$ and diameter $d$ to fall from an initial velocity ($V$) to a final velocity ($v$), the shot is assumed to move horizontally, ignoring the curving effect of gravity.

The ballistic coefficient ($C$), also referred to as the driving power of the shot, is defined by the formula $w/nd^2 = C$. Here, $n$ is a coefficient of reduction; for example, a value of 0.8 or less is considered a good average for modern rifle bullets. By using a well-chosen value of $n$, it is possible to utilize Bashforth's experimental results from old-fashioned muzzle-loading projectiles for more recent designs.

### Mathematical Calculations
The velocity is used as the argument of the ballistic table. Because $p$ is determined experimentally and tabulated as a function of $v$, the time $\Delta T$ is determined using the average value of $p$ in a given interval. The total time $T(v)$ is the sum of all preceding values of $\Delta T$ plus an arbitrary constant. For a shot with a ballistic coefficient $C$, the time is expressed as $t = C[T(V) - T(v)]$.

In regions of velocity where $p$ can be represented by an empirical formula consisting of a single power of $v$ (denoted as $v^m$), integration replaces summation. Colonel Zabudski, analyzing Krupp experiments, identified appropriate indices ($m$) for various velocity regions. For instance, at a velocity of 1800, $m=2$; at 1370, $m=3$; and at 1230, $m=5$.

## Application and Range Tables
Calculations for high-angle fire can employ the ballistic table for direct fire using equations modified by General Mayevski from the original work of Siacci. These equations help determine the time of flight, the coordinates $x$ and $y$, and the change in inclination.

When compiling a range table, several key values are determined:
*   **Tangent Elevation (T.E.):** Represented as $\phi$.
*   **Quadrant Elevation (Q.E.):** Calculated as $\phi - S$, where $S$ is the angular depression of the line of sight.
*   **Angle of Sight ($S$):** If the origin $O$ is $h$ feet vertically above point $B$ at a range of $R$ yards, $S$ is given by $\sin S = h/3R$.

When firing up or down a slope, the alteration of the tangent elevation is almost insensible, but the quadrant elevation requires the addition or subtraction of the angle of sight.
