# Continental Europe

## Electoral Systems and the Ballot
The use of the ballot is widespread across European countries, though the methods of implementation have varied significantly by nation and era.

### France
In France, the ballot (or *scrutin*) was used for deliberative voting in the chamber of deputies between 1840 and 1845. At the start of the Second Empire, the Organic Decree of February 2, 1852, regulated its use for elections to the Corps Legislatif. Under this system, a bureau consisting of a secretary, four unpaid assessors from the constituency, and a deputy returning-officer (the president of the section) superintended the voting. 

Voters obtained a polling-card from the Mairie containing their signature, date of birth, and designation to ensure identity. After the president mutilated this card, the vote was recorded via a "bulletin." These bulletins were not official; they were typically printed with a candidate's name and provided to the voter by an agent outside the polling place. The only requirements were that the bulletin be prepared outside the assembly, on white paper, and without external signs. 

The total number of votes was checked against "la feuille *d'appel* et inscription des votants," as the law assumed voters were called publicly. Voters unable to sign their polling-cards could provide a witness to sign for them. Certain bulletins were rejected, including those that were blank, illegible, lacked sufficient designation, contained the voter's identity, or named a person who had not taken the prescribed oath. Only votes declared bad by the bureau in the presence of representative scrutineers were preserved for the "Session pour verification des Pouvoirs." While the system lacked true secrecy—as observers could see which bulletin a voter took from an agent—determined voters could maintain secrecy by scratching out printed text and writing their own vote.

### Hungary and Prussia
The history of the ballot in Hungary is noted for its early lack of secret voting, which was unknown prior to 1848. The electoral law of 1848 left regulations to town and county councils, few of which adopted the ballot. The primitive method used involved candidates having large, color-coded boxes with their names on them; voters entered the room alone and placed a rod, 4 to 6 feet in length (to prevent the use of non-official rods), through a slit in the lid. By the electoral law of 1874, the ballot was abolished for parliamentary elections but made obligatory for town and county council elections, where voters voted for several people simultaneously.

In Prussia, the ballot was introduced for the election of the municipal assembly (*Stadtverordnetenversammlung*) via Stein's *Staedteordnung* (municipal corporation act) of 1808. Later, under the German constitution of 1867 and the new constitution of January 1, 1871, Reichstag elections were conducted via universal suffrage under the ballot, following the electoral law of May 31, 1869.

## Political History and Governance
The 19th century in Europe was marked by shifts between absolute government and constitutionalism. In Spain, a popular insurrection was crushed by a French army in 1823 under a mandate from a European congress, while Austria suppressed a revolution in Naples in 1824. In France, Charles X attempted to restore absolute government and destroy the liberty of universities and the press, voting a billion francs to compensate nobles for sequestrations and chateau burnings of 1789. This led to a 1830 uprising in Paris, which replaced him with Louis Philippe (1830-48), who served as a constitutional King for eighteen years.

Later, the establishment of the new German Empire and the fall of Napoleon III's empire led to a period of thirty-six years where European politics centered on the possibility of a Europe consolidated under German auspices. France, a rival to Germany for European ascendancy since the division of Charlemagne's empire, formed a close alliance with Russia. Germany linked itself with the Austrian Empire and, less successfully, with the kingdom of Italy.

## International Relations and Conflict
Great Britain historically maintained a position of detachment from continental affairs, viewing the idea of a British Army fighting in Europe as absurd. During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Britain and Germany maintained a strong commercial connection, acting as each other's best customers despite trade rivalries. Even during the Boer War and German attempts to form a European coalition against Britain, some British officials advocated for an alliance with Germany.

However, the aggressive development of a great German navy eventually forced Great Britain into a close association with the Franco-Russian group. The overseas enterprises of Emperor William II (1888-1918) further alienated Great Britain, as well as the United States and Japan.

## Industry and Agriculture
Continental Europe is a significant producer of willow species used in basket-work. Large quantities of these materials are exported from the continent to the United States and Great Britain. In the brewing industry, the continental "lager" system is noted as a point of comparison for American brewing methods, with the American product bearing a resemblance to the German beer.
