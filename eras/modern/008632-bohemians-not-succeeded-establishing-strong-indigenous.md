# The Governance and Political History of Bohemia

## Early Rulers and Territorial Expansion
The early history of Bohemia is marked by the adoption of Christianity and the rise of powerful local princes. While initial attempts to introduce Christianity from Germany were unsuccessful due to an innate distrust of the Germans, the faith was rapidly adopted after it arrived from Moravia via the Greek monks Cyrillus and Methodius. Around 873, Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius. One of the most notable early rulers was Wenceslas, surnamed the Holy, who was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized by the Church of Rome.

Under Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its frontiers significantly. Boleslav II established rule over Moravia, a large portion of Silesia, and a part of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. However, this empire did not endure; Boleslav III lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland. Boleslav III's reign was further characterized by constant civil war between him and his brothers, Jaromir and Ulrick. Eventually, the Bohemians dethroned Boleslav III due to his cruelty and chose the Polish prince Vladivoj—son of the Bohemian princess Dubravka—as their ruler.

## The Accession of the Habsburg Dynasty
Following the death of King Louis, Bohemia entered a period of great disorder and near anarchy. Because the previous two kings had resided primarily in Hungary, the Bohemians had failed to establish a strong indigenous government to replace the absentee monarchs. This vacuum led to several candidates claiming the throne, including Archduke Ferdinand of Austria, King Sigismund of Poland, the Bavarian dukes Louis and William, various German princes, and Bohemian noblemen such as Leo of Rozmital.

On October 23, 1526, a group of twenty-four electors—chosen equally from the three estates—elected Ferdinand of Habsburg. This event marked the permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne. Ferdinand sought to establish a hereditary right for his dynasty. Following a great fire in 1541 that destroyed state documents, Ferdinand replaced the original charter (which stated he was king by election) with a new one claiming he was recognized as king through the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna. This substitution caused significant dissatisfaction.

## Religious Conflict and the Habsburg Reign
The Habsburg rulers often balanced their traditional devotion to Rome with the complex religious landscape of Bohemia. Ferdinand I showed moderation early in his reign but used the "Compacts"—enactments granting freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists—as a tool to oppress advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren.

His successor, Maximilian, was initially supported by church reformers because he had been educated by Protestant tutors. However, Maximilian's reign did not meet these expectations; he published decrees against the Bohemian Brethren and eventually returned to the functions of the Roman church. His reign was characterized by religious skepticism and indifference during ecclesiastical disputes. When Maximilian requested funds for wars against the Turks in 1567, the Bohemian estates demanded that the Compacts cease to be part of the country's laws.

Later, King Matthias allied with Bohemian Protestants to struggle against his brother, but once in power, he adopted the traditional Habsburg policy favoring the Church of Rome. This led to strained relations and an eventual conflict. In 1617, Matthias's successor, Ferdinand (duke of Styria), was crowned. Ferdinand was a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome and a persecutor of Protestants. Tensions peaked in 1618 when the archbishop of Prague and the abbot of Brevnov suppressed Protestant services, leading the Protestants, led by Count Thurn, to confront Matthias's councillors at the Hradcany palace.

## National Identity and Language
The question of nationality and language remained a recurring theme in Bohemian politics. In 1615, a general diet reaffirmed enactments enforcing the use of the national language and decreed that Bohemian be the official language of the country. This national sentiment was also reflected in early literature; the rhymed chronicle often attributed to Dalimil, written during the reign of King John (1310-1346), expresses a strong racial antipathy toward the Germans.

In the 19th century, the struggle for national rights continued under Austrian rule. In 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the Habsburg empire by recognizing the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. While this failed due to a subsequent government with a "pronounced German tendency," later Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe succeeded in bringing Bohemian representatives back to the parliament in Vienna. Taaffe's government removed anomalies in the electoral system that had favored the German minority and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.

Further linguistic tensions arose in 1897 when Count Badeni decreed that government officials in Bohemia must know both Bohemian and German. This caused the fall of Badeni's cabinet. Subsequent governments under Count Thun and Count Clary repealed these equal-rights decrees, leading to repressed troubles in Prague as Bohemians continued to assert their national rights against the central government in Vienna.
