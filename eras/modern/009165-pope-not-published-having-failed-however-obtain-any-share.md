# The Papacy and Papal Influence in History

The papacy, as the headship of the Roman Church, has historically sought to establish a single rule and peace throughout Christendom. However, this ambition was often hindered by the processes used to select the Pope.

## Election and Administration of the Papacy
A significant weakness in the Roman Church's effort to secure leadership was the manner of papal election. The process lacked clear, definite, and unassailable forms, leaving it uncertain who was eligible to vote or whether the Holy Roman or Byzantine Emperors held a voice in the matter. 

Pope Gregory VII (Hildebrand, 1073-1085) attempted to regularize these elections by confining the voting power to the Roman cardinals. While he reduced the Emperor's role to a formula of assent, he failed to establish a system for a successor-designate. Consequently, disputes among cardinals occasionally left the See vacant for a year or more.

## Papal Authority and Political Conflict
The papacy frequently intersected with the political struggles of European monarchs and nobles. In the 14th century, Pope John XXII refused to recognize either Louis or Frederick of Austria as the ruler of Germany, asserting his own right to administer the empire during a vacancy. This conflict, supported by Philip V of France, led to military engagements; Louis sent an army into Italy that forced papal troops to raise the siege at Milan. Pope John XXII subsequently threatened Louis with excommunication and eventually placed him under a papal ban on March 23, 1324.

In other instances, the papacy acted as a diplomatic mediator or a source of legal relief. William Egon, the bishop of Strassburg, was saved from a life sentence in Vienna through the intervention of the papal nuncio. Later, Pope Paul III remonstrated with Francis I regarding the severity of the treatment of Protestants, which allowed individuals such as Marot to return to Paris upon recanting their errors.

## Ecclesiastical Appointments and Legal Rulings
The Pope held significant power over ecclesiastical appointments and the status of the clergy. Pope Innocent XI granted the cardinal's hat to William Egon in 1686. However, the papacy also exercised veto power; the pope interposed a veto against William Egon's election as coadjutor-archbishop of Cologne at the request of the emperor.

In the realm of scholarship and church office, Pope Innocent VIII nominated Ermolao Barbaro to the office of patriarch of Aquileia in 1491, though the Venetian senate refused to ratify this appointment. Additionally, the papacy provided legal releases for clergy; for example, Antoine Alexandre Barbier was released from his priest's orders by the pope in 1801.

The papacy also issued bulls to settle jurisdictional disputes. In 1411, Pope John XXIII issued a bull that recalled a previous bull by Pope Boniface IX, thereby removing the exemption of the university of Oxford from the archbishop's authority.
