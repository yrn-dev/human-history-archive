# Uniate Congregations and Churches

Within the broader landscape of Eastern Christianity, there exist various small congregations and ecclesiastical bodies known as "Uniat." These groups are characterized by their relationship with the Roman Catholic Church while maintaining specific Eastern traditions.

## Uniat Basilians
Outside the Orthodox Church, there are small congregations of Uniat Basilians. These are associated with Catholic Basilian monasteries located in several regions, including Rumania, Galicia, Hungary, and Poland, as well as Grottaferrata. Additionally, the Uniat Syrians, also known as Melchites, include Basilian elements. 

The Basilian tradition includes a history of nuns, beginning with St. Macrina, the sister of St. Basil, who established a nunery under his direction. These nuns are devoted to a purely contemplative life and are numerous throughout the East. In Russia, there are approximately one hundred such nunneries, where nuns are prohibited from taking final vows until they reach the age of sixty.

## The Uniate Church in Macedonia
In Macedonia, a "uniate" church exists among a certain number of Bulgars, specifically in areas such as Kukush and elsewhere. This church is distinguished by its acceptance of the dogma and authority of Rome, though it preserves its own distinct characteristics. 

In terms of population, the small Catholic minority in Macedonia is composed primarily of Uniate Bulgarians, numbering approximately 3,600. These individuals mainly occupy the districts of Doiran and Kukush.

## Historical Development and Political Context
The emergence of the Bulgarian Uniate church was linked to the national and ecclesiastical struggles of the 19th century. During a period of opposition to the ecclesiastical ascendancy of the Greeks, some Bulgarian leaders entered into negotiations with Rome. This resulted in the recognition of a Bulgarian Uniate church by the Porte. In 1861, the pope nominated a bishop for this church, though this individual was mysteriously deported to Russia a few days after his consecration.

The religious landscape of the region was further complicated by the establishment of the Bulgarian exarchate in 1870. Following this, many recently converted uniates offered their allegiance to the exarch.

## Administrative Classification
Under the Turkish government's system of classification, which used religion rather than nationality as the basis for organization, different religious communities were organized into *millets*. The Catholics, including the Uniates, were categorized under the *Katolik* millet. This system stood in contrast to the *Rum* (Roman/Greek) millet, which included those acknowledging the authority of the Oecumenical patriarch, and the Bulgar millet, which comprised those accepting the rule of the exarchate.
