# The General Physiognomy of the Indian Flora

The general physiognomy of the Indian flora is primarily determined by the conditions of humidity within the climate. This environmental factor creates a stark contrast between different regions of the subcontinent. In areas such as the west coast of the Indian peninsula, Ceylon, eastern Bengal, and the Malay peninsula, the flora is characterized by impenetrable, shady forests. Conversely, the drier regions of the north-western Himalaya and central India are characterized by districts that are more loosely timbered.

## Regional Variations and Forest Types
The forest areas of India exhibit a wide range of growth patterns based on local conditions. The Tarai jungles, located at the foot of the eastern Himalaya, are known for their luxuriant growth and dense vegetation. In contrast, wide stretches of loosely-timbered country are a prevailing feature in parts of Madras and the Central Provinces. In the lowlands, the flora is further modified by human activity and water availability; where these areas are highly cultivated, they are adorned with planted wood, whereas areas cut off from rain are nearly completely desert.

## Composition and Diversity of Species
The region of south-eastern Asia—which encompasses the Malayan peninsula and islands, as well as India from approximately the 105th to the 65th meridian—was estimated by Sir J.D. Hooker to contain between 12,000 and 15,000 indigenous plant species. This region is a heterogeneous assemblage of alpine, temperate, and tropical plants. While there is significant variation between arid and humid regions, as well as between the plains and higher mountain ranges, the tropical plants are dominant and define the character of the flora as a whole.

The Indian flora is noted for providing a more general and complete illustration of almost all the world's chief natural families than any other country. The principal orders, listed by numerical importance, include:
* Leguminosae
* Rubiaceae
* Orchidaceae
* Compositae
* Gramineae
* Euphorbiaceae
* Acanthaceae
* Cyperaceae
* Labiatae

While Orchidaceae are present in specially large proportions and Euphorbiaceae and Scrophulariaceae are universally present, other groups vary by location. For example, Compositae are comparatively rare, and in certain places, there are deficiencies of Labiatae, Leguminosae, ferns, Gramineae, and Cyperaceae.

## Altitudinal and Geographic Transitions
The flora of the hotter and wetter regions of eastern India is continuous with that of the Malayan islands and peninsula. This tropical flora extends along the lower ranges of the Himalaya, though it becomes less marked and is restricted to lower elevations as one moves westward, where winter cold increases and rainfall diminishes.

The higher, cooler, and less rainy ranges of the Himalaya maintain a greater uniformity of character and more closely approach European forms. At the highest elevations, many alpine plants are found that are believed to be identical to species from the north Arctic regions. Upon reaching the Tibetan plateau, the flora assumes features of the Siberian type due to increased dryness, including many Siberian genera and species. Some of these Siberian forms extend into the plains of upper India and the rainy parts of the mountains. Additionally, Tibet features assemblages of marine plants growing near salt lakes at elevations of 15,000 to 14,000 feet above the sea.

## Regional Connections and Specific Flora
There is a botanical connection between the Indian region and other areas. Analysis of the Hong Kong flora shows that about three-fifths of its species are common to the Indian region. Similarly, the cultivated plants of China are, with few exceptions, the same as those of India.

In south India, certain orders are more largely developed than elsewhere, including Jasmineae, Ebenaceae, Balsaminaceae, Dipterocarpaceae, and Aurantiaceae, though few of these contain as many as 100 peculiar Indian species. The Balsaminaceae are very abundant in the peninsula but rare in the eastern region.

A distinct connection exists between the flora of Ceylon and the peninsula and that of eastern tropical Africa. This is evidenced by the identity of genera and families, the similarity of truly tropical forms, and a likeness between the mountain flora of the peninsula and Abyssinian forms. A further point of connection is the absence of cycads, conifers, and oaks from both the peninsula and Ceylon, despite the fact that the latter two families are abundant in the north and east as mountains rise to similar elevations.

Finally, certain genera show specific distributions; for example, *Nepenthes* is specially developed in the Malayan area and is found in Ceylon and as far north as the Khasi hills, but it is absent from the peninsula of India and the Himalaya.
