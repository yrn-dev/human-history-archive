# English Examples and Historical Developments

The history of English examples across various disciplines—including art, literature, linguistics, and law—reveals a progression from early forms to the established standards of the modern era.

## Book-plates and Decorative Art
In the study of English book-plates, the archive notes that no examples have been discovered of an older date than the gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon. While a hand-painted armorial device attached to a folio once belonging to Henry VIII exists in the British Museum's King's library, it is not considered a book-plate in the modern sense. The next chronological example is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585.

Until the final quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number and characterized by a simple composition that lacked German elaborateness. These were typically plain armorials featuring symmetrical mantling and occasionally wreaths or palms. Following the Restoration, book-plates became common accessories for well-ordered libraries. These later examples adopted tincture lines and dots based on a method devised by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae*. Additionally, the mantling became more elaborate, resembling the periwigs of the period. This style was imported from France, and until the French Revolution, English decorative modes in book-plates generally followed French tastes with a delay of several years.

## Literary Forms and the Epigram
In English literature, the epigram is a diminutive form utilized by various writers, though the term was used with "excessive laxity" by earlier authors. Early examples, such as those by Robert Crowley (1550) and Henry Parrot (1613), are described as worthless regarding form. John Weever’s 1599 collection is noted primarily for its allusion to Shakespeare.

More noble examples are found in the *Underwoods* of Ben Jonson, as well as in the works of Herrick and Spenser. Other writers who successfully attempted the form include Dryden, Swift, Addison, Johnson, Goldsmith, Young, Cowley, Waller, Prior, and Parnell. Alexander Pope is identified as the writer most "to the manner born" regarding the epigrammatical spirit. In more modern poetry, while many writers may have written an epigram or two, none have established a distinct reputation as an epigrammatist. Landor might have achieved such a reputation had he not written his best minor poems in Latin.

## Evolution of the English Language
Modern English is dated from Caxton, reaching a state that is nearly flectionless. During the 16th century, the southern *-eth* remained the literary prose form for the third person singular, while the northern *-s* form appeared in poetry and familiar speech, as evidenced in the works of Shakespeare. Early 16th-century usage also favored fuller forms such as *mine*, *thine*, *an*, and *none* in positions where *my*, *thy*, *a*, and *no* are now used.

The 16th century was a period of syntactic license and experimentation. Because old distinctions of case and mood had vanished before custom decided on replacement prepositions or auxiliary verbs, writers used constructions that now seem indefensible. This period also saw partial analogy in verb forms; for example, the past participles of *take* and *write* were often rendered as *took* and *wrote* to match the contracted participles of *break* and *bind* (*broke* and *bound*). Furthermore, the disappearance of inflections led to words serving multiple grammatical roles; *iron* functioned as a noun, adjective, and verb, while *clean* served as an adjective, verb, and adverb.

## Legal History and Influence
English law is viewed by some German historians as a "daughter" of Frankish or French law. This is supported by H. Brunner's finding that the germ of trial by jury exists in the prerogative procedure of Frankish kings but not in Anglo-Saxon laws. For a long period, English lawyers thought and wrote in French, and many technical terms in private law remain of French origin. By the 13th century, English law closely resembled the *coutumes* of northern France.

This development occurred between 1066 and 1189, driven largely by Henry II. While William the Conqueror intended to govern by English law, and Henry I promised a restoration of the law of the Confessor (*Lagam Eadwardi regis vobis reddo*), subsequent attempts to modernize this law were mostly led by men of French birth. This resulted in legal tracts such as the *Leges Henrici Primi*, which drew from the code of Canute and older English dooms, as well as foreign sources like the *Lex Ribuaria* and the Breviary of Alaric.

## Art and Architecture
English illumination experienced a brilliant but short-lived epoch following a foreign infusion, possibly Bohemian, at the close of the 14th century. This style is seen in the Wilton triptych and the portrait of Richard II in Westminster Abbey, characterized by careful modelling of human features, stippled gold, and brilliant coloring. Examples include a magnificent Bible (Royal MS. 1, E. ix.) and missal cuttings in the British Museum. By the middle of the 15th century, native English illumination ceased as foreign art was imported. Notable surviving works from the early 15th century include the Sherborne Missal and a fragmentary Lectionary executed by the Dominican John Siferwas.

In architecture, the church of Lyminge contains remains from before the Conquest, including portions founded by Æthelburga and rebuilt in 965 by St Dunstan. Norman examples include the church at Barfreston. Early English examples are represented by Hythe church and others such as Faversham and Westwell. The "Kentish Decorated" tracery is found in Chartham church, while the church of Cobham holds one of England's richest collections of ancient brasses.
