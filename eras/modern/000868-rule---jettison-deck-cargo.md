# Rule I: Jettison of Deck Cargo

The regulations regarding the jettison of deck cargo are governed by the York-Antwerp Rules, which provide specific provisions for maritime sacrifices and expenditures. Under Rule I, no jettison of deck cargo is permitted to be made good as General Average (G.A.). For the purposes of this rule, any structure that is not built into the frame of the vessel is considered part of the vessel's deck.

## Relationship to Common Law
The York-Antwerp Rules generally do not significantly alter English common law regarding acts that constitute G.A. sacrifices as opposed to expenditures. However, they do provide definite provisions in areas where previous authority was doubtful or scanty. Rule I represents a specific departure from the common law rule; under the York-Antwerp Rules, the jettison of deck cargo is not allowed as G.A., even in instances where the cargo was carried on deck in accordance with an established custom of that particular trade.

## Other G.A. Sacrifices and Damages
While deck cargo jettison is excluded, other forms of sacrifice and damage for the common safety may be made good as G.A. under different rules:

*   **Common Safety and Water Damage:** Damage to a ship or cargo resulting from a sacrifice made for common safety is allowed as G.A. This includes damage caused by water entering through openings made for the purpose of making a jettison for common safety or through opened hatches.
*   **Fire Suppression:** Damage caused by water or other means—including scuttling or beaching a burning ship—to extinguish a fire on board is allowed as G.A. However, compensation is not provided for separate packages of cargo, bulk cargo, or portions of the ship that were actually on fire.
*   **Refloating and Stranding:** Damage to machinery and boilers caused by an actual intention to refloat a ship in peril for common safety is allowed. Similarly, damage to spars and sails caused by forcing a ship off the ground or driving her higher up the ground for common safety is admissible. Conversely, if a ship is afloat, no loss or damage caused by carrying a press of sail is made good as G.A.
*   **Intentional Stranding:** If a ship is intentionally run on shore for common safety, the resulting loss or damage is allowed as G.A., unless the ship would have inevitably sunk or driven onto rocks/shore regardless of the action.
*   **Fuel Consumption:** Cargo, stores, and ship's materials burnt for fuel for common safety during a time of peril are admitted as G.A., provided an ample supply of fuel had been provided. In such cases, the cost of the estimated quantity of coals that would have been consumed (calculated at the price of the last port of departure) is credited to the G.A. and charged to the shipowner.

## Port of Refuge and Operational Expenses
Rule X provides a broad view regarding ports of refuge. If entering a port or returning to a port of loading is necessary for common safety due to sacrifice, accident, or extraordinary circumstances, the expenses of entering and subsequently leaving that port are admitted as G.A.

The cost of discharging cargo is admissible as G.A. if it is necessary for common safety or to enable repairs to the ship (caused by accident or sacrifice) that are required for the safe prosecution of the voyage. This applies whether the repairs occur at a port of call or a port of refuge. When discharging costs are admitted, the costs of storing the cargo on shore and reloading/stowing it back on board are also admitted, along with any incidental damage or loss. However, if the ship is condemned or the voyage is abandoned, storage expenses incurred after that date are not admitted.

Additionally, the wages and maintenance of the master, officers, and crew during the extra period of detention in such ports are admitted as G.A., provided the ship is not condemned or the voyage abandoned.

## Adjustments and Contributions
The amount made good as G.A. for sacrificed goods is based on the loss sustained by the owner, using market values at the termination of the adventure or the date of the vessel's arrival. Contributions to G.A. are based on the actual values of the property at the termination of the adventure, plus the amount made good for sacrificed property. 

Certain deductions are made from the shipowner's passage-money and freight at risk, specifically port charges and crew wages that would not have been incurred had the ship and cargo been totally lost at the time of the sacrifice. Furthermore, passengers' personal effects and luggage not shipped under a bill of lading do not contribute to G.A.

Regarding repairs, Rule XIV specifies that no "new for old" deductions are made for temporary repairs. For permanent repairs to iron or steel ships, deductions are applied based on the age of the vessel; for example, ships up to one year old have all repairs allowed in full, except for the painting or coating of the bottom, from which one-third is deducted.
