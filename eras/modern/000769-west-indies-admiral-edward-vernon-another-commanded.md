# West Indies under Admiral Edward Vernon

## British Operations Against Spain (1739–1741)
Great Britain declared war on Spain on October 23, 1739. At the onset of the conflict, there was a universal belief that Spanish colonies would fall immediately upon being attacked. To achieve this, the British government devised a plan for combined military and naval operations from both the east and the west. Admiral Edward Vernon was tasked with leading the force assaulting the colonies from the West Indies, while Commodore George Anson (later Lord Anson) was ordered to round Cape Horn to attack the Pacific coast.

The initial phase of the scheme was hindered by dockyard corruption, bad preparations, delays, and unpatriotic squabbles between the involved naval and military officers. Despite these obstacles, Admiral Vernon succeeded on November 21, 1739, in capturing the Spanish harbour of Porto Bello, located in the present-day republic of Panama. This was considered a trifling success, as Vernon failed to prevent Spanish convoys from reaching Europe. Meanwhile, Spanish privateers operated with destructive effect against British trade in European waters and the West Indies.

## The Siege of Cartagena
Following the capture of Porto Bello, Admiral Vernon was joined by a strong body of troops and naval reinforcements under Sir Chaloner Ogle. This combined force launched an attack on Cartagena, in what is now Colombia, from March 9 to April 24, 1741. 

The operation failed, resulting in a dreadful loss of life for the assailants. This failure was attributed largely to the incompetence of the military officers and the "brutal insolence" of Admiral Vernon. Additionally, the delays in the British operation had provided the Spanish admiral, Don Bias de Leso, sufficient time to prepare the defenses. After two further unsuccessful attacks on Spanish territory, the war in the West Indies died down and did not revive until 1748.

## Later British Naval Activity in the West Indies
Following the period of Vernon's command, British naval policy became more energetic and coherent between 1747 and the close of the war in October 1748. The British government took effectual means to intercept communications between France and its American possessions. In 1747, a French convoy intended for the East and West Indies was crushed and captured in the Channel by a force under Anson. Later, on October 14, a French squadron of eight ships under Admiral Desherbiers de l'Etenduere fought a gallant resistance against a superior British squadron of fourteen ships led by Sir Edward Hawke in the Bay of Biscay. Although the warships were engaged, merchant vessels continued toward the West Indies; however, most were intercepted and captured in those waters.

In later conflicts, other British officers achieved success in the region. Captain Hood, serving as a commissioner in Trinidad and later as Commodore of the Leeward station, captured numerous islands, leaving the French with scarcely a foothold in the West Indies outside of Martinique. Hood also commissioned Diamond Rock as a sloop-of-war to blockade Martinique. 

Additionally, Sir John Thomas Duckworth served in the West Indies on the flagship "Princess Royal" under Admiral Byron, engaging the French fleet under Count D'Estaing. Later, as a rear-admiral in 1799, Duckworth captured a Spanish convoy of eleven merchantmen and, in March 1801, commanded the force that reduced the islands of St Martin and St Bartholomew.
