# BASEMENT

In architectural terms, a basement is the lowest storey of any building that is placed either partly or wholly below the level of the ground. 

## Technical Definition and Common Misapplications
The term is specifically reserved for storeys located below ground level. It is considered an incorrect application of the term to refer to the ground storey of a building as a basement, even in instances where the ground floor is constructed of rusticated or plain masonry. An example of this misapplication occurs in the case of Somerset House in London, where the ground floor supports an upper storey decorated with pilasters or columns and divided up, yet the ground floor itself does not constitute a basement.

## Regional and Historical Variations
The use of basements and elevated foundations varies significantly across different cultures and eras:

### East Asian and Panasiatic Styles
In China, Burma, Siam, and Java, a specific style of palace construction consists of a superstructure of timber—often gilded and carved—resting upon an elevated masonry basement. This architectural form is suggested to be an ancient Panasiatic fashion, with similarities noted in the palaces of Nineveh. In the Mogul Audience Halls of Agra and Delhi, this ancient form persists, although the timber pavilion has been replaced by a marble arcade. 

Regarding the "Lord's Great Palace" (the *Ta-ming tien* or Hall of Great Brightness), Marco Polo recorded that the basement was raised some ten palms above the surrounding soil. Other sources, such as the *Ku kung i lu*, specify that the basement of the *Ta-ming tien* is raised approximately 10 *ch’i* above the soil.

### Japanese Fortifications
In Japan, the fortress built at Azuchi under Oda Nobunaga (completed between 1576 and 1579) featured a structure without precedent in the region. The central tower, which reached ninety feet in height, stood upon a massive stone basement that was seventy-two feet high.

### Middle Eastern Architecture
In Cairo, the houses of wealthier citizens are built in an elaborate arabesque style. In these residences, the basement storey is generally constructed of specific materials, though the provided records do not specify the material. Additionally, the city's numerous public fountains (sebils) are typically two-storeyed; the lower chamber, which encloses a well, serves as the base for an upper room often utilized for scholastic purposes.

## Related Structural Concepts
While distinct from the technical definition of a basement, other low-level structural elements serve similar foundational or containment purposes:

*   **Foundations:** In Romano-British buildings, such as those at Calleva, the foundations and the lowest courses of walls often survive below ground even after the surface buildings have vanished, allowing for the recovery of the town's plan.
*   **Dungeons:** In the context of a castle keep, the dungeon (derived from the Norman *donjon*) is a prison typically located in the lowest storey of the structure.
*   **Bases:** In a broader sense, a "base" refers to a foundation or starting point. This terminology extends to geometry (the face or line on which a solid stands), heraldry (the lowest part of a shield), and crystallography (the basal plane).
