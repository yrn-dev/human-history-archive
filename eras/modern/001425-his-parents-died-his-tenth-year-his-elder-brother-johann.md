# Johann Sebastian Bach

## Early Life and Education
Johann Sebastian Bach was baptized on March 23, 1685, at Eisenach. During his tenth year, both of his parents died. Following this loss, he was taken into the care of his elder brother, Johann Christoph, who served as an organist at Ohrdruf. Johann Christoph provided Sebastian with music instruction, though it is said that the elder brother felt jealous of Sebastian's talent.

This jealousy reportedly led Johann Christoph to forbid Sebastian from accessing a manuscript volume containing the works of great organists, including Buxtehude and Froberger. For a period of six months, Sebastian responded by waking up every night and copying the volume by moonlight, reaching the pages by putting his hand through the lattice of the bookcase. This effort resulted in the permanent ruin of his eyesight, a condition evident in his later blindness and reflected in all extant portraits of him from a later age. Once the copying was complete, his brother discovered the duplicate and took it away.

## Development in Lueneburg
In 1700, at the age of fifteen, Sebastian was forced to rely on his own resources following the death of his brother. He traveled to Lueneburg, where he was appointed as a chorister at the school of St Michael due to his beautiful soprano voice. While his position provided for his general education and the routine of the choristers, he appeared to focus more on instrumental music than vocal music.

The archive contains little information regarding his specific musical instructors during this time. Bach's musical development was primarily the result of his own incessant study of contemporary and classical composers. These included:
*   Frescobaldi (c. 1587)
*   Caspar Kerl (1628-1693)
*   Buxtehude
*   Froberger
*   Muffat the elder
*   Pachelbel
*   Johann Joseph Fux (1660-1741), the author of *Gradus ad Parnassum*, a work used for the training of all later classical composers.

## Influences and Experiences
Bach frequently made holiday expeditions on foot to Hamburg to hear Reinken, a great Dutch organist. One account describes an occasion when Bach sat outside an inn, unable to afford dinner, only to find two ducats inside two herring-heads that had been flung out of a window. He used the money to return to Hamburg rather than going home.

While in Hamburg, Bach's imagination was fired by Keiser, who was establishing German opera on a splendid scale. Although this exposure influenced his imagination, it never directly influenced Bach's own musical style.
