# Towns of the Province of Barcelona

The province of Barcelona, located in north-eastern Spain, is characterized by a diverse landscape and a high level of industrial activity. Formed in 1833 from districts of the ancient kingdom of Catalonia, the province is bounded by Gerona to the east and north-east, the Mediterranean Sea to the south, Tarragona to the south-west, and Lerida to the west and north-west. While much of the surface consists of wooded mountain ranges and deep ravines—outliers of the Pyrenees—there are tracts of level country found near several of its principal towns, including Sabadell, Manresa, Igualada, and Vich.

## Industrial and Economic Profile
The province serves as the center of Spanish commerce and industry. Its economy is primarily driven by manufactures and transit trade rather than agriculture or stock-keeping. The region is described as a "hive of human activity," with towns and villages filled with factories and the sound of machinery. Mineral resources worked in the area include salt, coal, lignite, zinc, and lead, and the province is also noted for numerous mineral springs.

The population of the province is the largest in Spain and grows more rapidly than in any other province. This growth is attributed to the industrial nature of the region, which attracts many immigrants, rather than an excess of births over deaths. In the final quarter of the 19th century, the population increased by more than 200,000 people, while yearly emigration remained below 2,000. Additionally, the province is among the leaders in Spain regarding education; by 1880, school registers showed 97,077 enrolled children.

## Infrastructure and Geography
The province's geography includes three climatic zones: a temperate zone by the sea where orange and palm trees grow, a colder zone in the inland plains and valleys, and the coldest zone in the mountains, where some peaks remain snow-clad for much of the year. Notable landmarks include the Montserrat and Monseny ranges, which reach heights of 4,000 to 6,000 feet, and the Sierra del Cadi, featuring the peak of Tosa at 8,317 feet.

Waterways include the river Ter in the east and the Llobregat, which rises at the base of the Sierra del Cadi and flows into the sea near Barcelona. None of the rivers are navigable. While roads are generally considered insufficient and indifferent, the province possesses 349 meters of railways. Major lines radiate from Barcelona toward Perpignan in France and Gerona to the north-east, Tarragona and Valencia to the south-west, and Madrid and Saragossa to the west. Local railways further connect the principal towns.

## Igualada
Igualada is a town situated on the left bank of the river Noya, a tributary of the Llobregat. It serves as the northern terminus for the railway connecting Igualada, Martorell, and Barcelona. With a population of 10,442 in 1900, the town acts as the central market for a district rich in wine production and agriculture.

The town is divided into two sections: an old town characterized by ramparts, the remains of a fortress, and narrow, irregular streets, and a new town featuring fine houses and spacious, regular streets. Since 1880, local industries have expanded significantly to include the manufacture of:
*   Textiles: Cotton, linen, wool, cloth, and ribbons.
*   Chemicals and Goods: Soap and chocolate.
*   Other: Leather, nails, cards, and brandies.

The convent and mountain of Montserrat are located 12 miles to the east of Igualada.
