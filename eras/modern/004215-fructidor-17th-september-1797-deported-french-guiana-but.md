# The Coup of 18 Fructidor and Deportation to French Guiana

The events surrounding the 18 Fructidor (September 1797) represent a significant period of political instability and repression during the French Revolutionary era. This period was characterized by a series of political purges, the use of deportation as a tool of state control, and the targeting of royalists, moderates, and religious figures.

## The Coup of 18 Fructidor
The *coup d'état* of the 18 Fructidor occurred in September 1797. It is described as a republican triumph and a victory for the Jacobins in Paris. The political upheaval had immediate international and domestic consequences; for instance, it frustrated the hopes of the British statesman Pitt for peace, as it disrupted negotiations for peace with Great Britain that had been taking place in Lille during the summer of 1797.

## Political Purges and Deportations
Following the coup, the French government arrested numerous political figures. Among those targeted were members of the Directory and the Council of Five Hundred who were suspected of royalist leanings or who belonged to reactionary parties.

Francois, Marquis de Barthelemy, who had been elected to the Directory in May 1797 through royalist influence, was arrested during the coup on September 17 and deported to French Guiana. Similarly, Francois, Marquis de Barbe-Marbois, who had been suspected of being a royalist due to his moderate attitude and opposition to the exclusion of nobles and *emigres* from public life, was arrested on the 18th Fructidor (September 4) and transported to French Guiana.

Other figures narrowly avoided similar fates. Du Pont, a member of the Council of Five Hundred who resisted the Jacobins, saw his house sacked by a mob following the republican triumph on the 18th Fructidor. He only escaped transportation to Cayenne through the influence of M.J. Chenier.

## French Guiana as a Penal Colony
French Guiana served as a primary destination for those deported by the French state. The practice of using the region for criminals dated back to 1763, though early attempts were disastrous. An initial expedition of 14,000 people, described as the most evil elements of the Paris population, resulted in the death of every participant. A second attempt in 1766 met with the same result.

The colony was characterized by a "pestilential climate," malarious conditions, and "unkindly soil," which rendered agricultural development and plantations a dead loss. French officials eventually acknowledged that establishing a penal colony on the equator was "utterly futile." Despite this, the region continued to be used for exiles. In 1823, a scheme by Baron Milius to create a community on the banks of the Mana through the marriage of exiled convicts and degraded women resulted in "ghastly horrors."

By 1854, deportation was reintroduced into the French penal code with the stated goal of regenerating criminals in new lands. However, the settlements failed to prosper, and more than half of the thousands exiled there faced certain death. Eventually, the state ceased sending native-born French exiles, sending instead convicts of subject races, including Anamites, Arabs, and Asiatic blacks.

## Religious Persecution and Deportation
Beyond political figures, the French government utilized deportation to suppress religious opposition, particularly in annexed territories. Following the annexation of Belgium and the principality of Liège in 1795, the French treated the conquered territory with harshness. Followers of the Catholic religion were severely persecuted, and churches were closed. Priests were sentenced to death or deported to the islands of Oléron and Ré, as well as to French Guiana. During this time, Catholic worship was suppressed and replaced by the worship of the "Goddess of Reason."

## Aftermath and Recovery
Many of those deported or exiled during this era eventually returned to France or sought refuge abroad. Marquis de Barthelemy escaped from French Guiana, traveling to the United States and England before returning to France after the 18 Brumaire to enter the senate in February 1800. Marquis de Barbe-Marbois was transferred from Guiana to Oleron in 1799 and owed his eventual liberty to Napoleon after the 18th Brumaire. Barbe-Marbois later documented his experiences of transportation in his two-volume work, *Journal d'un deporte non juge* (1834).
