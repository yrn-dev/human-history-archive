# Aviaries and Aviculture

An aviary, derived from the Latin *avis* (bird) and referred to by older writers as a "volary," is a structure designed to keep birds in a state of captivity. While the practice of keeping birds in cages is ancient, structures that qualify as aviaries were likely first utilized by the ancient Romans. During the time of Varro (116-127 B.C.), these structures, known as "ornithones," were common. They served two primary purposes: some were constructed for pleasure to house song-birds like nightingales, while others were used by poulterers to fatten birds for market or the owners' tables. While Lucullus utilized aviaries for both pleasure and food, the keeping of birds solely for pleasure was rarely indulged in during this era.

## Historical Development
The modern habit of keeping birds in aviaries for pleasure and the study of their habits is of a comparatively recent date. This trend began with 15th-century geographical research, as explorers encountered beautiful bird-life that sparked a desire to study these creatures at home. By the 16th century, aviaries became common features in the gardens of the wealthy; for example, the canary-bird was introduced to Europe during the early part of that century, and Francis Bacon mentioned aviaries in his 1597 essay on gardening. Another historical example is Elizabeth of Bohemia, daughter of James I of England, who as a child maintained an outdoor aviary at Coombe Abbey near Coventry. This structure featured a roof and back made of natural rock and housed various species from many different countries.

## Scientific Importance of Aviculture
Aviculture is regarded as more than a pastime; it is of significant scientific importance. Large aviaries allow for the study of living birds in a manner that is otherwise impossible, particularly for species that are rare, shy, or inhabit remote native haunts. In a suitable large aviary, researchers can accurately record and observe courtship, nesting habits, incubation, display, and moult. It is recognized that birds in large aviaries reveal secrets of their nature that they would not exhibit in small aviaries or cages.

## Design and Construction
Aviaries vary greatly in size, ranging from the "Crystal Palace aviary" (named for its shape) to structures as large as a church. While the term sometimes refers to a house room with wire-netting on the windows, it generally describes outdoor structures made of wire-netting on a framework of woodwork or iron. 

### Structural Requirements
For hardy birds, a framework of wire-netting supplemented by rough wooden shelters, rock-work, or growing shrubs is sufficient. However, delicate tropical species require a wooden or brick house that can be shut and artificially warmed during inclement months. 

Construction details include:
*   **Framework:** Iron frameworks may use stout gas-piping for strength. Wood frameworks should be painted with three coats of lead paint or creosoted under pressure.
*   **Netting:** Galvanized wire-netting is preferred. A small mesh (half or five-eighths of an inch) is recommended as it is practically vermin-proof.
*   **Vermin Protection:** To exclude weasels, stoats, and rats, the netting should be sunk two feet into the ground and turned outwards for an additional foot.
*   **Placement:** Aviaries should have a southern or southeastern aspect and be sheltered from north, north-east, and north-west winds by banks, high walls, or belts of fir-trees.

An ideal layout may include several compartments with a rear passage to allow the keeper to visit one section without disturbing birds in others. The outer areas should be turfed, planted with deciduous and evergreen shrubs, and provided with a gravel path and pure water for bathing and drinking.

## Avian Care and Breeding
Successful aviculture requires the imitation of a bird's natural surroundings. Breeding is generally successful if proper nesting sites are provided. For example, parrots—which naturally lay eggs in rock holes or hollow trees without building a nest—require boxes or hollow logs with small entrance holes. Other species may use small baskets, bundles of brushwood in sheltered spots, or evergreen shrubs.

### Dietary Needs
Dietary requirements vary by species and life stage:
*   **Finch-like birds:** African weaver-birds primarily eat canary-seed. Australasian grass-finches and African waxbills eat millet and canary seeds, with flowering grasses providing additional food.
*   **Insectivores:** Purely insectivorous birds can be accustomed to "insectile" food—a mixture of dried flies, dried ants' cocoons, preserved egg yolk, dried powdered meat, and bread or biscuit crumbs, moistened with water or mashed boiled potato. These birds often still require spiders, grubs, caterpillars, and mealworms.
*   **Fruit-eaters:** New World sugar-birds and tanagers require abundant ripe fruit alongside a staple insectile diet.
*   **Waders and Predators:** Waders require a diet similar to insectivores but with added mealworms and chopped raw meat. Birds of prey require small, freshly killed mammals and birds, kept with their fur or feathers to aid digestion.
