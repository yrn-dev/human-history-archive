# Discovery

In the context of human history and exploration, discovery encompasses the geographical uncovering of new lands, the identification of natural resources, and the scholarly recovery of ancient texts and artifacts. These processes often rely on the application of diligence, accuracy, and systematic examination.

## Geographical Discoveries and Exploration

Maritime exploration has historically been driven by the search for new routes and territories. In the early 17th century, English navigator William Baffin (1584–1622) sought a North-West Passage. Serving as chief pilot under Captain James Hall in 1612, Baffin later entered the service of the Muscovy Company in 1615. Sailing as pilot of the ship *Discovery* alongside Captain Robert Bylot, Baffin carefully examined the Hudson Strait. His astronomical and tidal observations from this voyage were later confirmed in 1821 by Sir Edward Parry.

In the following year, Baffin again sailed on the *Discovery* and discovered a large bay to the north, now known as Baffin Bay, as well as the Smith, Jones, and Lancaster Sounds. During this expedition, Baffin sailed over 300 miles further north than his predecessor, Davis, reaching approximately latitude 77° 45'. This record remained unsurpassed in that sea for 236 years. Although hopes of finding a passage to India via this route eventually faded and Baffin's findings were doubted for a time, they were re-discovered by Captain Ross in 1818.

In North America, the discovery of British Columbia was made by the Spaniard Perez in 1774, followed by the geographical exploration of the coast beginning with Cook's visit in 1778. Between 1792 and 1794, Vancouver surveyed much of the coast for the British government. The interior was entered by traders of the N.W. Company and Mackenzie. Later, the discovery of gold in 1858 led to the mainland territory being established as a colony. Further discoveries in the province included the Boundary and Kootenay mining districts.

## Tools and Accuracy in Discovery

The accuracy of early discoveries was often limited by the technology of the era. Mariners used plane-charts, which indicated the equator, tropics, polar circles, and equidistant parallels. While the cross-staff, astrolabe quadrant, or quadrant allowed for the determination of latitude with some accuracy, longitude relied on dead reckoning. Because astronomical ephemerides were not trustworthy, errors in longitude of up to 30° were not rare until the 1626 publication of Kepler's *Rudolphine Table*. Additionally, the variation of the compass presented difficulties, leading Pedro Reinel to use two scales of latitude on his 1504 map of the northern Atlantic.

## Archaeological and Historical Discovery

Discovery also applies to the examination of physical remains and the recovery of lost knowledge. In antiquity, the traveler Pausanias carefully examined a Sarmatian cuirass preserved in the temple of Æsculapius at Athens. Similarly, the discovery of the Aristotelian *Constitution of Athens* provided new evidence that affected the interpretation of Greek history, specifically regarding the work of Grote.

In the study of prehistoric excavations, such as dene-holes, discovery is used to determine original purpose. While some theories suggested these holes were used as dwellings or for extracting chalk, the discovery of a shallow dene-hole at Stone helped negate the chalk-extraction theory. Evidence suggests they were most likely underground storehouses for grain, protected from damp by straw.

## Scholarly Examination and Methodology

The process of historical discovery requires a commitment to diligence and accuracy. Historians, such as the author of the *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, emphasize the necessity of carefully examining all original materials to illustrate a subject. This includes navigating the "perplexity" of manuscript titles; for example, various biographers from the reigns of Constantine and Diocletian are often grouped under the general title of the *Augustan History* due to disputes among critics regarding their individual identities.

In the field of Greek history, the "Legendary" and "Historical" divisions of Grote's work demonstrate how discovery—through epigraphic and archaeological research—can render previous historical accounts obsolete or necessitate a more careful weighing of evidence.
