# BORACITE

Boracite is a mineral noted for its optical anomalies. It was first observed in 1787 within the salt deposits at Luneburg in Hanover, where small crystals with sharply defined faces are found embedded in anhydrite and gypsum.

## Chemical Composition and Varieties
Chemically, boracite is a chloride and magnesium borate with the formula $\text{Mg}_7\text{Cl}_2\text{B}_{16}\text{O}_{30}$. The mineral is soluble in hydrochloric acid but insoluble in water. It is subject to slow alteration through the absorption of water by the magnesium chloride; this altered form is referred to as parasite. 

Some samples contain small amounts of iron. When half of the magnesium is replaced by ferrous iron, the resulting iron-boracite is called huyssenite. Additionally, a massive variety known as stassfurtite occurs as nodules in the salt deposits at Stassfurt in Prussia. The stassfurtite found in the kainite layer is yellowish or reddish in colour and earthy and soft, while the variety from the carnallite layer is white or greenish and compact, resembling fine-grained marble.

## Physical Properties
Boracite crystals range from transparent to translucent and possess a vitreous lustre. They are typically white or colourless, though they are often tinged with green, yellow, or grey. The mineral has a specific gravity of 3.0 and a hardness of 7 on the Mohs scale.

In external form, the crystals are cubic with inclined hemihedrism, sharing the same symmetry as tetrahedrite and blende. The habit of the crystal depends on whether the cube, the tetrahedron, or the rhombic dodecahedron predominates. In some instances, penetration twins are observed with a tetrahedron face serving as the twin-plane.

## Pyroelectric and Optical Properties
As observed by R.J. Hauy in 1791, boracite crystals are markedly pyroelectric. When a cube is heated, four of its corners become negatively electrified while the four opposite corners become positively electrified. In crystals where the rhombic dodecahedron predominates, the larger and bright tetrahedral faces ($\text{s}'$) are located at the antilogous poles, and the smaller, dull tetrahedral faces ($\text{s}$) are at the analogous poles; the latter become positively electrified upon heating.

While the external characters of the mineral align with cubic symmetry, Sir David Brewster observed in 1821 that the crystals are doubly refracting when examined in polarized light. Thin sections reveal a division into definite optically biaxial areas and twin-lamellae. Through the cutting of sections in suitable directions, it can be demonstrated that a rhombic dodecahedral crystal is actually composed of twelve orthorhombic pyramids. The bases of these pyramids coincide with the dodecahedral faces of the pseudo-cubic crystal, and their apices meet at the center.

## Thermal Modifications
The optical characters of boracite change when the mineral is heated. At a temperature of 265 degrees, the crystals suddenly become optically isotropic. Upon cooling, the complexity of the internal structure reappears.

Various theories have been proposed to explain these anomalies. Some observers suggest they are caused by internal strains or alteration. However, evidence suggests the existence of two crystalline modifications of the boracite substance: an orthorhombic (or monoclinic) modification that is stable at lower temperatures, and a cubic modification that is stable above 265 degrees. This is considered strictly analogous to silver iodide (iodyrite), which has rhombohedral and cubic modifications at different temperatures. However, unlike silver iodide, only pseudo-cubic crystals of boracite have been encountered in nature.
