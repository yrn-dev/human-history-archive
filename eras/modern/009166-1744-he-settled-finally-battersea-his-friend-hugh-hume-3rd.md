# Bolingbroke and Hugh Hume, 3rd Earl of Marchmont

## Retirement and Settlement at Battersea
Following a period of disappointment and baffle in political affairs, Bolingbroke retired to France in June, where he resided primarily at the chateau of Argeville near Fontainebleau. During this time, he authored the *True Use of Retirement* and *Letters on the Study of History*, the latter of which was printed privately before his death and later published in 1752. 

Bolingbroke made several returns to England, visiting in 1738, 1742, and 1743. In 1738, he became a leading adviser and friend to Frederick, prince of Wales, who led the opposition. For this purpose, Bolingbroke wrote *The Spirit of Patriotism*, *The State of Parties at the Accession of George I.*, and *The Patriot King*. These works were entrusted to Pope and remained unpublished at the time. After failing to secure a share in politics, Bolingbroke returned to France in 1739 and subsequently sold Dawley.

In 1744, Bolingbroke settled finally at Battersea. He resided there with his friend Hugh Hume, the 3rd earl of Marchmont.

## Political Activities and Later Life
While settled at Battersea, Bolingbroke remained active in political spheres. In 1744, he was heavily involved in assisting negotiations to establish the new "broad bottom" administration. He also recommended the tutor for Prince George, who later became George III. Regarding the Jacobite expedition of 1745, Bolingbroke showed no sympathy.

Around 1749, he wrote an unfinished pamphlet titled *Present State of the Nation*. His later years were marked by a bitter altercation with Warburton. This conflict was stirred by the discovery that the poet Pope had secretly printed 1,500 copies of *The Patriot King*. In response, Bolingbroke published a correct version of the work in 1749. When Warburton defended Pope against Bolingbroke's "bitter aspersions," Bolingbroke published a *Familiar Epistle to the most Impudent Man Living*, a conduct that was generally reprehended.

Bolingbroke died on December 12, 1751; his wife had predeceased him in 1750. Both were buried in the parish church at Battersea. A monument was erected to their memory featuring inscriptions and medallions composed by Bolingbroke. His final recorded words were: "God who placed me here will do what He pleases with me hereafter and He knows best what to do."

## Hugh Hume, 3rd Earl of Marchmont
Hugh Hume (1708–1794) was the 3rd earl of Marchmont and one of the most intimate friends of Bolingbroke. He entered parliament in 1734, the same year as his twin brother Alexander. Throughout his career, he served as the keeper of the great seal of Scotland and the lord clerk register of Scotland. Additionally, he served as one of the executors for the poet Pope.

The 3rd earl was the son of Alexander, the 2nd earl (1676–1740), who had taken the name Campbell instead of Hume following his 1697 marriage to Margaret, the heiress of Sir George Campbell of Cessnock. The 2nd earl had served as a lord of session, lord clerk register, and ambassador to Denmark. 

The lineage of the earldom began with Sir Patrick Hume (or Home, 1641–1724), the 1st earl of Marchmont. A member of the Scottish parliament in 1665, Patrick Hume was imprisoned for opposing the earl of Lauderdale's policies toward the Covenanters. He later associated with the duke of Monmouth in London and participated in deliberations regarding a projected invasion of Great Britain while in exile in the Netherlands. After accompanying William of Orange to England in 1688, he returned to the Scottish parliament in 1689, became Lord Polwarth in 1690, served as lord high chancellor of Scotland in 1696, and was created earl of Marchmont in 1697.
