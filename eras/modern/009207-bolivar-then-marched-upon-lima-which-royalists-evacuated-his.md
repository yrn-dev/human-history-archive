# Simon Bolivar

Simon Bolivar (1783–1830) was a central figure in the South American independence movements, achieving the liberation of three states: Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia. Born on July 24, 1783, in Caracas, Venezuela, to noble parents Juan Vicente Bolivar y Ponte and Maria Concepcion Palacios y Sojo, Bolivar was educated in Europe and resided in Madrid for several years. During his studies, he traveled through southern Europe and visited Paris, where he witnessed the final stages of the French Revolution. After returning to Madrid and marrying the daughter of Don N. Toro, he returned to Venezuela to manage his large estate. However, the death of his wife from yellow fever led him back to Europe. Upon his return home in 1809, he traveled through the United States, observing the operation of free institutions.

## Early Revolutionary Efforts
Bolivar became a promoter of the April 1810 insurrection in Caracas, receiving a colonel's commission from the revolutionary junta. Following Venezuela's declaration of independence on July 5, 1811, Bolivar was entrusted with the command of Puerto Cabello, though he was eventually forced to evacuate the post. After fleeing to Curacao, he traveled to Haiti in September 1812, where President Petion provided him with a small force. 

Bolivar launched an expedition that landed in December 1816 at Margarita and Barcelona. After defeating royalist forces under Morillo in February 1817, Bolivar was recognized as commander-in-chief. By the end of the year, he established headquarters at Angostura on the Orinoco. In 1819, after surrendering his authority to a congress in Angostura and then being asked to resume it, Bolivar led his troops across the Cordilleras to join General Santander in New Granada. This campaign resulted in the victory of Boyaca on August 7, 1819, granting Bolivar possession of Bogota and all of New Granada.

## The Liberation of Ecuador and Peru
Following the expulsion of Spaniards from Colombia, Bolivar sought to liberate the neighboring provinces of Ecuador and Peru. He led an army to Quito in Ecuador, where his colleague Sucre routed the Spaniards at the battle of Pichincha, allowing republicans to enter Quito in June 1822.

Bolivar then marched upon Lima. The royalists evacuated the city upon his approach, and Bolivar entered the capital in triumph, where he was granted absolute power as dictator. However, intrigues among republican factions in Peru forced him to withdraw to Truxillo, leaving Lima to be re-occupied by the Spaniards under Canterac. Bolivar reorganized the liberating army by June 1824 and defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin on August 6. While Bolivar returned to Lima, Sucre pursued the retreating royalists into Upper Peru, securing a decisive victory at Ayacucho. This victory dispersed the Spanish forces, leaving their possessions in Peru limited to the castles of Callao, which were maintained by Rodil for over a year.

## The Formation of Bolivia and Later Years
In June 1825, Bolivar visited Upper Peru. Having detached itself from the government of Buenos Aires, the region became a separate state named Bolivia in honor of the liberator. In August 1825, the first congress of the new republic declared Bolivar its perpetual protector and requested that he prepare a constitution.

In September 1826, Bolivar left Lima, entrusting the government to a council led by Santa Cruz, and arrived in Bogota on November 14. He assumed extraordinary powers to address rebellions and later traveled to Venezuela to stop the effusion of blood. On December 31, he reached Puerto Cabello and issued a general amnesty decree. 

In February 1827, Bolivar formally resigned the presidency of the republic to retire to his patrimonial estate and refute accusations of ambition. However, Santander and a resolution of congress urged him to resume his functions to appease the country's agitations. Before returning to Bogota to take the oaths of office, Bolivar issued three decrees: one for a general amnesty, one convoking a national convention at Ocana, and one to establish constitutional order throughout Colombia.

## Legacy and Death
Bolivar spent nine-tenths of his personal patrimony in service to his country. Despite having unlimited control over the revenues of Bolivia, Peru, and Colombia for a period, he died without any public money in his possession. Beyond military victory, he is credited with purifying the administration of justice, fostering national interests, and encouraging the arts and sciences.

His memory has been honored with several monuments: his remains were moved to Caracas in 1842, a statue was erected in Bogota in 1846, an equestrian statue was placed in Lima in 1858, and a statue was erected in New York's Central Park in 1884. Official documents regarding his career were published in Caracas between 1826 and 1833.
