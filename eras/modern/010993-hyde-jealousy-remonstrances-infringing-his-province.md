# Hyde's Jealousy and Remonstrances

## Conflict with John Ashburnham
During the English Civil War, a conflict arose between Hyde and John Ashburnham. Ashburnham, the son of Sir John Ashburnham of Sussex, had entered the service of the king early in his career and eventually became one of the king's chief advisers, earning his full confidence. During the Civil War, Ashburnham was appointed as the treasurer of the royal army. 

In this capacity, Ashburnham infringed upon the province of Hyde, who served as the chancellor of the exchequer. This infringement led Hyde to express jealousy and offer remonstrances regarding Ashburnham's actions.

## Other Historical Figures and Remonstrances
The archive contains other instances of remonstrances and jealousy in different historical contexts. In ancient Sparta, the commander Lysander faced complaints from maritime cities. These complaints were strengthened when Pharnabazus joined his own remonstrances to the rest. This collective pressure led to a peremptory order for Lysander's recall from his command in the Hellespont. Following his return to Sparta, Lysander experienced a loss of power and comparative disgrace. He eventually undertook a pilgrimage to the temple of Zeus Ammon in Libya, as well as visits to the temples of Dodona and Delphi. This strategic withdrawal served to soften the jealousy directed against him, allowing him to regain influence and ascendency after a year or two.

While an edict of toleration was passed in 1614, it roused the jealousy of the Calvinists, which further intensified the controversy.

## Scholarly Works of Hyde
The archive also references the scholarly contributions of Hyde regarding Persian theology. Hyde authored a treatise titled *de Religione Persarum* (or *de Religione veterum Pers.*). His work discusses the Magi, noting that both he and Prideaux applied terms associated with the Christian hierarchy to the Magian. 

Hyde's research involved the *Sadder*, a work written by a Magus for popular use. Hyde noted that the *Sadder* was written in verse, whereas Zoroaster always wrote in prose. Furthermore, Hyde considered the *Sadder* to be no more than 200 years old.
