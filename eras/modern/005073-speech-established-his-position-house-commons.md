# Parliamentary Speeches and the House of Commons

In the history of the British House of Commons, individual speeches have frequently served as pivotal moments for establishing a member's political standing, defining their public image, or advancing specific legislative agendas. These oratorical contributions range from maiden speeches and rhetorical appeals to argumentative debates on national laws.

## The Rise of John Bright
John Bright entered the House of Commons during a period of significant agitation against the Corn Laws, led by figures such as Bright himself and Richard Cobden. While Bright was already regarded as the "undisputed master of the feelings of his audiences" in public settings—where his "more stately genius" was well-recognized—his initial progress within the House of Commons was slower. 

During a debate on Villiers's annual motion against the Corn Laws, Bright's rhetorical style was met with such impatience that he was forced to sit down. In contrast, the argumentative approach of Cobden was viewed more sympathetically by the House. However, Bright's position in the House of Commons was established during the 1845 session. In that session, he moved for an inquiry into the operation of the Game Laws. Despite advice from Peel to county members to avoid discussion and simply grant the committee without debate, Bright delivered a speech that Cobden described as admirable, winning "golden opinions from all men."

## George Digby and the Long Parliament
George Digby, the 2nd Earl of Bristol, demonstrated an early aptitude for speech, appearing at the bar of the House of Commons at age twelve to plead for his father. Later, as a member for Dorsetshire in both the Short and Long parliaments of 1640, Digby used his oratorical skills to oppose King Charles. 

Digby's parliamentary career was marked by both initiative and controversy. He moved for a committee to consider the "deplorable state" of the kingdom on November 9 and was part of the committee for the impeachment of Strafford. However, he later opposed the attainder of Strafford, delivering an eloquent speech on April 21, 1641, in which he highlighted the injustice of *ex post facto* legislation and the weakness of the evidence provided by Vane. This speech resulted in great hostility from the parliamentary party; consequently, the House of Commons ordered the speech to be burnt by the hangman on July 15. Digby also delivered an important speech on February 8 advocating for the reformation of episcopacy while opposing its abolition.

## Macaulay and the Reform Movement
Thomas Babington Macaulay entered the House of Commons in February 1830 via a "pocket borough" at Calne, offered by Lord Lansdowne. Macaulay's entry occurred as the party of reform began to challenge a reactionary administration that had held a crushing majority for nearly fifty years.

Macaulay delivered his maiden speech on April 5, 1830, regarding the second reading of the Bill for the Removal of Jewish Disabilities. Following the dissolution of parliament and a visit to Paris, he returned to the Commons. On the second night of the debate over the Reform Bill, introduced on March 1, 1831, Macaulay delivered the first of his reform speeches. This speech was a success, with Sir Robert Peel noting that portions of it were "as beautiful as anything I have ever heard or read."

## Other Parliamentary and Naval Matters
The House of Commons also serves as the venue for the presentation of national estimates. In early March, during a period of tension regarding the German Navy Law, a speech on naval matters had to be delivered on a purely hypothetical basis. This was necessary to avoid a breach of faith with the German Emperor, as the speaker could not reveal that the text of the Navy Law was already known. The speech outlined actions to be taken if no further increases were made to the German Fleet, while noting that a Supplementary Estimate would be required if rumors of fleet increases proved true.
