# Physical Geography of British Columbia

British Columbia is characterized as an essentially mountainous country. Its geography is defined by the Cordillera, or Cordilleran belt of North America, which the province comprises practically in its entirety between its specified parallels of latitude.

## Mountain Systems
The region is dominated by two ruling mountain systems. The Rocky Mountains are situated on the north-east side, while the Coast Range is located on the south-west or Pacific side. Between these two primary systems are subordinate ranges with various local names. Additionally, the "Interior Plateau" exists as an elevated tract of hilly country with accordant summit altitudes, located to the east of the Coast Range.

These ranges were produced by successive foldings of the earth's crust in a direction parallel to the Pacific Ocean border, resulting in a common trend that runs south-east and north-west. The province is described as a series of parallel mountain ranges separated by long, narrow valleys. Furthermore, Vancouver Island and the Queen Charlotte Islands are remnants of another mountain range that runs parallel to the coast, though it is now almost entirely submerged beneath the Pacific waters.

## The Rocky Mountains
The Rocky Mountains are composed primarily of palaeozoic sediments from the Cambrian to the Carboniferous periods, with subordinate infolded areas of Cretaceous that contain coal. Along the boundary with the United States, the range has an average height of 8,000 ft. The range reaches its culmination between the latitudes of 51° and 53°. 

Within the Canadian Rockies, the highest peak is Mount Robson at 13,700 ft. However, the highest peak in the entire province of British Columbia is Mount Fairweather, located on the International Boundary, which rises to 15,287 ft. Other significant peaks in the Canadian Rocky Mountains include:
* Columbia: 12,740 ft.
* Forbes: 12,075 ft.
* Assiniboine: 11,860 ft.
* Bryce: 11,686 ft.
* Temple: 11,626 ft.
* Lyell: 11,463 ft.

## Mountain Passes
Several passes exist over the Rocky Mountains. From south to north, these include:
* South Kootenay (or Boundary Pass): 7,100 ft.
* Crow's Nest Pass: 5,500 ft. (traversed by the southern branch of the Canadian Pacific railway and crossing great coal fields).
* Kicking Horse (or Wapta Pass): 5,300 ft. (traversed by the main line of the Canadian Pacific railway).
* Athabasca Pass: 6,025 ft.
* Yellow Head Pass: 3,733 ft. (likely to be used by the Grand Trunk Pacific railway).
* Pine River Pass: 2,850 ft.
* Peace River Pass: 2,000 ft., through which the Peace river flows.

## River Systems and Hydrography
A remarkable valley of considerable geological antiquity exists along the south-western side of the Rocky Mountains. This valley, measuring 1 to 6 miles in width and extending for at least 800 miles, contains the upper courses of some seven great rivers of the Pacific slope, including the Finlay, Fraser, Columbia, and Kootenay.

The Fraser River rises in the Rocky Mountains, flows north-west for a long distance, and then turns south to cross the Coast Range via a deep, canton-like valley before emptying into the Strait of Georgia, south of Vancouver. The Columbia River rises further south in the same range and flows north for approximately 150 miles, crossing the Canadian Pacific railway at Donald. It then bends abruptly south, recrossing the railway at Revelstoke and flowing through the Arrow Lakes in the Kootenay country into the United States, eventually emptying into the Pacific Ocean at Astoria, Oregon.

Other notable rivers include:
* The Skeena: flows south-west into the Pacific in the north-western part of the province.
* The Stikine: rises in British Columbia and crosses the Alaska coast strip before entering the Pacific.
* The Liard: rises in the same district as the Stikine but flows east into the Mackenzie, which empties into the Arctic Ocean.
* The Yukon: its headwaters are situated in the northern part of the province.

These rivers are characterized as swift and frequently interrupted by rapids, making them of indifferent value for commercial communication. Their importance has further diminished wherever railway lines have been constructed. In southern British Columbia, the large lakes, including the Arrow Lakes, remain open throughout the winter.
