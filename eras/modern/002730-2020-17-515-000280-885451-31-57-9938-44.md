# 2020 Legal Proceedings Regarding the CVRA

## The Mandamus Petition of Jane Doe I
In late 2019, a petitioner identified as "Jane Doe I" filed a petition for a writ of mandamus in the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit on September 30. The petitioner sought a review of a district court order that had denied all of her requested remedies. The case centered on the application of the Crime Victims' Rights Act (CVRA).

During the proceedings, the government argued that legal obligations under the CVRA do not attach before the government charges a case. Consequently, the government maintained that the CVRA was not triggered in the Southern District of Florida because no criminal charges had been brought. However, during oral arguments, the government conceded that the USAO had not been "fully transparent" with the petitioner and had made a mistake by leading her to believe the case was ongoing when a Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA) had actually been signed.

## Court Rulings and the Divided Panel
On April 14, 2020, a divided panel of the Court of Appeals denied the petition. The court concluded that the CVRA does not apply before the commencement of criminal proceedings and therefore did not provide the petitioner with judicially enforceable rights. In reaching this decision, the court analyzed the statute's language, previous court decisions, and legislative history. The court further noted that its interpretation aligned with the Department's 2010 OLC opinion regarding victim standing and the Department's implementing regulations.

The court also expressed separation of powers concerns, stating that a different interpretation of victim standing would interfere with prosecutorial discretion. Additionally, the court dismissed a previous case, *In re*, as "dictum" because it lacked analysis of the CVRA's structural underpinnings, history, or text.

## Judicial Criticism of Government Conduct
Despite denying the petition, the court was highly critical of the government's behavior in the underlying case. The court stated that the government appeared to have deferred to Epstein's lawyers regarding the information provided to victims about the NPA. The court characterized the government's efforts as having graduated from "passive nondisclosure" to something close to "active misrepresentation." While the court found it "obvious" that the government should have consulted with the petitioner and other victims before executing Epstein's NM, it ruled that the government was not legally obligated to do so.

A dissenting judge issued a strongly worded opinion, arguing that the majority's interpretation was "contorted" and that the plain text of the CVRA does not contain a temporal restriction requiring a post-indictment trigger.

## Rehearing and Subsequent Actions
Following the April 14 decision, a petition for rehearing en bane was filed on May 5, 2020. On August 7, 2020, the Court of Appeals granted this petition and vacated the previous panel's opinion. Following this action, a briefing schedule was issued, and oral arguments were scheduled for December 3, 2020.

## The Crime Victims' Rights Act (CVRA)
The CVRA defines a "crime victim" as any person directly and proximately harmed by the commission of an offense in the District of Columbia or a Federal offense. The act was presented as a compromise measure by Senators after they determined that a proposed amendment to the Bill of Rights was unlikely to be approved.

Under the CVRA, crime victims are afforded eight specific rights:
1. Reasonable protection from the accused.
2. Reasonable, accurate, and timely notice of any release or escape of the accused, or any parole or public court proceeding involving the crime.
3. The right not to be excluded from public court proceedings, unless the court finds by clear and convincing evidence that the victim's testimony would be materially altered by hearing other testimony.
4. The right to be reasonably heard at any public proceeding in the district court involving sentencing, plea, release, or any parole proceeding.
5. The reasonable right to confer with the government's attorney in the case.
6. Full and timely restitution as provided by law.
7. Proceedings free from unreasonable delay.
8. Treatment with fairness and respect for the victim's privacy and dignity.
