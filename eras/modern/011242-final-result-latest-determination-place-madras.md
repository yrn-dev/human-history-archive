# Madras

## Geodetic Determination and Positioning
Recent determinations in the field of earth measurement and geodetic triangulation have provided updated coordinates for the Madras observatory. Based on a latest determination, the observatory is placed 2' 27" to the west of the position previously adopted through absolute astronomical determinations. 

This process involved the measurement of arcs with a multitude of observations and precautions. One element of uncertainty in these measurements was the retardation of the current between Teheran and Potsdam (3000 m.), which took 0^S.20 to travel. However, it is probable that the final value is correct to within 0^S.05.

## Surveying and Cartography
In India, geodetic triangulation serves as the foundation for exact surveys extending as far east as the eastern boundaries of Burma, approximately 100 deg. E in longitude. By the close of the 19th century, the final links of the great geodetic triangulation of India were forged regarding the peninsula. While a direct process of earth measurement to bring Asia into connection with Europe is still awaited, a topographical connection has been established between Indian and Russian surveys. This connection demonstrates that the deductive methods used by both nations to determine the co-ordinate values of fixed points agree sufficiently that no adjustment difficulties are expected for future Asiatic cartography.

## Geography and Flora
The flora of the Indian peninsula is primarily determined by climatic humidity. In parts of Madras, the landscape is characterized by wide stretches of loosely-timbered country. This contrasts with the impenetrable shady forests found on the west coast of the Indian peninsula, Ceylon, and the Malay peninsula and eastern Bengal. In areas where the lowlands are highly cultivated, they are adorned with planted wood, whereas areas cut off from rain are nearly complete deserts.

## Historical and Political Context
Madras has played a significant role in the administrative and political history of India. In 1681, the region was separated from Madras to erect a presidency. Later, the Maurya empire, which reached its greatest extent under Asoka (264-227 B.C.), ruled from Afghanistan to Madras. Asoka, a zealous Buddhist, utilized his position to spread the faith across Ceylon and all of India, providing an early example of a missionary religion.
