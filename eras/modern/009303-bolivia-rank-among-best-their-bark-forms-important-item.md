# Flora and Forest Industries of Bolivia

## Overview of Bolivian Flora
The flora of Bolivia is characterized by extreme diversity, reflecting the country's varied altitudes and climatic zones. These range from the luxuriant tropical forests found in the Amazon basin to the scanty Arctic vegetation of the lofty Cordilleras. Due to the inaccessibility of these inland regions, the flora of Bolivia has been studied less than that of neighboring republics.

## Regional Vegetation and Agriculture
Vegetable life varies according to the soil and climate of the specific region:

*   **Puna Region:** This area is characterized by coarse grasses, quinoa (*Chenopodium quinoa*), oca (*Oxalis tuberosa*), and the potato. Barley is also widely grown here as an exotic for fodder.
*   **Temperate and Warm Regions:** Indian corn was cultivated here before Europeans arrived. Following the establishment of mining settlements, the cultivation of fruits, vegetables, and cereals followed. Europeans introduced oats, rye, wheat, pease, beans, and various Old World fruits and vegetables.
*   **Sub-tropical and Tropical Zones:** Indigenous plants include peanuts, pineapple, guava, pawpaw (*Carica papaya*), chirimoya (*Anona cherimolia*), cassava (*Manihot utilissima* and *M. aipi*), and sweet potato. Other valuable plants include vanilla, sarsaparilla, *ipecacuanha* (*Cephaelis*), copaiba, tolu (*Myroxylon toluiferum*), and false jalap (*Mirabilis jalapa*).

The region also produces a variety of hardwoods, including cedar, mahogany, walnut, rosewood, quebracho, colo, and jacaranda, as well as cotton and rubber-producing trees. Common exotic fruits include the date, fig, grape, lime, lemon, and orange. The origin of the aguacate (alligator pear), caju (cashew), and banana is disputed.

## Commercial and Indigenous Plants
Two indigenous products receive significant attention: quinoa, which is a staple food for natives, and coca. Coca is highly esteemed by natives who masticate the leaf; it is also exported for medicinal purposes. It is cultivated on the eastern slopes of the Andes at altitudes of 5,000 to 6,000 feet, where frosts are unknown, particularly in the province of Yungas and the departments of La Paz and Cochabamba.

Other commercially valuable plants include coffee of the best flavor in the warmer eastern Andes, and superior grade cacao grown in the warm Andean valleys of Cochabamba and La Paz, as well as in the department of Beni. Tobacco of fair quality is produced in the east and the *yungas* valleys. In eastern Bolivia, sugar-cane is grown; however, due to limited demand for sugar, it is primarily used to manufacture rum. Rice is also grown in eastern tropical districts, though production does not meet local needs.

## Forest Industries and Cinchona Bark
The industries yielding the greatest immediate profit to Bolivia are those involving the exploitation of forest products. 

**Rubber:** Rubber-collecting began between 1880 and 1890. Before Bolivia's best rubber forests were transferred to Brazil in 1903, the registered annual output reached nearly 3,500 metric tons. Rubber-producing *Hevea* can still be found in extensive forest areas on the Madre de Dios and the Beni.

**Cinchona (Quina or Calisaya):** The collecting of cinchona bark is one of Bolivia's oldest forest industries. Quina, or calisaya, is a natural product of the eastern Andes, found at altitudes between 3,000 and 9,000 feet above sea-level. The calisaya trees of Bolivia rank among the best, and their bark is an important item in the country's foreign trade. The Bolivian product is considered the best because it yields a high percentage of quinine sulphate. However, the industry is destructive in its methods of collection, and the natural sources and areas of cinchona forests are steadily diminishing.
