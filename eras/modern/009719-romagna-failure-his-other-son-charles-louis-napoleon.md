# Romagna and the Bonaparte Family

## Insurrection in the Romagna
During the period of 1830-1831, the Romagna region was the site of insurrectionary activity. Pierre Napoleon, the son of Lucien Bonaparte, joined the insurrectionary bands in the Romagna at the age of fifteen. This period marked the beginning of a life of adventure for Pierre, which subsequently took him to Colombia in 1832 to join General Santander and to the United States to join his uncle Joseph.

## The Life and Career of Pierre Napoleon
Born in Rome on September 12, 1815, Pierre Napoleon's early political involvement in the Romagna was followed by a period of instability. After his travels, he returned to Rome, where he was imprisoned by order of the pope between 1835 and 1836, eventually seeking refuge in England. 

He returned to France during the revolution of 1848 and was elected as a deputy for Corsica to the Constituent Assembly. In this role, he identified as an "out-and-out republican," aligning himself with socialists and supporting national workshops while opposing the *loi Falloux*. His political stance helped provide popular confidence to his cousin, Louis Napoleon (Napoleon III). Although Pierre initially disapproved of the *coup d'etat* on December 2, 1851, he eventually reconciled with the emperor and accepted the title of prince. This reconciliation led republicans to abandon him.

In his later years, Pierre Napoleon lost political importance and led a debauched life, turning his attention to the publication of mediocre poems. He became prominent again in January 1870 following a controversy with Paschal Grousset. After Grousset sent two journalists to provoke him to a duel, a violent discussion ensued in which Pierre Napoleon killed one of the men, Victor Noir. Although the republican press demanded a trial, the High Court acquitted him. Pierre Napoleon died in obscurity at Versailles on April 7, 1881.

## Charles Lucien Jules Laurent
Another member of the family, Charles Lucien Jules Laurent (1803-1857), the prince of Canino and son of Lucien Bonaparte, pursued a path focused on science rather than politics. He established a scientific reputation through the publication of *American Ornithology* (1825-1833) and subsequent works in zoology, including catalogs of European birds, fish, and mammals. His academic contributions earned him honorary memberships in the academies of Berlin and Upsala, as well as a position as a correspondent of the Institute of France.

Despite his scientific focus, Charles Lucien took part in Italian political agitation around 1847. While presiding over scientific congresses, specifically in Venice, he declared his support for the expulsion of the Austrians and the independence of Italy. In 1848, he entered the Junto of Rome and was elected as a deputy by Viterbo to the national assembly. Following the failure of the revolution, he was forced to leave Italy in July 1849, moving first to Holland and then to France, where he returned to his scientific studies until his death.

## Other Bonaparte Relations
The family's involvement in Italian causes was a point of interest for Louis, the brother of Napoleon and former king of Holland. Louis spent his later years in Rome, where he found pleasure in the devotion of his sons to the national and democratic cause in Italy between 1830 and 1831, though this was overshadowed by the death of his elder son, Napoleon Louis.

Additionally, Mathilde Letitia Wilhelmine (1820-1904), the daughter of Jerome, resided in Paris after the election of Louis Napoleon to the presidency of the republic. She served as a patron of men of letters and art and performed the honors of the Elysee until the marriage of Louis Napoleon.
