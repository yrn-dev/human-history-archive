# Schizomycetes and Spore Formation

## Overview of Schizomycetes
Schizomycetes, commonly known as bacteria, are minute vegetable organisms that lack chlorophyll and an ordinary nucleus. They are characterized by a lack of ordinary sexual reproduction and multiply through repeated bipartitions. These organisms exist as single cells—which may be cylindrical, oblong, or spherical in shape—or as other aggregates of cells, including filamentous forms. 

Due to their manner of division and the absence of a nucleus and sexual reproduction, Schizomycetes resemble the Schizophyceae (blue-green algae or Cyanophyceae). Consequently, these two groups are typically united in the class Schizophyta, reflecting the view that most typical bacteria are derived from Cyanophyceae. However, some forms show different relationships; for example, "Sarcina" has algal analogues in the Palmellaceae, and the Myxobacteriaceae group suggests a relationship with Myxomycetes.

## Spore Formation and Types
Spore-formation is a characteristic method of reproduction in many forms of Schizomycetes, where the spores serve as reproductive bodies representing a resting stage of the organism. The archive distinguishes between two types of spores:

*   **Arthrospores:** These are not considered true spores, but are ordinary vegetative cells that separate and enter a condition of rest. These can occur in species that do not form endospores, as well as in those that do, such as *B. subtilis*.
*   **Endospores:** These are true spores. The process begins with a minute granule appearing in the protoplasm of a vegetative cell. Over a few hours, this granule absorbs all the protoplasm and secretes a resistive, thin envelope. The resulting ripe ovoid spore is smaller than the mother-cell and lies loosely within it.

In the simplest and most minute Schizomycetes, such as *Micrococcus*, no definite spores have been discovered; these are provisionally termed "asporous" because any vegetative micrococcus can begin a new series of cells through division and growth.

## Characteristics of Ripe Spores
The ripe spores of Schizomycetes are extremely minute and can be spherical, long-ovoid, or ovoid in shape. They are colourless or very dark—likely due to their minute size and high index of refraction—and are highly refractive. The membrane of the spore may be relatively thick and can exhibit strata or shells.

As an example of their size, the spores of *Bacillus subtilis* measure 0.0006 mm in breadth and 0.0012 mm in length according to Zopf. Generally, only one spore is formed per cell, usually within a bacillar segment. The cell-wall may change form to accommodate the contents, and the spore may be formed in long or short segments. In certain instances, the spore-forming protoplasm produces a blue reaction when exposed to iodine solutions.

## Development and Germination
Spores can develop even in cells that are actively swarming, as the process does not interfere with movement. Some forms, referred to by older writers as "Koepfchenbacterien," are bacterioid segments with a spore at one end, where the mother cell-wall has adapted to the spore's outline.

During germination, the process varies by species. In *B. subtilis*, the germinal rodlet grows at a right angle to the long axis of the spore. In contrast, the axis of growth for the spore of *Clostridium butyricum* coincides with the spore's long axis.

## The Zoogloea Stage
A remarkable phenomenon in the life-history of Schizomycetes is the zoogloea stage, which is a resting condition similar to the "palmella" condition of lower Algae. This stage manifests as irregular clumps, branched masses, or a membrane on the surface of a medium. It consists of a gelatinous matrix—formed by the secretion of cell-wall substance that absorbs many times its volume of water—enclosing bacteria, cocci, or other elements.

The matrix may be composed chiefly of a proteid substance or cellulose, and its consistency can range from a thick solution of gum to a horny, resistant material. Young zoogloea can assume various forms, including lamellar, fruiticose, filamentous, reticular, ovoid, or spherical. If the medium contains small quantities of iron, older zoogloeae may precipitate oxide of iron within the matrix. Under favourable conditions, the elements within the zoogloea become active again and move out into the surrounding medium to multiply.

## History of Study
Scientific knowledge of these organisms was limited before 1860. While A. van Leeuwenhoek figured bacteria in the 17th century and O. F. Mueller identified forms in 1773, Ehrenberg began scientific grouping in 1830 and proposed sixteen species across four genera by 1838. 

Modern knowledge largely stems from the researches of F. J. Cohn between 1853 and 1872. Cohn's classification, published in 1872 and extended in 1875, dominated the field. He based his system on the constancy of forms, noting that rod-like bacilli and spherical micrococci regularly produced similar offspring. Cohn is credited with discovering that Schizomycetes produce spores in 1857, and by 1876, he had observed them germinate—a discovery later confirmed by Brefeld, Koch, de Bary, van Tieghem, and Pratzmowski.
