# Lucien Bonaparte

Lucien Bonaparte (1775–1840), the prince of Canino, was a Corsican political figure and orator known for his early commitment to democratic ideals and his volatile relationship with his brother, Napoleon. Born in Ajaccio on May 21, 1775, he was educated at the schools of Brienne and Autun. Although he initially desired a career in the French army, defective sight led him toward the church, and he entered the seminary at Aix in Provence in 1786. However, his volatile and excitable nature clashed with the seminary's discipline.

## Political Activity in Corsica and France
Following the outbreak of the French Revolution in 1789, Lucien embraced the anti-clerical and democratic movements. Upon returning to Corsica, he became a prominent speaker for the Jacobin club in Ajaccio. During this period, he pushed Napoleon toward more decisive action and urged his brothers to break ties with Pasquale Paoli. Paoli led a conservative party that sought an alliance with England to oppose the regicide republic of France. Lucien subsequently led a Corsican deputation to France to solicit aid for the democrats and denounce Paoli.

When the Paolists gained the upper hand on the island, the Bonapartes left Corsica and joined Lucien at Toulon. In southern France, Lucien continued his work for the Jacobin cause, adopting the name "Brutus" in the Jacobin club of St Maximin, a town renamed Marathon. On May 4, 1794, while still a minor and without family consent, he married Mlle Catherine Boyer, an act that resulted in near penury and disgrace.

## Rise and Conflict with Napoleon
The *coup d'etat* of Thermidor on July 28, 1794, forced Lucien to flee St Maximin. After a period of arrest and imprisonment, he was released through Napoleon's influence and appointed as a commissioner in the French army in Germany. Disliking the post because it offered no opportunity for political intrigue or oratory, he returned to Corsica. He refused an appointment in the army of Egypt in 1798 to seek election as a deputy, subsequently entering the Council of Five Hundred in Paris.

Lucien played a significant role in helping Napoleon triumph over parliamentary institutions in France. However, this event proved fatal to the democratic cause Lucien had championed. He had previously written to his brother Joseph expressing suspicion that Napoleon possessed an ambition that surpassed his love for the general weal, suggesting Napoleon might "ride on the crest of events" during a counter-revolution.

## Diplomatic Career and Exile
During the consulate (1799–1804), relations between the brothers became strained. Lucien served as minister of the interior but was removed due to personal and political differences with the First Consul. In November 1800, Napoleon appointed him ambassador to the court of Madrid. In June 1801, Lucien and the Spanish minister Godoy signed peace preliminaries at Badajoz, which frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon. Following a remonstrance from the First Consul, Lucien resigned and returned to Paris to join the opposition in the Tribunate.

The final breach between the brothers occurred over Lucien's personal life. After the death of his first wife in 1800, Lucien became enamoured with Mme Jouberthou. Despite an express prohibition from Napoleon—who wanted Lucien to marry the widow of the king of Etruria for reasons of state—Lucien secretly married Mme Jouberthou on October 23, 1803. Upon discovering this, Napoleon ordered Lucien to leave French territory. Lucien departed for Italy with his wife and son, publicly giving his wife the name Bonaparte.

## Later Years and Death
Lucien lived primarily in Italy and Rome, where he displayed marked hostility toward the emperor. In December 1807, Napoleon offered to let Lucien become a French prince if he annulled his marriage, a condition Lucien refused. He later traveled to America but was captured by a British ship and held in Malta and England under surveillance until the peace of 1814.

During the Hundred Days in 1815, Lucien returned to Rome and offered his help to Napoleon, standing by him at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris and defending his prerogatives during his second abdication. He spent his remaining years in Italy and died in Rome on June 29, 1840. He is survived by four sons and six daughters. His literary works include the epic *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree* (1814), *La Verite sur les Cent Jours*, and an unfinished set of *Memoirs*.
