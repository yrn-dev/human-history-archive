# Relations of the Baluch with the Government of India

The relations between the Baluch people and the government of India are defined by a complex mixture of strategic military interests, territorial administration, and diplomatic treaties. The Baluch are not a homogeneous race, consisting of peoples of Persian and Dravidian stock intermixed with those of Arab extraction. The Rind Baluchis, including the Bugti and Marri tribes, are almost certainly of Arab origin, having arrived in Sind either with or after the Arab conquerors.

## Administrative Divisions of Baluchistan
Baluchistan is a country within the borders of British India extending from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea, and from the borders of Afghanistan and Persia to those of Sind and the Punjab. The territory is divided into two primary divisions:

1.  **British Baluchistan**: A portion of British India under a chief commissioner. This area, covering 9,403 sq. m., consists mainly of tracts ceded by Afghanistan under the treaty of Gandamak in 1879 and formally declared part of British India in 1887.
2.  **Foreign Territories**: These are under the superintendence of the same officer acting as agent to the governor-general. This section includes:
    *   **Directly-administered districts**: Areas including the Bolan Pass, the eastern portion of the Quetta tahsil, and the whole of the Chagai and Zhob political agencies, totaling 36,401 sq. m. Some of these are tribal areas where revenue is collected, while others are held on lease from the khan of Kalat.
    *   **Native States**: The states of Las Bela and Kalat (which includes Kharan and Makran), covering 79,382 sq. m.
    *   **Tribal Areas**: Regions in the possession of the Bugti and Marri tribes, covering 7,129 sq. m.

## Strategic Interests and Control
The British government maintains a presence in Baluchistan to ensure order and safeguard the borders of India. The occupation of the Zhob valley serves to hold the restless Pathan tribes of the Suliman hills in check. Additionally, the central position at Quetta is used to secure the peace of Kalat and the hills occupied by the Bugtis and Marris, while simultaneously barring any advance upon India via Kandahar.

Direct British control is maintained over the northern boundary, the north-eastern corner, and the railway traversing Baluchistan from Quetta to New Chaman on the Afghan-Baluch frontier.

## Diplomatic Treaties and the Khan of Kalat
Nominally, all districts and provinces of Baluchistan, excluding British Baluchistan, fall under the suzerainty of the khan of Kalat. However, it is noted that this suzerainty may not have been complete without the assistance of the British government.

Following the conquest of Sind in 1843, there was little diplomatic intercourse between the British and Baluch states until 1854. Under the governor-generalship of the marquess of Dalhousie, General John Jacob concluded a treaty with Nasir Khan, the chief of Kalat, on May 14, 1854. This treaty annulled a previous 1841 agreement and established the following terms:
*   Nasir Khan and his successors were bound to oppose all enemies of the British government and act in subordinate co-operation.
*   The Baluch chief could not enter negotiations with other states without British consent.
*   British troops were permitted to occupy any positions within Kalat territory deemed advisable by British authorities.
*   The Baluch chief was required to prevent his subjects from plundering within or near British territory.

## Regional Baluch Influence and States
Baluch influence extends into other parts of the region, such as the Derajat (the districts of Dera Ismail Khan and Dera Ghazi Khan), which came into British possession after the Sikh War in 1849. These towns were founded by Baluch chieftains; Dera Ghazi Khan was named after the son of Haji Khan, and Dera Ismail Khan after Ismail Khan.

In Sind, the Talpur family—a Baluch family—rose to power following the fall of the Kalhora dynasty. The state of Khairpur, led by the Talpur mirs, had its individuality recognized by the British government in an 1832 treaty securing the use of the roads of Sind and the river Indus. During the first Kabul expedition, the mir of Khairpur, Ali Murad, supported British policy. Consequently, after the battles of Daba and Meeanee placed Sind under British disposal, Khairpur was the only state permitted to retain its political existence under the protection of the paramount power.

## Character and Social Relations
From the perspective of the administration, the Baluch are viewed as easier to control than the Pathan due to their tribal organization and lack of blind allegiance to priests. While described as frank, open, and faithful to their word, the Baluch are also characterized by a tradition of banditry, robbery, and raiding. 

In terms of governance and economy, the Baluch are noted as unsatisfactory revenue payers due to a lack of industry and a cultural pride that views manual labor as degrading. While they are expert riders and first-rate fighting men, they rarely enlist in the Indian army.
