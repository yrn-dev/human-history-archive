# The Bonaparte Family

The Bonaparte family, originating from Corsica, rose to significant prominence in Europe and the Americas through the career of Napoleon I. The family's trajectory was shaped by the contrasting influences of the parents, Carlo Buonaparte and Letizia Ramolino, and the subsequent promotion of Napoleon's siblings during his time as First Consul and Emperor.

## Origins and Parental Influence
Carlo Buonaparte (1746–1785), the father of Napoleon I, was a law graduate from the university of Pisa. Following the French conquest of Corsica, he served as assessor to the royal court of Ajaccio and surrounding districts. Described as a schemer by nature and a Voltairian by conviction, Carlo's restless disposition led him to engage in risky business ventures and intrigue for various posts, which compromised the family's financial stability for many years. He died of stomach cancer in Montpellier in 1785.

In 1764, Carlo married Letizia Ramolino, a high-spirited woman from a well-connected Corsican family. Letizia is characterized as devout, frugal, and courageous, traits that were evident during the troubles of the French conquest. While Carlo pointed the family toward careers in France, Letizia helped bind her children to their Corsican roots. Even as Napoleon achieved world-shaking success and sent her money to impress their neighbors, Letizia remained unimpressed, continuing her customary economies and saving the funds.

The couple had several children; the first two died in infancy. Their surviving children included Joseph (born 1768), Napoleon (born 1769), and Marianne Elisa (born 1777).

## Siblings and Imperial Promotions
During the Consulate, Napoleon actively advanced the fortunes of his siblings, a practice described as clannish and Corsican. He promoted his brothers to high positions despite them being regarded as ordinary men.

Marianne Elisa (1777–1820) was born in Ajaccio. Through her brothers' efforts, she attended the establishment of St Cyr near Paris as a "king's scholar" until its disruption by revolutionists in 1792. In 1797, she married Felix Bacciochi, a well-connected Corsican. Following the foundation of the French empire in 1805, Napoleon granted her the principality of Piombino and later Lucca. In 1808, she acquired the grand duchy of Tuscany. Because Bacciochi was considered almost a nullity, Elisa's own ability and pride exerted a significant influence on Italian affairs and administration. Her relationship with Napoleon was often strained, and she abetted Murat in his enterprises between 1813 and 1814. After Napoleon's fall, she lived as the countess of Compignano in Bologna and later Santo Andrea near Trieste, where she died in 1820.

Other prominent members of the family included Joseph, Lucien, and Jerome Bonaparte.

## The Bonapartes of Baltimore
A branch of the family settled in America through Jerome Bonaparte and his marriage to Elizabeth Patterson, the daughter of a Baltimore merchant. The marriage took place on December 24, 1803. Although valid under American law and recognized by Pope Pius VII, Napoleon disliked the union and refused to acknowledge its legality. Under pressure from Napoleon, Jerome separated from his wife. Elizabeth returned to Baltimore, where she lived until her death in 1879.

The couple had one child, Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte (1805–1870), who was born in England but resided primarily in Baltimore. He was noted for his marked resemblance to the emperor. His own son, also named Jerome Napoleon Bonaparte (1832–1893), served in the French army during campaigns in Italy and the Crimea.
