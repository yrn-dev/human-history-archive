# France

## Literary Studies and Popular Poetry
In the mid-19th century, M. Ampere contributed to the study of French popular poetry. Between 1852 and 1853, a committee was appointed to search for the remains of ballads in France, and M. Ampere provided specific instructions to the collectors regarding the characteristics they should seek. These included the use of textual repetition (similar to the style found in Homer) for the speeches of persons, the use of assonance instead of rhyme, and a brusque character of recital. 

Furthermore, M. Ampere directed collectors to look for the constant use of certain numbers, specifically seven and three, and the tendency to represent common every-day objects as being made of silver and gold. Other potential characteristics of French ballads include the use of talking-birds as messengers (a "bird chorus"), the repetition of plots found in other countries, and ghostly superstitions. These ballads may also display stories of metamorphosis, a belief in fairies and elves, and non-Christian ideas regarding the future world and death, mirroring ballads found in Brittany, Provence, Greece, Scotland, and Denmark.

In his work *Histoire Littéraire de la France*, M. Ampère also discusses the "Saga," which he identifies as a special product of the intellect. He distinguishes the Saga as something that cannot be correctly designated as philosophy, fiction, or history, noting its correspondence with the Greek *logos*, *mythos*, or *epichōrios logos*. Additionally, M. Ampère has written on the legends of the Saints, though other authors such as L. F. Alfred Maury and M. Guizot have also addressed these biographies.

## Book Collecting and Binding in France
France has a long history of appreciation for fine book bindings. The high prices often quoted for French first editions and Elzevirs are frequently attributed more to their 17th and 18th century jackets than to the content of the books themselves. 

Historically, some prominent figures in France gathered significant libraries. Jacques Auguste de Thou (1553-1617), a famous historian, collected the best books of his era across all departments of learned literature, housing them in handsome leather jackets often enriched with heraldic badges and coats of arms. Similarly, Jean Grolier (1479-1565), who served as the treasurer of France in 1545 and was the viscount d'Aguisy, was a major collector of fine books. Grolier's books were bound for himself and his friends, often bearing his name (*Io Grolierii et Amicorum*) and the legend *Portio mea domine sit in terra viventium*. Grolier's collecting was driven by a desire to provide his friends with the most recent fruits of Renaissance scholarship and to encourage the best printers of his time, rather than by antiquarian instincts.

Geoffrey Tory, who served as the engraver and printer to Francis I of France, designed several fine bindings, some of which were for himself and potentially some for Jean Grolier.

## Bibliographic Works
Several works have been produced regarding the history of book collecting and bibliography in France, including Guigard's *Nouvel armorial du bibliophile* (1890). Additionally, the prestige of first editions of French literary masterpieces has seen an immense increase.
