# Bolingbroke and the Commercial Treaty

## Political Career and the Treaty of Utrecht
Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke (1678–1751), was an English statesman and writer, the son of Sir Henry St John and Lady Mary Rich. Educated at Eton, he acquired an exceptional knowledge of French during travels abroad in 1698 and 1699. Despite his high political responsibilities, his youth was marked by extreme extravagance and dissipation, including riotous orgies and public intoxication. His intimate friend, Swift, noted that Bolingbroke desired to be viewed as the Petronius or Alcibiades of his age.

In July 1712, St John was created Baron St John of Lydiard Tregoze and Viscount Bolingbroke. In August of that year, he traveled to France to conduct negotiations, signing a four-month armistice on the 19th. These efforts culminated in the Treaty of Utrecht, signed on March 31, 1713, by all allies except the emperor. Bolingbroke is held primarily responsible for the manner of this peacemaking, which was characterized by a violation of solemn international assurances and a series of underhand conspiracies with the enemy rather than open conferences with allies. This process, along with the abandonment of the Catalans, left a lasting stain on national credit and honor.

## The Commercial Treaty and Domestic Conflict
Following the peace, Bolingbroke sought to establish free trade with France through a commercial treaty. However, this treaty was rejected in June. During this period, the stability of the Tory administration was undermined by the dissolving friendship between Bolingbroke and Harley. Tensions rose when Harley obtained the earldom of Oxford and the position of lord treasurer in May 1711, while St John felt disappointed in July for receiving only a viscountcy instead of an extinct family earldom and for being passed over for the Garter. Despite a vain attempt by Swift in September 1713 to reconcile the two men, their differences persisted.

## Fall from Power and Exile
The declining health of the queen increased the ministers' anxiety, as they anticipated their own downfall upon the accession of the elector of Hanover. Both Bolingbroke and Oxford had maintained secret communications with James. Upon the accession of George I, Bolingbroke was immediately dismissed from office, despite the "fine and remarkable" bonfires and illuminations held at his house in Golden Square.

Bolingbroke retired to Bucklebury, where he is said to have written a response to the *Secret History of the White Staff*, which had accused him of Jacobitism. After a failed attempt to defend the previous ministry in parliament in March 1715, he fled in disguise to Paris on March 28, 1715, to avoid an attack by Walpole regarding the authors of the Treaty of Utrecht. Before leaving, he sent a letter from Dover to Lord Lansdowne protesting his innocence.

## Activities in France
In Paris, Bolingbroke became the secretary to the Pretender in July. He was attainted on September 10. Although he drew up James's declaration, his efforts to secure the Church of England were cancelled by Irish priests and other refugees whose counsel was preferred over his own. Bolingbroke advised against the expedition of 1715, but his advice was neglected. Following petty intrigues and jealousies, Bolingbroke was dismissed from his office upon the return of James. He subsequently renounced all further efforts for the Pretender, telling Mary of Modena that he wished his arm might rot off if he ever again used a pen or sword in their service.

## Literary and Philosophical Legacy
Bolingbroke's later life included the production of several philosophical and political works. His collected works were first published in a 1754 edition by Mallet, which Samuel Johnson denounced as a "blunderbuss charged against religion and morality." His writings include:
* *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge*
* *On the Folly and Presumption of Philosophers*
* *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*
* *On Authority in Matters of Religion*
* *A Collection of Political Tracts* (published 1748)
