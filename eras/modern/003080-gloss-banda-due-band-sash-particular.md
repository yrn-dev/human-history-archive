# BANDA

The term "Banda" appears in various contexts within the archive, referring to a town and district in India, a group of islands in the Dutch East Indies, and as a linguistic root for the English word "band."

## Etymology and Linguistic Usage
The English word "band," when used to describe a company or troop, likely derives from the French, with origins in Romanic languages such as Portuguese, Spanish, and Italian (*banda*), eventually entering Teutonic languages. This usage is potentially connected to *bindan*, meaning to bind. According to Ducange (*Gloss.* s.v. *banda*), the term is attributed to a sash or "band" of a specific color worn by a troop of soldiers as a distinctive mark. Alternatively, some sources link it to the medieval Latin *bandum* (banner), referring to a strip of cloth fastened to a pole. In this capacity, the term is frequently applied to military bands in the navies or armies.

In other contexts, "bands" refer to two small strips of white linen worn at the neck as part of academic, clerical, and legal dress, surviving from 17th-century falling collars. While usually white, the secular clergy of the Roman Church wear black bands with white edges. The term "band-box" originates from boxes formerly used to carry these neck-bands. In bookbinding, "bands" are used in hand-sewn books, where the loose ends are drawn through and fastened to the boards.

## Banda, India
Banda is a town and district located in the Allahabad division of the United Provinces in British India. The town is situated near the right bank of the river Ken, 95 miles southwest of Allahabad. As of 1901, the town's population was 22,565, and it featured 168 Hindu temples and 65 mosques. While it previously served as a military cantonment, it no longer does.

The district covers an area of 3,061 square miles and is described as the most backward and barren part of the province. Its geography varies from irregular uplands and elevated plains with granite rocks to marshy lowlands that often flood during the rainy season. The Jumna river flows along the northeast boundary for 125 miles, and the Vindhya chain of hills forms a natural boundary to the southeast. The district's black soil produces oil-seeds, cotton, rice, pulse, food-grains, and millet; notably, Banda cotton is highly regarded in the market.

Historically, Banda is part of Bundelkhand and has been a site of struggle for Indian sovereignty. The capital, Kalinjar, was besieged by Mahmud of Ghazni in 1023, taken by Kutab-ud-din (general of Muhammad Ghori) in 1196, and captured by Shere Shah in 1545, who died during the assault. Around 1735, the territory was bequeathed to the Mahratta peshwa Baji Rao, later passing to the Company via treaties in 1802-1803. During the Mutiny, the over-taxed and poverty-stricken district joined the rebels; General Whitlock recovered the town of Banda on April 20, 1858. Following a remodeling of the fiscal system, the district saw increased prosperity, though this was occasionally interrupted by famine. In 1901, the district population was 631,058, an 11% decrease over the previous decade due to famine.

## Banda Islands
The Banda Islands are a group in the Dutch East Indies located in the Banda Sea, south of Ceram and belonging to the residency of Amboyna. The group consists of three main islands—Great Banda (Lontor), Banda Neira, and Gunong Api—and several smaller ones, including Pisang, Suwangi, Wai (or Ai), and Run. The total land area is approximately 16 square miles. Lontor, Neira, and Gunong Api form a crater circle similar to Santorin in the Aegean Sea. Gunong Api is an active volcano 2,200 feet high; its earthquakes and eruptions have caused destruction, most notably in 1852 via a massive sea wave.

The chief town, Banda, is located on Neira and is commanded by two 17th-century Dutch forts, Belgica and Nassau. While Lontor is the largest island, it was considered too unhealthy for the principal settlement. The islands feature a good harbour between Lontor, Neira, and Gunong Api.

The primary commercial products of the islands are mace and nutmeg, the latter being indigenous and grown under the shade of trees such as the *canari*. Annual production reaches nearly 1,500,000 lb of nutmegs and 350,000 lb of mace. Jatti wood is grown on the small island of Rosingen.

The islands were discovered and annexed by the Portuguese Antonio D'Abreu in 1512 but were taken by the Dutch in the early 17th century. The British established a factory on Wai in 1608, which the Dutch demolished. The British later held Banda Neira and Lontor, adding Run and Wai in 1620, before being expelled by the Dutch. In 1654, Cromwell forced the Dutch to restore Run, but the Dutch retook it in 1664. The British captured the islands again in 1796, restored them in 1800 (Treaty of Amiens), captured them once more, and finally restored them in 1814 (Treaty of Paris).

Regarding labor, the Dutch cleared off the native population, using convicts and slaves to work the plantations (*perken*) until emancipation in 1860, after which Chinese and Malay labourers were introduced. The government maintained a monopoly on produce at a fixed rate until it was abolished in 1873.
