# The Ashanti Empire and the Assin Conflict

## Origins and Expansion
The Ashanti power was established primarily through the efforts of Osai Tutu, who is regarded as the real founder of the empire. Osai Tutu expanded the empire's reach through conquests to the east and west, which included the subduing of the neighboring state of Denkera in 1719, as well as the Banna and Gaman (Mahommedan) countries. He is also credited with either building or significantly extending the city of Kumasi. Following his defeat and death in 1731, his successor, Osai Apoko, continued the empire's growth by making further acquisitions toward the coast.

## The Conflict with the Assin and Fanti
In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina became king. An ambitious leader, he sought to open communications with white nations. Around 1807, a conflict arose involving two chiefs of the Assin who had been defeated in battle by the king. These chiefs sought refuge among the Fanti, the ruling people on the coast. When the Fanti refused to deliver the fugitives to the Ashanti, Osai Tutu invaded Fanti territory, defeating them and driving them toward the sea.

The Ashanti forces reached the coast near Anamabo, where a British fort was located. Despite the governor's efforts to mediate and his exhortations for the townsmen to reach terms, the inhabitants resolved to continue the contest. This resulted in the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants and the destruction of the town. The Ashanti suffered losses of over 2,000 men and were unable to storm the English fort, although the fort's garrison was reduced from twenty-four men to eight.

## British Mediation and the Fate of the Assin Chiefs
A truce was eventually established, though the king would only negotiate with the governor of Cape Coast. Colonel G. Torrane, who served as governor from 1805 to 1807, traveled to Anamabo to meet the king. To resolve the dispute, Torrane decided to surrender the fugitive Assin chiefs. While one of the chiefs succeeded in escaping, the other was handed over and subsequently put to death by the Ashanti.

Following this, Torrane entered into an agreement with the Ashanti. Under the terms of this agreement, the British acknowledged the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland. Torrane delivered half of the fugitives in the Anamabo fort to the Ashanti; most of the remaining fugitives were sold as slaves by Torrane and his council members. Additionally, the governor agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for both Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort. Colonel Torrane died on the coast in 1808.

## British Annexation and Administration
On September 26, 1901, Ashanti was formally annexed to the British dominions via an order in council. The territory was given a separate administration under the control of the governor of the Gold Coast. In the governor's absence, a chief commissioner represented him, supported by a staff of four commissioners and four assistant commissioners. To maintain order, a battalion of the Gold Coast regiment was stationed in the country with headquarters at Kumasi.

The 1901 order in council served as the first constitution granted to Ashanti by the British. It stipulated that the governor must respect native laws regulating the civil relations of populations, tribes, or chiefs when issuing ordinances on revenue, the administration of justice, or other matters, provided those laws were not clearly injurious to the welfare of the natives or incompatible with British sovereignty.

## Later Relations and Governance
Following the 1901 annexation, relations between the governed and the governing power improved. By 1906, Mr. F.C. Fuller, who had succeeded Sir Donald Stewart as chief commissioner in early 1905, reported that suspicion regarding the ulterior motives of the "white man" was decreasing among the Ashanti. This shift in confidence was attributed to the support given to lawful chiefs and the maintenance of the tribal system. Furthermore, the natives showed a marked preference for the civil and criminal courts established by the British, indicating faith in the impartial treatment provided by those courts.
