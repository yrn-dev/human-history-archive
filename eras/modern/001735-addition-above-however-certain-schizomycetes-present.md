# Schizomycetes

Schizomycetes, commonly known as bacteria and also referred to as microbes, micro-organisms, microphytes, bacilli, or micrococci, are minute vegetable organisms. They are characterized by the absence of chlorophyll, an ordinary nucleus, and ordinary sexual reproduction. These organisms multiply through repeated bipartitions and consist of single cells or aggregates of cells.

## Morphology and Classification
The cells of Schizomycetes may be spherical, cylindrical, or oblong. They can also appear in elongated forms that are straight, curved, sinuous, or twisted into corkscrew-like spirals. Slight sinuosity is identified as the *Vibrio* form, while more pronounced spirals are known as *Spirochaete* or *Spirillum*. These terms may apply to individual cells or to filaments composed of several cells.

Beyond single cells, certain Schizomycetes form aggregates such as solid or hollow irregular branched colonies, or plates. Such formations may result from displacements of cells after division or from successive divisions occurring in two or three planes rather than only across the long axis, as seen in *Sarcina*.

Historically, F. J. Cohn's researches between 1853 and 1872 dominated the study of these organisms. Cohn based his classification on the assumption that bacterial forms—such as the rod-like bacillus or spherical micrococcus—were practically constant. However, this view of constancy was challenged in 1873 by Lankester, who noted that *Bacterium rubescens* (later named *Beggiatoa roseo-persicina*) passes through various conditions. This led to discussions on pleomorphism (the mutability of shape). While some forms show different cell types during their life-history, it is generally agreed that the majority are uniform.

## Distribution and Habitat
Schizomycetes are ubiquitous saprophytes. They are found in the sea, running streams, rivers, still ponds, and ditches. They are especially prevalent in bogs, refuse heaps, drains, soil, and any organic infusions left to stand. Any solid food-stuff (such as vegetables or meat preserves) or liquid containing organic matter (such as beer, milk, urine, or blood) will soon swarm with bacteria if moisture is present and temperatures are not abnormal.

Their distribution in the air varies. In closed spaces or laboratories where cultivation is promoted, the air may be considerably laden with them. Conversely, in open country or at heights—particularly in the north—few or no Schizomycetes may be detected. Their distribution is easily influenced by wind, rain, temperature changes, and air movements.

## Life Cycle and Biological Processes
A significant phenomenon in the life-history of Schizomycetes is the formation of the zoogloea stage, a resting condition where cells are glued together by swollen, diffluent cell-walls. This results in a gelatinous matrix—composed of proteid substance or cellulose—that may form membranes on a medium's surface or submerged branched masses. These matrices can be horny and resistant or resemble a thick gum solution. Under favorable conditions, the elements within the zoogloea become active and move back into the surrounding medium to grow and multiply.

Schizomycetes also produce spores. First discovered by Cohn in 1857 and confirmed by others including Koch and Brefeld in 1876, these spores are highly resistant to cold, heat, and desiccation. They can remain dormant for years or germinate within a few hours in a suitable medium. During germination, the spore swells and elongates, eventually growing into the typical form of the specific Schizomycete. The conditions for spore formation vary by species; aerobic species require a free supply of oxygen, while anaerobic species require little.

## Ecological and Medical Roles
Schizomycetes serve various roles as both parasites and beneficial organisms. As parasites, they inhabit the organs of animals and humans. They exist in every part of the animal alimentary canal, except perhaps where acid secretions prevail. While some may cause incidental damage, such as destroying teeth, the vast majority are not pathogenic and are indispensable aids in natural operations.

In agriculture and industry, they are vital for carbon-assimilation, fixing free nitrogen, and various fermentation processes. These include the preparation of tobacco, indigo, hay, ensilage, and the soaking of skins for tanning.

The "germ theory of disease" emerged from the understanding that certain Schizomycetes are pathogenous. In 1857, Pasteur demonstrated that lactic fermentation depends on an organism, and by 1862, he linked the ammoniacal fermentation of urea to a minute Schizomycete. In the medical field, it was discovered that bacilli in the blood cause anthrax (splenic fever), a finding proved by Davaine and later refined by Koch in 1876. Other diseases brought within the range of biology include tuberculosis, septicaemia, fowl-cholera, glanders, and relapsing fever. Additionally, Oertel showed in 1871 that a *Micrococcus* is intimately concerned in producing diphtheria.
