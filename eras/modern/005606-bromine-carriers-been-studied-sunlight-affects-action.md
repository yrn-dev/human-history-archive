# Bromine

Bromine is a chemical element that, at ordinary temperatures, exists as a mobile liquid of a fine red colour, appearing almost black in thick layers. It possesses a specific gravity of 3.18828 (at 0/4°), a latent heat of fusion of 16.185 calories, and a latent heat of vaporization of 45.6 calories. Its specific heat is 0.1071.

## Physical Properties and Behavior
Bromine solidifies at -21° C into a dark brown solid and boils at 59° C. When dried over phosphorus pentoxide, its boiling point is 58.85° C, and when dried over sulphuric acid, it is 57.65° C (under a pressure of 755.8 mm.). The resulting deep red vapour is poisonous and irritating to the respiratory organs.

Bromine is soluble in ether, alcohol, and chloroform. It is also soluble in water, with a solubility of 3.226 grammes per 100 grammes of solution at 15° C.; this solubility increases slightly if potassium bromide is present. While the orange-red aqueous solution is permanent in the dark, exposure to light causes it to decompose into oxygen and hydrobromic acid, eventually becoming colourless. Cooling this aqueous solution produces hyacinth-red octahedra of a crystalline hydrate (composition Br·4H_2O or Br_2·8H_2O).

## Chemical Properties and Reactions
The chemical properties of bromine are generally intermediate between those of iodine and chlorine. It does not combine directly with carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen. To achieve union with hydrogen, it requires a fairly high temperature or a catalytic agent. Bromine unites with other elements to form bromides, sometimes with explosive violence; for instance, phosphorus inflames in its vapour and detonates in its liquid form. Potassium reacts energetically with bromine, whereas sodium must be heated to 200° C to react.

In organic chemistry, bromine is used extensively as an oxidizing and substituting agent, and for preparing addition compounds. Studies have been conducted on reactions using bromine in vapour, liquid, or solution, as well as in the presence of "bromine carriers." The action of bromine vapour on organic compounds is affected by sunlight, which may accelerate or retard the reaction, or result in different products.

Bromine water is used in analytical chemistry for its oxidizing action and its ability to bleach organic colouring matters. Some reactions requiring nascent bromine are achieved using solutions of bromate and sodium bromide with a calculated amount of sulphuric acid. Common diluents for bromine include water, acetic acid, hydrochloric acid, chloroform, ether, and carbon bisulphide.

## Compounds of Bromine
### Hydrobromic Acid
Hydrobromic acid (HBr) is the only compound formed from bromine and hydrogen. It is a colourless gas at ordinary temperatures that fumes in moist air and has an acid reaction. It can be condensed into a liquid (boiling at -64.9° C) or into colourless crystals (melting at -88.5° C). It is a strong acid and is readily soluble in water. A saturated aqueous solution at 0° C has a specific gravity of 1.78.

### Bromides
Metallic bromides are generally solids at ordinary temperatures that volatilize and fuse readily upon heating. Most are soluble in water, with exceptions including palladious bromide, mercurous bromide, silver bromide, and lead bromide (though the latter is soluble in hot water). Non-metallic bromides are usually liquids that decompose readily in water.

### Oxy-acids
While no oxides of bromine have been isolated, three oxy-acids are known:
*   **Hypobromous acid (HBrO):** An unstable, light yellow aqueous solution with strong bleaching properties.
*   **Bromous acid (HBrO_2):** Formed by adding bromine to a saturated silver nitrate solution.
*   **Bromic acid (HBrO_3):** Known only as an unstable aqueous solution that decomposes into bromine, oxygen, and water when heated to 100° C. Its salts are called bromates.

## Manufacture and Applications
The bromine industry is centered primarily in the central district of Michigan and Stassfurt. It is manufactured from magnesium bromide found in "bittern" (the mother liquor of the salt industry) via two methods:
1.  **Continuous Process:** Chlorine and steam are passed through a tower of broken bricks containing hot bittern, liberating bromine.
2.  **Periodic Process:** This involves the interaction of a bromide, sulphuric acid, and manganese dioxide (pyrolusite) in sandstone stills heated to 60° C.

Commercial bromine often contains impurities such as bromoform, hydrobromic acid, and chlorine. Pure bromine can be prepared by J.S. Stas through the purification and heating of bromates.

Bromine and its salts have various applications:
*   **Photography:** Silver bromide is widely used.
*   **Medicine:** Bromides of sodium, ammonium, and potassium are employed, sometimes in combination with other substances or alkaloids.
*   **Antiseptics:** "Bromum solidificatum" consists of bromine (about 75% by weight) impregnated into kieselguhr.
*   **Gold Extraction:** Use of bromine was proposed by R. Wagner and others, though high costs have limited its application.
