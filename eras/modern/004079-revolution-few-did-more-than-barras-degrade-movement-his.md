# Paul François Nicolas Barras

Paul François Nicolas Barras (1755–1829), the Comte de Barras, was a prominent French politician and a member of the executive Directory from 1795 to 1799. Born at Fox-Amphoux to a noble family from Provence, Barras began his career in the military. At sixteen, he joined the regiment of Languedoc as a "gentleman cadet" and sailed for India in 1776. He participated in the defense of Pondicherry, which capitulated to the British on October 18, 1778. After returning to France and taking part in another expedition to the East Indies between 1782 and 1783, he left the army to pursue the frivolities typical of his nature and social class.

## Revolutionary Career and Rise to Power
At the start of the Revolution in 1789, Barras adopted the democratic cause and served as an administrator for the department of the Var. By June 1792, he held a seat in the high national court at Orleans. Following the outbreak of war with the kingdom of Sardinia later that year, he became a deputy for the Var in the Convention (the third national assembly of France) and served as commissioner to the French army of Italy. In January 1793, Barras voted with the majority in favor of the death of Louis XVI.

During his missions to the south-east districts of France, Barras became acquainted with Bonaparte at the siege of Toulon. In 1794, Barras aligned himself with those seeking to overthrow the Robespierre faction. Their success during the *coup d'etat* of 9 Thermidor (July 27) elevated him to the front ranks of political influence.

## The Directory and Relationship with Bonaparte
In 1795, the Convention appointed Barras to command troops defending it against the malcontent National Guards of Paris. Barras nominated Bonaparte as one of his subalterns, leading to the vigorous measures that dispersed royalists and malcontents near the Tuileries on 13 Vendemiaire (October 5, 1795). Following these events, Barras became one of the five Directors controlling the executive of the French republic.

Barras maintained intimate relations with Josephine de Beauharnais and facilitated her marriage to Bonaparte. Evidence is considered defective, though some claim Barras procured Bonaparte's appointment to command the army of Italy in early 1796. The stability of the Directory was bolstered by Bonaparte's achievements. When royalist and constitutional opposition grew in the summer of 1797, Bonaparte sent the Jacobin General Augereau to repress the movement via the *coup d'etat* of 18 Fructidor (September 4).

## Decline and Later Life
The government of the Directory became odious due to the high-handedness of Barras and the violent Jacobins. This environment allowed Bonaparte to overthrow the Directory during the *coup d'etat* of 18-19 Brumaire (November 9-10). While Barras recognized the necessity of change and acted as an accomplice to Bonaparte's designs to some extent, he failed to suspect the ambition and power of the man.

Following the transition to the rule of three Consuls, Barras was sidelined, ending his political career. He had amassed a large fortune and spent his remaining years in "voluptuous ease." He is characterized by some as a "rogue" and as a man whose notorious immorality in both private and public life contributed to the downfall of the Directory. It has been asserted that few men of the Revolution did more to degrade the movement than Barras.

## Legacy and Records
Barras left memoirs in a rough state, which were later drawn up by his literary executor, M. Rousselin de St Albin. While the extent of the alterations made by the executor is unknown, the editor M. George Duruy provided proofs of their genuineness upon their publication in 1895. However, the memoirs are noted for inaccuracies; for example, Barras claimed the royalist force at Toulon numbered 30,000 men when it was actually fewer than 12,000, and he attempted to minimize Bonaparte's role in the city's capture.
