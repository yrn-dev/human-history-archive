# Kalat and British Baluchistan

## Geography and Location
The fortress of Kalat is situated approximately 6,800 feet above sea level on the western edge of a cultivated plain surrounded by hills. It is positioned midway between the sources of the Mulla and the Bolan. The region serves as a hydrographic "hub" from which the Bolan, the Mulla, and the southern Lora affluents radiate. To the north lie the Sarawan ridges, which enclose narrow fertile valleys and extend toward the Kharan desert. To the east and south are the rugged bands of Jalawan, where the Mulla rises and passes through magnificent defiles to reach the Gandava plain.

## Historical Governance of the Khans
Nasir Khan was a warlike and politic prince who fortified the city of Kalat and re-established internal government across his provinces. Following the death of Nadir Shah in 1747, Nasir Khan acknowledged Ahmad Shah (Durani), the king of Kabul. However, in 1758, Nasir Khan declared his independence, leading to a military conflict. Although Nasir Khan's army initially routed the Afghan forces, he was later worsted in a pitched battle by the king. After a vigorous defense of Kalat, a peace treaty was established. Under this agreement, the king married Nasir Khan's cousin, and the khan was exempted from paying tribute, though he was required to provide troops for the king's armies in exchange for a cash allowance equal to half their pay. For his subsequent services in the wars of Kabul, the king granted Nasir Khan several districts in entire and perpetual sovereignty.

Nasir Khan died in June 1795 and was succeeded by his eldest son, Mahmud Khan. During Mahmud Khan's reign, the country suffered from sanguinary broils and the withdrawal of allegiance by governors of several districts. Consequently, the dominions of the khans of Kalat diminished to a small portion of the provinces formerly held by Nasir Khan.

## British Intervention and Administration
Anarchy became widespread after the chiefs of Makran, Kej, the Bugtis, the Marris, and those of Wad and Las threw off their allegiance. This led to British government interference. A treaty of alliance, both offensive and defensive, originally established in 1854 was renewed in 1876 by Lord Lytton. This renewal increased the annual subsidy to the khan from Rs. 50,000 to a lakh of rupees and shifted the policy from non-intervention to one where the British government acted as a referee in disputes between the khan and the sirdars.

During this period, the British founded Quetta, constructed roads, projected railways and telegraphs, and established law and order. The administration of Kalat was further consolidated following Sandeman's 1883 expedition to Kharan and the reconciliation of the khan with Azad Khan, the great Naushirwani chief.

## British Baluchistan
British Baluchistan was incorporated into British India via a resolution on November 1, 1887. It was divided into two districts: Thai Chotiali and Quetta-Pishin, managed by a regular staff and a deputy-commissioner. Officially, British Baluchistan includes the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. While these districts had been nominally handed over to Kalat forty years prior to 1879, they had originally been Afghan and were transferred to British authority through the treaty of Gandamak in 1879.

These districts now form an official province of British Baluchistan within the Baluchistan Agency, which extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. While some districts within this agency remain highly independent, the province's overall political status is similar to that of the native states of the Indian peninsula. Supreme control is exercised by the agent to the governor-general of India and his staff of political assistants.

Nominally, all provinces and districts of Baluchistan—excluding the ceded territory of British Baluchistan—remain under the khan of Kalat, who is acknowledged as suzerain by all chiefs. However, this suzerainty is viewed as dependent upon the assistance of the British government.
