# Christian Faith, Heretics, and Schismatics

The history of the Christian faith is characterized by internal efforts to establish orthodoxy and the subsequent conflicts involving those labeled as heretics and schismatics. These tensions manifested in theological disputes, ecclesiastical divisions, and legal restrictions across various empires and eras.

## Orthodoxy and the Bampton Lectures
In the English tradition, efforts to confirm and establish the Christian faith were formalized through the Bampton Lectures. Established by the will of John Bampton (c. 1690–1751), an English divine and member of Trinity College, Oxford, these lectures were designed to confirm the Christian faith and confute all heretics and schismatics. The lectures cover several core subjects, including the divinity of the Holy Ghost, the divinity of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ, the authority of the Holy Scriptures, and the articles of faith found in the Nicene and Apostles' Creeds. Additionally, they address the authority of the primitive fathers regarding the practice and faith of the primitive Church.

Beginning in 1780, these lectures have been delivered annually (and since 1895, in alternate years) by Masters of Arts from Oxford or Cambridge. Notable lecturers have addressed diverse topics such as the Resurrection of the Body, Christian Mysticism, the Incarnation, and the Organization of the Early Christian Churches.

## Internal Schisms and Heresies
The Christian community has frequently been divided by schisms, such as the one involving the Donatists in the provinces of Africa. This division lasted over three hundred years and was fueled by the Donatists' refusal to obey leaders whose spiritual powers they denied. The Donatists claimed that the Apostolical succession had been interrupted and that the Catholic church's prerogatives were limited to the African believers who had preserved their faith and discipline.

The Donatists viewed other bishops in Europe and Asia as infected by guilt and schism. Consequently, they rejected the validity of baptisms and ordinations performed by those they deemed heretics or schismatics, requiring new converts—including infants and bishops—to undergo public penance and repeated rites. They also purified churches used by their Catholic adversaries by scraping walls, burning wooden altars, and casting the Holy Eucharist to dogs.

Beyond the Donatists, other sects such as the Marcionites, Carpocratians, and various Gnostics were identified as deviating into paths of heresy. While some of these groups were still governed by the precepts of Christianity, they were often the subject of accusations regarding licentiousness and scandalous manners.

## Ecclesiastical Discipline and Excommunication
The Christian church exercised the power to exclude members from its communion, a practice primarily directed at scandalous sinners (such as those guilty of fraud, murder, or incontinence), followers of condemned heretical opinions, and those who engaged in idolatrous worship after baptism.

Excommunication carried both spiritual and temporal consequences. The excluded individual was deprived of the oblations of the faithful and faced the dissolution of private and religious friendships, often becoming an object of abhorrence. Spiritually, the condemned feared the loss of eternal life, believing that the Deity had entrusted the keys of Paradise and Hell to the ecclesiastical governors. While some heretics found comfort in their own separate assemblies, believing they had discovered the true path to salvation, many others sought restoration to the Christian communion.

## External Persecutions and Legal Restrictions
Christians and their internal dissidents faced persecution from external powers. Under the Persian King Artaxerxes, an edict prohibited all worship except that of Zoroaster. This persecution targeted not only Christians and the "stubborn" Israelites (Bani Israel) but also the heretics and schismatics within the Persian nation. These actions reduced the number of schismatics within the empire to approximately eighty thousand.

In the legal sphere, the early church recognized "disparity of faith" as a barrier to marriage. Under canon law, matrimony was prohibited between Christians and pagans, as well as between the orthodox and heretics.

## Conflict Between Eastern and Western Churches
Religious zeal also fueled animosity between the Greek (Oriental) and Latin (Frankish) churches during expeditions to the Holy Land. Despite their shared Christian identity, the two groups viewed each other with hostility, frequently using the terms "schismatic" and "heretic." 

The Greek clergy viewed the sacrifice of French priests as defiling, leading them to wash and purify altars. Tensions were further exacerbated by differences in theology and rules of discipline. In some instances, the patriarch is accused of suggesting that the faithful could obtain the redemption of all their sins by the extirpation of the schismatics.
