# Banffshire

Banffshire is a county located in the north-east of Scotland. It is bounded to the north by the Moray Firth, to the east and south by Aberdeenshire, and to the west by Inverness and Elgin. The county encompasses an area of 633-1/2 square miles, or 403,364 acres.

## Physical Geography
The surface of Banffshire is diversified, with a distinct difference between its northern and southern halves. The northern region consists primarily of rich, highly-cultivated soil and is characterized as a fine, open, and undulating country. In contrast, the southern region is mountainous, though its fertile glens contain extensive farms. The mountains in the south vary in appearance; some are covered in brown heath, some are thick with forests, and others feature a beautiful intermixture of copse and rock.

The principal mountains are located in the south. These include:
* **Cairngorm**: Situated on the confines of the shires of Inverness and Banff, it reaches 4,084 ft. and is famous for its amber-coloured quartz crystals, used in Scots jewelry.
* **Ben Rinnes**: 2,775 ft.
* **Corryhabbie**: 2,563 ft.
* **Cook's Cairn**: 2,478 ft.
* **Carn an t-Saidhe**: 2,401 ft.
* **Buck of Cabrach**: 2,368 ft.

The county's largest lochs are found in the extreme south, including Loch Etchachan (3,100 ft. above the sea), Loch Avon (2,300 ft.), and Loch Builg (1,586 ft.).

## Hydrography
No great rivers belong entirely to Banffshire. The county's boundaries are partially formed by the Spey to the west and the Deveron to the east. While the Spey and Deveron are famous for their salmon, the other streams in Banffshire are comparatively short. Chief among these are the Livet, Deskford (which features a series of cascades), Buckie, Isla, Fiddich, and Avon. Most of these streams are stocked with trout. The region is also noted for its romantic scenery within its great glens, specifically Glen Rinnes, Glen Livet, Glen Isla, Glen Fiddich, Glen Barry, and Glen Avon.

## Population and Administration
As of 1901, the population of Banffshire was 61,488, down slightly from 61,684 in 1891, resulting in a density of 97 persons per square mile. In 1901, 499 persons spoke English and Gaelic. 

The county returns one member to parliament. The royal burghs of Cullen and Banff are part of the Elgin group of parliamentary burghs, which also includes Peterhead, Kintore, Inverurie, Elgin, and Macduff. Administratively, Banffshire forms a sheriffdom along with the shires of Kincardine and Aberdeen. A resident sheriff-substitute is based in Banff, though he also sits in Dufftown, Buckie, and Keith.

Education in the county is largely under school-board jurisdiction. The county council uses a "residue grant" to subsidize classes in cookery and laundry work, as well as veterinary science, navigation, and agriculture. Additionally, schoolmasters in Banffshire, Elgin, and Aberdeen shires benefit from a bequest made by James Dick (1743-1828), a West India merchant, who left over L110,000 to promote higher learning. This provides an annual income of L4,000 to dominies who pass a qualifying examination.

## Principal Towns
The chief towns of the county are Banff, Buckie, and Keith. Other notable towns include Dufftown, Portsoy, and Cullen.

### Banff
The capital of Banffshire, Banff is a royal, municipal, and police burgh situated on high ground on the left bank of the mouth of the Deveron. Its population in 1901 was 7,161. The town has a long history, receiving its first charter from Malcolm IV in 1163, followed by privileges granted by Robert II in 1372 and Robert Bruce in 1324. 

The town's public buildings include the town hall (with a 100 ft. spire), the county buildings, a masonic hall, and the academy. The academy features a museum in the Grecian style containing examples of the mechanical genius of the astronomer James Ferguson. Thomas Edward, known as the "working naturalist," served as the museum's curator for several years. Other notable landmarks include the modern Banff Castle, belonging to the earl of Seafield, and Chalmers hospital, founded by shipowner and merchant Alexander Chalmers of Clunie.

The economy of Banff is supported by the fishing trade and various manufactures, including leather, woollens, sails, and rope, as well as timber-yards, brick-yards, iron foundries, distilleries, and breweries. Exports consist mainly of potatoes, dairy produce, fish, cattle, and grain, while coal and timber are the primary imports.

### Macduff
Macduff is a seaport and police burgh located on the right bank of the mouth of the Deveron, one mile east of Banff. Its population in 1901 was 3,431. Originally a fishing village called Doune, the site was purchased around 1732 by the 1st earl of Fife. The 2nd earl changed the name to Macduff and obtained a royal charter in 1783 to make it a burgh, commemorating the event by rebuilding the market cross in front of the parish church.

The harbour was constructed by the duke of Fife and transferred to the burgh in 1808; it is considered more accessible and safer than the harbour at Banff. A stone bridge across the Deveron connects Macduff to Banff. The local economy is centered on the herring fishery, though the town also supports saw-mills, oilcake mills, manure works, rope-and-sail making, and boat-building. Macduff is a summer destination for visitors due to its mineral well, bracing climate, and bathing facilities.
