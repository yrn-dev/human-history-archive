# Ballet in England

## Origins and Early Development
The term "balette" was introduced into the English language by Dryden in 1667. The first instance of a descriptive ballet performed in London occurred in 1702 at Drury Lane, with the production titled *The Tavern Bilkers*. Following this introduction, ballet in England was characterized as being purely exotic, as it merely followed the trajectory of developments occurring in France.

## The Nineteenth Century and Notable Dancers
The first half of the 19th century is regarded as the palmy days of English ballet. During this era, the art form served as a fashionable attraction maintained through the expenditure of royal revenue. Several famous dancers distinguished themselves during this period, including:
* Carlotta Grisi
* Mdlle Taglioni, who is noted for having turned the heads of an entire generation
* Fanny Elssler
* Mdlle Cerito
* Miss P. Horton
* Miss Lucile Grahn
* Mdlle Carolina Rosati

## Evolution of Style and Skirt-Dancing
In later years, the art form saw the emergence of Kate Vaughan, a dancer of a new type described as remarkably graceful. According to Sir Augustus Harris, Vaughan contributed significantly to the elevation of modern art. She is credited as the first person to make skirt-dancing popular. 

While skirt-dancing is considered a beautiful exhibition, it is viewed by some students of the art as a departure from true dancing because the steps are of little importance. The development of this style eventually extended into exhibitions of whirling draperies illuminated by many-coloured lime-lights. Miss Sylvia Grey and Miss Letty Lind were the best known disciples and imitators of Miss Vaughan, with both contributing to the art in their own right. Meanwhile, Adeline Genee became the finest exponent of the older, classical school of ballet-dancing in London.

## Transition to Spectacle
Over time, ballet-dancing was affected by a trend in modern entertainment that prioritized the eye—the sense most easily affected—over the intellectual appreciation and intelligence of the public. Consequently, the art gradually evolved into a spectacle where the primary interest became independent of the dancing itself. 

This transition is evidenced by the expenditure of thousands of pounds on the costumes of a "small army of women." These performers primarily march across the stage and arrange themselves according to designs of mass and colour. In such productions, the intelligence of the audience is asked only to accept the spectacle, such as believing that a ballet featuring military uniforms serves as a glorification of or compliment to the military.
