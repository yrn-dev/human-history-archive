# The Treaty of Karlowitz and the Settlement of 1713-1714

The Treaty of Karlowitz and the diplomatic settlements of 1713-1714 represented a pivotal turning point in the history of Austria, redefining the monarchy's position in both eastern and western Europe. These agreements collectively ended periods of conflict and shifted the geopolitical balance of power, marking a new starting point for the House of Austria.

## Impact on Eastern Europe and the Ottoman Empire
The Treaty of Karlowitz signaled the failure of Turkey's efforts to regain ascendancy in eastern Europe at the expense of the Habsburgs. Following this treaty, Turkish strategic efforts were restricted to resisting the steady expansion of Austria toward Constantinople. This shift followed a period of significant conflict, including the defeat of the last Turkish attack on Vienna in 1683 by John III of Poland.

## The War of the Spanish Succession and the Settlement of 1713-1714
The broader European landscape was shaped by the War of the Spanish Succession (1702-1713), a conflict that Leopold left as an "evil inheritance" to his sons, Joseph I (who died in 1711) and Charles VI. While the war resulted in the aggrandizement of the House of Austria, it did not reach the extent originally hoped for, as British and Austrian troops failed to remove Philip V from the Spanish throne. Furthermore, other European powers viewed the potential revival of the empire of Charles V under Charles VI as undesirable.

The resulting settlements of 1713-1714, specifically the Treaty of Utrecht, redistributed power across the continent. Under the terms of the Treaty of Utrecht:
* Spain remained with the House of Bourbon.
* The House of Austria received Naples, Sardinia, and the Spanish Netherlands.

## Consolidation of Austrian Power in Western Europe
The position of the Austrian monarchy in western Europe was further re-established and strengthened through the treaties of Rastatt, Baden, and Utrecht. These agreements effectively ended the era of French invasions of Germany for a time. Austria achieved supremacy in Italy and replaced Spanish domination in the Netherlands as a form of revenge for the attacks carried out by Louis XIV.

The Treaty of Baden, signed in 1714 in the town's *Rathaus* (town hall), concluded the war between the Empire and France, thereby completing the Treaty of Utrecht.

## The Barrier Treaties and the Netherlands
Parallel to these settlements were the Barrier Treaties between Great Britain and the states-general of the United Netherlands, designed to protect Holland from French aggression. 
* A treaty signed on October 29, 1709, established a barrier of several towns, including Lille, Tournai, and Namur, while the Dutch guaranteed the Protestant succession in England for the house of Hanover.
* A second Barrier Treaty, signed on January 29, 1713, reduced the number of strong places to include the citadel of Ghent, Ypres, Menin, Tournai, Mons, Charleroi, Furnes, and the fort of Knocke. Great Britain committed to securing the right for the Dutch to garrison these locations from the future sovereign of the Spanish Netherlands. These terms were later included in the Treaty of Rastatt (signed March 7, 1714) between France and the emperor.
* A third Barrier Treaty followed in November 1715.

## British Territorial and Political Context
During this period of settlement, Great Britain acquired several territories. In 1713, Nova Scotia was recovered and St. Christopher became wholly British. The Treaty of Utrecht was signed on March 31, 1713, by all allies except the emperor. The peacemaking process was controversial in England; the Whigs and figures such as the actor Booth demonstrated indignation against the peace, and the abandonment of the Catalans was viewed as dishonourable. Much of the responsibility for the manner of these negotiations was attributed to Bolingbroke.
