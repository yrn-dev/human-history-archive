# Victoria Nyanza

Victoria Nyanza is a large lake situated within a basin of lower elevation in the western portion of the East African plateau. It is drained to the north by the Nile. The lake is surrounded by diverse geographical features, including high plateaus, escarpments, and various linguistic regions.

## Geography and Topography
The region surrounding Victoria Nyanza is characterized by significant elevation changes. To the east of the lake lies the Mau plateau, which reaches a height of 9,000 ft. on the equator. The Mau Escarpment, located between the equator and 1° S., is marked by a wall of cliffs that exceeds 8,000 ft. in height opposite Lake Naivasha. The crest of this escarpment is covered by a vast forest. 

West of the Elgeyo Escarpment and the treeless plateau of Uasin Gishu—where the land rises above 8,000 ft.—is the mountain mass of Elgon. To the south of the Mau plateau, the land transitions into open country drained toward the Dogilani plains. The cultivated districts of Lumbwa and Sotik, which are broken by wooded heights, fall toward Victoria Nyanza.

## Climate and Flora
The country bordering Victoria Nyanza is typically tropical, with annual rainfall exceeding 60 inches. Due to these conditions, the region is considered quite unsuitable for Europeans. The flora in the districts bordering the lake resembles that of Uganda. 

Vegetation in the broader region varies by zone. The Mau escarpment is home to vast forests of junipers and olives. In other areas, such as the Kikuyu escarpment, bamboo, fig, and cotton are found. Some regions contain dense forests with great trees whose lowest branches start 50 ft. from the ground. Additionally, two varieties of rubber-vine, *Landolphia Kirkii* and *Landolphia florida*, are found in the forests and near the coast.

## Infrastructure and Communications
A significant engineering achievement in the protectorate was the construction of a railway from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza. Preliminary surveys for the line were conducted in 1892, and construction began in 1896. The railway is a mountain line of metre gauge (3.28 ft.) and extends 584 miles. 

The line ascends the plateau to the Kikuyu Escarpment (7,600 ft.), descends to the rift-valley floor (6,011 ft.), and then ascends the Mau escarpment to its highest point of 8,321 ft. The railway eventually reaches Victoria Nyanza at Port Florence on the Kavirondo Gulf, where the altitude is 3,738 ft. The first locomotive arrived at the lake on December 26, 1901, and the permanent way was practically completed by March 1903. The project was funded by the British government at a cost of £5,331,000.

The railway had several major impacts:
* It provided a more efficient alternative to the carriage of goods by men, which dealt a final death-blow to the slave trade in that part of East Africa.
* It facilitated the occupation and development of Uganda by reducing the prohibitive cost of transporting goods from the coast, which had previously cost £60 per ton.
* It opened the high plateaus to European colonization by providing necessary transport facilities.

In addition to the railway, a trunk road was established from Mombasa, passing through Lake Naivasha and Kibwezi in the upper Sabaki basin, ending at Berkeley Bay on Victoria Nyanza.

## Linguistics and Ethnic Groups
The areas around Victoria Nyanza are home to various Bantu dialect groups:
* **The Kavirondo-Masaba section:** This group includes the *Luwanga*, *Lukonde*, *Lu-nyara*, and *Igizii*. They are found on the eastern and north-east shores of Victoria Nyanza, as well as in the northern Kavirondo and Mount Elgon territories.
* **The Uganda-Unyoro group:** This group encompasses dialects on the south coast of Victoria Nyanza and the inhabited islands of the lake, extending as far east as Bukerebe. The languages of the west coast of Lake Victoria, the Sese Islands, and Busoga are closely related to the language of the kingdom of Uganda.

## Military History
During military operations involving British, Belgian, and German forces, strong British columns moved southward to the west of Victoria Nyanza. Upon reaching the southern shores of the lake, a concerted forward movement was launched by British and Belgian columns from both Tanganyika and Victoria Nyanza. This movement continued until the occupation of Tabora and the central railway in early September 1916.
