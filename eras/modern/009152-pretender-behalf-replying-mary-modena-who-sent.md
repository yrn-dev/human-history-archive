# Lord Bolingbroke and the Pretender

## Political Transition and Exile
Following the accession of George I, Lord Bolingbroke was immediately dismissed from his office. While the celebrations at his house in Golden Square, featuring bonfires and illuminations, were noted as "particularly fine and remarkable," his political standing shifted rapidly. Bolingbroke retired to Bucklebury, where it is said he wrote a response to the *Secret History of the White Staff*, a work that had accused him of Jacobitism.

In March 1715, Bolingbroke attempted to defend the previous ministry within the new parliament, though these efforts were in vain. Upon the announcement that Walpole intended to attack the authors of the treaty of Utrecht, Bolingbroke fled to Paris in disguise on March 28, 1715. Before leaving, he sent a letter from Dover to Lord Lansdowne, in which he protested his innocence and challenged his "most inveterate of his enemies" to provide any evidence of criminal correspondence.

## Service to the Pretender
Upon arriving in Paris, Bolingbroke was well received. By July, he had fully identified himself with the interests of the Pretender and subsequently became the Pretender's secretary. This alignment led to his attainder on September 10.

Despite his official role, Bolingbroke found that his counsel was neglected in favor of Irish priests and "ignorant refugees." This tension was evident in the planning of the 1715 expedition, which was decided upon against Bolingbroke's advice. While Bolingbroke drew up the declaration for James, the priests cancelled the assurances he had included regarding the security of the Church of England.

## Dismissal and Renunciation
Bolingbroke remained in Paris, where he attempted to establish relations with the regent. However, due to jealousies and "petty intrigues," he was eventually dismissed from his office upon the return of James.

Following this dismissal, Bolingbroke renounced all further efforts on behalf of the Pretender. His break from the cause was marked by a stark response to Mary of Modena, who had sent him a message deprecating his ill-will. In his reply, Bolingbroke expressed such a strong desire to sever ties that he wished his arm might rot off if he ever used a sword or pen in their service again.
