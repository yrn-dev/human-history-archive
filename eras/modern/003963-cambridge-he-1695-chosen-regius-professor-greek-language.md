# Regius Professor of Greek at Cambridge

The Regius Professorship of Greek at the University of Cambridge is a distinguished academic chair held by several noted classical scholars over the centuries. The position has been occupied by individuals who contributed to the study of Greek orators, poets, and philosophers, as well as those who applied their linguistic expertise to the revision of sacred texts.

## Early Appointments and the 17th Century
In the late 16th century, Andrew Downes (c. 1549–1628), a scholar educated at Shrewsbury and St John's College, Cambridge, was appointed to the regius professorship of Greek in 1585. Downes held the position for nearly forty years and is credited with reviving the study of Greek at the university during a period when it was at a very low ebb. He was regarded as "the ablest Grecian of Christendom" and focused his scholarly attention on Greek orators, editing Lysias' *Pro caede Eratosthenis* and writing *Praelectiones in Philippicam de pace Demosthenis*.

In 1639, James Duport (1606–1679) was appointed regius professor of Greek. Despite the disruptions of the Civil War, which led to the loss of his professorship and clerical offices, Duport continued to deliver his lectures. He is best remembered for his *Homeri gnomologia* (1660), a collection of maxims and opinions from the *Odyssey* and *Iliad*.

Following Duport, the professorship was held by others including an individual appointed in 1660 who initially intended to lecture on the tragedies of Sophocles but instead chose Aristotle's rhetoric. These lectures were subsequently lost. Joshua Barnes (1654–1712), an English scholar educated at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, was chosen as the regius professor of Greek in 1695. Barnes was noted for speaking and writing the language with "the utmost facility" and published editions of Homer (1711) and Euripides (1694).

## Later Scholars and 19th Century Appointments
In the 19th century, the chair was held by Benjamin Hall Kennedy (1804–1889). Educated at St John's College, Cambridge, Kennedy had a distinguished career as a classical lecturer and headmaster of Shrewsbury before being elected regius professor of Greek at Cambridge in 1867. In addition to his professorship, he served as a canon of Ely and was a member of the committee for the revision of the New Testament from 1870 to 1880. He was also a proponent of university education for women, contributing to the establishment of Girton and Newnham colleges.

## Relation to Biblical Scholarship
The expertise of Greek professors at Cambridge and Oxford has frequently been utilized in the translation and revision of the Bible. During the appointment of revisers for the "authorized" version, the list of scholars included nine individuals who were professors of Greek or Hebrew at Oxford or Cambridge. Andrew Downes, for instance, served as one of the seven translators of the *Apocrypha* and was among the six learned men appointed to revise the new version after its completion.

## Other Academic Contributions
Scholars associated with Greek studies at Cambridge often held multiple roles or transitioned between disciplines. For example, John Bagnell Bury (1861–), though primarily a historian of the later Roman Empire, was a fine Greek scholar who edited Pindar's *Isthmian* and *Nemean Odes*. While he served as the regius professor of Greek in Dublin starting in 1898, he later moved to the university of Cambridge in 1902, where he was elected regius professor of modern history and a fellow of King's College.
