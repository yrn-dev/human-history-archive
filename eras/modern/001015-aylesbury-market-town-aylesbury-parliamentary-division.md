# AYLESBURY

Aylesbury is a market town situated in the Mid or Aylesbury parliamentary division of Buckinghamshire, England. Located 38 miles northwest by west of London, the town sits on a slight eminence within a fertile tract known as the Vale of Aylesbury, which extends northward from the foot of the Chiltern Hills. As of 1901, the population of the urban district was 9,243.

## History and Administration
In Saxon times, Aylesbury (historically referred to as Aeylesburge, Eilesberia, or Aillesbir) was known as the supposed burial place of St Osith. In A.D. 571, the town was among those captured by Cuthwulf, the brother of Ceawlin, king of the Saxons. Records from the Domesday survey indicate that the king owned the manor at that time. The town's early commercial importance is evidenced by its market tolls, which were assessed at £25 during the time of Edward the Confessor and £10 at the time of the Domesday survey.

In 1554, Queen Mary issued a charter constituting Aylesbury as a free borough corporate. This was granted as a reward for fidelity during the rebellion of the duke of Northumberland. The charter established a common council comprising a bailiff, 12 chief burgesses, and 10 aldermen. From 1554 until the Redistribution Act of 1885, the borough returned two members to parliament, although other privileges appear to have lapsed during the reign of Elizabeth.

Administratively, Aylesbury serves as the assize town for the county, and the shire court of the former county was held there. While Buckingham is the county town, Aylesbury remains a central administrative hub. The hundred of Aylesbury is still designated as the "three hundreds of Aylesbury," a name reflecting the union of three ancient hundreds.

## Architecture and Landmarks
The town's streets are characterized as picturesque, though they are mostly irregular and narrow. The principal public buildings are the town-hall and the county-hall, both of which overlook a broad market-place. A grammar school was established in the town in 1611.

The church of St Mary is a large cruciform building. While primarily Early English in style, it features numerous additions from later periods. Notable architectural elements include:
* A transitional Norman font.
* A small pre-Norman crypt located beneath part of the church.
* Chancel stalls dating from the 15th century and several Decorated canopied tombs.
* An ornate clock-turret from the second half of the 17th century atop the central tower.

In the vicinity of Aylesbury is Hartwell, a Jacobean mansion that served as the residence for the French king Louis XVIII during his exile from 1810 to 1814.

## Economy and Industry
Aylesbury is noted for a large agricultural trade, benefiting from the extremely fertile soil of the Vale of Aylesbury. The district is especially famous for the rearing of ducks. Other local industries include the manufacture of condensed milk, printing works, and straw-plaiting.

The town's history as a center of trade is supported by various grants. In 1239, Henry III granted John, son of Geoffrey FitzPeter, an annual fair on the feast of St Osith (June 3rd), which was later confirmed by Henry VI in 1440. Queen Mary's charter instituted fairs at the Invention of the Holy Cross and the Annunciation, as well as a Wednesday market. In 1579, John Pakington obtained a grant for a Monday market for the sale of merchandise, grain, and horses and other animals, along with two annual fairs.

## Transport and Communications
Aylesbury is served by several railway lines, including the Great Western, Metropolitan, and Great Central railways, which utilize a common station. Additionally, it is served by a branch of the London & North-Western railway. Specific rail connections include a branch from Cheddington and a branch from Prince's Risborough. The Metropolitan-Great Central joint line serves Aylesbury and joins the North-Western Oxford branch at Verney Junction.

For water transport, the town is connected by a branch of the Grand Junction canal.
