# The Invisible Backbone: The Geopolitics of Subsea Cables

While previous installments of this series have focused on the logical architecture of the internet—TCP/IP, DNS, and the evolution of the World Wide Web—the physical reality of global connectivity is often overlooked. The internet is not a "cloud," nor is it primarily satellite-based. Instead, it is a physical network of fiber-optic cables resting on the ocean floor, a legacy of 19th-century telegraphy evolved for the digital age.

Understanding the internet through the lens of physical infrastructure reveals a complex intersection of corporate interests, national security, and geography. The "global village" is, in reality, a series of fragile glass strands stretching across the abyss.

## The Shift from State to Corporate Ownership

In the early days of global telecommunications, undersea cables were largely the province of state-owned monopolies (PTTs). However, the late 20th century saw a paradigm shift. As the internet exploded in the 1990s and 2000s, the demand for bandwidth outpaced the capacity of government-funded projects.

This led to the rise of "private" cables. Today, a significant portion of the world's subsea infrastructure is owned and operated by "hyperscalers"—tech giants like Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon. By investing in their own cables, these companies bypass traditional telecom carriers to optimize latency and control the flow of data between their global data centers. This shift has sparked scholarly debate among economists and political scientists regarding the "privatization of the commons," questioning whether the physical layer of the internet should be managed by profit-driven entities.

## Chokepoints and Strategic Vulnerability

The geography of these cables is not random; it follows the paths of least resistance and highest economic demand. This has created strategic "chokepoints." For example, the Luzon Strait and the Suez Canal represent critical bottlenecks where a high concentration of cables converge.

From a historical and geopolitical perspective, these cables are the modern equivalent of the shipping lanes of the British Empire. The ability to monitor, tap, or sever these cables provides immense strategic leverage. This vulnerability has driven nations to invest in "cable diversity"—creating redundant routes to ensure that a single earthquake or a deliberate act of sabotage cannot isolate an entire region from the global network.

## Key Facts
* **Material:** Most modern cables use fiber optics, transmitting data as pulses of light through strands of glass as thin as a human hair.
* **Protection:** Cables are armored with layers of steel wire and polyethylene to protect against shark bites and fishing trawlers.
* **Capacity:** A single modern cable can transmit terabits of data per second, dwarfing the capacity of early satellite links.
* **Layout:** Approximately 99% of international data is carried by subsea cables rather than satellites.
* **Maintenance:** Specialized "cable ships" are the only means of repairing breaks, using robotic grapnels to retrieve cables from the seabed.

## Did You Know?
* **The Telegraph Legacy:** The first successful transatlantic telegraph cable was laid in 1858, establishing the precedent for the physical connectivity we use today.
* **Shark Interference:** In the early 2000s, evidence suggested sharks were biting undersea cables, leading some companies to wrap cables in Kevlar.
* **The "Cloud" is Heavy:** The physical infrastructure required to maintain the "cloud" involves millions of miles of cable and massive cooling systems in coastal landing stations.
