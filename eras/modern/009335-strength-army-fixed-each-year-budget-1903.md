# Army Strength and Budgetary Provisions

The determination of military strength and the allocation of funds for defense are central components of national budgets across various governments. These financial frameworks dictate the size of standing forces, the recruitment of personnel, and the overall administrative capacity of military institutions.

## Military Strength in the Republic
In certain republics, the strength of the army is fixed specifically within each year's budget. For the year 1903, the budget provided for a force consisting of 2,933 officers and men. This total was composed of 1,906 rank and file, 558 non-commissioned officers, 275 commissioned officers, and 181 musicians.

By 1905, the military forces of the republic included 2,890 regulars and an enrolled force of 80,000 men. This enrolled force was further divided into a second reserve of 40,000, a first reserve of 30,000, and 10,000 territorial guards, although this force remained unarmed and unorganized. While a conscription law from 1894 mandated compulsory service for those between twenty-one and fifty years of age—including two years of actual service for those aged twenty-one to twenty-five—the law was considered practically a dead letter. Supporting infrastructure includes an arsenal located at La Paz and a military school containing 60 cadets.

## Joint Budgets and the Dual Monarchy
In the Dual Monarchy, a common budget exists alongside the individual budgets of each state to cover common affairs, including the army, the ministry of finance, and foreign affairs. The revenues for this joint budget are derived from the joint ministries, the net proceeds of customs, and proportional contributions (the quota) from the two states.

The quota for these contributions was fixed for several years. Until 1897, Austria contributed 70% and Hungary 30% of the remaining joint expenditure. Between 1897 and July 1907, the quota was adjusted to 66-46/49 for Austria and 33-2/49 for Hungary; by 1907, Hungary's contribution rose to 36.4%. Additionally, 2% of total charges are debited to Hungary due to the incorporation of the former military frontier.

Administration of these services is handled by three common ministers—for war, finance, and foreign affairs—who are responsible to a Delegation of sixty members elected from the Austrian and Hungarian parliaments. While the minister of war controls the common army, laws regarding recruitment must be voted on separately in each parliament. The minister of finance presents the common budget to the Delegations, but the actual funds must be provided by the separate parliaments.

## Budgetary Processes and Legislative Oversight
National budgets generally fall into two categories: those emitted on executive authority and those passing under parliamentary scrutiny. In the United Kingdom, the executive places the budget before the House, where it is scrutinized by a committee. In the United States, the budget emanates from Congress; estimates from executive departments are submitted to the House of Representatives, where the committee on appropriations handles expenditure and the committee on ways and means handles taxation.

In Japan, the annual budget for state revenue and expenditure requires the consent of the Imperial Diet and is first laid before the House of Representatives. Certain fixed expenditures based on the Emperor's supreme powers or legal obligations cannot be reduced or rejected by the Diet without government concurrence. To handle unforeseen needs, the government may establish a reserve fund for unavoidable deficits or request a continuing expenditure fund for a fixed number of years. If the Diet fails to vote on a budget, the government carries out the budget from the preceding year.

## Comparative National Expenditures
Budgetary records provide insight into the financial priorities of various states. In one instance, the national budget for 1903 showed total revenue of 632,416,810 francs and total expenditure of 627,975,568 francs. Of the principal expenditure items for 1903, the department of war was allocated 63,972,473 francs. Other significant costs included railways (165,086,019 francs) and the service of debt (143,065,352 francs).

In other historical contexts, budgets have been used to enforce military limitations. Following certain peace agreements, the German Army's strength was capped at 100,000, including 4,000 officers, with a limit of three cavalry and seven infantry divisions. Furthermore, the army administrative service was required to be reduced to one-tenth of the total found in the 1913 budget.
