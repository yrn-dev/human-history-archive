## Binomial Theorems and Reasoning
In correspondence dated October 24, 1676, addressed to Oldenburg, Newton provided the reasoning used to devise a specific theorem. This theorem involves the calculation of the $(m/n)$th power or root of a quantity $(p + pq)$. The formula is expressed as:
$m \cdot m - n \cdot m - 2n (p + pq)^{m/n} = p^{m/n} + \frac{a q}{n} + \frac{b q}{2n} + \frac{c q}{3n} \dots$

In this context, $p$ represents the first term of the quantity, $q$ is the quotient of the remaining terms divided by $p$, and $m/n$ is the power, which can be a fraction or a positive or negative integer. The terms $a, b, c$ are determined sequentially, where $a = p^{m/n}$, $b = \frac{(m-n)aq}{n}$, and so on.

## Analysis of Functions and Convergence

### Uniform Convergence
Convergence is considered "uniform" in an interval if, after a positive number $\epsilon$ is specified, a single number $n$ suffices for all points in the interval to ensure that $|f(x) - f_m(x)| < \epsilon$ for all $m$ exceeding $n$. If $n$ becomes a function of $a$ that tends toward infinity as $a$ varies, the series does not converge uniformly in the neighborhood of $a$.

### Singular Points and Poles
A pole is defined as a singular point of a function $f(z)$ that is not a singularity of the function $1/f(z)$. Because $1/f(z)$ can be represented by a series $\sum a_n(z - z_0)^n$ about the point $z_0$, and because $a_0$ must be zero for $z_0$ to remain a singular point for $f(z)$, a pole is always an isolated singularity.

### Regions of Existence
A function may be represented by a power series in $z - z_0$ about every interior point $z_0$ of its region of existence. If the region contains all points where $|z| > R$, the function is representable by a power series in $z^{-1}$ (or $\zeta$), meaning the region contains the point $z = \infty$. However, a function whose region of existence contains all finite points of the plane cannot contain the point $z = \infty$, as seen in the case of $\exp(z) = \sum z^n/n!$.

## Integral Functions and Elliptic Curves
The archive describes integral functions $f(z)$. If $f(z)$ has no finite positions where it vanishes, it can be seen as $f(z) = \exp[\phi(z)]$. If it vanishes at a finite number of values $z = a_1, \dots, a_m$, then $f(z) = \exp[\phi(z)](z - a_1)^{h_1} \dots (z - a_m)^{h_m}$. For functions with an infinite number of zeros $a_1, a_2, \dots$ where $|a_n| \to \infty$, the function is represented as $f(z) = \exp[\phi(z)] \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \{(1 - z/a_n) \exp[\phi_n(z)]\}$.

Additionally, the coordinates of any point of a plane curve $f$ of order $n$ with $1/2(n - 3)n$ double points can be expressed as elliptic functions. For a cubic curve $y^2 = 4x^3 - g_2x - g_3$, if a straight line cuts the cubic at points $(u_1), (u_2), (u_3)$, the sum $u_1 + u_2 + u_3$ is zero or a period.
