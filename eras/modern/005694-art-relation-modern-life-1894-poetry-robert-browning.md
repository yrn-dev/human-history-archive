# Art and Relation to Modern Life (1894) and The Poetry of Robert Browning

## Stopford Augustus Brooke
Stopford Augustus Brooke (1832–) was an English man of letters and divine born in Letterkenny, Donegal, Ireland. Educated at Trinity College, Dublin, Brooke was ordained in the Church of England in 1857 and served various charges in London. His career included serving as chaplain to the empress Frederick in Berlin from 1863 to 1865 and as chaplain in ordinary to Queen Victoria starting in 1872. 

In 1880, Brooke seceded from the Church of England because he could no longer accept its leading dogmas. He subsequently served as a Unitarian minister at Bedford chapel in Bloomsbury for several years. Although Bedford chapel was pulled down around 1894, leaving him without a church of his own, Brooke continued to influence a wide circle through his powerful religious personality and eloquence.

A man of independent means and a fine critic of art and literature, Brooke authored numerous works. These include *Life and Letters of F. W. Robertson* (1865), a primer of *English Literature* (1876; revised 1900), *The History of Early English Literature* (1892), and *English Literature from the Beginnings to the Norman Conquest* (1898). His other publications include *Poems* (1888), *Dove Cottage* (1890), and *Theology in the English Poets—Cowper, Coleridge, Wordsworth, Burns* (1874). Specifically regarding the poets of his era, he wrote *Tennyson, his Art and Relation to Modern Life* in 1894 and *The Poetry of Robert Browning* in 1902. His later works include *On Ten Plays of Shakespeare* (1905) and *The Life Superlative* (1906).

## The Poetry of Robert Browning
Robert Browning is regarded by some as the typical poet of the Victorian era. His intellectual approach has been compared to a galvanic battery intended for "spiritual paralytics." However, critics note a grave defect in his work: his ideas are seldom completely won, often remaining in a "twilight" or a "Cimmerian" darkness.

Browning's work, including *Men and Women* (1855) and *Dramatic Romances and Lyrics* (1845), rarely depicts actual action. Instead, he focuses on interpreting the human mind as it approaches action. While other Victorian figures are characterized by different traits—such as Tennyson as the "ear" and Dickens as the "eye"—Browning is described as the "active brain itself," eternally expounding points of view that are remote in time and place from his own.

In his personal life, Browning dissembled his identity as a poet, successfully disguising his muse by appearing as a stock merchant. He was civil to others, though he felt nervous when dealing with bores.

## Literary Relations and Influences
Browning maintained an intimate friendship with the British colonial statesman and poet Alfred Domett (1811–1887). Domett, who lived a life of ease in London for ten years, was the subject of Browning's poem "Waring." This friendship is documented in a 1905 account by W. H. Griffin in *The Contemporary Review* and a 1906 edited work titled *Robert Browning and Alfred Domett*. Domett later dedicated his work *Flotsam and Jetsam* (1877) to Browning.

An example of Browning's philosophical inquiry can be found in *Bishop Blougram’s Apology*, where he uses the metaphor of a road viewed from a waste to discuss faith, suggesting that breaks in the path may be "consummate of contrivances" designed to train a man's eye and teach him the nature of faith.

## The Nature of Illustration and Art
In a general sense, illustration is the art of pictorially representing an idea expressed in words, a practice as old as art itself. However, the modern understanding of illustration is a more recent product of a cultural tendency where the interests of different arts overlap. This development is influenced by the theory of Wagner, which sought a combined appeal to artistic emotions.

This inter-relation means that a poem often suggests music or a picture, and conversely, painting or music may suggest poetry. The success of illustration depends on the spontaneous and instinctive transference of an idea from one medium to another.
