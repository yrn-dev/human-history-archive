# St John and the Peace with France

## Political Rise and Parliamentary Career
St John entered parliament in 1701, representing the family borough of Wootton Bassett in Wiltshire. A declared Tory, he aligned himself with the speaker, Harley (later Lord Oxford), whom he referred to as "dear master." St John became known for his eloquence in debate, which allowed him to eclipse his schoolfellow, Walpole, and establish an extraordinary ascendancy over the House of Commons.

During his early parliamentary tenure, St John held charge of the bill for securing the Protestant succession in May. He was involved in the impeachment of Whig lords regarding their conduct toward the Partition treaties and opposed the oath abjuring the Pretender. In March 1702, he was appointed commissioner for taking the public accounts. Following the accession of Anne, he supported bills against occasional conformity in 1702 and 1704 and played a leading role in disputes between the two Houses of Parliament.

## Government Offices and Administrative Roles
In 1704, St John took office as secretary at war alongside Harley. This position brought him into intimate relations with Marlborough, who treated St John with paternal partiality. This tenure ended in 1708 when St John and Harley quitted office following the failure of Harley's intrigue. St John retired to the country until 1710, at which point he represented Berkshire in parliament and became a secretary of state and a privy councillor in Harley's new ministry.

While in this role, St John supported a bill that required a real property qualification for those holding a seat in parliament. In 1711, he founded the Brothers' Club, a society consisting of men of letters and Tory politicians. That same year, he promoted two expeditions—one to Canada and one to the West Indies—both of which failed. In 1712, he authored a bill that taxed newspapers.

## The Peace with France
The primary objective of the new government was to establish peace with France. The ministry's design to terminate hostilities was supported by the people, the parliament, and the queen. This position was informed by the conduct of the Whigs, who had refused to grant terms in 1706 and again in 1709, despite Louis XIV offering to yield every point for which the allies claimed to be fighting. This refusal suggested that the war was being maintained for reasons other than English national interests.

However, the process of making peace was complicated by the diversity of aims among the allies. In this context, St John was induced to enter into certain actions contrary to the most solemn obligations.
