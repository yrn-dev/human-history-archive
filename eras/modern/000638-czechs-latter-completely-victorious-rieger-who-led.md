# Czechs

The Czechs are a people primarily situated in Bohemia, the richest part of the Austrian dominions, where they constitute over three-fifths of the population. Their political identity has been characterized by a strong racial feeling and an appeal to historic law, often expressed through the sentiment that they existed before Austria and would continue to exist after it.

## Political Goals and the Bohemian Monarchy
A significant Czech political party, led by figures such as Palacky and Rieger, sought the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy in its fullest extent, which included Silesia and Moravia. This movement insisted that the emperor be crowned as the king of Bohemia in Prague, following the precedent of his predecessors. Their objective was to secure a position within the monarchy similar to that held by Hungary.

This cause was supported not only by the Czechs but also by several great nobles of German descent, including Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg (the cardinal archbishop of Prague), Count Leo Thun, and Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz. These nobles hoped that a self-governing Bohemian kingdom would preserve their power against the threats posed by German Liberals. While the Czechs found support among fellow countrymen in Moravia and some nobles led by Count Belcredi, they faced opposition in Silesia, where Germans held a considerable majority and a large Polish element did not support Czech claims.

## Internal Divisions: Old Czechs and Young Czechs
The Czech political landscape was marked by internal conflict between two primary factions: the Old Czechs and the Young Czechs (also known as Radicals). 

The Old Czechs, led by Rieger, maintained a policy of passive opposition and an alliance with the clergy and the Feudalists. In contrast, the Young Czechs emerged as a party that disliked the alliance with the aristocracy and clergy. The Young Czechs advocated for universal suffrage, recalled Hussite traditions, and desired to take their seats in the diet to collaborate with Germans on political reform.

These divisions led to significant instability. For instance, twenty-seven members of the diet led by Stadkowsky and Gregr resigned their seats after being outvoted in the Czech Club. For a period of four years, the Old and Young Czechs were as occupied with opposing each other as they were with opposing the Germans. However, by the elections of 1891, the Young Czechs were completely victorious over the Old Czechs.

## Relations with the Austrian Government
The relationship between the Czechs and the Austrian government was often contentious. In 1868, the Czechs and Feudals supported a declaration that rejected compromise. Following the dissolution of the diets, the Czechs—supported by Clericals and Feudals—won a large majority in Bohemia. They took their seats in the diet only to declare it an informal assembly rather than the legal representative of the Bohemian kingdom, subsequently refusing to elect delegates for the Reichsrath.

Under the ministry of Taaffe, who practiced "unpolitical politics," the government attempted to maintain stability by balancing different parties. Taaffe formed an "iron ring" of support consisting of Poles, Czechs, and Clericals to oppose the German Liberals. In January 1890, Taaffe chaired conferences in Vienna with the German party, the Feudal party, and the Czechs to reach an agreement on a revision of the electoral law and the separation of German and Czech districts. This agreement failed because the Young Czechs were not invited to the conference and opposed the measures, arguing they threatened the unity of the Bohemian country.

## Electoral and Social Structure
In the allotment of constituencies, the Austrian system utilized separate registers for different races based on tax-paying capacity. In mixed districts, Czechs were required to vote for Czech candidates on their own register. This system ensured that while there could be internal victories—such as Czech Radicals defeating Young Czechs—there could be no victory of Czechs over Germans.

Historically, some areas shifted in demographic composition. For example, the town of Komotau (Chomútov) in Bohemia was originally a Czech market-place, but it was completely Germanized after coming into the possession of the Teutonic Order in 1252.

## National Sentiment and Resistance
The Czechs have demonstrated a burning patriotism and an uncompromising stand for freedom from the "Austrian yoke." This sentiment was evident among Czech prisoners in Russia, who took their own initiative to rise to power and fight for victory and France, distinguishing themselves from other Austrian prisoners who remained passive or merely wished to avoid military service.
