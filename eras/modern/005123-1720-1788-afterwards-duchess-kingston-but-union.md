# Elizabeth, Duchess of Kingston

Elizabeth, Duchess of Kingston (1720–1788), also known as the countess of Bristol, was a prominent figure in London society during the 18th century. The daughter of Colonel Thomas Chudleigh, who died in 1726, she was appointed as a maid of honour to Augusta, princess of Wales, in 1743. This appointment was likely facilitated by her friend, William Pulteney, earl of Bath.

## Marriage to Augustus John Hervey
Known for her beauty, Elizabeth attracted several admirers, including Augustus John Hervey (later the 3rd earl of Bristol) and James, 6th duke of Hamilton. After Hamilton left England, Elizabeth privately married Hervey at Lainston, near Winchester, on August 4, 1744. Because both parties were poor, the marriage was kept secret so that Elizabeth could maintain her position at court. Hervey, a naval officer, returned to his ship and did not return to England until late 1746.

The marriage was very unhappy, and the couple eventually stopped living together. However, when it became likely that Hervey would succeed his brother as earl of Bristol, Elizabeth sought proof of their marriage. Despite this, she became the mistress of Evelyn Pierrepont, 2nd duke of Kingston. Her social prominence continued, and in 1765, while in Berlin, she received the attentions of Frederick the Great.

## Legal Disputes and Second Marriage
By the mid-1760s, Hervey desired a divorce. While Elizabeth also wished to be free of the marriage, she wanted to avoid the public nature of divorce proceedings. Consequently, she initiated a suit of jactitation against Hervey. In a case described as doubtless collusive, Elizabeth swore she was unmarried, and the court pronounced her a spinster in February 1769.

Within a month of this ruling, Elizabeth married the 2nd duke of Kingston. This union ended four years later upon the duke's death in 1773. The duke left all his property to Elizabeth on the condition that she remain a widow.

## Bigamy Charge and Later Years
Following the duke's death, Elizabeth visited Rome, where she was received with honour by Clement XIV. However, she soon returned to England to face a charge of bigamy brought against her by the duke's nephew, Evelyn Meadows. In 1776, the House of Lords found her guilty. Despite the verdict, she retained her fortune and hurriedly left England to avoid further legal proceedings initiated by the Meadows family, who held a reversionary interest in the estate.

## The Pierrepont Family and Kingston Titles
The titles of the earls and dukes of Kingston-upon-Hull were held by the Pierrepont (or Pierrepoint) family from 1628 to 1773. The lineage began with Robert Pierrepont (1584–1643), who was created earl of Kingston-upon-Hull in 1628. He was succeeded by his son Henry Pierrepont (1606–1680), the 2nd earl and 1st marquess of Dorchester. The title of marquess became extinct upon Henry's death in 1680.

The 1st duke of Kingston was Evelyn Pierrepont (c. 1655–1726), who served as lord privy seal and lord president of the council. He was succeeded by his son, also named Evelyn Pierrepont (1711–1773), the 2nd duke. The 2nd duke was a general in the army who raised "Kingston's light horse" during the rebellion of 1745 and fought at Culloden. Horace Walpole described him as a man of great beauty and finest person in England, but a "very weak man."

The Kingston titles became extinct upon the 2nd duke's death without children on September 23, 1773. Following the death of Elizabeth in 1788, the estates passed to the duke's nephew, Charles Meadows (1737–1816). Meadows took the name Pierrepont and was created Baron Pierrepont and Viscount Newark in 1796, and Earl Manvers in 1806.
