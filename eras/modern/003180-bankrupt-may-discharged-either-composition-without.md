# The Discharge of Bankrupts: Composition and Non-Composition

In the legal frameworks governing bankruptcy, the discharge of a bankrupt individual serves as the mechanism by which they are released from their obligations. This process may be achieved either through a composition—an arrangement for the payment of debts—or without such a composition.

## Discharge Without Composition
In certain jurisdictions, such as those governed by the laws of Scotland, a bankrupt may be discharged without a composition through several distinct paths based on time and creditor concurrence:
* **Immediate Discharge:** This may occur via petition if all creditors concur.
* **Six-Month Discharge:** This is possible with the concurrence of a majority and four-fifths in value of the creditors.
* **Eighteen-Month Discharge:** This requires the concurrence of a bare majority in both number and value.
* **Two-Year Discharge:** A discharge may be granted after two years without any concurrence. However, a judge may refuse this application if the bankrupt is believed to have willfully failed to comply with the law or fraudulently concealed effects.

## Discharge by Composition and Schemes of Arrangement
A composition involves a proposal for the payment of debts or the liquidation of affairs. Under English law, a debtor may submit such a proposal to the official receiver either before adjudication or after adjudication has occurred. 

For a proposal to be binding, it must be entertained by a majority in number and three-fourths in value of the creditors who have proved their debts, and it must subsequently be approved by the court. The court must be satisfied that the proposal is reasonable, calculated to benefit the creditors, and that the debtor has committed no criminal offences connected to the bankruptcy. If a composition is approved after adjudication, the adjudication may be annulled. 

While a discharge via a scheme releases the debtor, their future acquired property does not pass to the creditors unless specifically stipulated. Furthermore, approval of a composition does not exempt the debtor from criminal law liabilities or the requirement to undergo a public examination.

## Conditions and Qualifications for Discharge
The court possesses the authority to grant a discharge absolutely or under specific conditions after the conclusion of a public examination and after hearing from the trustee, official receiver, and any creditor. However, several qualifications may lead the court to refuse or suspend the discharge:

1. **Criminality:** If a criminal offence connected to the bankruptcy was committed, the application must be refused unless the court determines special reasons exist.
2. **Financial and Conduct Failures:** The court may refuse, suspend for at least two years, or qualify the discharge if:
    * Assets are not equal to ten shillings in the pound of unsecured liabilities (unless the bankrupt is not responsible).
    * Proper books were not kept.
    * The bankrupt traded after knowing they were insolvent or contracted debts without a reasonable probability of payment.
    * The bankruptcy resulted from gambling, rash speculation, culpable neglect, or unjustifiable expenses.
    * The bankrupt failed to account for deficiency or took frivolous legal proceedings.
    * An undue preference was given within three months preceding the receiving order.
    * The bankrupt previously made arrangements with creditors or had been bankrupt.
    * There was a fraudulent breach of trust or fraud.

If a discharge is qualified, the court may order that judgment be entered against the bankrupt for the payment of unpaid balances from future property or earnings. A bankrupt may apply to modify these conditions after two years if they are unable to comply.

## Effects and Limitations of Discharge
An order of discharge generally releases the debtor from obligations, with notable exceptions. It does not release debts due to the crown, obligations of a public character (unless the Treasury consents), or debts incurred through fraud.

In the United Kingdom, a discharge carries significant legal consequences regarding public office. A bankrupt cannot serve as a member of the legislature, a justice of the peace, a mayor, or various other local roles (such as councillor, guardian, or member of a school or burial board) during their bankruptcy or until five years after discharge. This restriction is waived if the bankruptcy is annulled or if the court certifies that the bankruptcy was caused by misfortune without misconduct.

## International Variations
Bankruptcy laws vary significantly across different nations:
* **France:** Under the Commercial Code of 1807, bankruptcy terminates via a sale of assets, a composition by relinquishment of assets, or an ordinary composition (*concordat*). Notably, a discharge in France leaves the unpaid balance as a "debt of honour," and the trader is not fully rehabilitated until every penny is paid.
* **Italy:** Regulated by the Commercial Code of 1883, bankruptcy is limited to merchants. It can be closed by the auction of all property or by an arrangement with the assent of creditors representing three-fourths of the indebtedness. Italian law does not recognize composition before bankruptcy.
* **British Empire:** Many colonies, including Australia and New Zealand, model their laws on the English system. However, while English bankrupts are not obligated to apply for discharge, those in New Zealand, Victoria, and New South Wales must apply within a fixed period or face contempt of court.
