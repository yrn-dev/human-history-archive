# Persia and Kurdistan

The regions of Persia and Kurdistan, particularly the mountainous western territories, are characterized by a complex history of independent principalities, nomadic populations, and diverse cultural identities. Central to this geography is the province of Kermanshah and the broader region of Luristan.

## Kermanshah
Kermanshah (also known as Kermanshahan) is an important Persian province located south of Kurdistan, north of Luristan, and west of Hamadan, extending to the Turkish frontier. The province is inhabited by approximately 400,000 people, including many nomadic Lurs and Kurds who pay few taxes. The geography of the province consists of well-watered, fertile plains and hills with rich pastures that support large flocks of goats and sheep; these sheep provide a significant portion of the meat supply for Teheran. The province produces barley and wheat in quantities that could be exported if transport were improved. It generates a yearly revenue exceeding £20,000.

The capital, Kermanshah (referred to by Arab geographers as Kermisin), sits at an elevation of 5,100 feet. It is located approximately 250 meters from Teheran and 220 meters from Bagdad. While the town is surrounded by fortifications with five gates and a three-mile circuit, it is currently an open town because the moat is choked with rubbish and the walls are in ruins. With a population of about 40,000, the capital is situated on the high road between Teheran and Bagdad, facilitating a transit trade valued at approximately £750,000 per annum.

## Luristan
Luristan, the "Land of the Lurs," is a region of western Persia bounded by Turkish territory to the west. It extends roughly 400 meters from Kermanshah to Fars, with a breadth between 100 and 140 meters. The terrain is primarily mountainous, with ranges running northwest to southeast. The central range reaches heights of 13,000 feet or more, with some summits nearly reaching the line of perpetual snow. This range contains the sources of several major Persian rivers, including the Karun, Abi, Kerkheh, Jarahi, Zayendeh-rud, and Diz. While there are fertile plains and low hilly districts, they remain comparatively uncultivated due to intertribal feuds.

The Lurs are considered aboriginal Persians with a mixture of Semitic blood. Their language is a Persian dialect that does not differ materially from Kurdish. While outwardly Shiah Muslims, many show little veneration for the Koran or the Prophet. Some practice a mixture of Ali-Illahism, which includes a belief in successive incarnations and ancient, mysterious heathen rites.

## Political History of Luristan
Historically, Luristan was divided into two principalities: Great Lur (Lur i Buzurg) and Little Lur (Lurikuchik).

### Little Luristan
Located west of the River Dizfúl and extending toward the Plain of Babylonia, Little Luristan was governed by the Khurshidi dynasty of independent princes known as atabegs. This dynasty was founded in 1184 by the Kurd Shodja ed-din Khurshid and lasted until 1593, when Shah-Werdy lost his throne. In the early 17th century, Shah Abbas I removed the last atabeg, Shah Verdi Khan, and granted government of the province to Husain Khan, the chief of a rival tribe, under the title of vali. The descendants of Husain Khan continue to govern the Pushtkuh (ultra-montane) Lurs, who are now the only group to whom the denomination "Feili" is applied. The Feili Lurs are divided into the Pishkuh (cis-montane) Lurs in the east and the Pushtkuh Lurs in the west. Their population is approximately 350,000.

### Great Luristan
Great Luristan was ruled by the Faslúyah Atabegs, a Kurd dynasty founded by Emad ed-din Abu Thaher (1160–1228). This dynasty endured from approximately 1155 until 1424, when it was destroyed by the Timurids; the final prince was Ghiyas ed-din. Their territory lay in the mountainous district west of Ispahan and extended to the River of Dizfúl. Their primary stronghold was the hill fort of Mungasht, and they maintained a residence at Aidhej (or Mal-Amir) in the mountains south of Shushan. In 1258, the general Kitubuka is reported to have reduced the country and its Atabeg Teghele. Great Luristan is composed of the districts of the Kuhgilus and Mamasennis, as well as the Bakhtiari division of the Arabistan province.

## Kurdistan and Nomadic Tribes
Persian Kurdistan extends south beyond Kermanshah. According to Hamd Allah, the boundaries of Kurdistan included Azerbaijan, Diarbekr, Persian ’Irák, Khuzistán, and Arabian ’Irák. 

The region is home to various nomad and semi-nomad tribes, including the Kurds, Lurs, Karaunahs, and Shúls. The Bakhtiyaris of Luristan are noted for being individually brave but possessing a cruel and savage character. They are known for pursuing blood feuds with an exterminating spirit and are described as notorious and dextrous thieves. It is said proverbially in Persia that the Bakhtiyaris have been forced to abandon the reading of the Fatihah (prayer for the dead) because they would otherwise have no other occupation.
