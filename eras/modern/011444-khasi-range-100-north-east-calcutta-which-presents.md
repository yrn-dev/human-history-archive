# Khasi Range

The Khasi range is a mountain system located approximately 100 miles north-east of Calcutta. It forms part of the greater Assam range, a succession of plateaus and elevated lands situated to the south of the Brahmaputra valley and north of the Surma valley. The Khasi hills are grouped with other ranges in the region, including the Garo, Jaintia, Naga, and North Cachar hills.

## Geography and Topography
The region is characterized by a succession of steep ridges running east and west, separated by elevated table-lands. On the southern side, the mountains rise precipitously from the Surma or Barak valley. The topography consists of multiple plateaus; the first is approximately 4,000 feet above sea level, while a further plateau to the north hosts the station of Shillong at 4,900 feet. Behind this lies the Shillong range, which reaches a highest peak of 6,450 feet.

The table-lands generally appear as undulating downs covered in grass and are devoid of large timber. However, the highest ridges contain magnificent clumps of timber trees, including chestnuts, magnolias, and oaks, which have been preserved in sacred groves. Rare rhododendrons, orchids, and wild cinnamon grow beneath the shade of these trees. At elevations of 3,000 feet, indigenous pine forests predominate. The area's hydrology consists of mountain torrents that frequently flow through narrow gorges.

## Climate and Rainfall
The Khasi range is noted for recording the heaviest rainfall known to occur anywhere in the world. This is due to the range presenting an abrupt front to moist, vapour-bearing winds flowing up from the Bay of Bengal. At an elevation of about 4,500 feet, the average rainfall over ten years exceeds 550 inches. Records indicate that 610 inches of rain have fallen in a single year, with as much as 150 inches measured in one month.

## Natural Resources and Flora
The Khasi Hills have served as a source of lime for Lower Bengal since time immemorial, with inexhaustible quarries located along the southern slopes. Additionally, coal of fair quality is found in several places, leading to the establishment of a few small coal-mines.

Regarding flora, the genus *Nepenthes* is found as far north as the Khasi hills. This genus is developed in the Malayan area and extends to Madagascar and New Caledonia, though it is absent from the Indian peninsula and the Himalaya.

## People and Governance
The range is inhabited by the Khasi tribe, a peculiar race that speaks a language of the Mon-Anam family. The Khasis follow a rule of matriarchal succession and practice the tradition of erecting monolithic monuments over their dead. According to the 1901 census, the Khasi population was 107,500.

The British conquered the Khasi Hills in 1833. The inhabitants live in primitive communities under the leadership of elective chiefs known as *Siems*. There are 25 such chiefs who exercise independent jurisdiction and pay no tribute, though they remain in political subordination to the British government. In 1901, the district included 17,321 Christians, most of whom were converts of the Welsh Calvinistic Mission.

## History and Infrastructure
The district headquarters were moved from Cherrapunji to Shillong in 1864, and Shillong subsequently became the capital of the province of Assam. A cart-road measuring 97 miles in total length runs from Cherrapunji through Shillong to Gauhati on the Brahmaputra.

The region has experienced significant instability and natural disaster. The neighboring Jaintia Hills, inhabited by the cognate Synteng tribe, were annexed in 1835. The Syntengs rebelled against a moderate income tax in 1860 and 1862, a revolt that was ended by the Khasi and Jaintia Expedition of 1862-63. Later, on June 12, 1897, a great earthquake focused on the district destroyed every permanent building, caused numerous landslips, and broke up the roads. The earthquake resulted in 916 immediate deaths, followed by hundreds more who died of a malignant fever.
