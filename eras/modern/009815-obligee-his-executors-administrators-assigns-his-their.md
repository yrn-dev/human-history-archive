# Obligees, Executors, Administrators, and Assigns

In English law, the terms obligee, executor, administrator, and assign refer to specific roles within legal obligations, the transference of property, and the management of estates. These roles are frequently grouped together in legal instruments, such as bonds and indentures, to ensure that obligations and rights are maintained even after the death of a party or the transfer of a legal interest.

## The Obligee and the Bond
A bond in English law is an obligation created by deed. It is designed to ensure that an "obligor" (the person giving the bond) either performs a specific act, refrains from an act, or pays a sum of money. To secure this, the obligor binds himself in a penalty to the "obligee." 

A common money bond typically specifies that the obligor is bound to the obligee for a certain sum, which is to be paid to the obligee, his executors, administrators, or assigns (or their attorneys). To prevent the penalty from being enforced, a condition known as "defeasance" is added. If the obligor fulfills the specified act or pays the secured sum—which is usually half of the penalty—the bond becomes void. If the condition is not met, the bond remains in full force.

Bonds are used for various purposes beyond simple money payments, including:
* Guaranteeing the fidelity of a rent collector, a clerk, or a person in a public trust.
* Ensuring a building contract is completed.
* Securing that an intended husband settles a sum on his wife should she survive him.
* Preventing a rival business from being operated by the obligor within certain limits of time and space.

## Assignment and Assigns
The term "assign" (or assignee) refers to the recipient in a process of assignment or assignation. To assign is to make over a right or property, generally through a written transference rather than actual delivery. In England, this is termed "assignment," while in Scotland, it is called "assignation." The person who makes the transfer is known as the assignor or cedent.

An assignee may be designated in two primary ways:
1. **By deed**: For example, when a lessee assigns a lease to another person.
2. **In law**: For example, when property devolves upon an executor.

In legal indentures, an assignor may assign chattels to another party and their executors, administrators, and assigns as security for the payment of a principal sum and interest.

## Executors and Administrators
Executors and administrators are responsible for the management of a deceased person's assets. An executor is typically appointed by a testator. In contrast, an administrator is the English equivalent of a Scots law "executor-dative," who is an executor appointed by a court of law rather than by a testator.

### Management of Assets
Legal assets are divided into personal and real assets. Personal assets devolve *virtute officii* on the administrator or executor. Historically, real assets descended to an heir or were devised to a devisee. However, the Land Transfer Act 1897 provided that the real estate of a deceased person shall devolve upon the executor and be administered as if it were personal estate, largely removing the practical distinction between the two.

Assets are further categorized as legal or equitable based on the remedy available to a creditor:
* **Legal Assets**: Property from which a creditor must sue the executor at law to obtain payment.
* **Equitable Assets**: Property where the only remedy is to bring an action in chancery for the administration of the estate. Under equity, all unsecured creditors are treated equally, regardless of whether they are simple contract creditors or specialty creditors.

## Legal Protections and Court Discretion
Under the rules of the Supreme Court of Judicature, the costs associated with the administration of trusts and estates are generally at the discretion of the judge. However, an administrator, executor, trustee, or mortgagee who has not unreasonably resisted or carried on proceedings retains the right to costs out of a particular fund or estate.

Certain types of bonds are considered void if they contradict the policy of the law, such as marriage brocage bonds (used to procure a marriage) or bonds given to a woman for future cohabitation. Additionally, "post obit" bonds—given by an expectant legatee and payable after the death of the person from whom they expect an inheritance—may be set aside if the obligee has exacted unconscionable terms.
