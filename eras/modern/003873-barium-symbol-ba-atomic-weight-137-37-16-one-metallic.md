# BARIUM

Barium (symbol Ba) is a metallic chemical element categorized within the group of the alkaline earths. It has an atomic weight of 137.37 [O=16]. The element derives its name from the Greek word *barus*, meaning "heavy," due to its presence in heavy spar or barytes.

## History and Discovery
The study of barium began in 1602 with V. Casciorolus, a shoemaker from Bologna, who investigated heavy spar. He discovered that the substance became phosphorescent after ignition with combustible substances, leading it to be frequently referred to as "Bolognian phosphorus." In 1774, K. W. Scheele identified a new substance while examining a specimen of pyrolusite; he found that treating the mineral with sulphuric acid produced an insoluble salt, which was later determined to be identical to the salt found in heavy spar.

## Physical and Chemical Properties
In its pure metallic form, freshly cut barium possesses a silver white lustre and is slightly harder than lead. It is highly reactive, attacking both alcohol and water, and it oxidizes extremely easily when exposed. Additionally, the metal is soluble in liquid ammonia.

Barium and its salts can be identified through several methods:
* **Flame Test:** When moistened with hydrochloric acid and heated in a Bunsen flame, they produce a yellowish-green colour.
* **Spectroscopy:** Observation of their spectra reveals two characteristic green lines.
* **Chemical Precipitation:** In solution, barium salts form an immediate precipitate upon the addition of calcium sulphate (a method used to distinguish them from calcium salts) and a yellow precipitate of barium chromate when potassium chromate is added.

## Occurrence and Isolation
Barium occurs primarily as witherite ($\text{BaCO}_3$) and barytes or heavy spar ($\text{BaSO}_4$). It is also found to a lesser extent in various complex silicates, baryto-celestine, and baryto-calcite.

Isolating the pure metal is difficult. Sir H. Davy attempted to electrolyse baryta without success, but later succeeded in obtaining a barium amalgam by using barium chloride in the presence of mercury; the pure barium was then obtained as a silver white residue after the mercury was distilled off. In 1854, R. Bunsen obtained a barium amalgam by electrolysing a dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride thick paste at 100°C in the presence of mercury, subsequently separating the mercury via distillation. In 1901, A. N. Guntz used a mercury cathode to electrolyse a saturated solution of barium chloride to produce a 3% barium amalgam. By transferring this amalgam to an iron boat within a porcelain tube and heating it electrically to approximately 1000°C, a good yield of pure barium was achieved.

## Atomic Weight Determination
The atomic weight of barium has been calculated through various methods:
* **C. Marignac** determined the value by converting barium chloride into barium sulphate and by measuring the amount of silver needed to precipitate a known weight of the chloride, arriving at a mean value of 136.84.
* **T. W. Richards** (1893) obtained a value of 137.44 by determining the equivalent of barium bromide and barium chloride to silver.

For quantitative estimation, barium is typically converted into the sulphate.
