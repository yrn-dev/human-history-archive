## Pasteurization and Bacteriology
The process of pasteurization is named after the French chemist Pasteur. The primary objective of this process is the destruction of the tubercle bacillus in milk, which also kills bacilli associated with several other communicable diseases. 

Effective pasteurization depends on three primary factors:
1. The temperature to which the milk is raised.
2. The duration for which that temperature is maintained.
3. The use of mechanical agitation to prevent the formation of a "scalded layer" or pellicle on the surface.

Research indicates that tubercle organisms can resist heat at 140 deg. F. (60 deg. C.) for longer periods if a membrane forms on the surface of the milk. Consequently, efficient pasteurization is more readily achieved in closed receptacles—such as those used in commercial treatment—than in open vats or bottles. 

Specific standards for the destruction of tubercle bacilli include:
*   Heating milk in a tightly closed commercial pasteurizer for ten minutes.
*   A recommended standard of heating milk in closed pasteurizers for at least twenty minutes at 140 deg. F. to ensure the destruction of disease bacteria without injuring the consistency or creaming properties of the milk.
*   Maintaining temperatures of 170 deg. to 180 deg. F. for eight or ten minutes.

It is noted that milk begins to acquire an objectionable "cooked flavour" and a modified "body" at temperatures slightly above 150 deg. F.
