# The Modern Baptists

The Modern Baptists are a body of Christians distinguished by their specific view of the ordinance of baptism. The central and peculiar tenet shared by all Baptists is that baptism should be administered only to believers. While some groups, such as the Mennonites, continue to practice baptism via sprinkling or pouring, the "modern" Baptists are specifically characterized by the use of immersion. It is noted, however, that immersion is not exclusive to them, as it is recognized by Paedobaptists and practiced exclusively by the Orthodox Eastern Church.

## Origins in Great Britain and Ireland
The rise of the modern Baptist churches was independent of the Anabaptists, who were largely a foreign importation in England. The movement began to break away from Brownism during the time of John Smith (or Smyth, d. 1612). Smyth, an ordained clergyman and preacher in Lincoln, became a separatist around 1605 or 1606 and emigrated to Amsterdam with the Gainsborough Independents. 

In 1609, after coming under Mennonite teaching, Smyth separated from the Independents and baptized himself. Along with Thomas Helwys (ca. 1560–ca. 1616) and Morton—who were likely baptized by pouring in the Mennonite or Anabaptist fashion—Smyth formed the first English Baptist Church. In 1611, this group published a declaration of faith stating that baptism is the outward manifestation of walking in newness of life and dying unto sin, and therefore does not appertain to infants. This confession also asserted that the magistrate should not compel any form of religion or meddle with matters of conscience, marking the first known expression of absolute liberty of conscience in a confession of faith.

## Development and Denominations
Early Baptist history in England included the execution of Edward Wightman at Lichfield in 1612, the last person burned for heresy in the country. The movement later saw the rise of Calvinistic or Particular Baptist churches. The Jacob church in Southwark, which included members such as William Kiffin and Hanserd Knollys, was originally Independent before becoming Baptist. In 1633, a group dismissed from the Jacob church formed the first Particular Baptist Church.

By 1891, under the influence of General Baptist leader Dr. John Clifford, the General and Particular denominations united to form the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland. This Union is voluntary; some prosperous churches remain independent. Additionally, "Strict" Baptist churches exist as a separate denomination, often organized into geographical associations like the Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches. The Strict Baptists are further divided into the "Vessel" and "Standard" parties, named after their respective organs, the *Earthen Vessel* and the *Gospel Standard*.

## Global Expansion
The Baptist movement spread extensively across Europe and the British colonies during the 19th century. In Roman Catholic nations, churches were established by missionaries from America or England: France (1832), Italy (1866 and 1884), and Spain (1888). In Russia, Finland, and Protestant countries, the movement grew without such intervention. J. G. Oncken formed the first church in Hamburg in 1834, leading to the establishment of churches in Denmark (1839), Holland and Sweden (1848), Switzerland (1849), Norway (1860), Austria and Rumania (1869), Hungary (1871), and Bulgaria (1884).

In the British colonies, Baptist unions were established in New Zealand, Canada (four unions), South Africa, and several Australian states, including New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia, South Australia, and Tasmania. The Baptist South African Missionary and Colonial Aid Society in London supports work in South Africa.

In North America, William Screven and Humphrey Churchwood organized a congregation at Kittery, Maine, in 1682. Due to persecution, Screven moved to South Carolina, organizing the First Baptist church in Charleston around 1684. Meanwhile, Arminian (general) type Baptists appeared in Virginia starting in 1714, fostered by English General Baptist missionaries, and reached North Carolina by 1727.

## Education and Ministry
To provide an educated ministry, Baptists established various institutions. Early leaders like Dr. John Gill were noted for their learning, particularly in Hebrew. Training was furthered by bequests, such as those from Edward Terrill, leading to the creation of the Baptist Academy in 1720. This evolved into the Bristol Baptist College around 1811.

Other educational institutions include:
* A society formed in 1804 at Bradford, later moved to Rawdon.
* A college formed in London in 1810 at Stepney, moved to Regent's Park in 1856.
* The Pastors' College (1856) connected to the Metropolitan Tabernacle.
* The Baptist College at Manchester (1866), serving "Strict" Baptist views.
* The Midland Baptist College in Nottingham, originally supported by the General Baptist Association.
* Theological colleges in Glasgow, Ireland (one), and Wales (two).

Across these institutions, there are approximately 210 students.
