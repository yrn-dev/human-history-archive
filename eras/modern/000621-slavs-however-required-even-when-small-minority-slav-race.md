# Slavs in Central and Eastern Europe

The Slavs comprise a diverse group of races and linguistic groups across Central and Eastern Europe, including the Czechs, Poles, Slovenes, Croats, Serbs, and Ruthenes. Historically, their presence in the Balkan Peninsula began with a crossing of the Danube in the 3rd century, followed by a major immigration during the 6th and 7th centuries. These primitive pastoral and agricultural people overran the peninsula, pushing Albanians into the Mirdite country, Greeks to the Aegean shores, and the latinized population of Macedonia into highland districts such as Olympus, Agrapha, and Pindus. While they were often unsuccessful in attacking fortified towns, which remained centers of Hellenism, they assimilated many latinized races in central regions. In the east and center, they were conquered by the Bulgarians—an Ugro-Finnish horde—at the end of the 7th century, though the Bulgarians were eventually absorbed by the more numerous Slavs.

## Linguistic and Educational Struggles in Austria
In the Austrian territories, the Slavs engaged in significant struggles to maintain their languages against Germanization. The law of 1867 mandated that elementary education in Slav districts be conducted in Slovenian or Czech. Despite this, Slavs demanded that their own schools be provided even in towns where they were a small minority, so they would not be compelled to attend German schools; this demand was granted by the minister of education, Prazak, under Count Taaffe.

While Germans viewed German as the *Kultur-sprache* and hoped education would lead Slavs to abandon their native tongues, the opposite occurred. Economic factors drove Slavs to move west into manufacturing districts and towns, which served to spread their languages. In Vienna, the Czech population increased significantly, leading to the foundation of a Czech school. In Carniola, the Slovenian language was a point of contention; Count A. Auersperg once brought the entirety of Slovenian literature into the diet of Carniola to argue that the language was an unsuitable substitute for German in higher education.

## Regional Variations and Political Ideals
The various Slav races maintained distinct political goals and identities:

*   **Poles:** In Galicia, the Poles stood apart from other Slavs. They sought autonomy for their province and were largely uninterested in the centralization of other regions, provided Austria surrendered the Ruthenes to them. They were little influenced by pan-Slav agitation, preferring a strong, united Austria to maintain their own freedom and power, while still looking toward the restoration of the Polish kingdom.
*   **Southern Slavs:** This group lacked the historical traditions and organization of the Poles and Czechs. The Slovenes, a majority in Carniola and a minority in south Styria and Carinthia, demanded the use of their language in education and government. Their political ideal was an "Illyrian" kingdom encompassing Croatia and the coast district. An attempt was made to create a unified "Illyrian" language for both Slovenes and Croats.
*   **Serbs:** Though sharing a race and language with the Croats, the Serbs were separated by religion, belonging to the Orthodox Church. This connection attracted them to Russia and the hope of creating a great Servian kingdom, a goal that conflicted with loyalty to Austria.

## Dalmatia and the Serbo-Croatian Influence
In Dalmatia, the population is predominantly composed of Morlachs (96%), who belong to the Serbo-Croatian branch of the Slavonic race. The term "Morlachs" is derived from *Mavrovlachi* ("Black Vlachs" or "Sea Vlachs"), originally referring to Latinized inhabitants of central Illyria who were nomadic shepherds. By the 14th century, they migrated to northern Dalmatia and maritime Croatia, eventually adopting the language and manners of the Slavs.

Between 1872 and 1876, a series of laws raised Croatian to equality with Italian, which had previously been the language of business. Consequently, nearly all education in Dalmatia shifted to Croatian. By 1882, a Slav majority existed in the diet, and Italian was disused in its proceedings. These concessions were made by the Liberal ministry to secure the support of Dalmatian representatives and to cultivate loyalty against the Russian party active in the Balkans.

## Political Representation and Social Dynamics
The political landscape of Austria shifted to acknowledge that the country was preponderantly Slav with a German facade and dynasty. Following a redistribution of seats, the Germans were left in a permanent minority with 233 seats, compared to 259 Slav seats. The Ruthenes saw their representation trebled during this process.

The Roman Catholic Church also played a role in these dynamics, as Slavs provided more candidates for ordination than Germans. This led to an alliance between the Clerical party and Slav parties, which in turn sparked the "Los von Rom" movement among Germans in Styria and other territories, where some left the Church for Protestantism or Old Catholicism.

## Cultural Characteristics
Culturally, the Slav races are linked by folk traditions. The balalaika, a stringed instrument with a triangular soundboard and a fretted neck, is common among Slav races and is used to accompany folk-songs and dances. It is particularly popular among peasants in Russia and Ukraine.
