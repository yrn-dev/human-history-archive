# Immunity and Anti-Substances

Immunity is defined as the non-susceptibility of an organism to a specific disease or to experimental inoculation with a particular toxin or bacterium. This state is relative and depends on various conditions; for instance, an animal might be susceptible during experimental inoculation but rarely suffer from the disease naturally if the necessary conditions for infection are absent. Immunity can be natural or acquired. Animals that are naturally susceptible may acquire immunity either by surviving an attack of the disease or through various methods of inoculation.

## Varieties of Artificial Immunity

Artificial immunity is categorized into two primary types based on how it is produced:

*   **Active Immunity:** This occurs when a reaction or series of reactions is produced within the animal's body, typically through injections of bacteria or their products. This method is practically applied in preventive inoculation.
*   **Passive Immunity:** This is produced by transferring serum from an actively immunized animal to a fresh animal. It is termed "passive" because it does not involve an active change in the tissues of the recipient animal. This process forms the basis of serum therapeutics.

## Anti-Substances and Antigens

The process of active immunity is accompanied by the appearance of "anti-substances" in the blood serum. These substances allow for the transfer of passive immunity. Anti-substances are not produced solely in response to bacteria; they can also result from the injection of proteins, ferments, or various alien cells. Molecules that trigger the production of these anti-substances are known as antigens. Each antigen possesses a specific combining affinity for its corresponding anti-substance, functioning similarly to a lock and key.

Ehrlich categorized anti-substances into three main groups:
1.  Substances that simply combine with the antigen without producing a change (e.g., antitoxins).
2.  Substances that combine with the antigen and produce a recognizable physical change (e.g., agglutinins and precipitins).
3.  Substances that, after combining with the antigen, lead to the union of a third body called "complement" (also known as cytase or alexine), which is present in normal serum. This union often results in a digestive or dissolving action, as seen in bacteriolytic or haemolytic sera.

## Anti-Bacterial Serum Activities

Anti-bacterial sera are prepared using injections of bacteria (living, dead, or maceration products). While such sera provide powerful protective properties, they are generally not antitoxic. Their activities, which can be demonstrated outside the body, include:

**Lysogenic or Bacteriolytic Action**
This process involves a change in the bacterium causing it to swell, become granular, and ultimately undergo dissolution. This phenomenon requires two substances: a stable "immune body" (referred to by Bordet as *substance sensibilisatrice* or by Ehrlich as *Immunkoerper*) which resists temperatures of 60°C, and a more labile, ferment-like substance present in normal serum that is destroyed at 60°C.

**Agglutination**
Agglutination is the aggregation of bacteria into clumps in an indifferent fluid, which arrests the movement of motile bacteria. This process leads to sedimentation, where clumps settle and leave the upper fluid clear. This property has been used for serum diagnosis in diseases such as typhoid fever, cholera, plague, and Malta fever.

**Opsonic Action**
Opsonic action refers to the effect of serum in making bacteria more susceptible to phagocytosis by white blood corpuscles. This is measured using a "phagocytic index," which is the average number of bacteria within leucocytes in a tested case divided by the number found using normal serum. Normal serum contains a labile opsonin destroyed at 55°C. However, animals immunized against a specific bacterium develop thermostable immune opsonins that retain their property even after being heated to 55°C.

## Antitoxins and Toxins

An antitoxic serum has the power to neutralize a toxin. When injected before a toxin, it confers passive immunity; when injected after, it can have a curative action if the dose is sufficiently large. Antitoxins are developed by administering successive, gradually increasing doses of a toxin to a susceptible animal, frequently a horse. 

The potency of these sera is standardized. According to Ehrlich's standard, one unit of antitoxin is the amount required to antagonize 100 times the minimum lethal dose of a toxin in a 250 grm. guinea-pig, provided a fatal result does not occur within five days. In diphtheria cases, the power of the serum can reach or exceed 800 units per cubic centimetre. These laws of production apply not only to bacterial toxins but also to snake-venom and vegetable toxalbumoses.

## Natural Immunity and Phagocytosis

Natural immunity varies and cannot be explained by a single theory. It may depend on the absence of chemical affinity between tissue molecules and toxic molecules, or an insensitiveness to toxophorous groups. For example, the nervous system of the fowl is immune to tetanus toxin due to a lack of combining affinity.

Additionally, natural immunity involves the destruction of bacteria through phagocytosis aided by chemiotaxis. An animal's advantage depends on whether its phagocytes are attracted to a particular bacterium, a process likely dependent on the opsonic content of the serum. The final destruction of the bacteria depends on the digestive powers of the leucocytes, which vary by species. Some sera also exhibit direct bactericidal action; for instance, the serum of a white rat kills the anthrax bacillus, whereas the serum of a susceptible guinea-pig does not.
