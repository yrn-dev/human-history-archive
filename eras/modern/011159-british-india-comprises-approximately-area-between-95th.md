# British India

## Geography and Physical Features
British India is situated approximately between the 70th and 95th meridians, extending from the Indian Ocean in the south to the Tibetan table-land in the north. The region's topography is characterized by several distinct zones. The Indian peninsula, south of 25 deg. N., consists of a table-land with elevations ranging from 1,000 ft. to a maximum of 3,000 ft., though some western points rise above 8,000 ft. and higher hills typically reach around 4,000 ft.

Between the Tibetan plateau's Himalayan slope and the peninsular table-land lies the great plain of northern India. This plain, which includes the valley of Assam, covers an area of roughly 500,000 sq. m. and reaches a maximum height of about 1,000 ft. It is described as the most civilized, populous, and richest part of India. The plain is composed of such finely comminuted deposits that a traveler could potentially journey over 2,000 m. from the Bay of Bengal, through the Punjab, and down the Indus to the sea without encountering a single pebble.

## Provincial Divisions and Administration

### Bombay Presidency
The Bombay Presidency is a province comprising both British districts and native states. It is bounded by the Arabian Sea to the west, Madras and Mysore to the south, Indore, Hyderabad, and the Central Provinces to the east, and Rajputana, the Punjab, and Baluchistan to the north. Its total area, including Sind but excluding Aden, is 188,745 sq. m., with 122,984 sq. m. under British rule and 65,761 sq. m. under native rule. As of 1901, the population was 25,468,209.

The presidency is organized into twenty-six districts and four commissionerships: Sind, the southern (Carnatic), the central (Deccan), and the northern (Gujarat). It also includes the settlement of Aden and the native state of Baroda. Native states within the presidency total 353 units, grouped into regions such as North and South Gujarat, North and South Konkan, the Deccan Satara Jagirs, and the southern Mahratta states.

### Burma
Burma is a province of British India that includes British Burma—acquired during wars in 1826 and 1852—and the former independent kingdom of Burma, which was annexed on January 1, 1886. The province is divided into Lower and Upper Burma. It extends from the Pakchan river (9° 55' N) to the Kachin (Chingpaw) and Naga hills (between 27° and 28° N), and from the Bay of Bengal to the Mekong river.

The total area of Burma is estimated at 238,738 sq. m., consisting of the Shan States (59,915 sq. m.), the Chin hills (10,250 sq. m.), and Burma proper (168,573 sq. m.). Its eastern boundary is defined by the Mekong river, which serves as the limit between British interests in Burma and French interests in Tongking for approximately 200 m.

### Assam and Baluchistan
Assam was a former province amalgamated in 1905 with "Eastern Bengal and Assam." Located on the extreme frontier of the Indian empire, it covers 56,243 sq. m. and is bounded by Tibet and Bhutan to the north, Manipur and Burma to the east, and the Bengal districts of Rangpur and Mymensingh to the west. It encompasses the mountainous watershed between the Surma and Brahmaputra rivers.

Baluchistan is a country within British India extending from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea. It is split into two main divisions: British Baluchistan (9,403 sq. m.), which consists largely of tracts ceded by Afghanistan under the 1879 treaty of Gandamak and declared part of British India in 1887; and foreign territories. The latter includes tribal areas held by the Bugti and Marri tribes (7,129 sq. m.), native states such as Las Bela and Kalat (79,382 sq. m.), and directly administered districts including the Bolan Pass and the Zhob and Chagai political agencies (36,401 sq. m.).

## Language and Culture
Marathi is a significant Indo-Aryan language spoken in central and western India. In 1901, it had 18,237,899 speakers. The language is spoken across an irregular triangular area of about 100,000 sq. m., stretching from Daman on the Gulf of Cambay to Karwar on the Arabian Sea. It is the principal language of Portuguese India and the north-western portion of the Nizam's dominions, as well as the vernacular for over eight and a half million people in the Bombay province, where the standard form of speech is that of Poona.

## Military Contributions
During a period of Imperial concentration, British India contributed significantly to European theater operations. This involved the transportation of the equivalent of five divisions from India to Europe, who were replaced by three divisions of Territorials from England. Additionally, two Anglo-Indian divisions were added to the regular forces available in France by the end of November, contributing to a total of approximately 13 divisions of highly trained long-service troops.
