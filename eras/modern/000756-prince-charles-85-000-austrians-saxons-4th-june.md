# The Battle of Hohenfriedberg and the Campaign of 1745

## The Strategic Situation of 1745
The military events of 1745 were defined by three primary engagements: Fontenoy, Kesselsdorf, and Hohenfriedberg. The year began with the formation of the Quadruple Alliance on January 8, consisting of Saxony, Austria, Holland, and England. This political shift followed the death of Charles VII, which led to a new election for the imperial title. The Bavarian army suffered significant setbacks during this period; after being caught in scattered winter quarters during the action of Amberg on January 7, the army was driven from point to point, forcing the young elector to abandon Munich.

By April 22, the peace of Fuessen was established. Under its terms, the elector secured his hereditary states provided he supported the candidature of the grand-duke Francis, the consort of Maria Theresa. This agreement resulted in the immediate cessation of the "imperial" army, leaving Frederick isolated. Furthermore, Frederick could expect no assistance from France, as Louis XV and Saxe were focused on the Flanders campaign, where they besieged Tournay and defeated the duke of Cumberland's relieving army at Fontenoy on May 10.

## The Confrontation in Silesia
In Silesia, a "small war" had been ongoing for some time. The concentration of the Prussian army required severe fighting to achieve. By the end of May, Frederick had established a camp at Frankenstein, situated between Neisse and Glatz, with a force of approximately 65,000 men. 

Opposing him was Prince Charles, who was positioned behind the Riesengebirge about Landshut. Prince Charles commanded a force of 85,000 men composed of Saxons and Austrians.

## The Battle of Hohenfriedberg
On June 4, the two forces engaged in the battle of Hohenfriedberg (also known as the battle of Striegau). The engagement resulted in a complete defeat for Prince Charles, marking the greatest victory of Frederick's career to that date, surpassed perhaps only by his later battles at Rossbach and Leuthen.

Following the defeat, Prince Charles withdrew through the mountains. Frederick conducted a methodical pursuit of the retreating forces, as the terrain was barren and difficult, and the exact level of the enemy's demoralization remained unknown to him. The remainder of the summer was occupied by the maneuvers of both leaders on the upper Elbe, while political discussions continued regarding the imperial election and a potential understanding between Prussia and England.
