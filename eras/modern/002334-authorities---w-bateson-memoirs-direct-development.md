# Authorities and Memoirs in Scientific and Constitutional Study

The term "authorities" in historical and academic archives refers to the primary sources, specialized memoirs, and recognized experts whose works establish the foundation for a particular field of study. These range from biological treatises on animal development to legal analyses of national governance.

## Biological and Zoological Authorities

In the study of zoology and embryology, authorities are often established through detailed memoirs focusing on the development and anatomy of specific species.

### Enteropneusta and Balanoglossus
The study of Enteropneusta, specifically the genus *Balanoglossus*, is supported by several key authorities. W. Bateson published "Memoirs on the Direct Development of Balanoglossus" in the *Quart. Journ. Micr. Sci.* between 1884 and 1886. T. H. Morgan contributed "Memoirs on the Indirect Development of Balanoglossus" in the *Journ. Morph.* (1891 and 1894). Other significant contributors include W. B. Benham, who identified *Balanoglossus otagoensis* in 1899, and J. P. Hill, who documented the Enteropneusta of Funafuti (1897-1898). Additional authorities include Yves Delage and Ed. Herouard, S. F. Harmer, W. E. Ritter, J. W. Spengel, A. Willey, and the team of M. Caullery and F. Mesnil.

Research into these organisms reveals two development schemes: direct and indirect. Direct development, found in the family *Balanoglossidae*, achieves the adult form through a gradual succession of stages without striking metamorphosis. In this process, Bateson demonstrated that the three divisions of the coelom arise as pouches constricted from the archenteron (primitive gut), similar to the development of mesoblastic somites in *Amphioxus*. Conversely, the families *Ptychoderidae* and *Spengelidae* typically follow indirect development, involving a pelagic, transparent larva called *Tornaria*. This larva possesses a longitudinal ciliated band and a dorsal water-pore, suggesting a possible remote affinity to Echinoderms, such as sea-cucumbers, star-fishes, and sea-urchins.

### General Embryology and Palaeontology
The field of embryology was significantly advanced by Kölliker, who was influenced by the teachings of Karl Ernst von Baer. Kölliker introduced microscopic techniques, including staining, section-cutting, and hardening, to the study of mammalian, chick, and amphibian embryos. His 1844 memoir on the development of cephalopods is considered a classical work, and his 1861 *Lectures on Development* became a standard text.

In palaeontology, Charles Emerson Beecher (1856-1904) was a leading authority on fossil brachiopoda and crustacea. He earned his Ph.D. from Yale University in 1889 for his memoir on the *Brachiospongidae*, a group of Silurian sponges. Beecher also conducted noteworthy research on the development of brachiopoda and the Trilobites *Trinudeus* and *Triarthrus*.

## Entomological Foundations

Modern entomology was established through memoirs on anatomy and development from the 17th and 18th centuries. Key authorities include M. Malpighi, whose 1669 treatise on the silkworm is highly regarded, and J. Swammerdam, whose *Biblia naturae* (1737) provided observations on insect life-history. While Swammerdam proposed a theory of pre-formation—the idea that a miniature adult exists within the egg—he successfully demonstrated that pupal structures exist under the larval cuticle. Earlier views by Harvey and Aristotle had characterized the pupa as a second egg and the larva as a prematurely hatched embryo. Additionally, F. Redi used experiments to disprove the spontaneous generation of maggots, proving they develop from fly eggs.

Following the 1859 publication of C. Darwin's *Origin of Species*, entomology gained new impetus. Darwin and A.R. Wallace used insect evidence to support descent with modification. Subsequent authorities in the field include F. Brauer and A.S. Packard on phylogeny; H.W. Bates, F. Muller, E.B. Poulton, and M.C. Piepers on mimicry; and W. Bateson and M. Standfuss on variation.

## Constitutional and Legal Authorities

The study of the English Constitution is based on a wide array of legal and historical authorities. Key texts include:
* **Legal and Constitutional Theory:** A.V. Dicey's *Law of the Constitution* (1902), Sir W. Anson's *Law and Custom of the Constitution* (1896), and W. Bagehot's *The English Constitution*.
* **Constitutional History:** Works by W. Stubbs, H. Hallam, and Sir T. Erskine May.
* **Governance and Parliament:** A. Todd's *Parliamentary Government in England* (1867-1869), S. Low's *The Governance of England* (1904), and A. Lawrence Lowell's *The Government of England* (1908).
* **Development of Government:** M.T. Blauvelt's *The Development of Cabinet Government in England* (1902) and Hannis Taylor's *Origin and Growth of the English Constitution* (1889-1900).

Additional valuable information is derived from the memoirs and recollections of political figures, such as the third earl of Malmesbury's *Memoirs of an ex-Minister* (1884-1885), Greville's *Memoirs*, and Sir A. West's *Recollections* (1889).
