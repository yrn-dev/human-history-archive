# The South-West Monsoon in the Bay of Bengal

The south-west monsoon is a system of periodical winds that prevail north of the equator during the hot half of the year. These winds are caused by a significant decrease in atmospheric pressure over Asia, which becomes strongly marked during the temperature rises of April and May, while pressure remains relatively higher over the equator and regions to the south. This pressure diminution reaches its maximum in July, shortly after the solstice, leading to the full development of the south-west monsoon.

## Regional Characteristics and Direction
While the general law of periodical winds governs the seasons, local influences often modify wind directions. In the Arabian Sea, the south-west monsoon begins with north-westerly and westerly winds, which shift to south-west as the year progresses. However, in the Bay of Bengal, the strength of the south-west monsoon is characterized as being rather from the south and south-east. 

These winds are eventually succeeded by the north-east monsoon. In the Bay of Bengal, north-east winds follow after October, later giving way to north-westerly and northerly winds as the year advances. This transition occurs as temperatures fall north of the equator and barometric pressure is equalized within the tropics following the October equinox.

## Impact on Climate and Geography
The south-west monsoon has a direct effect on the climate of regions bordering the Bay of Bengal. In Eastern Bengal, specifically in the district of Backergunje, the strong south-west monsoon originates directly from the Bay of Bengal, which serves to keep the atmosphere cool, contributing to it being one of the healthiest climates in the region. However, this system also brings heavy rainfall and atmospheric humidity.

The monsoon winds are primary drivers of summer rains in south Asia. The heaviest rainfall occurs where vapour-bearing winds encounter mountain ranges. For example, the Khasi range, located approximately 100 m. north-east of Calcutta, presents an abrupt front to the moist winds flowing up from the Bay of Bengal; this area has recorded the heaviest rainfall known anywhere in the world, totaling no less than 600 inches annually.

## Cyclonic Activity
The interaction between different wind currents in the Bay of Bengal contributes to the formation of revolving storms or cyclones. These occur during the hot months from June to October, and more rarely in November. These cyclones appear to originate over the Nicobar and Andaman islands and are commonly propagated in a north-westward direction. 

These storms typically strike the east coast of the Indian peninsula and often advance with an easterly tendency over land, passing with extreme violence across the delta of the Ganges. The origin of these cyclones is attributed to the meeting of south-west monsoon currents with adverse currents from the north. In the Bay of Bengal, these cyclonic storm-waves can devastate islands located on the sea-front.

## Navigation and Maritime Influence
The south-west monsoon significantly impacts maritime travel and port safety. In the Bombay Presidency, the coast is generally rock-bound and difficult to access; only a few harbours, such as Karachi-in-Sind, Bombay, Karwar, and Marmagoa, are sufficiently land-locked to protect shipping during the prevalence of the south-west monsoon.

Historical maritime records indicate that the timing of the monsoon was critical for crossing the Bay of Bengal. For instance, vessels sailing from Fo-kien in January would reach Sumatra by April, but would find the south-west monsoon too near to permit a crossing of the Bay of Bengal. Such vessels would remain in port until September before proceeding toward Western India.
