# Bolan Pass

The Bolan Pass is a significant mountain pass located on the Baluch frontier. It serves as a vital connection between Quetta on the plateau and Sibi and Jacobabad in the plains. Due to its strategic location, the pass has historically occupied an important position in British military campaigns in Afghanistan.

## Geography and Climate
The pass is part of a drainage system that flows south-eastward from the central Quetta-Pishin plateau, eventually disappearing into the irrigated flats of the alluvial bay known as Kach Gandava. This bay extends 130 miles from the Indus to Sibi. The landscape of the pass is characterized by dramatic geological features; at the Khundilani gorge, the valley is enclosed by conglomerate cliffs reaching heights of 800 feet. At Sir-i-Bolan, the passage is so narrow between limestone rocks that it barely allows three persons to ride abreast.

The climate of the Bolan Pass is extreme. Summer temperatures are very high, while winter brings extreme cold near the head of the pass, where destructive, ice-cold winds rush down the narrow outlet.

## British Administration and Security
The Bolan Pass is part of the directly-administered districts of British Baluchistan, which include the Zhob and Chagai political agencies and the eastern portion of the Quetta tahsil. Following the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, which concluded the first phase of the Afghan War, the Bolan route was brought under direct British control.

Historically, the pass was subject to raids by plundering bands of Baluch marauders, primarily from the Marri tribe. However, since the founding of the Quetta agency in 1877, the British secured the pass from these attacks, rendering it as safe as any pass in Scotland.

## Railway Development
The Bolan route was selected for the first alignment of the Sind-Pishin railway, connecting the plains to the plateau. Starting from Sibi, the line runs south-west, skirting hills to Rindli. Initially, the railway followed the course of the Bolan stream to its head on the plateau. However, this alignment was abandoned due to the destructive nature of floods. The current railway follows the Mashkaf valley—which debouches into the plains near Sibi—and runs from the head of the Mashkaf to a junction with the Bolan at Mach.

While the Bolan loop remains available for emergencies, ordinary traffic now utilizes an alternative route through the Harnai valley. This route starts in the opposite direction from Sibi, entering the hills at Nari; although longer, it is the preferred path for standard traffic.

## Historical Events and Conflict
The pass has been the site of significant military and political tension. In 1839, during the British army's advance toward Afghanistan, British convoys were plundered in the Bolan Pass and Kach Gandava. These acts were attributed to Mehrab Khan, the ruler of Baluchistan, leading General Willshire to lead 1,050 men to assault Kalat. The town and citadel were stormed, resulting in the death of Mehrab Khan and over 400 Baluches, with 2,000 prisoners taken. 

Subsequent inquiries revealed that Mehrab Khan was not responsible for the treachery. Instead, the plundering had been instigated by his vizier, Mahommed Hussein, and certain chiefs. The vizier had deceived British political officers to ruin Mehrab Khan and seize power for himself, as Mehrab's successor was only a child. Following these events, Kalat changed hands several times before Nasir Khan II, the son of Mehrab Khan, was recognized by the British in 1841, shortly before the British evacuated the country.

Additionally, the Marri tribe attacked the communications of Sir John Keane's army in 1840 after it had passed through the Bolan. Later, during the second Afghan War in 1880, the Marris conducted frequent raids on British communications, including the plunder of a treasure convoy. This led to a campaign by Brigadier-General Macgregor with 3,070 troops, after which the tribe submitted and paid a fine.

## Regional Significance
The Bolan Pass is not only a military and transport corridor but also a route for nomadic movement. The scale of these movements is evidenced by the annual Brahui migration, during which as many as 15,000 camels have been counted in the pass within a single month.
