# Plantation and Colonial Administration

The term "plantation" has been applied across various historical contexts, ranging from the structured settlement of provinces and the cultivation of tropical raw materials to the agricultural production of specific crops.

## The Baronetage of Nova Scotia
In 1624, the baronetage of Nova Scotia was established as a mechanism to promote the "plantation" of the province. King James initially intended to create one hundred baronets. The conditions for this honor required each baronet to either support six colonists for a period of two years or pay 2000 marks as an alternative. Additionally, they were required to pay 1000 marks to Sir William Alexander, who had been granted the province by charter in 1621. In exchange, Alexander was to receive a "free barony" of 16,000 acres in Nova Scotia and become a baronet of "his Hienes Kingdom of Scotland."

Following the death of James, Charles I implemented the scheme, creating the first Scottish baronet on May 28, 1625. The creation charter stipulated that the number of baronets "of Scotland or of Nova Scotia" could not exceed 150 and that heirs apparent would be knighted upon reaching adulthood. To receive the honor, candidates had to fulfill the financial conditions, which totaled 3000 marks (£166, 13s. 4d.) toward the plantation of the colony.

On November 17, 1629, the king recognized that "contractors for baronets" had advanced large sums to Sir William Alexander based on the security of future payments. On the same date, Nova Scotia baronets were granted a badge to be worn on an orange tawny ribbon, featuring an azure saltire with a crowned inescutcheon of the arms of Scotland and the motto "Fax mentis honestae gloria." Because the required number of applicants was not met, Charles I opened the honor to Irish and English gentlemen in 1634, though only slightly more than 120 were created in total. By 1638, the grant of lands in Nova Scotia ceased to accompany the creation. Upon the union with England in 1707, Scottish creations ended, and baronetcies of Great Britain were issued to both Scotsmen and Englishmen.

## German Tropical Plantations
In East Africa, the German Empire adopted a plantation system designed to develop the region as a tropical possession for the cultivation of raw materials rather than an ordinary colony. This system systematically discouraged white settlement and small-scale European farming. Instead, the government granted tracts of land to men with large capital, syndicates, or companies on the condition that they cultivate tropical products.

This system relied on a collaboration between white capital and black labor. The government utilized a *corvée* system to compel natives to work for planters for a small wage during part of each year. With a native population of seven and a half million, this labor supply allowed the Kamerun and East Africa to develop rapidly into valuable assets for German industry.

## British Colonial Administration and Evolution
British colonial administration evolved through various structures. The crown received advice on colonial affairs from the secretaries of state for India and the colonies. The "Council of Trade and Plantations" served this purpose until it was suppressed in 1782. During this time, figures such as William, 2nd Earl of Dartmouth, served as president of the board of trade and foreign plantations (1765–1766 and 1772) and as secretary for the colonies (1772).

In 1791, Pitt granted representative institutions to Canada, with the home government maintaining responsible control; similar institutions were later provided to South Africa and Australia. Between 1845 and 1875, under the influence of the "Manchester school," colonial self-government reached its fullest development, often viewed as a preliminary to inevitable separation. 

Later, a shift toward "imperial consolidation" occurred. Lord Beaconsfield in 1872 and W. E. Forster in 1875 advocated for imperial federation to replace dependence with association. This movement led to the foundation of the Imperial Federation League in 1884 and the Colonial Conferences starting in 1887 (renamed the Imperial Conference in 1907).

## Agricultural Plantations in Kent
In England, the term "plantation" also refers to the cultivation of hops, particularly in the county of Kent. By the start of the 19th century, hops were cultivated in 290 parishes in Kent; a century later, this increased to 331 out of 413 parishes. These plantations are categorized by district and geological formation:
* **East Kents:** Grown on the Chalk and London Tertiaries.
* **Bastard East Kents:** Produced on alluvial soil and mixtures of clay, loam, marl, and chalk.
* **Mid Kents:** Derived from Greensand soils and London Tertiaries.
* **Wealds:** Grown on Hastings Sand, Tunbridge Wells Sand, and Weald Clay.

Recent rearrangements in these plantations have seen the grubbing of varieties such as Prolifics, Grapes, Jones's, and Colegate's in favor of Bramling and Goldings.
