# Christianity and Civilization

## Influence on Hellenism and Byzantine Culture
Christianity effected a momentous and radical change upon old Hellenism. This transition was not abrupt, as the path for the new religion had been prepared by the humane doctrines of the Stoics, the crumbling of ancient beliefs in the gods, and the mystic intellectual tendencies of Neoplatonism. Christianity adapted itself to popular ideas and usages and adopted significant portions of pagan literature. 

The educational system during Christian times remained remarkably based on the material and methods inherited from paganism. In the development of Byzantine civilization and the distinctive character of its literature, the influence of Christianity was paramount, alongside the influences of Rome and the East. In Egypt, the land of the Nile proved especially productive of Greek literature under the Cross, producing figures such as Arius, Athanasius, Origen, and Synesius.

## Expansion into Central Europe
In the region now known as Austria, Christianity and civilization obtained entrance into the land during the period of Roman rule. Before the Christian era, the area was inhabited by the Taurisci (a Celtic tribe later called the Norici), who were conquered by the Romans around 14 B.C. Under Roman administration, Vindobona (modern Vienna) became a location of importance. 

However, the subsequent weakness of the Roman empire left the region vulnerable to barbarian inroads. During the great migrations, the land was ravaged by various tribes, most notably the Huns. Following this period, the district was settled around 590 by the Slovenes (or Corutanes), a Slavonic people. They were later included in the kingdom of the Avars until 805, when the Avars were subdued and their land incorporated into the Frankish empire by Charlemagne.

## Early Christian Architecture and Sites
Early Christian influence is preserved in various architectural structures. In Rome, the basilica of S. Sabina on the Aventine, dating from approximately A.D. 430, retains much of its primitive aspect, featuring carved wooden doors of early Christian date and mosaic of cut marble (*opus sectile*). Other notable structures include the basilicas of S. Agnese and S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura; the latter was originally constructed in A.D. 432 with a short nave of six bays and an internal narthex.

In other regions, Christianity established various seats of authority and worship. In Egypt, the town of Syene became the seat of a bishopric in the early days of Christianity, and the surrounding area contains numerous ruins of Coptic convents. In eastern Palestine, the town of Kerak contains the church of St George in its Christian quarter, and the town's mosque is also noted as being of Christian origin.

## Religious Conflict and Diplomacy
The interaction between Christianity and other faiths often manifested in military conflict and subsequent treaties. In one instance, a confederate navy allowed Christians to land and be hailed by citizens under the ensign of the cross, though Africa was eventually lost to the "commander of the faithful."

In another historical event, Ladislaus led a confederate army of Polish and Hungarian subjects against the Turks. Following victories led by Huniades, the entrance into Buda was celebrated as both a military and religious triumph, where the pride of conquest was blended with the "humble temper of Christianity." This conflict resulted in a treaty where the followers of Jesus and Mahomet swore on the Gospel and the Koran, respectively, to attest to the word of God as the avenger of perfidy and guardian of truth. During these negotiations, the Christians refused a Turkish proposal to substitute the Eucharist for the Gospel, as they would not profane the real presence of the Catholic deity.
