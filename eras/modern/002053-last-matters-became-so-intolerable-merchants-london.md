# British Colonial Administration and Trade in the Bahamas

## Establishment of Crown Governance
In the early 18th century, conditions in the Bahamas reached a point where matters became intolerable. In response, the merchants of Bristol and London petitioned the crown to intervene to restore order and take possession of the territory. This led to the appointment of Captain Woodes Rogers, who arrived at New Providence in 1718 as the first crown governor. Following his arrival, families of good character began to settle in the Bahamas. While some progress was made in developing the colony's resources, these efforts were periodically interrupted by the tyrannical behavior of several governors who succeeded Captain Woodes Rogers.

## Agricultural Development and Challenges
The colony saw various attempts to establish agricultural industries. At Eleuthera, the pine-apple was introduced as a crop for cultivation. Later, during the American war of independence, a significant number of colonists arrived, bringing wealth and slave labour. This influx led to attempts to cultivate cotton on a large scale. By 1783, on Long Island, nearly 4,000 acres of land were under cultivation with 800 slaves working the land.

However, these agricultural efforts were plagued by misfortune. In 1788 and again in 1794, the red bug destroyed the cotton crops. Consequently, by the year 1800, the cultivation of cotton was almost entirely abandoned.

## Military Conflicts and Territorial Changes
The Bahamas experienced several shifts in control during the late 18th century. In 1776, Commodore Hopkins of the American navy captured the island of New Providence, though he soon abandoned it because it was untenable. In 1781, the Spanish governor of Cuba retook the island. The Spaniards maintained nominal possession of the Bahamas until 1783. Before peace was officially notified, New Providence was recaptured in June 1783 by Lieutenant-Colonel Deveaux, a loyalist from the South Carolina militia.
