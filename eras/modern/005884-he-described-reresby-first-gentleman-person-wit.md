# The Alcibiades of the 17th Century

The individual described by Reresby as "the first gentleman of person and wit I think I ever saw" was a prominent figure of the 17th century, often compared to Alcibiades. While his unscrupulous conduct and ostentatious licence were censured, he remained irresistible to his contemporaries due to his good looks, generosity, and good-natured disposition. He was recognized as the leader of fashion and an unsurpassed mimic.

## Personal Attributes and Talents
His contemporaries and critics alike noted his amusing wit, many examples of which have survived. He possessed unrivalled skill in fencing, dancing, and riding. Dean Lockier observed that his graceful movement made it impossible for observers not to follow him with their eyes when he entered a presence-chamber. His favorite sports were hunting and racing, and his name persisted for a long time in the hunting songs of Yorkshire.

## Intellectual and Artistic Pursuits
He served as a patron to several figures, including Wycherley, Matthew Clifford, Sprat, and Cowley. His interests extended into the sciences; he dabbled in chemistry and, according to Burnet, believed for several years that he was close to discovering the philosopher's stone. He also established glass works at Lambeth, the products of which received praise from Evelyn. Additionally, his biographer Brian Fairfax noted that he spent significant sums of money on the construction of *insanae substructions*.

## Literary Representations
His character and appearance have been captured in portraits by several writers, including Pope in the *Epistle to Lord Bathurst*, Dryden, Burnet, and Count Hamilton in the *Mémoires de Grammont*. Sir Walter Scott also drew his portrait in *Peveril of the Peak*. In the work *Absalom and Achitophel*, Dryden depicted him under the character of Zimri. This portrayal prompted a response from the subject in 1682, titled *Poetical Reflections on a late Poem ... by a Person of Honour*.
