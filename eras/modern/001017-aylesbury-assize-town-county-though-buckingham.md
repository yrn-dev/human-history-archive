# Buckinghamshire

## History and Administration
The shire of Buckingham was established during the reign of Edward the Elder following the division of Mercia. It was likely formed by aggregating pre-existing hundreds around the county town, which resulted in the irregular nature of its boundary line. At the time of the Norman invasion, the area was probably part of the earldom of Leofwine, son of Godwin; consequently, the county suffered sweeping confiscations after the Conquest due to the support it provided him at the battle of Hastings.

Historically, Buckinghamshire and Bedfordshire shared a common sheriff until the era of Queen Elizabeth. The shire court of the county was held at Aylesbury. The county's administrative structure has evolved from eighteen hundreds listed in the Domesday survey to eight. Three of these—Stoke, Burnham, and Desborough—are known as the "Chiltern Hundreds" and have remained unaltered in name and extent. The others were formed by uniting three ancient hundreds each; specifically, the hundred of Aylesbury is still referred to as "the three hundreds of Aylesbury."

The ancient county covers 475,682 acres, while the administrative county covers 479,358 acres. The population was 185,284 in 1891 and grew to 195,764 by 1901. The administrative county comprises 230 civil parishes. Ecclesiastically, the county is almost entirely within the diocese of Oxford, containing 215 ecclesiastical parishes.

## Government and Law
Buckinghamshire is situated in the midland circuit. While Buckingham serves as the county town, Aylesbury is the assize town for the county. The county is divided into thirteen petty sessional divisions and maintains one court of quarter sessions. The boroughs of Wycombe and Buckingham possess separate commissions of the peace.

Parliamentary representation is divided into three divisions: Southern (Wycombe), Mid (Aylesbury), and Northern (Buckingham), with each returning one member. Additionally, the county contains a small portion of the parliamentary borough of Windsor, which is primarily located in Berkshire.

## Towns and Urban Districts
The municipal boroughs include the county town of Buckingham (population 3,152) and Wycombe—officially Chepping Wycombe, also known as High or Chipping Wycombe (population 15,542). Other urban districts include:

* Fenny Stratford (4,799)
* Newport Pagnell (4,028)
* Eton (3,301)

* Beaconsfield (1,570)

Lesser market towns include Amersham, Winslow, Olney, Wendover, Stony Stratford, Prince's Risborough, and Ivinghoe. Wolverton is notable for housing the carriage works of the London & North-Western railway. Along the banks of the Thames, several villages have become centers of residence, including Bourne End, Wooburn, Burnham, Cookham, and Taplow.

## Aylesbury
Located 38 miles northwest by west of London, Aylesbury is a market town situated on a slight eminence in the fertile Vale of Aylesbury, which extends north from the Chiltern Hills. The town's streets are characterized as picturesque, narrow, and irregular. Its principal public buildings are the town-hall and the county-hall, both of which overlook a broad market-place. The town's grammar school was established in 1611.

The church of St Mary is a large cruciform building primarily of Early English style, featuring a transitional Norman font and a pre-Norman crypt. It contains 15th-century chancel stalls and Decorated canopied tombs, with a 17th-century ornate clock-turret atop the central tower.

Economically, Aylesbury is noted for a large agricultural trade, particularly the rearing of ducks. Other industries include printing works, the manufacture of condensed milk, and straw-plaiting. In the vicinity of Aylesbury, the Jacobean mansion of Hartwell served as the residence for the French king Louis XVIII during his exile from 1810 to 1814.

## Infrastructure and Communications
The county is served by several railway systems:
* **London & North-Western Railway:** The main line crosses the northeast. Bletchley serves as an important junction with branches to Oxford, Banbury, Bedford, Cambridge, and Fenny Stratford. Buckingham is served by the western branch, and a branch exists from Cheddington to Aylesbury.
* **Metropolitan-Great Central Joint Line:** This line serves Aylesbury, Amersham, and Chesham, joining the North-Western Oxford branch at Verney Junction. The Great Central main line continues northwest from Quainton Road, with a light railway connecting to the village of Brill.
* **Great Western Railway:** The main line crosses the extreme south via Taplow and Slough. A branch from Maidenhead to Oxford serves Prince's Risborough and High Wycombe, with further branches to Aylesbury and Watlington. A joint line with the Great Central company also passes through High Wycombe, Beaconsfield, and Prince's Risborough.

The Grand Junction Canal reaches the valley of the Ouse via the Ouzel valley and has branches extending to Buckingham and Aylesbury. Aside from the Thames, no rivers in the county are continuously navigable.

## Notable Events and Institutions
The county's proximity to London has involved it in various national events. During the conflict between King John and his barons, William Mauduit defended Hanslape Castle until its capture and demolition in 1216 by Falkes de Bréauté. The Black Death severely impacted the region, nearly depopulating districts such as Winslow. During the civil war, Buckinghamshire joined a mutual defence association supporting parliament, maintaining garrisons at Brill and Aylesbury. Conversely, royalist troops briefly garrisoned Newport Pagnell, and the king established headquarters at Buckingham in 1644.

The most notable institution in the county is Eton College, a famous public school founded by Henry VI.
