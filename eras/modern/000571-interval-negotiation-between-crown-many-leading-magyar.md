# Negotiations Between the Crown and Magyar Liberals (1903–1906)

Following a period of political instability in Hungary, an interval of negotiation took place between the crown and various leading Magyar Liberals. This period was characterized by tensions over military prerogatives, nationalist aspirations, and the constitutional balance of the Dual Monarchy.

## The Tisza Cabinet and the "Programme of the Nine"
By the end of October 1903, Count Stephen Tisza, son of Koloman Tisza, accepted a mission to form a cabinet after other candidates had declined. Tisza presented a programme, drafted by a committee of nine members and known as the "programme of the nine," which included several concessions from the crown to satisfy Magyar nationalist sentiment. These concessions focused primarily on military matters, including:
* The introduction of a two-year service system.
* The establishment of military scholarships for Magyar youths.
* The transfer of officers of Hungarian origin from Austrian to Hungarian regiments.
* Reforms regarding penal procedure and military badges.

Regarding the military language, the programme asserted that the cabinet's responsibility extended to the crown's military prerogatives and that parliament maintained legal influence over constitutional rights. However, for "weighty political reasons affecting great interests of the nation," the specific question of military language was excluded from the programme. The Liberal party adopted an addendum, sanctioned by the crown, maintaining that the king held the right to determine the language of service and command in the Hungarian army based on his constitutional prerogatives as recognized in clause 11 of law XII. of 1867.

## Conflict Over Military Language and Prerogatives
Despite these concessions, obstruction continued in parliament, led by Clericals and extreme Independents. While a "guillotine" motion introduced by Tisza in March 1904 temporarily ended obstruction—allowing for the adoption of the Recruits bill and the voting of £22,000,000 for the army and navy—tensions resurfaced in late 1904. 

The Hungarian Opposition demanded that German words of command in the joint army be replaced by Magyar words in regiments recruited from Hungary. This demand created a constitutional conflict centered on the Fundamental Law XII. of 1867:
* **Clause 11:** The crown argued this clause placed everything relating to the unitary direction, leadership, and inner organization of the whole army (including the Hungarian army) at the disposal of the monarch.
* **Clause 12:** Partisans of the Magyar words of command argued that because Hungary reserved the right to fix the conditions on which recruits were granted, the abolition of German commands could be made a condition of granting those recruits.

The crown viewed the demand for Magyar words of command as an encroachment on royal prerogatives and a move that would disintegrate the army by requiring that only Magyar officers command Hungarian regiments.

## The Coalition and the Constitutional Deadlock
The political situation deteriorated in December 1904 when the Opposition invaded and wrecked the Chamber. In January 1905, Count Tisza suffered an overwhelming electoral defeat to a Coalition composed of Independents, Clericals, dissentient Liberals, and a few Banffyites. The Independence party became the strongest political group, though adherents of the dual system maintained an actual majority in the Chamber.

A deadlock ensued from January 1905 until April 1906. The Coalition refused to take office unless the crown granted demands for customs separation from Austria and the use of Magyar words of command. The crown declined, believing such concessions would wreck the dual system as interpreted since 1867.

## The Fejervary Administration and Universal Suffrage
In June 1905, the Tisza cabinet was succeeded by a non-parliamentary administration led by General Baron Fejervary. To resolve the dispute with the Coalition, Fejervary and Minister of the Interior Joseph Kristoffy proposed that the conflict be settled through a test of universal suffrage, which would require a radical reform of the Hungarian franchise.

This scheme alarmed the Coalition for two reasons:
1. It threatened the political hegemony of the Magyar nobility and gentry.
2. It risked undermining the supremacy of the Magyar race by granting political equality to non-Magyars.

The deadlock only ended after the Chamber was dissolved by military force on February 19, 1906. Facing an open breach of the constitution, a depression in trade, and miserable public finances, the Coalition finally came to terms with the crown to form an administration. Their primary motive was to avoid universal suffrage or ensure it was introduced in a manner that safeguarded Magyar supremacy over other Hungarian races.
