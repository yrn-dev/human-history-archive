# The Internet: From ARPANET to Global Connectivity

## Overview
The evolution of the internet is not the story of a single "eureka" moment, but rather a decades-long convergence of military strategy, academic collaboration, and commercial ambition. It began during the Cold War as a project of the U.S. Department of Defense's Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA). The primary goal was to create a decentralized communication network that could withstand partial destruction—such as a nuclear strike—by ensuring that data could be rerouted if one node failed.

The fundamental breakthrough was "packet switching," a method developed independently by Paul Baran and Donald Davies. Unlike traditional circuit-switching used in telephony, packet switching broke data into small chunks, allowing multiple users to share the same network lines efficiently. By the 1970s, the development of the TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol) by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn provided a universal "language" that allowed disparate networks to communicate, effectively creating a "network of networks."

While the infrastructure was laid by the 1980s, the internet remained the domain of scientists and the military until Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989. By introducing HTML, HTTP, and the first web browser, Berners-Lee transformed a complex command-line system into a visual, navigable experience, sparking the global explosion of connectivity in the 1990s.

## Key Facts
*   **Decentralization:** The core architecture of the internet is designed without a central "hub," ensuring robustness and scalability.
*   **The Web vs. The Internet:** Historians and technologists emphasize that the *Internet* is the hardware and protocols (the tracks), while the *World Wide Web* is a service that runs on top of it (the train).
*   **The "Inventorship" Debate:** There is an ongoing historiographical debate regarding who "invented" the internet. While the U.S. government funded ARPANET, some historians argue that the contributions of French researchers (CYCLADES) and British theorists were equally pivotal in shaping the packet-switching logic.

## Did You Know?
1. **The First Message:** The first message sent over ARPANET in 1969 was intended to be "LOGIN," but the system crashed after the first two letters, making "LO" the first transmission.
2. **DNS Revolution:** Before the Domain Name System (DNS) was created in 1983, users had to maintain a local text file (`HOSTS.TXT`) containing the numerical IP addresses of every computer on the network.
3. **CERN's Gift:** In 1993, CERN placed the World Wide Web software in the public domain, ensuring it remained free and open for everyone to develop.
