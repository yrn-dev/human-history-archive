# DE BROGLIE

The de Broglie family is a noble French house of originally Piedmontese origin. The family emigrated to France in 1643, at which time the head of the family, François Marie (1611–1656), assumed the title of comte de Broglie. A distinguished soldier, François Marie served as a lieutenant-general and died on July 2, 1656, during the siege of Valenza.

## Military Tradition in the 17th and 18th Centuries

The family established a long history of high-ranking military service. Victor Maurice, comte de Broglie (1647–1727), served under Turenne, Condé, and other prominent commanders during the era of Louis XIV. His career progressed from *maréchal de camp* in 1676 to lieutenant-general in 1688, and he was eventually named marshal of France in 1724.

Their son, François Marie, Duc de Broglie (1671–1745), also entered the army at a young age, becoming lieutenant-colonel of the king's regiment of cavalry by age twenty-three. He served in the War of the Spanish Succession, including the battle of Denain and the engagement at Malplaquet. In 1710, he was made lieutenant-general, and by 1719, he served as director-general of cavalry and dragoons. Beyond military duties, he served as ambassador to England in 1724.

During the war in Italy in 1733 and 1734, François Marie fought at the battles of Guastalla and Parma, and was made marshal of France in 1734. He narrowly escaped capture on the night of September 14, 1734, when enemy forces raided his quarters on the Secchia. Later, he served as governor-general of Alsace. In 1742, he was appointed to command the French army in Germany. While characterized as a cautious "man of small means" lacking daring, he achieved a success at the action of Sahay on May 25, 1742, for which he was granted the title of duke.

## The Campaigns of 1742 and 1743

During the 1742 campaign, Broglie's forces on the Moldau were weakened by the departure of Saxon and Bavarian troops. By late June 1742, the French were nearly surrounded at Prague by the army of Prince Charles. Despite resisting with inferior forces, Broglie's position became perilous. To relieve him, the French government dispatched Marshal Maillebois from the lower Rhine. However, a disagreement arose regarding the mission: Marshal Belleisle and Broglie believed Maillebois was to secure a permanent foothold in Bohemia, whereas Maillebois viewed his role as merely covering Broglie's retreat.

Consequently, Broglie was called in haste to take over command from Maillebois on the Danube, east of Regensburg. Aided by the imperial army of Charles VII under Seckendorf, Broglie cleared Bavaria of Austrian forces.

The 1743 campaign began with difficulties due to quarrels between Broglie and Seckendorf, leading to a lack of connected resistance against the converging armies of Prince Lobkowitz, Khevenhueller, and Prince Charles. Following a severe Bavarian reverse near Braunau on May 9, 1743, Broglie entered a full retreat, and Bavarian strongholds surrendered. Eventually, Broglie—worn out by his exertions and age—was replaced by Marshal Coigny.

## Achille Charles Léonce Victor, Duc de Broglie

Achille Charles Léonce Victor, Duc de Broglie (1785–1870), was a diplomat and statesman born in Paris on November 28, 1785. During his childhood, his mother escaped to Switzerland to avoid imprisonment, returning to Paris after the fall of Robespierre. In 1796, she married M. d'Argenson, the grandson of Louis XV's minister of war, under whose care the young de Broglie received a liberal education.

Under the Empire, he entered aristocratic and literary society and was appointed a member of the council of state in 1809, where he served as an attaché on various diplomatic missions for Napoleon. Although he did not sympathize with the principles of the Empire, he feared the violent reaction that followed its downfall, seeking a reconciliation between the Revolution and the Restoration.

In June 1814, Louis XVIII summoned him to the Chamber of Peers. He gained distinction there for his courageous defense of Marshal Ney, being the only peer to both speak and vote for Ney's acquittal. In February 1816, he married the daughter of Madame de Staël at Leghorn. He returned to Paris later that year but refrained from political activity until September 1817, when a moderate assembly replaced the "ultra-royalists" of the *Chambre introuvable*.
