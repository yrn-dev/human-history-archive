# Robert Baillie and the Rye House Plot

## Early Life and Conflict in Scotland
Robert Baillie, known as Baillie of Jerviswood, was the son of George Baillie of St. John's Kirk in Lanarkshire. He became an enemy of the Scottish government in June 1676 after he rescued his brother-in-law, a Presbyterian minister named Kirkton. Kirkton had been illegally seized and confined in a house by an informer named Carstairs. As a result of this action, Baillie was imprisoned for four months and fined L500, though he was liberated after paying half of that fine to Carstairs.

## Political Activities and the Rye House Plot
By 1683, Baillie determined to emigrate to South Carolina due to despair over the state of his country, but this plan failed. In that same year, Baillie and several friends traveled to London. There, he entered into communication with the party of Russell and Monmouth to seek redress. Following the discovery of the Rye House Plot, Baillie was arrested.

During questioning by the king, Baillie denied any knowledge of the conspiracy. However, he admitted that he had been consulted regarding a potential insurrection in Scotland. Despite the lack of evidence connecting him to the plot, he was kept in close confinement and fined L6000.

## Trial and Execution
On December 23, 1684, while in a languishing state, Baillie was brought before the high court on charges of treason. He was found guilty the following day and was hanged on the afternoon of December 24 at the market cross in Edinburgh. His execution was marked by "all the usual barbarities," and his treatment is remembered as one of the worst crimes of the Stuart administration in Scotland.

Baillie was married to a sister of Lord Warriston, Sir Archibald Johnston. He had a son, George, who fled to Holland and later returned with William III to be restored to his estates. His cousin, Bishop Burnet, described Baillie as a man of great virtue and piety who followed Presbyterian principles and was learned in languages, mathematics, and the law.

## Related Figures and Executions
The period surrounding the Rye House Plot and the "rash and wicked enterprise of Monmouth" in 1685 saw significant government prosecution of nonconformists. This included the arrest of Henry, the exile of Howe, and the imprisonment of Baxter.

The era was also characterized by the use of beheading for high-ranking offenders. This included the execution of William, Lord Russell, on July 21, 1683, and the duke of Monmouth on July 15, 1685. Both executions were carried out by the public hangman John Ketch (also known as "Jack Ketch"), who was believed to have been appointed in 1663. 

Ketch's execution of Lord Russell was described as clumsy, leading Ketch to later issue an "Apologie" claiming the prisoner did not dispose himself suitably and that Ketch had been interrupted. During the execution of the duke of Monmouth, the duke referred to Ketch's treatment of Lord Russell. This reportedly unmanned Ketch, who required a knife and at least five strokes of his axe to sever Monmouth's head. Ketch was deposed and imprisoned at Bridewell in 1686, though he was briefly reappointed after his successor, a butcher named Pascha Rose, was hanged at Tyburn. Ketch died toward the end of 1686.
