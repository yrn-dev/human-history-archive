# His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras

"His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras" was the former official designation for the territory now known as British Honduras (also formerly called Balize or Belize). Located in Central America, the crown colony is bounded by the Bay of Honduras and the Caribbean Sea to the northeast and east, the Mexican province of Yucatan to the north and northwest, and Guatemala to the south and west. As of 1905, the colony had an area of 7,562 square miles and a population of 40,372.

## Origins and Early Settlement
The settlement originated in 1638, established by log-wood cutters who had previously been buccaneers. They were later joined by agents from the Chartered Company, which operated pearl fisheries on the Mosquito coast. Despite their industry, the settlers continued their former habits by conducting frequent raids on Spanish logwood establishments. 

The Spanish attempted to expel the British settlers on several occasions. The most significant effort occurred in April 1754, when a Spanish expedition of 1,500 men was organized inland at the town of Peten. However, as the force approached the coast, it was routed by 250 British defenders. Following this defeat, the log-wood cutters remained undisturbed for several years.

## Treaties and Conflict with Spain
The position of the settlers became sufficiently established that the treaty of 1763 with Spain included a clause stating that the cutters of logwood and their workmen were not to be "disturbed or molested, under any pretext whatever," though Great Britain agreed to demolish all fortifications erected in the Bay of Honduras. 

Despite this recognition, British settlers continued to encroach on Spanish territory. The Spaniards, alleging that the settlers were engaged in illicit practices and smuggling, launched a sudden attack on September 15, 1779. The establishment at Belize was destroyed, and the inhabitants were taken prisoner to Mérida in Yucatan and later to Havana. Most of the prisoners died in Havana; the survivors were liberated in 1782 and sent to Jamaica.

By 1783, the settlers returned with new adventurers to resume cutting woods. On September 3, 1783, a new treaty was signed between Spain and Great Britain, expressly granting British subjects the right to cut, load, and carry away logwood in the district.

## Establishment of Sovereignty
The legal status of the territory evolved through various conflicts and diplomatic agreements. A Spanish attempt in 1798 was defeated, which some viewed as an act of conquest establishing permanent British sovereignty. However, this view is contested because the British government did not claim rights based on that defense for many years; parliamentary acts between 1817 and 1819 continued to describe Belize as "a settlement, for certain purposes, under the protection of His Majesty." Furthermore, a 1814 treaty with Spain revived the provisions of an earlier agreement.

Following the independence of Central America (1819–1822), Great Britain secured its position by incorporating the 1786 treaty provisions into a new treaty with Mexico in 1826, as well as in draft treaties with the United States of Central America (1831) and New Granada (1825). In 1836, the British claimed the territories between the Sarstoon and Belize rivers.

Legal sovereignty over the entire colony, including the 1836 claims, was finally confirmed through:
* The peaceful progress of the country under British rule.
* The 1850 Clayton-Bulwer Treaty, which exempted Belize from the prohibition against Great Britain and the United States fortifying or colonizing the Central American mainland.
* The 1859 settlement of boundary disputes with Guatemala.

## Borders and Security
The frontier of British Honduras was defined by conventions in 1859 and 1893 with Guatemala. The boundary begins at the mouth of the river Sarstoon in the Bay of Honduras, follows that river to the rapids of Gracias à Dios, runs in a straight line to Garbutt's Rapids on the Belize river, and then proceeds north to the Mexican frontier. At the Mexican border, the boundary follows the river Hondo to its mouth in Chetumal Bay.

Between 1849 and 1901, the colony's northern and north-western marches were occasionally attacked by rebel bands of Indians who had risen against their Mexican rulers. These bands were finally subjugated in 1901, with the last serious raid being foiled in 1872.
