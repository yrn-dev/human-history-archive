# Lucien Bonaparte and the French Territory

## Early Life and Political Alignment
Lucien Bonaparte was born on May 21, 1775, in Ajaccio. He attended schools at Brienne and Autun. Although he initially desired to join the French army, defective sight prevented this, leading him toward a career in the church. He entered the seminary at Aix in Provence in 1786, but his volatile and excitable nature clashed with the discipline of the institution. 

Following the outbreak of the Revolution in 1789, Lucien became an eager supporter of the anti-clerical and democratic movements. Upon returning to Corsica, he served as the leading speaker for the Jacobin club in Ajaccio. He urged his brothers to break ties with Paoli, the leader of the conservative party that sought an alliance with England against the regicide republic of France. After the Paolists gained control, the Bonapartes left the island and joined Lucien at Toulon. In southern France, Lucien continued his work for the Jacobin cause, adopting the name "Brutus" in the Jacobin club of St Maximin, which had been renamed Marathon.

## Rise and Conflict with Napoleon
On May 4, 1794, Lucien married Mlle Catherine Boyer without family consent, as he was still a minor, resulting in a period of penury and near-disgrace. Following the *coup d'etat* of Thermidor on July 28, 1794, he fled St Maximin and took a small post at St Chamans, where he was imprisoned. He was released through the influence of Napoleon, who secured him a position as a commissioner in the French army in Germany. Disliking this duty, Lucien returned to Corsica and later entered the Council of Five Hundred in Paris in 1798.

Lucien assisted Napoleon in triumphing over parliamentary institutions in France, but he remained suspicious of his brother's ambitions. In letters to his brother Joseph, Lucien noted that Napoleon possessed an ambition that surpassed his love for the general weal and suggested that Napoleon might attempt to "ride on the crest of events" during a counter-revolution. During the consulate (1799–1804), relations between the brothers became strained. Although Lucien served as minister of the interior, he was removed from the post due to personal and political differences with the First Consul.

## Diplomatic Tenure and Resignation
To mitigate the impact of his removal, Napoleon appointed Lucien as ambassador to the court of Madrid in November 1800. During this time, France and Spain planned to partition Portugal. However, on June 6, 1801, at Badajoz, Lucien Bonaparte and the Spanish minister Godoy signed preliminaries of peace, an act attributed to the use of bribes by the court of Lisbon. This frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon. Following a remonstrance from the First Consul, Lucien resigned and returned to Paris, where he joined the opposition in the Tribunate against Napoleon's schemes.

## Exile from French Territory
The final breach between the brothers occurred following the death of Lucien's wife in 1800. In the early summer of 1802, Lucien became enamored with Mme Jouberthou, making her his mistress. Despite an express prohibition from the First Consul—who wanted Lucien to marry the widow of the king of Etruria for reasons of state—Lucien secretly married Mme Jouberthou at Plessis on October 23, 1803.

Upon learning of the marriage, Napoleon ordered Lucien to leave French territory. Lucien departed for Italy accompanied by his wife and infant son. Before leaving, he further annoyed Napoleon by publicly giving his wife the name of Bonaparte.

## Life in Italy and Later Years
Lucien lived in Italy for several years, primarily in Rome, where he maintained a marked hostility toward the emperor. In December 1807, Napoleon offered an arrangement where Lucien could become a French prince if he annulled his marriage; Lucien refused. He resided for a time at his estate of Canino, taking the papal title of prince of Canino.

Lucien later traveled to America but was captured by a British ship and taken to Malta and then England, where he remained under surveillance until the peace of 1814. He returned to Rome and offered his help to Napoleon during the Hundred Days in 1815, standing by him at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris and defending his prerogatives during his second abdication. Lucien spent the remainder of his life in Italy and died in Rome on June 29, 1840. He is survived by a family consisting of six daughters and four sons. His written works include *Memoirs* (incomplete), *La Verite sur les Cent Jours*, and the two-volume epic *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree* (1814).

## The Prince of Canino (Charles Lucien)
Lucien's son, Charles Lucien Jules Laurent (1803–1857), also known as the prince of Canino, was a scientist. He married his cousin Zenaide Bonaparte in 1822. He published *American Ornithology* (1825–1833) and various other zoological catalogs regarding European birds, fish, and mammals. He was an honorary member of the academies of Berlin and Upsala and a correspondent of the Institute of France. 

In 1847, Charles Lucien engaged in Italian political agitation, favoring the expulsion of Austrians and Italian independence. He was elected deputy by Viterbo to the national assembly and entered the Junto of Rome in 1848. Following the failure of the revolution, he left Italy in July 1849, traveling through Holland and France. He died in 1857, leaving eight surviving children.
