# Idylles prussiennes

The *Idylles prussiennes* (1871) is a collection of poems. It is listed among the works of an author whose other literary contributions include the poems *Trente-six ballades joyeuses* (1875), various prose tales such as *Les Saltimbanques* (1853), *Esquisses parisiennes* (1859), and *Contes feeriques*, as well as plays including *Le Feuilleton d'Aristophane* (1852), *Gringoire* (1866), and *Deidamia* (1876).

## The Idyl as a Literary Form

The term "idyl" or "idyll" is derived from the Greek *eidyllion*, meaning a descriptive piece, which itself comes from *eidos*, signifying a shape or style. In the Latin tradition, it is rendered as *idyllium*. Historically, an idyl is a short poem characterized by a rural or pastoral nature, typically preserving or evoking elements of landscape.

In antiquity, early commentators used the term to describe a wide variety of brief, homely poems that included descriptions of natural objects. The specific pastoral concept developed in connection with the Alexandrian school during the 3rd century before Christ, specifically through the works of Moschus, Bion, and Theocritus. At that time, *eidyllion* was not necessarily a formal name for a specific verse form but served as a diminutive meaning "a little piece in the style of" a particular subject. For instance, the pastoral poets wrote *eidyllia aipolika*, or "little pieces in the goatherd style."

The ten "Idyls" of Theocritus are often viewed as the prototype for the popular understanding of the genre, although the pieces themselves are diverse. For example, "Festival of Adonis" is a realistic mime, and the "Dirge for Daphnis" served as a prototype for the modern elegy rather than the modern idyl. The conception of the idyl was actually initiated by Theocritus's six little epical romances. By the 4th century after Christ, the Latin usage of the word expanded to cover short descriptive poems of various characters, as seen in the "Edyllia" section of the early manuscripts of Ausonius.

## Other Notable Uses of the Idyl

The idyll has been employed by various writers across different eras and languages:

*   **German Literature:** Kleist wrote charming idylls, along with odes and elegies. His chief work, the poem in hexameters *Der Frühling* (1749), focused on the beauties of nature.
*   **Swedish Literature:** Karl Fredrik Dahlgren (1791-1844), a humorist and poet, was noted for his realistic and idyllic descriptions. An example of his ornamented and sensuous style is the poem *Zephyr and the Girl*.
*   **British Literature:** Robert Williams Buchanan (1841-1901) published *Idyls and Legends of Inverburn* in 1865.
*   **French Literature:** The Comte de Kératry (1769-1859) published *Contes et Idylles* in 1791.
*   **Prose Adaptations:** Charles Kingsley produced a work titled *Prose Idylls* (1873), which included many of his essays.
