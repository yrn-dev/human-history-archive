# Amblystoma

The genus *Amblystoma* consists of various salamanders native to North America. Established by J. J. Tschudi in 1838, the genus was created to categorize species that had previously been described as *Salamandra* or *Lacerta*. In terms of general appearance, these animals differ little from European salamanders, possessing a smooth, shiny body with vertical grooves on the sides and a tail that is only feebly compressed. They feature moderately large eyes with movable lids and an upper lip that is nearly straight.

## Anatomical Characteristics
While they may resemble other salamanders externally, the dentition of the palate in *Amblystoma* is distinct. The small teeth are arranged in a single row, similar to the jaws, and form a long transverse series—either continuous or interrupted—located behind the inner nares, also known as choanae.

## Life Cycle and Metamorphosis
The *Amblystoma* is the "perfect" or imago form of the salamander. The animal typically leaves the water after completing its metamorphosis, a process where the final stage is characterized by the loss of its gills. 

It has been established that the axolotl (*Siredon*) is the larval form of the *Amblystoma*. While it once seemed impossible that an animal capable of propagating while retaining its gills for years could be anything other than a perfect form, subsequent discoveries confirmed this larval relationship. In aquarium settings, most axolotls remain in the branchiate (gilled) condition, and individuals that undergo transformation are considered very exceptional.

## Species and Distribution
The genus includes approximately twenty species. One of the most widely distributed and largest is *Amblystoma tigrinum*. This species is found in the cooler regions of Mexico as well as in the east and west of the United States. *Amblystoma tigrinum* rarely exceeds nine inches in length and varies in color, though it is usually blackish or brown with yellow spots that are sometimes arranged in transverse bands. This is the specific *Amblystoma* into which the axolotl has been ascertained to transform.

## Observations in Mexico
There has been historical debate regarding whether axolotls in the wild ever transform into *Amblystoma*. Some works stated that axolotls in the lakes near Mexico City never transform. However, this has been questioned because *Amblystoma tigrinum* examples have been received from various parts of Mexico, and Alfred Duges described an *Amblystoma* from the mountains near Mexico City.

In 1902, H. Gadow visited Mexico and provided further clarity on these conditions:
* **Lago de Texcoco:** Gadow ascertained that no axolotls live in this lake, which refuted theories by Weismann suggesting that salt-incrusted or brackish surroundings hemmed in the axolotl.
* **High Altitude Species:** Gadow confirmed Duges's statement that a second species of *Amblystoma* exists near Mexico at a higher altitude. This species undergoes normal metamorphosis, which may explain observations of regularly transforming *Amblystomas* near the city.
* **Lakes Chalco and Xochimilco:** These lakes, situated about 10 feet higher than Lago de Texcoco, are abundant with axolotls. Gadow described the environment as a "paradise" with fertile pastures, shrubs, trees, and streams. In Lake Xochimilco, axolotls breed in early February; larvae swarm in thousands and grow into "big, fat creatures" by June. Despite the presence of many opportunities for the animals to leave the water, native fishermen have never known them to metamorphose, and no perfect *Amblystomas* are found in the immediate vicinity of these two lakes.

## History of Axolotl Breeding
Axolotls kept alive in Europe were particularly abundant between 1870 and 1880. It is generally believed that these specimens were descendants of a stock bred in Paris and distributed by dealers, originally by P. Carbonnier. In recent times, specimens have become more difficult to procure and more expensive, particularly in France and England, likely due to close in-breeding without the introduction of new blood.
