# Liberty and the Arguments for Immortality

The concept of immortality—defined as the quality of being exempt from annihilation or death—has been applied to the human soul and body across various philosophical and religious traditions. While the term is sometimes used by analogy to describe individuals whose writings or deeds leave a lasting impression on human memory, the primary discourse focuses on the persistence of the soul after death.

## The Argument from Affection and Yearning
A specific argument for immortality is based on the yearning of human affections to regain communion with the dead. This perspective posits that it would be impossible to continue living if one believed that the separation caused by death were final. This particular line of reasoning was utilized by the English historian Henry Thomas Buckle (1821–1862). Buckle, the author of the *History of Civilization*, included this argument in a review of J.S. Mill's *Essay on Liberty*. This conclusion was reached under the immediate impression of the death of his mother on April 1, 1859. Notably, this argument appeared despite Buckle's previous assertions that history proves the extreme fallibility of consciousness.

Other thinkers have linked the desire for immortality to human instinct and nature. Adam Ferguson argued that the desire for immortality is an instinct that indicates what the author of that desire intends to do. Similarly, R. W. Emerson claimed that the impulse to seek proof of immortality is the strongest proof itself, as the desire arises from the truest and worthiest parts of the human soul. Emerson suggested that losing this belief leads to pessimism and a "moral paralysis" where right and wrong become ephemeral.

## Philosophical and Metaphysical Arguments
Various philosophers have proposed different justifications for the persistence of the soul:

*   **Platonic Arguments:** In the *Phaedrus* and *Laws*, Plato argues that the soul is immortal because it is self-moving. In the *Republic*, he suggests the soul does not perish like the body because sin or wickedness does not kill it in the way diseases wear out bodily life. In the *Phaedo*, the central argument is that the soul participates in the idea of life. However, in the *Timaeus*, Plato indicates that the immortality of souls and gods depends on the goodness and will of the Supreme Creator.
*   **The Juridical Argument:** This argument suggests that because the present life lacks a harmony between character and condition—where the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper—there must be a future life to correct these anomalies. This view is supported by Kant and Butler.
*   **The Probabilistic Approach:** Butler argued that while immortality may not be provable, experience does not forbid it. He suggested that the analogies of life lead to the conclusion that immortality is probable and that God's government is a moral scheme where virtue and vice are rewarded and punished.
*   **Metaphysical Possibility:** Some arguments focus on showing that the soul is not so absolutely dependent on the body that the dissolution of the body necessitates the cessation of the soul.

## Religious Perspectives on Immortality
Different sacred and religious traditions present immortality as a consequence of divine relationship or moral conduct.

**The Hebrew Nation and Early Faith**
The saints of the Hebrew nation believed that death could not sever them from the presence of God, as He had entered into fellowship with them. This is reflected in Psalms xvi and xvii. Jesus further supported this by declaring God to be the God of the living rather than the dead, suggesting that God's companions cannot become victims of death.

**The Qur'an**
The Qur'an presents a vision of immortality tied to the deeds of the individual in life. For those who performed good deeds, there is a Garden provided as an inheritance. In this Garden, souls will find what they yearn after, their eyes will delight, and they will have an abundance of fruit for satisfaction. Conversely, the guilty are described as immortal in the torment of hell. The text states that the punishment for sinners will not be abated and they will dwell there in despair. When the sinners cry out to Malik for their Lord to put an end to them, the response is that they shall abide. The text emphasizes that God is not unjust to them, but rather they were unjust to themselves.

## Liberty and Glory in a Temporal Sense
While much of the archive focuses on the soul, the concept of "immortal glory" has also been used as a motivational tool in a worldly context. Hannibal, for example, used the promise of "immortal glory" and the conquest of Italy to rouse his soldiers. To emphasize the necessity of victory, he forced prisoners to fight one another, promising the conquerors their liberty and rich presents, thereby illustrating to his army that they must either conquer or die.
