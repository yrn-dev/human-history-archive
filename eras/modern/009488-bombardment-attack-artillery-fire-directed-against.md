# BOMBARDMENT

Bombardment is defined as an attack by artillery fire directed against troops in position, fortifications, buildings, or towns. While the term is often used loosely to describe artillery attacks on fortified positions to prepare for infantry assaults, it has a stricter definition referring specifically to the targeting of undefended or defenceless objects, such as public buildings and houses.

## Objectives and Efficacy
In its strict sense, the purpose of bombardment is to dishearten an opponent. Specifically, the assailant aims to force the civil authorities and the civilian population of a besieged location to pressure their military commandant into capitulating before the actual defences have been rendered impotent. Consequently, such bombardments only succeed if the suffering inflicted upon non-combatants is sufficient to break their resolution and if the commandant allows himself to be coerced or influenced by those sufferers.

While the threat of bombardment may sometimes induce a surrender, instances where the actual fulfillment of the threat leads to success are rare. Generally, bombardments fail to achieve their objective when faced with a determined commandant. Furthermore, an intentionally terrific fire directed at a large target differs from "artillery attacks" directed at fortifications, which are slow, steady, and minutely accurate. Bombardments require the expenditure of large quantities of ammunition and cause the guns of the attack to wear out. Despite this, they are frequently used to test the temper of the civil population and the garrison, as seen in the instance of Strassburg in 1870.

## Technical Execution and Observation
The direction of artillery fire relies on reports from patrols of officers, advanced observation posts, and aviators to obtain the necessary range. In field operations, observation posts may be located on the crests of rises, where officers use telescopes mounted on tripods to observe the fire of mortar batteries. These officers then telephone the position of the hits in relation to the intended object.

In naval operations, observation may be arranged from the shore using naval balloons. Long-range guns, such as those mounted on monitors—specialized vessels that have proven well-suited for such operations—can be used to enfilade enemy attacks or maintain heavy fire on a flank.

## Historical Applications and Examples

### The Siege of Belfort (1870)
During the investment of Belfort beginning on November 3, 1870, the fortress's old works—including the town enceinte, the castle, and the entrenched camp—were vulnerable to overwhelming bombardment by rifled cannons because they had been planned for short-range guns. To counter this, the engineer Denfert-Rochereau fortified the Perches ridge and constructed two new forts, Bellevue and Des Barres, to maintain an advanced line. On December 3, bombardment opened on the western side of the river between Bavillers and Essert. Although some houses in Belfort were damaged, the garrison remained unintimidated and replied with spirited artillery fire.

### World War I Operations
During the First World War, bombardments were integrated into various strategic maneuvers:

*   **Trench and Land Warfare:** Infantry positions and artillery positions were often arranged in approximately parallel lines. The zone between these lines was subject to bombardment. In some instances, counterattacks were supported by heavy bombardments involving large quantities of gas. For example, the Thirty-sixth Division withstood very severe artillery bombardment during an attack against German positions before Rheims in October.
*   **Coastal and Naval Actions:** British warships and monitors have been used to bombard coastal zones, such as the area south of Ostend. In one operation off the Belgian coast starting on the 19th of a given month, a naval flotilla used powerful long-range guns to support the Belgian Army's left flank, inflicting substantial losses on German batteries and heavy guns.
*   **Specialized Tactics:** In certain operations, such as the attack on the harbor at Zeebrugge, the omission of a preliminary bombardment of the batteries and harbor was used as a primary element of surprise. Other operations involved the coordination of land-based siege batteries—some of the largest guns ever placed on land-mountings—with aerial bombardments of towns.

## Related Terminology
The term "bomb" was formerly used to describe an explosive shell fired by artillery, derived from the Greek *bombos*, meaning a buzzing or hammering noise. This is related to the term "bombard." In modern contexts, a bomb more frequently refers to an incendiary or shattering grenade, or an explosive vessel using a trip or clockwork mechanism. In early naval warfare, before shell guns were introduced, explosive projectiles were delivered by "bomb-vessels," also known as bombards or bombs.
