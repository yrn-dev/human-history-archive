# Count Eduard Taaffe and the Austrian Government

Count Eduard Taaffe was a prominent political figure in Austria who served as first minister and prime minister, most notably leading the government from 1879 to 1893. His tenure was characterized by a policy of "unpolitical politics," aimed at maintaining government stability by balancing various ethnic and political factions.

## Early Political Career and the Conciliation of Slavs
In the early 1870s, Taaffe served as minister of the interior in a reconstructed cabinet under Stremayr following the resignation of Auersperg in 1870. During this period, Taaffe and Alfred Potocki, the minister of agriculture, advocated for a policy of conciliating the Slav races, a direction recommended by Beust and likely supported by the emperor. However, this approach clashed with other ministers who sought to weaken the opposition by removing elections for the Reichsrath from the diets. This division led Taaffe and his associates to resign in January 1870.

By the late 1860s, the Austrian government faced a crisis as the Reichsrath became nearly impossible to govern; after Galician demands were rejected in March, the Polish club, Slovenes, and Tirolese left the House, leaving it composed almost entirely of Germans. To remedy this, a new government was formed by Potocki and Taaffe with the specific object of attempting reconciliation with the Slavs. Potocki, serving as minister-president, attempted to persuade the Czechs to accept the constitution through negotiations in Vienna and Prague. These efforts failed as the Czechs and Feudals adhered to the declaration of 1868 and refused compromise.

## Rise to Prime Minister and the "Iron Ring"
Taaffe became Austrian prime minister in 1879. He succeeded where Potocki had failed by reaching an agreement with the Czechs. Through private negotiations, Taaffe persuaded Bohemian feudal proprietors to grant seats to the excluded Feudalists and convinced the Czechs to abandon their policy of "passive resistance." Consequently, the Czechs agreed to take their seats in the parliament at Vienna, though they did so while maintaining the view that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary.

In recognition of this, Taaffe's government removed several gross anomalies in the Bohemian electoral system that had previously favored the German minority and consented to the establishment of a Bohemian university at Prague.

Taaffe's power base was solidified through a combination of parties known as the "iron ring," consisting of the Right, Poles, and Czechs. This alliance formed after the Liberals voted against the government on a military bill. Taaffe, who was not a party man and had previously served in a Liberal government, used this coalition to maintain power for fourteen years. His strategy was to destroy the influence of parliament by playing different parties against one another, thereby securing a clear field for the government.

## Domestic Policy and Social Legislation
Under Taaffe's leadership, the government moved away from *laissez-faire* doctrines toward state intervention to remedy social evils. Following a parliamentary inquiry by the Christian Socialists into the poor conditions of oil-mines in Galicia and factories in Moravia, the government introduced factory inspection in 1883. Subsequent laws in 1884 and 1885 established a maximum working day of ten hours in mines and eleven hours in factories, forbade Sunday labor, and limited the employment of women and children. In 1887-1888, the government introduced compulsory insurance against sickness and accidents.

Additionally, the state expanded its role in infrastructure. A 1877 law allowed for the acquisition of private railway lines; by the time Taaffe retired, the state possessed nearly 5,000 miles of railway. A minister of railways was appointed in 1889 to oversee these assets, driven by both economic and military considerations.

## Later Conflicts and Resignation
Despite his successes, Taaffe faced increasing difficulty with the Czechs. While the "Old Czechs" had accepted some concessions, they had not achieved their primary goal: the recognition of the Bohemian kingdom. They were increasingly pressured by the "Young Czechs," who were more impatient. In 1890, Taaffe attempted to reconcile the German party, the Czechs, and the Feudal party through conferences in Vienna. Although an agreement was reached regarding a revision of the electoral law and the separation of German and Czech districts, the measures failed in the diet. The Young Czechs, who had been excluded from the conference, used obstruction to block the bills, arguing that the measures threatened the unity of the Bohemian country. By the 1891 elections, the Young Czechs had emerged victorious over the Old Czechs.

Taaffe's downfall came in October 1893 when he introduced a bold reform bill for universal suffrage. The bill proposed granting the franchise to anyone who could read and write and had resided in one place for six months, while maintaining the *curiae* for chambers of commerce and feudal proprietors. This was opposed by the Liberals, who feared the growth of anti-Semitism and socialism. However, the bill also lacked support from the Conservatives—including Hohenwart—who feared transferring power to the indigent, and from the Poles, who opposed increased influence for the Ruthenes. Facing a hopeless position, Taaffe resigned.
