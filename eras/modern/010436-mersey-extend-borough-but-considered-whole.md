# The River Mersey and Associated Boroughs

The River Mersey serves as a central geographic and economic artery in Northwest England, supporting several major municipal boroughs, extensive dock systems, and critical transport infrastructure. The river's influence extends across the borders of Lancashire and Cheshire, facilitating trade, industry, and urban growth.

## Bootle
Bootle is a municipal and county borough located in the Bootle parliamentary division of Lancashire, England. Situated at the mouth of the Mersey, it forms a northern suburb of Liverpool. The borough covers an area of 1,576 acres and experienced rapid population growth, reaching 58,566 residents by 1901, an increase of nearly nine times over the preceding forty years.

The east bank of the Mersey features great docks that extend into the borough; however, these are considered as a whole under the administration of Liverpool. Similarly, the borough's water supply and communications are viewed as part of the larger systems of the city of Liverpool. In July 1903, a proposal to include Bootle within the city of Liverpool was rejected in parliament. Incorporated in 1868 and created a county borough in 1888, Bootle is governed by a corporation consisting of a mayor, 10 aldermen, and 30 councillors. Notable local institutions include a museum, free libraries, technical schools, several public pleasure grounds, and a handsome town hall.

## Birkenhead
Located on the east shore of the Wirral peninsula in Cheshire, Birkenhead is a seaport and a municipal, county, and parliamentary borough. Positioned opposite Liverpool and 195 miles northwest of London, it had a population of 110,915 in 1901. While the town is wholly of modern growth, the name "Byrkhed" is linked to a forest believed to have once spanned the area between the mouths of the Ribble in Lancashire and the Dee.

The town's development was significantly driven by William Laird, who in 1824 purchased land on the banks of Wallasey Pool, a marshy stream flowing into the Mersey. Despite opposition from Liverpool, parliamentary powers were obtained in 1843 to convert the pool into a shipping basin. This work was completed by James Rendel, and the 7-acre docks opened in 1847. In 1858, these docks were transferred to the Mersey Docks and Harbour Board, an entity created by an 1857 act to control harbourage on both sides of the river.

Birkenhead's infrastructure includes the Birkenhead, Wirral, and London & North-Western and Great Western joint railways. The town is also served by the Wrexham, Mold & Connah's Quay railway (taken over by the Great Central company in 1905), which transports mineral wealth from North Wales and Flint into the docks.

## Transport and Connectivity
Connectivity across the Mersey is maintained through tunnels and ferries. The Mersey tunnel, opened by the prince of Wales in 1886, connects Birkenhead with Liverpool, extending from Park and Rock Ferry stations in Cheshire to the low-level Central Station in Liverpool.

Ferry services also remain vital. The Woodside Ferry is considered the principal entrance to the Wirral and Birkenhead from Liverpool. Historically, the exclusive right of ferryage was granted to a Benedictine monastery in 1332. This monastery, founded around 1150 by Hamon de Mascy and dedicated to St James and St Mary, derived its main revenues from these tolls. The Birkenhead Commissioners purchased the ferry from the lord of the manor, Mr. F.R. Price, in 1842. By 1897, the corporation acquired additional rights over the New Ferry and Rock Ferry. Despite the tunnel, these ferries continue to transport millions of passengers and handle heavy goods traffic annually.

## Regional Tributaries and Engineering
The Mersey system includes tributaries such as the river Tame. Ashton-under-Lyne, a market town and municipal borough of Lancashire, is situated on the Tame. This town is a center for iron-founding, machinery works, hat-making, and cotton-spinning, with large collieries in the surrounding area.

The expansion of the Mersey's navigability was furthered by the engineer James Brindley (1716–1772). After constructing a canal from Worsley to Manchester, Brindley extended his work to the Mersey at Runcorn. He also designed the "Grand Trunk Canal," which connected the Mersey with the Humber and the Trent.
