# The War of the Austrian Succession and Related European Conflicts

## The War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748)
The War of the Austrian Succession began in 1740 when Frederick II of Prussia invaded Silesia. The conflict concluded with the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen) in 1748. While the primary enduring interest of the war was the struggle between Austria and Prussia over Silesia, the conflict involved nearly all European powers after 1741. France and Prussia were constantly allied with Bavaria and Spain, while England and Holland—traditional enemies of France—supported Austria. Among Austria's other significant allies during this period were Saxony and Sardinia.

## The Campaign of 1743
The 1743 campaign began poorly for the emperor. Internal quarrels between Seckendorf and Broglie hindered the coordination of French and Bavarian armies. This allowed Prince Charles's army to march along the Danube, while Prince Lobkowitz moved from Bohemia toward the Naab and Khevenhueller advanced from Salzburg toward southern Bavaria. Following a severe reverse for the Bavarians near Braunau on May 9, 1743, an Anglo-allied army under King George II advanced southward toward the Neckar and Main country.

Marshal Noailles collected a French army on the middle Rhine to oppose this force. Although George II was outmanoeuvred by Noailles in the defile between the river Main and the Spessart Hills, the allied troops forced their way through. On June 27, the British achieved a notable victory at the battle of Dettingen, inflicting heavy losses on the French. Consequently, Noailles and Marshal Coigny (who replaced the exhausted Broglie) were forced into a defensive position behind the Rhine. While Prince Charles prepared to force a passage in the Breisgau, King George II moved via Mainz to draw the attention of the French marshals. After the Anglo-allied army took Worms, the forces entered winter quarters. By this time, Sardinia, Holland, England, and Austria were allied.

## Conflict in Italy and the Netherlands
In Italy, Traun successfully held his position against Neapolitan and Spanish forces. A British squadron forced Naples to withdraw troops for home defense, and Spain sent a second army via France. Sardinia allied herself with Austria; because neither state was at war with France, unusual complications arose, including combats in the Isere valley between Spanish and Sardinian troops in which the French did not participate.

In the Netherlands, the later campaigns were dominated by Marshal Saxe. Following the battle of Fontenoy, the French advanced rapidly, aided by the withdrawal of most English troops to suppress the "Forty-Five rebellion" at home. In 1746, the French drove the Austrians and Dutch back toward the Meuse. On October 11, 1746, Saxe defeated the allies under Prince Charles of Lorraine at the battle of Roucoux. By April 1747, Saxe turned his attention to the United Provinces. On July 2, 1747, the duke of Cumberland and the prince of Orange suffered a severe defeat at Lauffeld. Subsequently, a corps under Marshal Loewendahl stormed Bergen-op-Zoom on September 18, and Maestricht surrendered on May 7, 1748.

## Russian and Swedish Involvement
Russia and Sweden had settled their own quarrel via the peace of Abo in 1743. In 1746, Russia allied herself with Austria, eventually marching a large army from Moscow to the Rhine. Although this army arrived on the Meuse too late to influence the final outcomes of the war, the movement held military significance.

## Later European Conflicts
In a separate historical context, Napoleon dominated Europe west of Russia, defeating Austria and Prussia. However, he failed to secure command of the sea from the British, suffering a conclusive defeat at Trafalgar in 1805. After invading Russia in 1812 with 600,000 men, Napoleon's army was largely destroyed by the Russians and the Russian winter. During the subsequent collapse of his empire, Sweden turned against him and Germany rose in opposition. Following his abdication in 1814 and final defeat at Waterloo in 1815 by the Prussians, Belgians, and British, the victorious allies met at the Congress of Vienna to restore order.
