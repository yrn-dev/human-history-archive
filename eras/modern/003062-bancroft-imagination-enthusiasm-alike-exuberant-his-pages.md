# George Bancroft

George Bancroft (1800–1891) was a prominent American statesman and historian. Born on October 3, 1800, in Worcester, Massachusetts, he came from a family that had been present in America since 1632. His father, Aaron Bancroft, was an author, clergyman, and distinguished revolutionary soldier.

## Education and Early Influences
Bancroft received an extensive education, attending Phillips Academy in Exeter and Harvard University. He furthered his studies in Europe at Berlin, Goettingen, and Heidelberg. While at Goettingen, he studied natural science with Blumenbach, New Testament Greek with Eichhorn, and Plato with Heeren. Bancroft was particularly influenced by Heeren, whom he considered the greatest living historical critic and a forerunner of the modern school; from Heeren, Bancroft acquired his enthusiasm for minute and painstaking erudition.

Following his studies, Bancroft took a European tour where he received attention from various distinguished figures in art, science, and letters, including Hegel, Goethe, Humboldt, Byron, Niebuhr, Manzoni, Constant, Cousin, Savigny, Bunsen, and Schleiermacher.

## Early Career and Challenges
Bancroft's father, a Unitarian, intended for his son to enter the ministry. However, after returning from Europe in 1822, Bancroft's initial attempts at preaching were unsatisfactory, as the theological teachings of the era had replaced faith with literature and criticism. He subsequently took a position as a tutor at Harvard.

As a humanist, Bancroft found the pedantic spirit and narrow curriculum of Harvard during that period unsatisfactory. Additionally, he brought a manner from Europe characterized by the affections of ardent youth, which did not fit easily into a society that was highly satisfied with itself, leading to considerable ridicule of the "young knight-errant." In 1823, he published a small volume consisting of original pieces and translations of poetry, though it did not bring him fame. Over time, his literary and personal style became recognized as a symbol of enormous underlying energy and a resolve to maintain a philosophic calm.

## Historical Works and Methodology
Despite the severe and exacting routine of the Round Hill school, Bancroft translated Heeren's work on *The Politics of Ancient Greece* and wrote frequently for Walsh's *American Quarterly* and the *North American Review*. 

In 1834, he published the first volume of the *History of the United States*, followed by a second volume in 1837, with subsequent volumes published as public life permitted. In 1835, he published an article in the *North American Review* titled "The Documentary History of the Revolution," which introduced the reading public to a new method and revealed the wealth of available material.

Bancroft is ranked as the foremost historian of the United States and is considered second to none among his European contemporaries in the same field. His work is noted for its solidity, the extent of his studies, and an animating philosophic spirit. He is credited with displaying the unity of American history with the "great central stream" and its heroic, epic value, thereby dispelling sentimental and visionary conceptions of the 18th century.

## Style and Critical Reception
Bancroft's writing is characterized by exuberant enthusiasm and imagination, featuring vivid generalizations and acute insight. He was dedicated to achieving a true style, often rewriting chapters and testing descriptions and philosophy in as many as eight different forms. While some critics noted that he occasionally failed to conceal his art—resulting in pages that rang with artificial tones—and lacked representative power to a certain extent, he is recognized for having sound maxims, thorough common-sense, and a perfect sense of proportion. He is described as a man of the present who justly valued the past.

## Public Life and Legacy
Bancroft's early volumes of history saw large sales in the United States and were translated into French, German, Italian, and Danish, as well as being reprinted in England. His international reputation was such that the universities of Munich, Heidelberg, and Berlin provided a testimonial of regard, and the Royal Academy in Berlin honored him as a guest at a public dinner.

In Washington, Bancroft served as the unofficial dean of the best society and remained a confidential advisor to statesmen throughout his life. He also served as the American Ambassador in Berlin from 1872 to 1875, during which time William Milligan Sloane served as his secretary.
