## The Fiscal Dispute of the Balfour Government

This tension resulted in a series of high-profile resignations. On September 18, Lord George Hamilton and Mr. Ritchie resigned, followed shortly by Mr. Arthur Elliot and Lord Balfour of Burleigh. These individuals were described as "strait free-traders." Simultaneously, Mr. Chamberlain also resigned from the government.

The conflict centered on the tension between traditional free trade and the proposal for a preferential tariff. Correspondence between Mr. Chamberlain and Mr. Balfour, dated September 9 and 16, revealed that Balfour was sympathetic to retaliatory duties and some form of fiscal union with the colonies, provided it was practicable. Despite this, Balfour encouraged Chamberlain to conduct "missionary efforts" outside the government to test and convert public opinion.

## The Manifesto of Insular Free Trade
On September 16, Prime Minister Balfour published a pamphlet titled "Insular Free Trade." In this manifesto, the Prime Minister reviewed economic history since the time of Cobden, arguing that the promises made by early free-traders had been falsified. He highlighted that England remained the only country that allowed free imports. 

Balfour asserted that he was acting in harmony with the "true spirit of free-trade" by advocating for the "freedom to negotiate that freedom of exchange may be increased." While this statement was initially viewed as the government's platform, it also served as the point of dissent for the resigning free-trade members. The political situation was further complicated by reports from Lord George Hamilton, who claimed Balfour had circulated a second, different document that was in closer agreement with Mr. Chamberlain. This atmosphere was characterized by economic difficulties and personal distrust, though the duke of Devonshire, seen as a typical orthodox figure, remained in the cabinet.

## Concepts of Freedom and Exchange in History
Beyond the specific fiscal policies of the Balfour government, the archive contains broader observations on the relationship between freedom, money, and negotiation.

### Economic Freedom and Money
The introduction of money is noted as having provided "freedom of movement and leisure" to individuals. Unlike payment in kind, which ties a worker to their labor and enjoyment, the universal convertibility of money allows a person to choose how to spend their resources—whether on learning, temples, or various "purchasable aids, eases, and indulgences." For the wealthy, money provided an "untethering of wealth," allowing them to change the locality and nature of their possessions. This shift led to the development of speculation and the practice of buying land for profit rather than use, a transition from the more stable world of barter and payment in kind seen in Babylon around 1000 B.C. and in China.

### Political Freedom and Negotiation
In ancient Greece, political freedom was linked to a sense of personal dignity and the conviction that the law was the only master. This contrasted with the Persian view under Xerxes, who viewed the concept of free citizenship as incomprehensible, relying instead on a system of masters and obedient subjects.

Negotiation also played a critical role in political exchanges. In 347 B.C., Athens sought peace with Philip of Macedonia to secure the release of captives taken at Olynthus. Philip refused to exchange these captives for money, instead using them as "an item in political negotiation." He preferred that negotiations take place in Macedonia, where he could exert more influence over the individual Athenian negotiators.

## Legal Frameworks for Exchange
The "freedom of exchange" in a legal sense is codified in the United Kingdom through the Bills of Exchange Act 1882. This act unified the law for England, Scotland, and Ireland, replacing a fragmented system of 17 statutes and approximately 2,600 scattered cases. 

The act governs negotiable instruments, including bills of exchange, promissory notes, and cheques. A key characteristic of these instruments is that an assignee who takes the instrument in good faith and for value holds it by an independent title, free from "all equities" or defects of title held by previous parties. Cheques, specifically defined as bills of exchange drawn on a banker payable on demand, have become the standard machinery for discharging most debts. To facilitate safe transmission and prevent fraud, a system of "crossing" (general or special) was developed and eventually re-enacted in the Bills of Exchange Act.
