# Bolingbroke and the Craftsman

## Political Opposition and the "Occasional Writer"
Following his return to London in June or July 1723, Bolingbroke attempted to offer his services to Walpole, specifically suggesting that he could procure the accession of several Tories to the administration. These offers were received coldly by Walpole. After spending the following winter in France gathering information for the government, Bolingbroke's legal standing in England improved in 1725 when an act was passed allowing him to hold real estate, though he was not permitted to alienate it. This act was the result of a peremptory order from the king, which occurred despite the wishes of Walpole, who successfully ensured Bolingbroke remained excluded from the House of Lords.

Bolingbroke subsequently purchased an estate at Dawley, near Uxbridge. It was during this period that he renewed his intellectual and social ties with Voltaire, Swift, and Pope, even participating in the literary disputes of the latter. In 1726, a rupture occurred between Pulteney and Walpole, providing Bolingbroke with an opportunity to organize a political opposition in conjunction with Windham and Pulteney. This effort culminated in 1727 when Bolingbroke began a celebrated series of letters published in the *Craftsman*. Writing under the pseudonym "Occasional Writer," he used these papers to launch attacks against the Walpoles.

## Financial and Social Maneuvers
During his period of political activity and opposition, Bolingbroke engaged in strategic bribery to influence the court. He managed to gain over the duchess of Kendal, the mistress of the king, by providing a bribe of £11,000, which was funded by the estates of his wife.

## Literary and Philosophical Pursuits
Beyond his political writings in the *Craftsman*, Bolingbroke was a prolific author of philosophical and political works. His collected works include philosophical essays such as *On Authority in Matters of Religion*, *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*, *On the Folly and Presumption of Philosophers*, and *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge*. These were first published in a 1754 edition by Mallet, which was denounced by Johnson as a "blunderbuss charged against religion and morality." He also authored *A Collection of Political Tracts*, published in 1748, and *The Patriot King*, a work from which George III and Bute derived political ideas.

In his retirement at La Source near Orleans, Bolingbroke studied philosophy and criticized the chronology of the Bible. He also wrote the philosophy for the *Essay on Man*. His writing style was noted for being spirited, dignified, and splendidly eloquent, though later critics characterized his political works as exceedingly rhetorical and his philosophical essays as lacking originality and solidity.

## Legacy and Biographical Accounts
Bolingbroke's reputation among posterity is described as weaker than it was among his contemporaries. While Swift regarded his eloquence as unrivalled, and Lord Chesterfield praised his flowing happiness of expression in conversation, his written works are often viewed as lacking permanent interest. Burke, for instance, imitated Bolingbroke's style in his *Vindication of Natural Society* but did so to refute his principles, later questioning who still read Bolingbroke in full.

Several biographies were produced to document his life. An *Authentic Memoirs* appeared around 1740, and other contemporary accounts followed in 1752 and 1754. A biography by Goldsmith was published in 1770. More modern accounts include works by C. de Remusat (1856), T. Macknight (1863), J. Churton Collins (1886), A. Hassall (1889), and Walter Sichel (1901-1902), the latter of which is described as brilliant but unduly eulogistic.
