# Reform and Conclusion in Historical Contexts

The concept of reform and the bringing of processes to a satisfactory conclusion appear across various historical, political, and scientific domains, ranging from the administration of dual monarchies to the evolution of religious orders and scientific methodology.

## Political and State Reforms
In the context of the dual monarchy, an important reform was brought to a satisfactory conclusion during a period of acute political difficulty. Despite the complicated machinery of the dual monarchy and numerous obstacles inherent in reforms affecting both countries, success was achieved in foreign policy, commercial affairs, and finance since 1870. These achievements included the disappearance of the chronic deficit, the stabilization of the currency, the rise of the state's credit, and the payment of part of the unfunded debt. Additionally, universal military service was introduced despite difficulties exceeding those found in any other civilized country.

In the mid-19th century, the Liberal party saw an influx of young men eager for reform following the elections of 1854, with Dorion chosen as their leader. Under a coalition between the Liberals of the lower province and the Tories of Upper Canada, arranged by McNab, old abuses were removed, including the abolition of clergy reserves and seigneurial tenure. However, tensions arose in 1856 regarding representation by population. Upper Canada, having increased in size and contributing more to revenue, demanded proportionate representation. ## Religious and Institutional Adjustments
The Dominican order experienced cycles of relaxation followed by revival and reform. Unlike other orders, the Dominicans kept such movements strictly within their own organization, meaning they lacked reforms of the same historical importance as those seen elsewhere. A significant shift occurred in 1425 when Martin V relaxed the law of corporate poverty for some houses, allowing them to hold property and fixed income. This mitigation was extended to the entire order by Sixtus IV fifty years later, causing the order to cease being mendicant. This change was accepted without the trouble seen among the Franciscans, as it was felt that St. Dominic's fundamental idea remained untouched.

## Scientific and Technical Developments
In the realm of physical science, Bacon sought to accomplish important reforms. He criticized the deductive methods used by Greek philosophers such as Aristotle and Plato when applied to physical subjects, arguing that they too hastily admitted inaccurate or false axioms without dispute.

In the study of yeasts, researchers have sought satisfactory proof regarding two theoretical questions. The first concerns whether yeasts are degraded forms of higher fungi; while resemblances exist between *Saccharomyces anomalus* and the hyphal fungus *Endomyces*, no satisfactory proof has been provided that *Saccharomycetes* are derivable from any higher form via culture. The second question concerns the nature of fermentation. Investigations by Buchner and others revealed that zymase (a ferment) can be extracted from yeast-cells to break sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Further research by Albert and Buchner showed that enzymes remain in yeast-cells even after they have been killed by acetone, ether, or alcohol, bringing alcoholic fermentation into line with other fermentations.

## Commercial and Industrial Evolution
The "milk trade," the traffic of country milk to urban districts, evolved significantly after 1860, facilitated by the expansion of the railway network in the latter half of the 19th century. This development was driven by an increase in urban population and a spirit of sanitary reform, which led to the disestablishment of urban cow-sheds. The outbreak of cattle plague in 1865 further accelerated this trend, forcing metropolis dairymen to source milk from across England. The subsequent invention of the capillary refrigerator ensured that milk production remained in the hands of dairymen living at a distance from the towns they supplied.

In the field of book-binding, book-sewing machines evolved into two primary types. One sews books on flat or round bands, which can weaken sections unless neutralized by careful head-banding. The second system replaces bands with a chain stitch, which brings the return thread inside each section. While this prevents the weakening of sections, it results in a flattening of the book's back, creating difficulties during the covering process. Although the inner threads of this second system are sometimes considered too bulky to be quite satisfactory, this is viewed as a minor issue compared to the cutting of the back's edges.
