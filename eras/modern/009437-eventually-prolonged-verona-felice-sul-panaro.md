# Verona and Related Regional Geography

## Geography and Infrastructure of Bologna
Bologna serves as a significant railway center, mirroring the role of the ancient Bononia as a junction for major roads. The city's railway network is extensive; the main line from Milan divides here, with one branch following the path of the ancient Via Aemilia from Piacenza toward Brindisi, Ancona, and Rimini. A second branch travels through the Apennines toward Rome and Florence. 

Additional rail lines connect Bologna to Padua and Ferrara. One specific line runs to S. Felice sul Panaro and is intended to be eventually prolonged to Verona. A third line connects to Portomaggiore (a station on the Ferrara to Ravenna line) and Budrio. Furthermore, steam tramways provide transport to Malalbergo, Pieve di Cento, and Vignola. The city is also noted for its local specialties, including liqueurs, *tortellini* (a variety of macaroni), and *salami* or *mortadella*.

## Historical Military Actions in the Panaro Region
The Panaro region has been the site of historical military conflict. On February 8, 1743, Spanish forces achieved a Pyrrhic victory over Traun at Campo Santo. However, this was followed by six months of inaction. Subsequently, Lobkowitz joined Traun with reinforcements arriving from Germany, which resulted in the enemy being driven back to Rimini.

## The City of Verona
Verona is described in historical records as having fortifications constructed by Gallienus. These ancient fortifications were less extensive than the city's modern walls and did not encompass the amphitheatre. The city has been the site of memorable actions, including a siege and battle. Additionally, the "wide and barren plain of Verona" is mentioned in the context of the defeat of the Cimbri, though some poetic geography suggests a distinction between Cimbric and Gothic fields.

During the first invasion of Italy by Alaric, the region surrounding Verona was subject to the rage of war. Historical accounts describe a husbandman who lived a simple life on a paternal farm in the neighborhood of Verona, whose cottage and family were threatened by the potential sweep of Gothic cavalry and the conflagration of the country.

## Genealogies and the Polo Family
Historical records regarding the Polo family involve various conflicting genealogies. One genealogy by Marco Barbaro, based on documents by Ramusio, claims the Castellano of Verona was the grandson of Marco Polo via a son named Maffeo. However, this is contested on chronological grounds, as it suggests a lady married in 1414 and had children whose father would have been born no later than 1271. 

Other genealogies are criticized for confounding two distinct families: the Polo of S. Giov. Grisostomo and the Polo of S. Geremia. It is suggested that Maria Trevisan and Marco the Castellano of Verona likely belonged to the S. Geremia branch. Additionally, a Marcolino Polo is identified as a probable near relation—possibly an illegitimate one—and the only male descendant of old Andrea of San Felice known to have survived Marco.
