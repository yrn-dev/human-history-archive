# Federalist Great Proprietors

The Federalist Great Proprietors were a political group within the parliamentary system of Austria. They represented a specific interest group that advocated for federalist principles in opposition to the centralizing policies of the Liberal parties.

## Political Position and Ideology
The Federalists were characterized by their opposition to a centralized government and bureaucracy. They claimed that any new constitution for the empire must be established through agreements with the various territories. This position placed them in direct conflict with the Centralists, who maintained that the constitution of 1861 remained valid and sought the restoration of a "constitutional" government.

The divide between the Centralists and Federalists was largely, though not entirely, a matter of race. While the Liberal party—dominant in Styria and Lower Austria—pushed for the development of central institutions, the Federalists found allies among the Conservative and Clerical parties. These groups supported local institutions as a means of protection against the influence of a centralized parliament and bureaucracy. Consequently, bishops and clergy often allied with the Federalists to further this struggle.

## Conflict over the Ausgleich
The Federalists played a significant role in the constitutional struggles of the 1860s. Belcredi, who came into power in 1865 as a Federalist, suspended the constitution of 1861 on January 2, 1867. He ordered new elections for the diets, which were intended to elect deputies to an extraordinary Reichsrath to consider the *Ausgleich* (the compact with Hungary). 

Because the Federalists were opposed to the *Ausgleich*, it was anticipated that a Reichsrath chosen under these circumstances would refuse to ratify the settlement. This led to a confrontation with Germans and Liberals, who objected to what they termed a "feudal-federal" constitution that would grant the Slavs a preponderance in the empire. This conflict resulted in the dismissal of Belcredi and the appointment of Beust as minister-president on February 7, 1867. Following this, diets with Federalist majorities—specifically those of Tirol, Carinthia, Moravia, and Bohemia—refused to elect representatives on terms acceptable to the government.

## Electoral Struggles and the Role of Landed Estates
In Bohemia, the struggle for political control often depended on the votes of feudal proprietors. To secure a German majority and crush opposition, a society of German capitalists from Vienna, popularly known as *Chabrus*, was formed. This group acquired portions of estates to which votes were attached, either through real or fictitious purchases. This strategy was successful, resulting in a large German majority where representatives from Vienna replaced figures such as the Schwarzenbergs and the Thuns.

The influence of the Federalists was further diminished by a new electoral law passed in 1873. This law transferred the right to elect members of the Reichsrath from the diets to a direct vote by the people. This deprived the Federalists of their "chief weapon," as it was no longer possible to use the legal representatives of a territory to refuse the appointment of deputies. Under the new system, if a Slovene or Czech member failed to take their seat, it only resulted in a single unrepresented constituency, thereby weakening the opposition.

## Parliamentary Representation
In the parliamentary records of the era, the Federalist Great Proprietors maintained a consistent presence. According to parliamentary tables from 1897 and 1901, the Federalist Great Proprietors held 16 seats in both periods.
