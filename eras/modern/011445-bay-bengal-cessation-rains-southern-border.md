# Bay of Bengal

The Bay of Bengal is a significant body of water in southern Asia, serving as a geographic boundary for several regions and playing a critical role in the climate, biology, and commerce of the surrounding territories. It is bordered to the east by Burma and to the west and north by the Indian subcontinent, specifically the regions of Bengal and Orissa.

## Geography and Hydrography
The Bay of Bengal forms the south-eastern boundary of Orissa and the southern border of the district of Backergunje. In the region of Backergunje, which is part of the joint delta of the Brahmaputra and Ganges, several major river systems debouch into the bay. The Meghna, representing the combined waters of the Ganges and Brahmaputra, flows south for approximately 100 miles before entering the bay through a large estuary containing islands such as Dakshin Shahbazpur. Similarly, the river known locally as the Haringhata, Baleswar, Madhumati, or Garai crosses the Sundarbans and enters the Bay of Bengal via a deep estuary.

The coast-line of Burma, which lies to the east of the bay, extends from the estuary of the Pakchan at Maliwun in the south to Taknaf in the north, covering a distance of about 1,200 miles. To the west, the bay borders Chittagong. In the southern coastal areas of Backergunje, the bay is flanked by the forest tracts of the Sundarbans, which are inhabited by leopards, tigers, and other wild beasts.

## Climate and Rainfall
The Bay of Bengal is a primary source of moist, vapour-bearing winds that drive the summer rains of south Asia. The heaviest rainfall in the world is recorded on the Khasi range, located about 100 miles north-east of Calcutta, where the range presents an abrupt front to the moist winds flowing up from the bay, resulting in annual rainfall of not less than 600 inches.

The influence of these winds varies based on geographic obstacles. While the Western Ghats of India and the west coast of the Malay peninsula receive heavy falls of rain due to mountain lines facing the winds, other areas experience different patterns. For instance, the cessation of rains on the southern border of Baluchistan, west of Karachi, is caused by the projection of the south-east coast of Arabia, which limits the length of the coast-line exposed to the south-west monsoon and the breadth of the air current. In Sind and along the Indus, rainfall is irregular and small because there are no obstacles to stop the vapour-bearing winds, which instead carry uncondensed rain to the western Himalaya and Afghanistan.

## Biological Diversity
The Bay of Bengal serves as a biological link between different regions of Asia. Among insects, species and genera are often identical on opposite sides of the bay, grouping the insects of southern Asia—including the Malayan Islands, Siam, China, and India south of the Himalaya—into a single group.

The marine life of the bay is equally diverse. The mango fish, considered a great delicacy, inhabits the seas from the Bay of Bengal to Siam. The *Sciaenidae* family of fish extends from the Bay of Bengal to China, though they are not found further west. Other fish distributed through the seas of south Asia include mackerels, gobies, blennies, mullets, sword fishes (*Xiphidae*), lancet fishes (*Acanthuridae*), and scabbard fishes (*Trichuridae*).

## Historical and Commercial Significance
The bay has been a site of significant maritime activity and conflict. During 1914, the merchant shipping routes in the Bay of Bengal, including the trade route between Colombo and Calcutta, were disrupted by the depredations of the *Emden*. The *Emden* bombarded the Burma Company’s oil tanks off Madras and sank numerous merchant ships, leading to widespread alarm. In response, a large-scale concentration of naval forces was proposed for Indian waters, including ships such as the *Hampshire*, *Yarmouth*, *Sydney*, *Melbourne*, and several Russian and Japanese vessels.
