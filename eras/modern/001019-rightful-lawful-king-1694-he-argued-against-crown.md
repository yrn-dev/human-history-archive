# Rightful and Lawful Kingship

The concept of a rightful and lawful king varies across different historical and cultural contexts, involving considerations of lineage, religious consecration, constitutional law, and the perceived contract between a monarch and the people.

## Lineage and Divine Right
In some societies, the right to the crown was tied strictly to ancestral race. In Sparta, an oracle warned that the kingdom would be "lame of one foot" if men of base birth were allowed to share the crown with those belonging to the "true race of Herakles." Lysander used this argument, alongside his own personal influence, to ensure that Agesilaus became king.

In Norway, the requirements for lawful kingship involved both lineage and religious validation. Erling argued that Magnus was a suitable candidate for the throne because his mother was the daughter of a king and queen born in lawful wedlock, and Magnus himself was the son of a queen and a lawfully married wife. Erling further suggested that while Magnus may not have been chosen according to old customs, he could be given "consecration as king, as God's law prescribes," through anointing to sovereignty. Erling cited the examples of William Bastard in England and Svein Ulfson in Denmark—neither of whom were sons of kings—to demonstrate that consecration and crowning could establish a lasting royal race.

## Constitutional Prerogatives and Law
In the Austro-Hungarian context, the rights of the crown were defined by specific legal frameworks, such as the Hungarian Fundamental Law XII. of 1867. Clause 11 of this law recognized the monarch's constitutional military prerogatives, placing everything related to the inner organization, leadership, and unitary direction of the whole army (including the Hungarian army) at the disposal of His Majesty. 

This led to a constitutional crisis regarding the language of command. The crown viewed the demand to replace German words of command with Magyar words as an encroachment on these royal prerogatives. While some argued that the right to fix the conditions for granting recruits (under Clause 12) allowed for the abolition of German commands, the Liberal party eventually adopted a standpoint—sanctioned by the crown—that the king maintained the right to fix the language of service and command based on the constitutional prerogatives of Clause 11.

## The Crown and the Social Contract
In English history, the legitimacy of a king has been debated in relation to the contract between the monarch and the people. During the convention parliament following the flight of James II in 1688, a famous vote was held asserting that James had broken the contract between the king and the people, thereby leaving the throne vacant. Heneage Finch, 1st Earl of Aylesford, was one of the few members of the House of Commons to oppose this vote. A Tory and crown lawyer, Finch advocated for a regency in the name of James and remained an opposer of the House of Orange.

## Legal Rights of the Crown
The "crown" also functions as a legal entity with specific rights over the citizenry. Under the Norman system in England, homicide became a "plea of the crown." This shifted the right to compensation and vengeance away from the kindred of the deceased and toward the king's right to forfeitures in cases of felony. English law operated on the principle that every homicide was presumed felonious unless proven otherwise, and in all cases not justifiable by law, a forfeiture was incurred in favor of the crown.
