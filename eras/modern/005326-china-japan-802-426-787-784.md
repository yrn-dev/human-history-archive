# China and Japan

The relationship between China and Japan has historically encompassed the exchange of religion, literature, and philosophy, as well as diplomatic disputes over territorial sovereignty and regional administration.

## Cultural and Religious Exchange
Early interactions between the two nations established a bridge for the importation of Chinese ethics, philosophies, arts, and literature into Japan. This exchange was facilitated by embassies and students; for example, the envoy Imoko was accompanied by eight students specializing in religion and literature.

In the eighth century, Japanese Buddhism was elevated through the efforts of Dengyo Daishi (also known as Saicho). Sent to China by Emperor Kwammu, Saicho traveled to Changan in 802 in the train of the ambassador Sugawara Kiyokimi. While in China, he and his companions visited the monastery of Tientai (Tendai), where they studied a "middle route" to Saving Knowledge. This path emphasized meditation and wisdom, requiring the "accomplishment of external means"—such as regulating food and raiment and observing precepts—and the "chiding of evil desires," including the lust for touch, taste, perfumes, sound, and beauty. Saicho returned to Japan with many religious books and became the lord-abbot of a monastery on Hiei-zan, a hill northeast of the palace in Kyoto. Two years after Saicho's departure, Kobo Daishi (Kukai) also traveled to China, accompanying Fujiwara Kuzunomaro.

## Territorial and Diplomatic Disputes
The two nations have engaged in significant disputes regarding the Ryukyu Islands and the administration of Korea. In 1876, Japan extended its system of local government to Ryukyu. China protested this move, claiming the islands had always been a tributary of the Chinese empire. However, records indicate that Ryukyu had been subdued by Satsuma for two centuries without Chinese intervention. While a conference in Peking in 1880, suggested by General Grant, initially proposed dividing the islands between the two nations, China eventually drew back from the convention. Consequently, Japan retained the islands.

Tensions also arose regarding Korea. Japan proposed a union with China to restore order and amend the administration of Korea, but China refused the proposal. China expressed surprise that Japan would suggest reforming Korea's administration while simultaneously asserting the country's independence. Because China refused to reach an understanding regarding the future peace and good government of Korea, Japan undertook the administration of the country single-handedly.

## Treaties and Manchuria
Japan and Russia entered into agreements that directly impacted Chinese sovereignty in Manchuria. Under these terms, Japan and Russia agreed to evacuate Manchuria simultaneously, except for the territory leased by the Liao-tung Peninsula, and to restore all other portions of Manchuria to the exclusive administration of China. Russia declared it held no preferential or exclusive concessions in Manchuria that impaired Chinese sovereignty. Both Japan and Russia pledged not to obstruct general measures China might take to develop industry and commerce in the region.

With the consent of the Chinese government, the Imperial Russian Government transferred the lease of Talien, Port Arthur, and adjacent territory—including all public works, properties, and territorial waters—to the Imperial Government of Japan. Additionally, the railway between Port Arthur and Chang-chun (Kuan-cheng-tzu), its branches, and associated coal mines were transferred to Japan without compensation, also with Chinese consent.
