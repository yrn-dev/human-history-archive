# Shadworth Hodgson and Philosophical Perspectives on Culture

## Shadworth Hodgson
Shadworth Hodgson was a thinker and writer involved in various intellectual and literary circles. He is noted as a contributor to *De Quincey and his Friends* (1895), a collection of essays edited by James Hogg. Additionally, Hodgson was a member of a group that held meetings to read and discuss papers on a variety of complex subjects, including the immortality of the soul and the ultimate grounds of belief in the moral and objective sciences. This group included a wide array of figures such as Gladstone, Tennyson, Huxley, and H. Sidgwick.

## Theory of Automatism
In his work *Metaphysic of Experience and Theory of Practice*, Shadworth Hodgson maintained a specific theory regarding automatism. In philosophical terms, automatism is used in two primary ways: in ethics, to describe the view that humans are not responsible for their actions (and thus those actions have no moral value), and in psychology, to describe actions that do not result from conscious endeavour or conation.

Hodgson, along with T. Huxley in *Science and Culture*, supported a theory where mechanical phenomena of action are accompanied by phenomena of consciousness, but the two are entirely disconnected. According to this view, physical changes in the brain result in a specific action, while the accompanying volition or mental desire is not prior to, nor causally connected with, that physical change. This perspective is distinct from the "double aspect theory" or psychophysical parallelism, which posits that both physical phenomena and mental states result from a single substance known as "mind stuff."

## Concepts of Culture and Metaphysics
The archive contains varying perspectives on the role of culture and metaphysics in intellectual development. In the context of Epicurean philosophy, there was a notable aversion to purely intellectual training. Epicurus advised a young disciple to "steer clear of all culture," viewing purely scientific pursuits as misleading and one-sided. This stance was a recoil from the systematic metaphysics of Aristotle and Plato, who tended to subordinate the practical man to the philosopher. 

Epicurus argued that systematic knowledge of truth is not the same as right action. Consequently, he sought a foundation for ethical doctrine based on nature and sensation—the "only touchstone of truth"—rather than on reasonings, words, or the artificial civic forms of social life.

In contrast, early Grecian philosophy saw the emergence of metaphysics through thinkers like Anaximander. Anaximander introduced the concept of a beginning or principle and opened discussions regarding the continuous versus the variable, and the eternal versus that which passes away. Unlike the hypothesis of Thalês, which was based on physical considerations and could be verified through physical phenomena, Anaximander's hypothesis was discussed dialectically through general language and reasonings. Throughout much of Grecian philosophy, the departments of physics and metaphysics remained entangled.

## Related Philosophical Terms
The archive further defines "immanence" as a philosophical and theological term meaning the condition of being entirely within something, often used in contrast to "transcendence." In theology, it refers to the conception of God existing throughout the created world. Metaphysical speculation regarding the relationship between mind and matter has often led to a conviction of an underlying unity of all existence, resulting in the metaphysical identification of God and the universe. Depending on the extent of this identification, the result may be naturalism (regarding deity as the sum of nature's forces) or pantheism (expressing the universe as a mode of deity).
