# The Jacobinism of the Vienna Democracy and the French Jacobin Movement

## The Vienna Democracy and National Movements
In the German parts of Austria, the Jacobinism of the Vienna democracy failed to represent any widespread opinion. Its loud-voiced Germanism created lively opposition among other races within the empire, who utilized the troubles of March to press their own national claims. During this period, the government exhibited a yielding spirit toward these various movements.

In Bohemia, a separate constitution was proclaimed on April 8th after attempts to hold elections for the Frankfort parliament failed due to opposition from the conservative German aristocracy and the Czechs. Similarly, on March 18th, Count Stadion proclaimed a new constitution for Galicia. In Croatia, the diet of Agram confirmed the election of Baron Joseph Jellachich as ban on March 23rd, a move intended as a concession to southern Slav agitation. Even in instances where the government provided no formal sanction to national claims, such as with the Rumans and Serbs, the emperor was viewed as the ultimate guarantee of their success, leading various provinces to send deputations to Innsbruck to protest their loyalty.

## The Jacobin Club and the French Revolution
The Jacobins were a central force in the French Revolution, evolving from a political club into a dominant faction. By 1790, the Jacobin Club served as a platform for violent anti-royalist orators. Following the flight of Louis XVI to Varennes, members of the club demanded the establishment of a federal republic. By July 1791, these demands were being printed and distributed to branch societies across France.

The Jacobins possessed a "dangerous integrity" and a strong grip on the imagination of the Parisian mob. This influence was augmented by the royalists, who mistakenly believed they could use the Jacobins as tools to undermine the moderates. The royalists supported a Jacobin proposal to make members of the National Assembly ineligible for the subsequent Legislative Assembly, hoping that a politically incompetent body would eventually force France back into the hands of its legitimate masters. Furthermore, the royalists backed the election of a Jacobin as the Mayor of Paris. However, they failed to account for the strongly Jacobin Commune of Paris installed at the Hôtel de Ville.

## The Jacobin Republic and the Terror
The ascendency of the Jacobins was marked by increasing violence, including the flooding of the Tuileries on June 20, 1792, and the massacre of the Swiss guard on August 10, 1792, which resulted in the royal family being imprisoned. The Jacobin republic (1792–1794) saw the trial and execution of Louis XVI in January 1793 and the subsequent execution of the queen in October.

Within the National Convention, Jacobin influence led to the proscription of the Girondins in June 1793. A program outlined at the Jacobin club was gradually realized by the Convention, which included:
* The creation of a revolutionary army.
* The expulsion of non-naturalized foreigners.
* The establishment of an impost on the rich.
* The deprivation of citizenship rights for "anti-social" men.
* The death penalty for generals who were unsuccessful.
* The licensing of all officers who were former nobles.

The Committee of Public Safety furthered these goals by decreeing the arrest *en masse* of all suspects and establishing an official Revolutionary Tribunal. This era of the "men and methods of the Terror" ended with the execution of Robespierre in 1794.

## Jacobinism in Corsica
The influence of the Jacobin Club extended to Corsica after the island was incorporated into France as a separate department. Branches of the Jacobin Club were established in Corsican towns, where they tended to usurp regular government functions and introduce discord. 

Paoli, who had been acclaimed as the "father of the country" and "hero and martyr of liberty," was out of sympathy with the new order and the methods of the Terror. Despite being appointed governor and lieutenant-general of the forces in 1792 by a French government anxious to secure the island, Paoli eventually defied the Convention. On May 27, 1793, he summoned representatives of the communes to meet in a diet at Corte. Paoli asserted that he was rebelling not against France itself, but against the dominant Jacobin faction. Consequently, on June 26, the Convention declared Paoli and his sympathizers *hors la loi*.

## Aftermath and the Directory
Following the summer of 1794, France entered a period of political instability characterized by a series of insurrections by both royalists and Jacobins. A "hooligan class" in Paris frequently engaged in fighting and looting for either side. Despite this, the Convention established the Directory, a five-member government that maintained French unity for five years. The final major revolt in October 1795 was suppressed by the young general Napoleon Bonaparte.
