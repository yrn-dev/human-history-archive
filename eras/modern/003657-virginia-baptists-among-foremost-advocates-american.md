# Virginia Baptists and the Cause of Religious Liberty

The Baptist movement in Virginia during the 18th century was characterized by a rapid expansion of membership and a persistent struggle against state control of religious matters. This growth was driven largely by the emergence of the "Separate" Baptists, who became prominent advocates for religious equality and the complete separation of church and state.

## Growth of the "Separate" Baptists
Between 1760 and 1770, the "Separate" Baptist body experienced phenomenal growth in Virginia and the Carolinas. This expansion was fueled by evangelists such as John Waller (1741–1802) and Samuel Harris (1724–c.1794), who established churches in areas where the Baptist name had previously been unknown. The influence of the "Separate" Baptists permeated Virginia from North Carolina, eventually extending further into Tennessee and Kentucky.

The growth was rapid; between May 1771 and May 1773, the number of "Separate" Baptists in Virginia more than doubled. This movement was led in part by "New Light" evangelists such as Shubael Stearns and Daniel Marshall (d. 1784). Stearns, an evangelist of great power, settled at Sandy Creek, North Carolina, where he built a church with over six hundred members. From this center, the Sandy Creek Association grew to include churches across several colonies.

## Conflict with Colonial Authorities
The religious landscape of colonial Virginia was marked by early cruelty and intolerance. While the colonial government eventually granted limited toleration to "Regular" Baptists—those influenced by missionaries from the Philadelphia Association, such as John Gano, Benjamin Griffith, and others who formed the Ketockton Association in 1776—the "Separate" Baptists faced a different experience.

Because the "Separate" Baptists were highly sensitive to the evils of state control in religious affairs and were characterized by their enthusiasm, they refused to take out licenses for their meetings. This stance brought them into sharp conflict with the Virginia authorities.

## Internal Organization and Theological Shifts
The "Separate" Baptists underwent several organizational experiments. In 1774, some members believed the apostolic office should be perpetuated, leading the association to appoint Samuel Harris as an apostle, followed by Elijah Craig (1743–1800) and John Waller for the northern district. This arrangement was later abandoned and was likely influenced by Methodist superintendency.

Theological tensions also emerged in 1775 when Jeremiah Walker and two apostles contended for the concept of universal redemption. To prevent a schism and preserve harmony, the body adopted the Philadelphia Confession of Faith. Although this was a Calvinistic document, it was adopted with provisions against too rigid a construction, marking a step toward harmonization with the "Regular" Baptists of the Philadelphia type.

## Advocacy for Religious Liberty
By 1780, the "Regular" Baptists began cooperating heartily with the "Separate" Baptists in a struggle for religious liberty and equality. In 1783, when the General Association was sub-divided, a General Committee consisting of delegates from each district association was created. The primary objective of this committee was to continue the successful agitation for religious equality and the entire separation of church and state.

## Broader Baptist Context and Legacy
The Baptist movement in the South also saw significant participation from enslaved people. In Virginia, the Carolinas, and Georgia, many negro slaves became Baptists. While most joined the churches of white Baptists, they were encouraged to establish their own churches in certain towns, including Richmond.

By the 19th century, the Baptist denomination in America grew significantly in scale and infrastructure. From 1812 to 1906, membership increased from fewer than 200,000 to more than 5,000,000. This growth was accompanied by the establishment of numerous educational institutions, including the Virginia Institute for Women (founded in 1884) and Columbian College in Washington. The denomination also expanded its reach through foreign missions, notably in India and among the Lahu or Muhsö hill tribesmen in Burma.
