# Barcelona

Barcelona is a major city and port located in Spain, serving as the center of Spanish commerce and industry. The city is situated within a province characterized by three distinct climatic zones: a temperate zone near the sea where orange and palm trees grow, a colder zone in the inland plains and valleys, and a coldest zone among the mountains, some of which remain snow-clad for much of the year. As of 1900, the city had a population of 533,000.

## History
Traditionally founded in the 3rd century B.C. by the Carthaginian Hamilcar Barca, the city was known as Barcino. Following the Roman conquest, Augustus gave it the status of a Roman colony and the name Julia Faventia (later Augusta and Pia). By the 2nd century A.D., it had become a leading mart of the western Mediterranean, rivaling Massilia and Tarraco. The Roman city was located on Monte Taber.

The bishopric of Barcelona was established in 343. In 415 and 531, the Visigoths utilized the city as a temporary capital, and church councils were held there in 540 and 599. The city, then called Barcinona or Bardjaluna, was captured by the Moors in 713 before passing to the dominion of the Franks in 801. Starting in 874, the counts of Barcelona ruled as independent monarchs.

The city reached its zenith of wealth and fame after the union of Catalonia and Aragon in 1149. During this era, Barcelona's merchant ships traded as far east as Alexandria and as far west as the Baltic and North Seas, competing with Venice, Ragusa, and Genoa. In 1258, James I of Aragon authorized the *Consulado del Mar*, a maritime law code adopted by many European states. The city was also an early adopter of marine insurance.

The union of Castile and Aragon in 1479, followed by the 1492 discovery of America, shifted commercial supremacy to western Spanish ports. This decline led to political tension; in 1640, Barcelona became the center of a rebellion against Philip IV and sought French protection. It returned to its allegiance in 1652 but was captured by the duke of Vendome in 1697.

## Port and Infrastructure
Originally an open beach sheltered by hills, the port of Barcelona underwent centuries of development. In 1438, Don Alphonso V granted a license to build a mole, and the Moll de Santa Creu began in 1474. However, the area remained shut off from the open sea by a sand-bank until modern times, making the entrance of large vessels impossible. Satisfactory works were not undertaken until the 17th century.

Modern improvements included an extension of the former mole and the construction of another from the foot of Montjuich, creating shelter for heavy battleships. Systematic improvements from 1873 added new quays and storage. By 1903, the port featured an inner harbour (18 to 30 ft. deep at low tide) and an outer harbour (20 to 35 ft. deep). In the following year, nearly 5,000,000 tons of cargo entered via 8,075 vessels.

## Economy and Communications
The province is a hub of industry and enterprise, with towns and villages filled with factories. While agriculture and stock-keeping are relatively unimportant, the region works salt, coal, lignite, zinc, and lead. Prosperity is primarily driven by manufactures and transit trade.

Communication within the city is facilitated by tramway lines running from the port to other chief suburbs, including Gracia. While roads are generally insufficient and rivers are not navigable, the province is well-supplied by 349 miles of railway. Major lines radiate from Barcelona to:
*   Perpignan (France) and Gerona to the north-east.
*   Valencia and Tarragona to the south-west.
*   Madrid and Saragossa to the west.

## Demographics and Education
The population of the province is the largest and fastest-growing in Spain, driven by industrial life attracting immigrants rather than a high birth rate. In the last quarter of the 19th century, the population increased by over 200,000, while yearly emigration remained below 2,000. The province is among the leaders in Spanish education; by 1880, school registers showed 97,077 enrolled children.
