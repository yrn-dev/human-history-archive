# The Struggle Over Civil Marriage and Divorce Laws

The struggle over civil marriage and divorce laws has manifested across various historical periods and jurisdictions, often intersecting with political tensions, religious authority, and the evolution of legal codes. These conflicts typically center on whether the state or a religious institution holds the primary authority to regulate the union and dissolution of marriage.

## Political Conflict in Hungary
In Hungary during the 1890s, the struggle over civil marriage and divorce laws occupied a significant portion of the political landscape. This conflict occurred during a period of decay for dualism and the rise of an extreme nationalist Magyar spirit. Liberal leaders, who had been the mainstay of dualism since 1867, may have intended the struggle over these laws to serve as a diversion to support the Liberal-dualist standpoint. This occurred alongside broader nationalist tensions and negotiations between Hungarian premiers, such as Banffy and Szell, and various Austrian premiers regarding the renewal of the economic *Ausgleich* (Customs and Trade Alliance).

## Ecclesiastical vs. Civil Authority
The Roman Catholic Church has historically claimed exclusive control over marriage. The council of Trent anathematized the view held by Reformers, including Luther, that matrimonial causes were properly the subject of civil courts. Under canon law, the Church does not recognize divorce—the annulment of marriage for any cause other than an impediment that makes the marriage *ipso facto* void. 

Instead, the Church allows for a judicial decree of nullity (*annullatio matrimonii*), which is a solemn declaration that a marriage never existed, rather than a severing of a real marriage. Another option is "separation from bed and board" (*a thoro et mensa*), which may be perpetual but does not grant either party the right to remarry while the other spouse is alive. However, if non-consummation is proven, a marriage may be dissolved by papal dispensation or by one or both parties taking religious vows, as marriage is not regarded as a sacrament until it is consummated. This ecclesiastical stance became a point of extreme political importance in Catholic countries when civil codes established civil marriage as the only legally binding form.

## Legal Frameworks in the United States and England
In the United States, the absence of ecclesiastical courts led to a reliance on English common law and equity tribunals. Marriage and divorce laws are handled as state laws and statutes, as the national power lacks legislative or judicial cognisance of these matters within the states. While some states have altered the age of consent for marriage, American jurisprudence generally holds that a marriage valid under common law remains valid despite state statutes, unless the statute explicitly mandates nullity. For example, in Pennsylvania and New Hampshire, marriages conducted without specific statutory requirements (such as a set number of witnesses or a licensed minister) were still held to be valid. Massachusetts, however, provided an exception by ruling that parties could not solemnize their own marriage.

In England, the law distinguishes between void and voidable marriages. A voidable marriage (such as those between persons within prohibited degrees before the Marriage Act 1836) is sufficient to support a charge of bigamy, whereas an absolutely void marriage is not. English courts do not recognize a divorce *a vinculo* granted by a foreign country for persons domiciled in England if the grounds for that divorce are not recognized in England. However, if a person can prove they were legally divorced by the law of the country where they were domiciled at the time, it serves as a valid defense against bigamy charges in England.

## Roman Law of Divorce and Dos
In ancient Rome, divorce was effected through written notice provided by either the husband or the wife, as was the case during the time of Cicero. A central component of Roman divorce was the *dos* (marriage-portion), property transferred to the husband to help him bear the burden of a wife and family. 

The rules regarding *dos* varied by the form of marriage. In *conventio in manum*, the wife came into the husband's power, and all her property became his. In other forms, property not designated as *dos* remained the woman's own. Because the *dos* was provided for a specific purpose, it was generally returned upon the dissolution of the marriage, a requirement that acted as a check against husbands separating from their wives lightly. If a divorce was caused by the wife's adultery, the husband was entitled to retain a sixth of the marriage-portion, according to Ulpian.

## Spiritual Jurisdiction in Bulgaria
In the legal system of Bulgaria, which is largely based on Ottoman law, the state provides a hierarchy of courts ranging from the village *kmet* to the court of cassation in Sofia. However, the state does not hold exclusive jurisdiction over all domestic matters; the Jewish, Mahommedan, and Greek communities maintain their own spiritual jurisdiction regarding questions of inheritance, divorce, and marriage.
