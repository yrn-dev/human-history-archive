# Bills of Quantities

## Definition and Preparation
Bills of quantities are documents prepared by a quantity surveyor. The primary function of the quantity surveyor in this process is to measure the entirety of the materials required for a structure based on the provided drawings. These materials, along with their respective amounts or quantities, are stated in the form of a bill. This bill is typically produced on specially ruled foolscap paper.

The purpose of this detailed listing is to allow builders to provide a price for each individual item, including the labour necessary to fix and work the material. This process enables the parties to arrive at a lump sum for which the builders will agree to erect the building.

## Role in Building Contracts
Bills of quantities are generally designed to form a part of the building contract and are explicitly mentioned as such. A building contract is a critical document signed by the builder before work begins. In this agreement, the builder commits to carrying out the work for a specific sum of money, adhering to the instructions of the architect, the drawings, the specifications, and the bills of quantities.

The contract further details the responsibilities of the builder, the description of workmanship and materials, the manner of execution, and the schedule of payments. It also includes completion timelines—with allowances for strikes, fire, or bad weather—and penalty clauses for non-completion, as well as clauses that indemnify the employer against various risks and accidents involving employees. While the architect generally prepares this form of contract, it may be varied to meet specific requirements.

## Management of Alterations
The use of bills of quantities is considered absolutely necessary for anyone undertaking a build, as it results in considerable savings for the client. This is particularly important because deviations from the original plan are frequent; in four-fifths of building contracts, the client or another interested party will alter a portion of the building after work has commenced.

Because the quantity surveyor has the prices of different materials already established, they can easily remeasure the altered portion. By adding or deducting the necessary amounts, the surveyor can accurately ascertain the financial difference caused by the alteration.

## Risks of Omitting Bills of Quantities
When bills of quantities are not prepared, a common alternative is to send the specification and drawings to several builders for quotations. While the architect may accept the lowest price and the builder may begin the job, this method often leads to disputes. If the owner instructs alterations during the progress of the works, the builder will eventually claim for "extras" upon settlement. Without the established prices provided by a bill of quantities, the architect has no prices to work upon, which frequently results in litigation.

## Professional Requirements for Architects
While the measurement of work is usually performed by the quantity surveyor at an advanced stage, an architect must also be practically acquainted with all trades and arts used in building. To be considered a master of the profession, an architect should know how to prepare quantities and measure work, as this allows them to minutely estimate the absolute cost involved in executing a proposed structure. In ordinary practice, architects may "cube" a building at a price per foot cube.
