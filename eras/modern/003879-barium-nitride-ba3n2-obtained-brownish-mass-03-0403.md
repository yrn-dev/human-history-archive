# Barium and Barium Compounds

Barium (symbol Ba) is a metallic chemical element belonging to the alkaline earth group. Its name is derived from the Greek word *barus*, meaning heavy, due to its presence in heavy spar or barytes. The element was first investigated in 1602 by V. Casciorolus, who observed that heavy spar became phosphorescent after ignition with combustible substances, leading to the name "Bolognian phosphorus." In 1774, K. W. Scheele identified a new substance in pyrolusite that produced an insoluble salt when treated with sulphuric acid, which was later found to be identical to the salt in heavy spar.

## Physical and Chemical Properties of the Metal
Pure barium is a silver-white metal with a lustre when freshly cut. It is slightly harder than lead and oxidizes extremely easily upon exposure to air. While it is soluble in liquid ammonia, it readily attacks alcohol and water. The metal's specific gravity varies by preparation: 1.54 for metal prepared by electrolysis, 1.56 after remelting, and 1.52 after distillation. It sublimes at a temperature lower than its melting point, which is approximately 800°.

Isolation of the pure metal is difficult. Sir H. Davy attempted to electrolyse baryta and later used barium chloride with mercury to produce an amalgam; distilling the mercury from this amalgam yielded a silver-white residue of barium. In 1854, R. Bunsen electrolysed a thick paste of barium chloride and dilute hydrochloric acid at 100°C in the presence of mercury to obtain a barium amalgam. More recently, A. N. Guntz (1901) produced a 3% barium amalgam by electrolysing a saturated barium chloride solution with a mercury cathode, subsequently heating the amalgam in an iron boat within a porcelain tube to obtain pure barium at about 1000°C.

## Detection and Atomic Weight
Barium and its salts are detected by the characteristic yellowish-green colour they produce when heated in a Bunsen flame after being moistened with hydrochloric acid, or by the two green lines seen in their spectra. In solution, barium salts are identified by the yellow precipitate of barium chromate formed upon adding potassium chromate, or by the immediate precipitate produced by the addition of calcium sulphate, which distinguishes them from calcium salts.

Quantitative estimation is performed by conversion into sulphate. C. Marignac determined the atomic weight as 136.84 by converting barium chloride into barium sulphate or by measuring the silver required to precipitate the chloride. T. W. Richards (1893) obtained a value of 137.44 by determining the equivalent of barium bromide and chloride to silver. The archive lists the atomic weight as 137.37.

## Barium Compounds
Barium occurs naturally primarily as witherite ($\text{BaCO}_3$) and barytes or heavy spar ($\text{BaSO}_4$), and to a lesser extent in baryto-calcite, baryto-celestine, and complex silicates.

### Nitrides, Amides, and Carbides
Barium nitride ($\text{Ba}_3\text{N}_2$) is obtained as a brownish mass by passing nitrogen over heated barium amalgam. It evolves hydrogen when decomposed by water and forms barium cyanide when heated in a current of carbonic oxide. Barium amide ($\text{Ba}(\text{NH}_2)_2$) is derived from barium bromide and potassammonium. Barium carbide ($\text{BaC}_2$) is prepared similarly to calcium carbide, or by distilling a mixture of carbon and barium amalgam in a stream of hydrogen.

### Oxides and Hydroxides
Barium hydroxide ($\text{Ba}(\text{OH})_2$) is a white powder. On a large scale, it is produced by heating heavy spar with small coal to create barium sulphide, which is then heated in moist carbon dioxide to form barium carbonate, and finally decomposed by superheated steam. Its solution, known as baryta-water, is used in gas-analysis to determine atmospheric carbon dioxide and as a hydrolysing or condensing agent in organic chemistry. Barium dioxide ($\text{BaO}_2$) is a grey powder produced by heating barium monoxide with air under pressure (the Brin process) or by adding excess baryta-water to hydrogen peroxide solution.

### Halides and Other Salts
*   **Barium Chloride ($\text{BaCl}_2 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$):** Obtained by dissolving witherite in dilute hydrochloric acid or from heavy spar via ignition in a reverberatory furnace with coal, limestone, and calcium chloride. It is a strong poison with a bitter taste.
*   **Barium Bromide ($\text{BaBr}_2 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$):** Prepared by decomposing barium carbonate with hydrobromic acid or saturating baryta-water.
*   **Barium Nitrate ($\text{Ba}(\text{NO}_3)_2$):** Prepared by mixing hot saturated solutions of sodium nitrate and barium chloride, or by dissolving barium sulphide or carbonate in dilute nitric acid. It is used in pyrotechny to create green fire.
*   **Barium Sulphate ($\text{BaSO}_4$):** The most abundant natural barium compound, it is an amorphous white powder used as a pigment called "permanent white" or *blanc fixe*.
*   **Other Salts:** Barium chlorate ($\text{Ba}(\text{ClO}_3)_2$) is obtained by adding barium chloride to sodium chlorate solution; barium iodate ($\text{Ba}(\text{IO}_3)_2$) is produced by adding sodium iodate to barium chloride solution or by the action of iodic acid on hot caustic baryta solution; barium bromate ($\text{Ba}(\text{BrO}_3)_2$) is prepared by the action of excess bromine on baryta-water.
