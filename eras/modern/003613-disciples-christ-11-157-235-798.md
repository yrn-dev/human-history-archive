# Disciples of Christ

The term "Disciples of Christ" refers both to the immediate followers of Jesus during his earthly ministry and to a specific modern religious denomination.

## Early Disciples and Teachings
During his ministry, Jesus provided instruction to his disciples regarding the Kingdom of God and the nature of faith. In one recorded instance, Jesus remarked to his disciples on the difficulty rich individuals face when attempting to enter the Kingdom of God, stating that it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter. This statement left the disciples astonished.

The disciples were also noted for their departure from certain formal religious traditions. When Pharisees and scribes questioned why the disciples did not follow the tradition of the elders by eating bread with unwashed hands, Jesus responded by calling the critics hypocrites who honored God with their lips while their hearts remained far from him, teaching the commandments of men as doctrines.

## Early Christian Development
The Bible used by Jesus and his disciples was the Old Testament, and they appeared content with this source. For approximately two decades following the death of Christ, there were no specifically Christian writings. This lack of written records among the first generation of Christians was due to an "eschatological" attitude; they lived in daily expectation of the Coming of the Messiah in power and glory, believing it to be near. Because they expected to be caught up to meet the Lord in the air, they felt no motive to write instructions for future generations.

Following the death of Christ, the "apostle of the Gentiles" spread the gospel across provinces extending from the Ionian Sea to the Euphrates. These seeds of faith were diligently cultivated by his disciples. During the first two centuries, the most significant body of Christians was located within these limits, with ancient societies established in Antioch, Berea (or Aleppo), and Damascus. The religion also spread to the seven churches of Asia—Philadelphia, Laodicea, Sardes, Thyatira, Pergamus, Smyrna, and Ephesus—as well as to the islands of Crete and Cyprus, and the provinces of Macedonia and Thrace. Christian republics were subsequently founded in Athens, Sparta, and Corinth.

## The "Disciples of Christ" Denomination
In a modern context, the "Disciples of Christ" is a religious body with a global presence. According to archival data, the denomination has a significant presence in the United States, where it is listed alongside other groups such as the Southern Baptist Convention and the National Baptist Convention. In one specific state record from 1906, the Disciples of Christ accounted for 136,110 communicants out of a total of 858,324 across various denominations.

The denomination's global distribution includes:
*   **North America:** 11,157 churches and 1,235,798 members in the U.S.A. (which includes 8,894 churches and 986,821 members in thirty-five Northern States); 985 churches and 103,062 members in Canada.
*   **Europe:** The United Kingdom (2,934 churches; 426,563 members), Sweden (567 churches; 43,305 members), Germany (180 churches; 32,462 members), and smaller populations in Russia and Poland, Finland, Norway, Austria Hungary, Italy, France, the Netherlands, Spain, and Rumania and Bulgaria.
*   **Asia and Oceania:** India (1,215 churches; 121,716 members), China (137 churches; 12,160 members), Australasia (270 churches; 23,253 members), Japan, Ceylon, and Palestine.
*   **Africa and Americas:** The West Indies (318 churches; 42,310 members), Mexico and Central America, South America, South Africa, and regions of West Africa and the Congo.

In 1909, the comparative totals for all listed churches were roughly 72,988, with 7,480,940 members.
