# British Museum

The British Museum is a major institution housing extensive collections of historical documents, rare books, and ancient artifacts. Its holdings span a vast chronological and geographical range, from early Mesopotamian surveys to significant milestones in the history of the printed Bible.

## Library Collections and Benefactors
The museum's library has been significantly expanded through the acquisition of private collections. In 1823, it received the library of George III, a collection reflecting the literary spirit of the era. This noble library contained over 80,000 pamphlets and books, along with 440 manuscripts. It was rich in English classics and works from Greece and Rome, featuring typographical first-fruits from Venice, Rome, and Mainz, as well as works from the first presses of Oxford, London, and Westminster.

Prior to the acquisition of George III's library, the museum had received a smaller, carefully selected collection from the Rev. C.M. Cracherode, who died in 1799. In 1846, the library was further enriched by the collection of Thomas Grenville, who died at the age of ninety-one. Other contributors to the museum's holdings include Garrick, who bequeathed a fine collection of English plays, and Sir Joseph Banks, who provided natural history books.

## Biblical and Religious Manuscripts
The British Museum holds a wide array of critical religious texts and manuscripts. These include:
*   **Ancient Codices and Versions:** The *Codex Alexandrinus*, a probable Northumbrian copy of the *Codex Amiatinus*, and a 9th-century Hebrew *Pentateuch*.
*   **Latin and Early English Bibles:** A Vulgate written in A.D. 1148 for the monastery of Ste Marie de Parco, a 13th-century Latin Bible formerly belonging to Robert de Bello, and an early Wycliffite version from the late 14th century that belonged to Thomas of Woodstock, duke of Gloucester.
*   **Printed Bibles:** The 42-Line Bible (printed at Mainz, 1452-6), the first printed English Bible (1535), and the first edition of the Authorized Version (1611). The museum also holds the only remaining fragment of Tyndale's Quarto Edition of the New Testament, printed in Cologne in 1525.
*   **Other Religious Works:** A late 12th-century Psalter of Westminster Abbey (Royal MS. 2A. xxii) and a late 13th-century Lectionary of the use of Paris (Add. M.S. 17,341).

## Ancient Artifacts and Art
The museum's collections extend to the earliest recorded history of human civilization. It houses a series of circular clay tablets dating back to 2100 or 2300 B.C. from Babylonia. These tablets contain land surveys from the age of Sargon of Akkad (3800 B.C.), including one that depicts lower Babylonia encircled by the "salt water river," Oceanus.

In terms of art and illuminated manuscripts, the museum holds works such as the 14th-century *De Divinis Officiis* by Durandus (Italian School) and a work by Valerius Maximus from approximately A.D. 1475, executed for Philippe de Comines. Additionally, the museum is noted for its collection of historical papyri, in which it excels even the museum of Egyptian antiquities at Giza.

## Administration and Cataloguing
The organization of the museum's vast resources has involved several notable bibliographers and librarians. Samuel Ayscough, known as "The Prince of Indexers," worked in the cataloguing department and was appointed assistant librarian around 1785. Ayscough published a two-volume catalogue of the museum's then undescribed manuscripts in 1782 and contributed a third share to the British Museum catalogue of 1787. He also catalogued 16,000 ancient charters and rolls.

Thomas Hartwell Horne, an English theologian and bibliographer, was appointed senior assistant librarian in the department of printed books in 1824. Horne was involved in the preparation of the museum's alphabetical catalogue after a project to create a classified catalogue was abandoned. He remained connected with the museum until shortly before his death in 1862.
