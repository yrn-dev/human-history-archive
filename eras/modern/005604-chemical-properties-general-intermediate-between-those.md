# Bromine

## Chemical Properties and Reactivity
Bromine is an element whose chemical properties are generally intermediate between those of iodine and chlorine. Due to these properties, the union of bromine with hydrogen requires either a fairly high temperature or the presence of a catalytic agent. Bromine does not combine directly with carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen.

When reacting with other elements, bromine forms bromides, a process that often occurs with explosive violence. For example, phosphorus inflames when exposed to bromine vapour and detonates in liquid bromine. While potassium reacts energetically, sodium must be heated to 200° C to react.

## Applications in Chemistry
In analytical chemistry, the primary use of bromine is based on the oxidizing action of bromine water. Both bromine water and bromine are capable of bleaching organic colouring matters. In the field of organic chemistry, bromine is used extensively as an oxidizing and substituting agent, as well as for the preparation of addition compounds. These reactions may be carried out using bromine in vapour, liquid, or solution, or in the presence of "bromine carriers."

The action of bromine vapour on organic compounds can be affected by sunlight, which may accelerate the reaction, retard it, or result in different products. Certain reactions that are only possible through the aid of nascent bromine are performed using solutions of bromate and sodium bromide, with the amount of sulphuric acid calculated according to the equation: 5NaBr + NaBrO₃ + 6H₂SO₄ = 6NaHSO₄ + 3H₂O + 6Br.

## Solvents and Extraction
The choice of solvent is important because it can vary the nature of the product and the velocity of the reaction. Common diluents used with bromine include:
* Water
* Hydrochloric acid
* Acetic acid
* Chloroform
* Ether
* Carbon bisulphide

Less common solvents include hydrobromic acid, potassium bromide, and alcohol. Excess bromine is typically removed from these mixtures by shaking with mercury, using sulphurous acid, or by heating.

Additionally, the use of bromine for the extraction of gold was proposed by others and R. Wagner (in *Dingler's Journal*), though its general application has been restricted by its cost.
