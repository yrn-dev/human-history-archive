# Bahr-el-Ghazal

The Bahr-el-Ghazal, also known as the Gazelle river, is the primary western affluent of the Nile river in North-East Africa. It joins the Nile at 9deg 30' N., 30deg 25' E. The river is described as a drainage channel rather than a true river, characterized by a deep stream formed by the junction of numerous rivers.

## Geography and Basin
The basin of the Ghazal is extensive, stretching from the Congo watershed in the south-west to Darfur in the north-west. The Bahr-el-Ghazal itself flows north from the confluence of the Lol and the Jur, featuring lagoon-like expansions with little depth (never exceeding 13 ft). It then turns north-east, where the channel becomes deeper and narrower with occasional reaches of papyrus, before finally turning east to reach Lake No. The banks in this section are typically marked by scrub and anthills; in one valley, the density of anthills is such that they resemble a "gigantic graveyard."

The region's hydrography is characterized by significant seasonal changes. In flood time, the river's rise is barely 3 ft, yet this is sufficient to place an enormous area of country under water.

## Tributaries and Affluents
The Jur is the most important of the rivers that form the Bahr-el-Ghazal. Just above the confluence with the Lol, the Jur broadens into Lake Ambadi, which is over a mile broad at low water and 10 m long. This lake is home to many "swimming" variety sudd plants, though ambach and papyrus are absent. The lake also supports large numbers of *Balaeniceps rex*.

The Bahr-el-Homr serves as the main northern feeder and marks the southern frontier of Darfur for approximately 200 m. It is the only important affluent with tributaries originating north of the main stream. Its headwaters lie west of 24deg E, where the watersheds of the Congo, Nile, and Shari meet. It is reinforced by rivers from Dar Fertit and intermittent streams from the hills of Darfur. In 1906, the lower reaches of the Bahr-el-Homr were completely blocked by sudd, though the Sudan government later sent engineering parties to restore the waterway.

The Lol (also called the Bahr-el-Arab or Kir) is the most westerly affluent. It rises in Dar Fertit as the Boro or Telgona and receives the Bongo, Chel, Sopo, and Raga from the south and south-west. Near the Biri tributary of the Chel (7deg 40' N., 26deg 10' E.) lies Dem Zobeir, formerly the chief station of Zobeir Rahama.

## Physical Characteristics and Sudd
The southern affluents of the Ghazal flow across a plateau of ferruginous laterite with steep valley banks in their upper courses. North of 7deg 20' N., where rapids occur, the valleys open into tortuous channels often choked by sandbanks. Around 9deg N., this alluvial region transitions into a sea of swamps that are part of the larger Nile swamp region, making it nearly impossible to trace river courses.

Like the Bahr-el-Jebel, the Bahr-el-Ghazal is prone to being choked by sudd. During the rule of the Mahdists, the river became almost blocked by these obstructions. Gessi Pasha was imprisoned in the sudd for approximately six weeks. British officers removed these blocks from the Jur, the Bahr-el-Ghazal, and other rivers in 1901 and subsequent years, enabling uninterrupted steamboat communication between Wau and Khartum (a distance of some 930 m) during the flood season.

## Exploration History
Early Greek geographers imagined the Nile's source was westward toward Lake Chad due to rumors of the Bahr-el-Ghazal. The first reasonably accurate map of the river's course was published by the French cartographer d'Anville in 1772. Following Egyptian expeditions from 1839-1842, John Petherick, a Welsh mining engineer, explored the Jur and the main stream between 1833 and 1865.

In 1859, Giovanni Miani, a Venetian, penetrated the southern regions of the basin and provided the first reports of the Welle, a great river flowing west beyond the Nile watershed. Later explorations included:
* **1862:** Lejean, a Frenchman, surveyed the main river and published a map.
* **1863:** Alexandrine Tinne led a party, including German scientists Hermann Steudner and Theodor von Heuglin, to determine the western extent of the Nile basin. Five of the nine white members died of blackwater fever.
* **1869-1871:** Georg Schweinfurth traversed the southern district and discovered the Welle.
* **1879-1881:** Gessi Pasha, the Italian governor under General C. G. Gordon, explored tributaries while attempting to destroy the slave trade.
* **Late 19th Century:** Wilhelm Junker explored southern tributaries. The Jur (Sue) was followed throughout its course by Lieutenant A. H. Dye and members of a French mission under Colonel J. B. Marchand, who reached Fashoda on the White Nile in July 1898 after crossing from the Congo.
* **1905-1907:** R. C. Bayldon, Capt. C. Percival, and Lieut. D. Comyn explored northern and western affluents to clarify the region's nomenclature and hydrography.
