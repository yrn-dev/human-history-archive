# The Home Rule Bill

The Home Rule Bill refers to a series of legislative attempts by the British government to grant the Irish people control over their own internal affairs. These efforts were characterized by a fundamental struggle between Liberal politicians seeking a new footing for Irish-British relations and those within the Conservative Party and the "new British Imperialism" who sought to maintain their ascendancy over the empire.

## The First Home Rule Bill (1886)
In 1886, Mr. Gladstone introduced the first Irish Home Rule Bill. This was described as a genuine attempt to hand over Irish affairs to the Irish people for the first time in history, driven by Gladstone's idea of nationality. However, the proposal was considered faulty and dangerous in several respects. Specifically, it failed to provide satisfactory assurances to the Protestant Irish, particularly those in Ulster, who feared potential injuries from the "priest-ridden illiterates of the south."

The introduction of this bill resulted in political disaster for Gladstone, as it broke the Liberal Party asunder. Consequently, the Gladstone government was replaced by a coalition known as the Unionist Government.

## The Third Home Rule Bill and the Ulster Crisis
A third Home Rule Bill was introduced in 1912 by Prime Minister Mr. Asquith. This bill was the subject of intense parliamentary debate throughout 1913 and the early part of 1914. While the bill initially proposed Home Rule for all of Ireland, an Amending Act was promised to exclude Ulster under specific conditions.

From 1909, the Chancellor of the Exchequer and other officials had advocated for the exclusion of Ulster based on a "county option" or a similar process. For years, this was resisted by the argument that such a concession should only be a final means of securing a settlement. By the time the issue reached its "supreme climax," the Cabinet generally agreed that progress was impossible without providing for Ulster's exclusion.

When the Government informed Irish leaders of this resolution in March, the leaders resisted vehemently. They feared that any weakening of the Bill would lead to the repudiation of the Bill and their own leadership by the Irish people. They also possessed the power to turn out the Government, potentially with support from within the Liberal Party. However, the Government remained resolute and refused to shrink from being defeated and broken up on this point.

## The Amending Act and Safeguards
Following the yield of the Irish leaders, amendments were framed to provide a practical safeguard for the Protestant North. These amendments granted any Ulster county the right to vote itself out of the Home Rule Bill until two successive General Elections had occurred in the United Kingdom. This arrangement ensured that Irish unity could only be achieved through the free consent of the Protestant North after a Dublin Parliament had been "on trial" for a period of at least five years, while still preserving the principle of Irish unity.

## Impact of the Great War
The struggle over Home Rule lasted until the outbreak of the Great War in August 1914. Royal assent was given to the Home Rule Bill after the war had actually begun, along with a second bill that suspended the coming into force of Irish Home Rule until the war's conclusion. Both were placed upon the Statute Book.

During this period, figures such as Sir Edward Carson denounced the placing of the Bill on the Statute Book, while Mr. John Redmond, leader of the Irish party in the House of Commons, called for the Irish people to contribute equally to the war effort. 

The work of reconciliation, which had been initiated by Gladstone in 1886 and brought near to completion in 1914, was eventually "completely and finally wrecked." This decline was further exacerbated in 1915 when the Liberal Government was replaced by a coalition that included Sir Edward Carson as Attorney-General—a move viewed as a gross insult to a friendly people, given Carson's association with "Ulster sedition" and bloodshed at Howth and Londonderry.
