# The Pragmatic Sanction and the Succession of Charles

## The Pragmatic Sanction of 1713
In the early 18th century, Charles, the ruler of the Habsburg hereditary dominions, faced the challenge of securing the future of his monarchy. Because he was without a son, Charles sought to ensure that the throne would pass to his daughter, Maria Theresa. To achieve this, he issued the Pragmatic Sanction on April 19, 1713. This legal instrument pronounced the indivisibility of the monarchy and settled the succession on his daughter in the event that there was no male heir.

## Diplomatic Efforts for Adhesion
By 1731, Charles found himself in a state of isolation, making it his primary object to secure the adhesion of the European powers to the Pragmatic Sanction. His efforts met with varying degrees of success among the different nations:

*   **Great Britain and Holland:** In 1731, these two powers agreed to respect the instrument. This agreement was made in return for the cession of Guastalla, Piacenza, and Parma to Don Carlos.
*   **The Bourbon Powers:** These powers remained hostile to the sanction, which led to the War of Polish Succession in 1733.
*   **Spain and Sardinia:** Following the conflict, both Spain and Sardinia eventually adhered to the Pragmatic Sanction.

## Outcomes of the War of Polish Succession
The War of Polish Succession resulted in several territorial shifts. France acquired Lorraine, while Don Carlos obtained Sicily, Naples, and the Tuscan ports. The Habsburg monarchy's influence in northern Italy was strengthened through the acquisition of Piacenza, Parma, and Guastalla. 

As part of these arrangements, Francis, the duke of Lorraine who had been dispossessed, was to be compensated with Tuscany. Francis and the archduchess Maria Theresa were married on February 12, 1736. Subsequently, on May 11 of that year, Francis signed the formal act that ceded Lorraine to France.

## Earlier Strategic Context
The diplomatic pressures regarding the succession followed a period of European entanglement and shifting alliances. In 1718, Charles had joined the Triple Alliance (which later became the Quadruple Alliance) due to anxieties regarding Italy, where Spain—guided by Alberoni—had occupied Sicily and Sardinia. This coercion of Spain led to a peace agreement in which Charles exchanged Sardinia for Sicily. While these gains were significant, they illustrated a pattern in which the true interests of the Habsburg hereditary dominions were often sacrificed to European entanglements; for instance, continuing the war might have allowed Austria to extend conquests down the Danube.
