# England (1851)

The year 1851 in England is marked in the archive's records through various academic publications, the deaths of notable figures, and the legal frameworks governing the society of the period.

## Notable Figures and Deaths
The archive records the passing of John Kidd on September 7, 1851. Born in Westminster in 1775, Kidd was a prominent English geologist, chemist, and physician. His academic career was centered at Oxford, where he graduated B.A. in 1797 and M.D. in 1804. Kidd held several prestigious positions, including the first Aldrichian professor of chemistry (elected 1803), the regius professor of medicine (appointed 1822), and the keeper of the Radcliffe Library (appointed 1834). 

Kidd is noted for his role as an instructive lecturer who delivered courses on geology and mineralogy in the dark chambers beneath the Ashmolean Museum. These lectures provided the foundational training for several figures, including W. Buckland, C. G. B. Daubeny, and J. J. and W. D. Conybeare. His efforts contributed to the establishment of the geological chair, which was first held by Buckland. His published works include *Outlines of Mineralogy* (1809), *A Geological Essay on the Imperfect Evidence in Support of a Theory of the Earth* (1815), and the Bridgewater Treatise *On the Adaptation of External Nature to the Physical Condition of Man* (1833).

## Legal Status of the Deceased
Under the common law of England, a corpse is defined as a dead human body and is not considered the subject of property, nor is it capable of holding property. Consequently, the act of stealing a corpse is not classified as larceny. However, the removal of grave-cloths or a coffin is treated differently, as these items remain the property of the individuals who buried the body.

English law identifies several misdemeanors regarding the treatment of the dead, including:
* The disinterment of a body without proper authority.
* The exposure of a naked corpse to public view.
* The prevention of a dead body's burial.
* The disposal of a body without notifying a coroner when an inquest is required.

While relatives and friends are expected to provide a Christian burial, this duty falls to the parish if no such persons are available. Legally, no one is required to incur debt to provide a burial. Furthermore, a corpse cannot be arrested, detained for debt, or taken in execution.

## Burial Practices and Regulations
The mode of burial in England is a matter of ecclesiastical cognizance or the discretion of the burial ground owners. Regulations are managed by the Local Government Board for grounds provided under the Burial Acts, and by the Public Health Act 1879 for cemeteries.

Historically, before 1880, burial in consecrated ground required the service of the Church, performed by the parish incumbent or an authorized person. Certain individuals were refused this service based on the prayer-book and canons, including:
* Unbaptized persons.
* Persons against whom a verdict of *felo de se* had been found.
* Persons who committed a "grievous and notorious crime" (resulting in *majori excommunicatione*) and were unable to testify to their repentance.

The Burial Laws Amendment Act 1880 modified these requirements, allowing those entitled to be buried in parochial burial grounds (churchyards or graveyards) to be buried without the Church of England service. This may be done with no religious service or with a Christian and orderly service conducted by a person invited by the funeral's manager.

## Historical and Academic Works
The mid-19th century saw the publication of several significant historical texts. In 1851, A. Strickland published volume ii of *Lives of the Queens of England*. Additionally, a new edition of Thomas Carte's *Life of James, duke of Ormond* was published in Oxford in 1851 in six volumes. 

Other academic interests of the era included the study of the Domesday Book and the constitutional history of England. Records mention Hubert, the last of the great justiciars, who was a popular hero described by Matthew Paris as the man "who restored England to the English."
