# The Burmese Boundary and the Mekong River

## Boundary Demarcation and Course
The eastern boundaries of Burma have undergone various stages of definition, particularly along the Chinese frontier. At a point level in latitude with Mogaung, near the northern termination of the Burmese railway system, the boundary is defined by the eastern watershed of the Nmaikha, which is the eastern of the two great northern affluents of the Irrawaddy. From there, the boundary follows an irregular course southwards to a position south-east of Bhamo at 24 deg. latitude.

The line then defines the northern edge of the Shan States before striking the Mekong river at approximately 21 deg. 45' latitude. From this point, the river serves as the boundary between Tongking and the Shan States for approximately 200 miles. In this section, the channel of the river defines the limits of occupation between British and French subjects, although it does not entirely define the limits of interest.

## The 20th Parallel and Southern Limits
Approximately on the parallel of 20 deg. N. lat., the Burmese boundary leaves the Mekong river. From this point, the boundary runs westwards toward the Salween river. It then follows the eastern watershed of the Salween basin, which serves to divide the Lower Burma provinces from Siam.

## Keng Tung and Regional Geography
Keng Tung is the most extensive of the Shan States within the province of Burma. Located in the southern Shan States' charge, it lies almost entirely east of the Salween river. The state covers an area of slightly over 12,000 square miles. Its boundaries are defined as follows:
* **North:** The states of Mang Lön, Möng Lem, and Keng Hung (Hsip Hsawng Panna), with the latter two under Chinese control.
* **East:** The Mekong river, with French Lao territory situated on the farther side.
* **South:** The Siamese Shan States.
* **West:** Generally the Salween river, though there are places where the state overlaps the river.

Known to the Chinese as Mêng Kêng and to the Burmese as "the 32 cities of the Gôn" (Hkön), the state's classical name is Khemarata or Khemarata Tungkapuri. Under British control, Keng Tung expanded to include the cis-Mekong portions of Keng Cheng as well as the districts of Möng Pu, Möng Hsat, Hsen Mawng, and Hsen Yawt.

## Hydrography and Topography of the Region
The geography of Keng Tung is characterized by a high and generally continuous watershed range, with some peaks exceeding 7,000 feet and elevations rarely falling below 5,000 feet. The terrain is dominated by rugged hills and successive north-south hill ranges, leaving scattered valleys. 

The state's drainage is split between two major river basins: 63% of the area lies in the basin of the Mekong river and 37% in the Salween drainage area. 

### Salween Tributaries
The chief tributaries of the Salween in this region are the Nam Hsim, Nam Pu, Hwe Lông, and Nam Hka. The Nam Hsim rises on the central watershed range, while the Nam Hka rises in the Vü or Wa states. Both the Nam Hsim and Nam Hka are considerable rivers, though they are unnavigable due to rapids and rocks; however, timber is transported down the Nam Hsim. The lower portions of these two rivers form the boundary of the Keng Tung state.

### Mekong Tributaries
The Mekong's chief tributaries in the area include the Nam Kôk, Nam Lin, Nam Hôk, Nam Yawng, Nam Lwe, and Nam Nga. The Nam Lwe is the primary tributary and is navigable within the interior of the state, though it enters the Mekong through a rocky gorge. The Nam Lin and Nam Kôk are also significant streams, with the lower course of the Nam Kôk passing through Chieng Rai in Siamese territory.
