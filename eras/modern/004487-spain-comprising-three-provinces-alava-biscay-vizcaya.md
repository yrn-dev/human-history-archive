# Basque Provinces

The Basque Provinces (*Provincias Vascongadas*) are a division of north-eastern Spain consisting of three provinces: Alava, Biscay (or Vizcaya), and Guipuzcoa. As of 1900, the region had a population of 603,596 and an area of 2,739 square miles, making it the third most densely populated area in Spain. The territory is shaped like a triangle, bordered to the north by France and the Bay of Biscay, to the east by Navarre, and to the south and west by the provinces of Logrono, Burgos, and Santander.

## Geography and Climate
The region is characterized by a bold and rugged coastline, particularly in Biscay, where it extends from Ondarroa to just east of Castro Urdiales. The interior is predominantly mountainous, traversed toward the south by the great Cantabrian chain, though it is diversified by small plains and narrow valleys. While some mountains consist of naked calcareous rock, many were previously covered in pine, chestnut, and oak forests, which were destroyed for fuel. Common vegetation includes arbutus, holly, furze, and heath.

The climate is variable and inclement, though snow is infrequent outside the highest summits and temperatures rarely drop below freezing. Rainfall is higher here than in any Spanish province except those in the extreme north-west. The soil is generally not very fertile, except on sheltered hillsides and in valleys, where it produces barley, wheat, rye, maize, flax, grapes, apples, peaches, and other fruits.

The primary river of size is the Nervion (also known as the Ansa or Ibaizabal), upon which the city of Bilbao is situated. Other watercourses are primarily large mountain streams.

## The Province of Biscay
Biscay is a maritime province bounded by the Bay of Biscay to the north, Guipuzcoa to the east, Alava to the south, and Santander and Burgos to the west. In 1900, its population was 311,361 across an area of 836 square miles. A small strip of isolated territory on its western border is officially part of Santander.

The province is known for its traditional Basque farm-houses, called *caserios*, located on mountainous slopes. Peasantry here operate under the *metayer* system, sharing soil profits with absentee landlords. Most farms are small and worked by manual labor. Along the coast, a hardy race of fishers actively pursues fisheries.

Bilbao, the capital of Biscay, is the greatest of the Basque towns and a principal Spanish seaport. Located on the river Nervion about 8 miles inland from the Bay of Biscay, it had a population of 83,306 in 1900. The city is divided into an old town on the right bank, featuring narrow streets and 14th-century structures like the church of Santiago and the San Antonio stone bridge, and a new town on the left bank. After 1876, the left bank was developed with broad streets, public gardens, hotels, and palatial buildings.

## People and Language
The Basques inhabit the three Basque Provinces and Navarre in Spain, as well as the French arrondissements of Mauleon and Bayonne. Approximately 600,000 "pure Basques" reside in Europe, with another 100,000 emigrants, primarily in the La Plata region of South America. The term "Basques" is derived from *Vascones*. The people refer to themselves as *Eskualdunak* ("those who possess the Eskuara") and their land as Eskual-Herria.

Their language, Eskuara (also uskara or euskara), is an agglutinative, polysynthetic, and incorporating system of speech. It remains absolutely isolated from other European tongues, though grammatically it resembles Finnic and Magyar languages.

## History and Governance
Anciently, the region's boundaries may have corresponded to the territories of the Vascones, Varduli, Caristi, and Autrigones tribes. In the Roman era, these areas were part of the province of Tarragona. Later, the provinces were divided in their allegiances between Castile and Navarre. Guipuzcoa was definitively united to Castile in 1200, Alava in 1332 via the confederation of Arriaga, and Biscay in 1370.

Despite their union with the Spanish crown, the Basque Provinces remained a "land apart" (*tierra apartada*). They maintained a high degree of autonomy through their *fueros* (customary laws and charters). Spanish kings were required to swear to observe these *fueros* upon their accession. Royal orders or decrees of the Cortes had no force in the provinces unless countersigned by the local juntas. This autonomy was reflected in their international relations; for example, Guipuzcoa signed a treaty of friendship and free trade with England in 1482, and the Basques are mentioned separately from Spain in the 1713 treaty of Utrecht.

The Basque municipal institutions were derived from the Roman *civitates* and *respublicae*. Officers were elective, using various methods ranging from nomination by lot to universal suffrage. The provincial juntas often met in the open air, most notably under the oak of Guernica in Biscay. This system of self-government lasted until 1833, following the death of Ferdinand VII.
