# ASQUITH, HERBERT HENRY

Herbert Henry Asquith (1852– ) was an English statesman born on September 12, 1852, in Morley, Yorkshire. The son of Joseph Dixon Asquith, he came from a middle-class family characterized by Nonconformist and Liberal views.

## Early Life and Education
Asquith was educated under Dr. Edwin Abbott at the City of London school before attending Balliol, Oxford, as a scholar. His time at Oxford was distinguished; he won the Craven scholarship, took a first-class degree in classics, and was elected a fellow of his college. He also served as president of the Union. His intellectual ability was noted by his contemporaries, including Dr. Jowett, who predicted his future success.

## Legal Career
After leaving Oxford, Asquith entered the bar and became a K.C. by 1890. He was recognized as a lawyer of clarity and unusual mental distinction, though his forensic success was less conspicuous than that of some peers. His legal work included serving as junior counsel to Sir Charles (later Lord) Russell for the Irish Nationalists before the Parnell Commission in 1889. In 1887, he unsuccessfully defended John Burns and R.B. Cunninghame Graham regarding their involvement in the Trafalgar Square riot.

Following a period in government, Asquith returned to the bar in 1895. This move was considered unprecedented for a minister who had exercised judicial functions, but it was necessitated by his lack of sufficient independent means.

## Early Political Career
Asquith's political involvement began during his university days, specifically through work for the Eighty Club. He was elected as the member of parliament for East Fife in 1886, a seat he held in subsequent elections. His speaking ability and political judgment attracted Mr. Gladstone, who selected him in August 1892 to move the vote of want of confidence that overthrew the government of Lord Salisbury.

As a result, Asquith was appointed home secretary in the new Liberal ministry. In this role, he opened Trafalgar Square to Labour demonstrations and became the first to appoint women as factory inspectors.

## Liberal Imperialism and Party Leadership
The Liberal defeat in 1895 removed Asquith from office for eleven years. During the Boer War, he identified as a Liberal Imperialist and a follower of Lord Rosebery, which aligned him with the "Imperialist" wing of the party. This position created tension with the party leadership under Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, who was associated with "pro-Boer" policies. Asquith became a founder of the Liberal League and was viewed as Lord Rosebery's chief lieutenant.

Regarding Irish Home Rule, Asquith upheld Gladstonian policy in theory, but Irish Nationalists felt his practical advocacy was limited. However, he helped mitigate party difficulties and, starting in 1903, championed free-trade and "orthodox" English political economy against the tariff-reform movement led by Mr. Chamberlain.

## Premiership and Government
Asquith's political importance grew during the premiership of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman. By 1907, as Campbell-Bannerman's health declined, leadership in the Commons devolved to Asquith, who was then the chancellor of the exchequer. Though initially viewed coldly by some Radicals due to his sobriety and common sense, he became the only viable successor to the prime minister.

On April 5, 1908, it was announced that Campbell-Bannerman had resigned. Asquith, who was staying at Biarritz, was sent for by the king and officially became prime minister on April 8, resigning his chancellorship.

As Prime Minister, Asquith led a cabinet that functioned as a veiled coalition between Liberal Imperialists and Radical-Pacifist elements. While he maintained an impartial official position, his sympathies lay with the Admiralty, the War Office, and Foreign Secretary Sir Edward Grey. He allied himself with the rising reputation of Mr. Lloyd George, who succeeded him as chancellor of the exchequer.

His early leadership was marked by the introduction of a system of old-age pensions in the budget of May 1908 and the conduct of the Licensing Bill. While the Licensing Bill passed the Commons, it was later thrown out by the Lords.

## Personal Life
Asquith married Miss Helen Melland in 1877; she died in 1891, leaving him with a family. In 1894, he married Margaret ("Margot") Tennant, the daughter of Sir Charles Tennant. Mrs. Asquith was a well-known member of the London society coterie known as the "Souls" and was identified as the original for the character "Dodo" in E.F. Benson's 1893 work.
