# The Tariff Reform Movement of Mr Chamberlain

The tariff reform movement was a political and economic campaign initiated by Mr Chamberlain in 1903. The movement sought to reorganize the British budget by introducing a system of taxes on imports, including food, with the primary goals of granting preference to colonial goods and protecting imperial trade through the imposition of retaliative duties on foreign goods in specific instances.

## Origins and Objectives
The movement emerged as a natural evolution of imperialist thought, supported by United Kingdom trade statistics which demonstrated that trade with the colonies was comprising an increasingly large proportion of overall trade. The immediate catalyst for Mr Chamberlain's "crusade" was the remission of the shilling import-duty on corn by Mr Ritchie. 

Mr Chamberlain advocated for a definite scheme to reorganize the budget to increase taxes on imports. He proposed that these taxes be adjusted to stimulate employment and improve the economic position of the working classes. His case rested largely on the alleged diminution of British trade, a claim that necessitated investigation via the Board of Trade and the appointment of a Tariff Commission in December 1903.

## Political Conflict within the Unionist Party
The proposal for a general tariff and new food taxes represented a radical alteration of the established fiscal system, leading to a deep rift within the Unionist party. "Strait free-traders" within the cabinet, such as Mr Ritchie and Lord George Hamilton, and those outside it, including Lord Hugh Cecil and Mr Arthur Elliot, were entirely opposed to the plan. In response to Mr Chamberlain's Tariff Reform league, the free-trade Unionists—led by the duke of Devonshire, Lord James, Lord Goschen, and Lord Hugh Cecil—established the Free Food league. This opposing group rejected food taxes, discredited Mr Chamberlain's statistics, and repudiated the assumption that retaliation would be desirable.

Prime Minister Mr Balfour occupied a complex position during this struggle. While he had supported the desirability of retaliating against "dumping" and unfair foreign competition, he expressed uncertainty regarding the details of Mr Chamberlain's scheme and the readiness of the country to accept food taxation. Mr Balfour professed to have "no settled convictions" on the matter and avoided taking a definitive side for two years, a stance that drew criticism in the Liberal press.

## Escalation and Government Collapse
The tension within the Unionist ranks culminated in September 1903. Following the publication of Mr Balfour's pamphlet on "Insular Free Trade," several ministers resigned, including the free-traders Lord George Hamilton and Mr Ritchie, as well as Mr Chamberlain himself. Correspondence between Balfour and Chamberlain revealed that while Balfour sympathized with retaliatory duties and a possible fiscal union with the colonies, he could not support the taxation of food. He encouraged Mr Chamberlain to test and convert public opinion through "missionary efforts" outside the government.

The conflict reached a breaking point in November 1905. After Mr Balfour appealed for party unity at Newcastle on November 14, Mr Chamberlain insisted at a Bristol speech on November 21 that his programme be adopted. This forced Mr Balfour to abandon his tactical neutrality. Mr Balfour resigned on December 4, 1905, and Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman formed a government.

## Opposition and Electoral Impact
The Liberal party utilized the tariff reform movement as an opportunity to rally behind the banner of free trade. Mr Asquith, who had been a lecturer on "orthodox" English political economy, became a chief champion of free trade during the critical years of Mr Chamberlain's crusade from 1903 to 1906.

In the general election of January 1906, the Unionists suffered a crushing defeat due to their divided counsels. However, the tariff reformers saw comparative success; Mr Chamberlain achieved a signal personal triumph in all the divisions of Birmingham, whereas Mr Balfour was defeated by a large majority in Manchester.

## Legacy and Imperial Adoption
Despite the 1906 defeat of the Unionist party and the accession of a Liberal government opposed to policies inconsistent with free trade, the movement for colonial preference continued to gain ground in the United Kingdom and was definitely adopted by the Unionist party. By the Imperial Conference of 1907, the movement found broad support among colonial premiers. These leaders pointed to the progress their own states had already made in granting tariff preferences to British goods and to the goods of other colonies.

The movement also found support among English economists, most notably William James Ashley. A professor of commerce and finance at Birmingham University and dean of its faculty of commerce, Ashley was prominent among the economists who supported Mr Chamberlain's reforms, authoring the work *The Tariff Problem* (2nd ed. 1904).
