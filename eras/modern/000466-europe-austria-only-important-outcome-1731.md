# Austria

## Origins and Geographic Significance
The foundations of the Austrian dominions began around A.D. 800 with the establishment of a mark along the south bank of the Danube, east of the river Enns. This mark was created as a defense for the Frankish kingdom against the Slavs. The name *Oesterreich* (Austria), meaning "east empire" or "realm," first appeared in a charter dated 996. 

For over a thousand years, Austria has occupied a unique position in Europe as a meeting-place for the Teuton and the Slav, standing sentinel over the middle Danube valley and barring Slav advances into Germany. This development progressed through two main dynastic periods: first under the house of Babenberg, which focused on enlarging and strengthening the mark, and subsequently under the house of Habsburg, which extended its rule over neighboring territories. One of the principal provinces of the Austrian empire is Bohemia, a kingdom situated in the geographical center of the European continent. Bohemia serves as a borderland between the Slavonic and German worlds and is bounded by Prussian Silesia, Moravia, Lower Austria, Upper Austria, Bavaria, and Saxony.

## Early Habsburg Conflicts and Expansion
Under the 48-year reign of Leopold I, who succeeded his father Ferdinand in 1657, Austria's external policy was defined by struggles with France, Sweden, and the Ottoman Empire. A war with Sweden (1657-60) concluded with the peace of Oliva, which safeguarded the frontier of Hungary and secured Polish independence. 

Austria's campaigns against the Turks (1662-64 and 1683-99) drove Ottoman power from Hungary. An initial conflict caused by Ottoman aggression in Transylvania ended with a victory at St Gothard on August 1, 1664, though the subsequent peace of Vasvar (August 10) left the Turks in possession of Ersekujvar and Nagyvarad. In 1683, the Turks burst into Hungary and reached the walls of Vienna, but were defeated on September 12 by John Sobieski.

## The 18th Century: Succession and Diplomacy
In the early 18th century, the house of Habsburg faced challenges regarding the balance of power in Europe. In 1718, Charles joined the Triple Alliance (later the Quadruple Alliance) to address Spanish threats in Italy, eventually obtaining Sicily in exchange for Sardinia. By 1731, Charles found himself isolated. Lacking a son, he sought to secure the throne for his daughter, Maria Theresa, via the Pragmatic Sanction of April 19, 1713, which declared the monarchy indivisible.

While Great Britain and Holland agreed to respect this sanction in 1731 in exchange for the cession of Guastalla, Piacenza, and Parma to Don Carlos, the Bourbon powers remained hostile. This led to the War of Polish Succession in 1733. The conflict resulted in France acquiring Lorraine, while Don Carlos acquired Sicily, Naples, and the Tuscan ports. The Habsburg monarchy strengthened its hold on northern Italy by acquiring Parma, Piacenza, and Guastalla. Francis, the dispossessed duke of Lorraine, was compensated with Tuscany and married Maria Theresa on February 12, 1736.

Later, from 1740 to 1748, the War of the Austrian Succession occurred, sparked by Frederick II of Prussia's invasion of Silesia. This struggle involved nearly all European powers; Austria was supported by England and Holland, while France and Prussia were allied with Spain and Bavaria.

## The Napoleonic Era and the 19th Century
The late 18th and early 19th centuries brought significant territorial shifts. The second partition of Poland (January 23, 1793) removed a buffer state, bringing Austrian and Russian frontiers into direct contact. The treaty of Campo Formio (October 17, 1997) confirmed the loss of Belgian provinces and, despite the gain of Venice, signaled a breach in Habsburg supremacy in Italy.

The rise of Napoleon led to a revolution in Austria's relations with German states. Following the treaty of Luneville (February 9, 1801), Francis II sought to prevent the subordination of his family to the Bonaparte dynasty. On August 11, 1804, he assumed the title of Francis I, hereditary emperor of Austria. He formally abdicated as Holy Roman emperor on August 6, 1806, after the treaty of Pressburg (January 1, 1806) resulted in the loss of Tirol and Venice.

Austria suffered further humiliations, including the treaty of Vienna (1809), until Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign provided an opportunity for recovery. Under the diplomacy of Metternich, Austria initially acted as a mediator but eventually joined the Grand Alliance via the treaty of Trachenberg (July 12, 1813). This led to the battle of Leipzig in October 1813 and Napoleon's abdication on April 11, 1814.

In 1848, revolutions swept Europe. In Austria, Kossuth delivered a speech on March 3 in the Pressburg diet, calling for the "fraternization of the Austrian peoples" and the liberation of Hungary. However, the revolution failed as the new ideal of German unity clashed with the aspirations of other races within the monarchy.

## Late 19th and Early 20th Century Relations
In the mid-19th century, the Crimean War erupted after Tsar Nicholas I of Russia occupied the Danubian principalities in 1853, referring to the Sultan as the "sick man of Europe." This led to an alliance between England and France to support Turkey, resulting in a Russian repulse.

By the early 20th century, Austria-Hungary's policy in the Balkans was managed through agreements with Russia, such as the "February Programme" of 1903 and the "Muerzsteg Programme." However, after 1906, Baron von Aerenthal pursued a bolder policy. In January 1908, he obtained a concession from the sultan for a railway from the Bosnian frontier to Mitrovitza, undermining the agreement with Russia and causing resentment in Servia. This policy was supported by Germany, with whom Austria-Hungary had maintained intimate relations, notably siding with Germany during the Algeciras Conference on March 3, 1906, regarding the dispute between France and Germany over Morocco.
