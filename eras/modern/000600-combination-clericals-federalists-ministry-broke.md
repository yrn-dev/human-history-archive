# The Breakdown of the Ministry and the Rise of the Clerical-Federalist Combination

In the political history of Austria, the period preceding the combination of the Clericals and Federalists was marked by severe ministerial instability, internal division, and a struggle between centralist and federalist ideologies. This instability centered on the difficulty of governing a diverse empire and the failure of successive constitutional arrangements to reconcile the various ethnic and political factions.

## Internal Divisions and Ministerial Collapse
Before the eventual alliance of the Clericals and Federalists, the ministry suffered a breakdown caused by internal disagreements over how to handle the Slav races. Within the government, Counts Taaffe and Alfred Potocki (the minister of agriculture) advocated for a policy of conciliation toward the Slavs, a position supported by Beust and likely sympathized with by the emperor. However, other members of the ministry sought to neutralize the opposition by removing elections for the Reichsrath from the diets.

These divisions led to the resignation of Taaffe and his associates in January 1870. The remaining majority was short-lived; by March, the government definitively rejected new demands from Galicia. This prompted the Polish club, along with Slovenes and Tirolese representatives, to leave the House. This exodus left the parliament consisting of only 110 members—primarily Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia—rendering the parliament impossible to govern.

## Attempts at Reconciliation
Recognizing that the new constitution appeared to have failed similarly to the old one, the ministry resigned, and Potocki and Taaffe formed a new government specifically to attempt reconciliation with the Slavs. Potocki, serving as minister-president, sought to persuade the Czechs to accept the constitution. He summoned Thun and Rieger to Vienna and traveled to Prague himself. However, these efforts ended in disappointment; the Czechs and Feudals adhered to the declaration of 1868 and refused any compromise.

The situation was further complicated by the outbreak of war between France and Germany in July. To avoid alienating either the Slavs or the Germans during this crisis, the emperor dissolved all diets and the Reichsrath. In Bohemia, the Czechs—supported by Clericals and Feudals—won a large majority. However, they refused to recognize the diet as the legal representative of the Bohemian kingdom, viewing it instead as an informal assembly, and declined to elect delegates for the Reichsrath.

## The Hohenwart Ministry and the Federalist Shift
By February 1871, following the end of the European crisis, the emperor appointed a ministry led by A. E. F. Schaeffle and Count Hohenwart. This ministry was chosen not from the Liberals, but from the Federalists and Clericals. Their strategy was to grant the Federalists all their demands. To achieve a legislative majority, they ordered new elections in diets where Liberals held the majority, utilizing the support of the Clericals to put the Liberals in a minority in the Reichsrath.

To bring the Czechs into the parliament, the government agreed to the terms of the Declaration. On September 12, a royal message recognized the separate existence of the Bohemian kingdom and promised that the emperor would be crowned as king in Prague. While this was welcomed in Bohemia, it caused the German minority to leave the diet and prepare for resistance. In Moravia, Carinthia, and Upper Austria, Germans seceded after being outvoted by the Clericals. This movement threatened to overthrow the work of 1867 and endanger the union with Hungary. Consequently, the emperor decided the Bohemian demands could not be accepted, leading to the resignation of Hohenwart.

## The "Iron Ring" and Taaffe's Governance
Following these upheavals, Taaffe became first minister. He initially attempted to govern through a cabinet of moderates from nearly every party. However, after the Liberals voted against the government on a critical military bill, Taaffe turned to the Right for support. The German members of the government resigned and were replaced by Czechs, Poles, and Clericals. 

This created a political bloc known as the "iron ring," consisting of these three parties, which left the German Liberals in a helpless minority. Taaffe maintained this position for fourteen years. His approach, termed "unpolitical politics," involved avoiding contentious legislation and ruling according to the constitution. His strength lay in his position between the Right and the constitutionalists; the Right supported him to prevent the constitutionalists from regaining a majority.

## Ideological Context: Centralists vs. Federalists
The overarching struggle was between the Centralists and the Federalists. Centralists argued that the constitution of 1861 remained valid and demanded a "constitutional" government. Federalists, conversely, claimed the constitution must be established through agreement with the territories. 

This divide was largely racial and institutional. The "kernel" of the empire consisted of purely German districts, including Salzburg, Vorarlberg, Upper and Lower Austria, most of Styria, and most of Carinthia. While Liberals in Styria and Lower Austria pushed for centralized institutions, a strong Clerical and Conservative party supported local institutions. The bishops and clergy allied with the Federalists to protect local structures against the influence of a centralized bureaucracy and parliament.
