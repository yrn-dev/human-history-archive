# Temperature Variations in Siberia

## Temperature Extremes and Ranges
Siberia is characterized by very great variations in temperature. The difference between the mean temperatures of the hottest and coldest months is hardly anywhere less than 60 deg. Fahr. Near the coast, this variation between monthly means can amount to more than 100 deg. Fahr., with even greater differences observed between the extreme temperatures of those months.

The extreme temperatures of the region are marked by significant highs and lows. Maxima are considered to lie between 80 deg. and 90 deg. Fahr., while minima fall between -40 deg. and -70 deg. Fahr.

## Seasonal Characteristics and Regional Comparisons
In the northern portion of Asia, including Siberia, winters are long and characterized by extreme severity. Even as far south as the circle of 35 deg. N. lat., minimum temperatures can drop almost as low as zero Fahrenheit. Conversely, summers in the northern latitudes are hot, though short; the maximum summer heat is described as being comparatively little less than the heat observed in tropical countries further south.

When compared to other regions in Asia, Siberia's temperature fluctuations are more pronounced. For example, the variation between the hottest and coldest monthly means is 80 deg. Fahr. on the Sea of Aral, more than 50 deg. at Astrakhan on the Caspian, 45 deg. at Tiflis, and 55 deg. at Peking. In contrast, these variations are much lower in southern Asia, reducing to about 5 deg. under the equator.

## Factors Influencing Climate
Several geographical factors contribute to the extreme temperatures of the region. The great elevations of the central part of Asia tend to exaggerate the cold of winter. During the summer, the sterility of the surface—caused by low rainfall—increases the heat. Solar heat accumulates on the dry surface soil and cannot be released upwards through evaporation, as would occur if the soil were covered in vegetation or moist.

In winter, a similar process occurs in a negative direction. Heat is lost through radiation during long, clear nights. This effect is intensified in areas where the air has little vapour or where the surface is covered with snow.

## Permafrost and Soil Conditions
The climate of northern Asia is further illustrated by the frozen soil found in the vicinity of Yakutsk. In this region, the earth is permanently frozen to a depth of more than 380 ft. At this depth, the temperature remains 5 deg. or 6 deg. Fahr. below the freezing point of water. At a depth of 50 ft., the temperature is approximately 15 Fahr. below the freezing point. During the summer, heat only thaws the surface to a depth of about 3 ft. Despite these soil conditions, the land is capable of producing crops of other grain and wheat from fifteen to forty fold.

## Geographic Boundaries and Drainage
The region is influenced by various mountain ranges. The Altai, located near the 50th parallel and rising to 10,000 or 12,000 ft., separates the waters of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria from the great rivers of western Siberia. Further west, a line of elevation continues to the Ural Mountains, which divides the drainage of south-west Siberia from the great plains situated north east of the Aral Sea.
