# The Attack on Belize (September 1779)

## Origins of the Settlement
The territory formerly known in official documents as "His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras" began in 1638. Its founders were log-wood cutters who had previously been buccaneers. Over time, they were joined by agents of the Chartered Company, which was involved in exploiting the pearl fisheries of the Mosquito coast. 

Despite their industrial activities, the settlers maintained habits of making frequent descents upon Spanish logwood establishments. The Spaniards attempted to expel the British settlers on several occasions, though these efforts were generally resisted successfully. One of the most significant attempts occurred in April 1754; because the position was difficult to approach by sea, the Spaniards organized an inland expedition of 1,500 men at the town of Peten. However, this force was completely routed by 250 British men as it neared the coast.

## Diplomatic Status and Encroachment
For a number of years following the 1754 conflict, the log-wood cutters remained undisturbed. Their position became sufficiently established that the treaty of 1763 between Great Britain and Spain included specific protections for them. While Great Britain agreed to demolish all fortifications erected by English subjects in the Bay of Honduras, it insisted on a clause stating that the cutters of logwood, or their workmen, were not to be molested or disturbed under any pretext in their places of loading and cutting logwood.

Following this recognition from the crown, the British settlers began making new encroachments into Spanish territory. The Spaniards responded by asserting that the settlers were engaged in illicit practices and smuggling.

## The Attack of September 1779
In response to these activities, the Spaniards organized a large military force. On September 15, 1779, this force launched a sudden attack on the establishment at Belize. The attack resulted in the destruction of the establishment and the capture of the inhabitants.

The prisoners were first taken to Mérida in Yucatan and subsequently transported to Havana. A majority of the captives died in Havana. Those who survived were liberated in 1782 and permitted to travel to Jamaica.

## Recovery and Subsequent Treaty
The settlers returned to the region in 1783, accompanied by many new adventurers, and resumed the cutting of woods. On September 3, 1783, a new treaty was signed between Spain and Great Britain. This agreement expressly granted the subjects of His Britannic Majesty the right to carry away, load, and cut logwood in the district located between specific boundaries.
