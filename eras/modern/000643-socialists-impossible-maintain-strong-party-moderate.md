# Socialists

The term "Socialists" encompasses various political movements and parties across Europe, characterized by differing approaches to reform, governance, and the acquisition of political power. These movements have appeared in various forms, ranging from democratic parliamentary parties to revolutionary governments.

## Socialism in Austria
In Austria, the development of socialism as a political force was initially hindered by limited suffrage and national divisions, which prevented the creation of a centralized party. While the working classes sought political power through mass demonstrations and petitions for universal suffrage in 1867, the subsequent growth of the movement in Vienna and Styria was interrupted after 1879 by the introduction of anarchical doctrines. This period saw the circulation of Most's paper, *Freiheit*, and the rise of anarchists under Peukert. Between 1883 and 1884, serious strikes and collisions with police occurred, followed by assassinations involving robbery and murder. The government responded with severe repressive measures, leading to the flight or expulsion of leading anarchists.

The socialist party began to revive in 1887 under the leadership of Dr. Adler after the "party of violence" had died away. The party's primary political demand became universal suffrage, and the 1st of May became a widely celebrated festival of Labour.

By the late 19th century, Socialists began replacing Liberals in Vienna. Following a franchise reform bill in 1896—which added a fifth *curia* allowing almost anyone residing in one place for six months (excluding domestic servants) to vote—fourteen Social Democrats won seats in the 1897 elections. The political landscape became fragmented, making strong government difficult. By the general election of May 14, 1907, the Social Democrats achieved a sweeping victory, increasing their representation from 11 to 87 members.

## French Socialism and the Unified Socialist Party
In France, Aristide Briand emerged as a leader of the French Socialist party. Briand's early political career involved writing for the anarchist journal *Le Peuple* and founding the publication *L'Humanité* with Jean Jaurès. He was a prominent figure in the formation of labour unions, securing the adoption of the labour union idea at the 1894 congress of working men in Nantes against the followers of Jules Guesde.

Briand advocated for the "union of the Left" (the *Bloc*) to counter reactionary deputies of the Right. He was a primary author of the law regarding the separation of church and state. However, his decision to accept a portfolio of public instruction and worship in the bourgeois Sarrien ministry in 1906 led to his exclusion from the Unified Socialist party in March 1906. This exclusion stemmed from a disagreement with Jaurès; Briand argued that Socialists should actively cooperate with Radicals on reform matters rather than remaining aloof until their ideals were completely fulfilled.

## The Russian Revolution and the Soviets
In Russia, the pressures of war led the masses to seek an end to hostilities. In Petrograd, a body representing common soldiers and workers known as the Soviet was formed. The Soviet pushed for an international conference of socialists in Stockholm to seek a peace on democratic lines. While a small majority of the British Labour Party responded favorably, Western allies refused the conference for fear of a worldwide outbreak of republicanism and socialism.

Following the failure of a final offensive in July 1917 and subsequent mutinies in the Russian armies, the government of Kerensky was overthrown on November 7, 1917. Power was seized by the Soviet Government, which was dominated by the Bolshevik socialists under Lenin. The Bolsheviks pledged to make peace regardless of the Western powers, leading to Russia's exit from the war and the signing of a separate peace with Germany at Brest-Litovsk on March 2, 1918.

## Other European Movements
In Belgium, the "extreme democratic and socialistic party" attempted to stir up a revolutionary movement with French aid during the crisis of 1848. However, these efforts were spasmodic and failed to gain popular sympathy, as the Belgian nation maintained trust in the moderation of King Leopold.

Additionally, the archive notes the existence of "Christian Socialists" in Austria, led by Dr. Lueger. This group, characterized by strong nationalist elements directed against Hungarians and Jews, won predominant influence in Vienna and eventually amalgamated with the conservative Catholic party to become a powerful group in parliament.
