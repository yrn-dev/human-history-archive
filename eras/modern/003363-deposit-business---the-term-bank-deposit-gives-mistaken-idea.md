# Deposit Business

The deposit business is a primary function of modern banking, characterized by the receipt of funds from customers which are then utilized to support trade and general industry. In the historical classification of European banks from the 16th century onward, "banks of deposit" are distinguished from "exchange banks." While exchange banks—such as the Bank of Hamburg and the Bank of Amsterdam—were established to facilitate trade with other countries and deal with foreign exchange, banks of deposit, including the Bank of England, the Bank of France, and the Bank of Germany, focus on receiving deposits and making loans.

## Legal Nature of Deposits
The term "bank of deposit" can be misleading regarding the actual legal relationship between a banker and a customer. In the 1848 House of Lords decision in *Foley v. Hill*, it was determined that the relationship is one of debtor and creditor. It is not a relationship of trustee and *cestui que trust*, nor of depositee and depositor. 

Legally, when a customer pays money into an account, they are simply lending that money to the banker. This arrangement does not establish a fiduciary relation, and the banker is not responsible to the customer for how the money is used. Because this is considered a mere debt, the Statute of Limitations bars a customer's right to recover funds after six years, provided there has been no payment of interest or principal and no sufficient acknowledgment to bar the statute (*Pott v. Clegg*). In practice, this rarely occurs in current accounts because earlier withdrawals are typically attributed to earlier payments, causing credit and debit items to cancel each other. An exception exists when a person wrongfully deposits money held in a fiduciary capacity into their own account; in such cases, it is presumed the individual drew out their own funds first, and any remaining balance is attributed to the trust money.

## Historical Development
The earliest example of a bank for the receipt of deposits operating on modern lines is found in the commercial city of Venice during the Middle Ages. Private banking there began as an adjunct to the business of *campsores* (dealers in foreign moneys). While security for the government was required of them as early as 1270, an act dated September 24, 1318, titled *Bancherii scriptae dent plegiarias consulibus*, recognized the existing practice of *campsores* receiving deposits and provided for better security for those depositors. This transition from money-changing to banking was later mirrored by similar classes of men in Amsterdam and by goldsmiths in London.

## Operational Principles and Economic Role
According to David Ricardo in *Proposals for an Economical and Secure Currency*, a bank cannot prosper solely through the employment of its own capital. The community benefits when a bank employs the capital of others. A bank uses its own capital to establish the standing necessary to collect deposits from others, but it does not hoard these funds. Instead, it holds them until they can be used for advances.

While some deposits remain for the customer's everyday needs, the majority enable the bank to make advances to borrowers who employ the funds in "reproductive industry." This is defined as use that not only returns a value greater than the original loan but also assists the business development of the country by maintaining profitable enterprises. To maintain safe business lines, the absolute rule for loans is that advances should consist of floating capital rather than fixed capital.

## Evolution of Deposits and Note Circulation
In the United Kingdom, note circulation was a vital part of banking business in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, but deposits became more important thereafter. In Scotland, for example, the Bank of Dundee operated from 1764 to 1792 solely through the issue of notes before it began receiving money from the public. By the time it amalgamated with the Royal Bank of Scotland in 1864, its deposits reached approximately £700,000, while its note circulation was £41,000.

In England and Wales, many provincial banks were banks of issue until the Act of 1844 restricted their power to issue notes. Evidence from a 1875 House of Commons inquiry showed that the proportion of notes in circulation relative to banking deposits diminished greatly between 1844 and 1875. In some instances, deposits increased three-fold, five-fold, or even twelve and a half times, while the circulation as a percentage of deposits dropped significantly (for example, from one-third to 9%, or from 22% to 1.5%).

## Global Statistics and Institutional Examples
The scale of deposit business varies across different regions and institutions:

*   **United Kingdom:** Estimates for total deposits in England, Wales, Scotland, and Ireland ranged from £250,000,000 to £360,000,000 in 1851, increasing to £430,000,000–£450,000,000 by 1872, and reaching approximately £997,000,000 by the close of 1906.
*   **Bank of England:** Originally established to advance a £1,200,000 loan to the government, it has remained the government's banker. While it once controlled larger deposits than any other single bank, other competitors now hold larger deposits, and the collective deposits of other London-based banks many times exceed its own.
*   **British Colonial Banks:** More than 80 joint-stock British banks operate in colonies with total deposits of £360,000,000. This includes 29 banks in Canada (£140,000,000 in deposits), 24 in Australia and New Zealand (£130,000,000), 15 in India (£36,000,000), and at least 8 in South and West Africa (£50,000,000).
*   **Foreign British Banks:** About 20 British banks operate in foreign countries (including South America and the East) with total deposits of £135,000,000.
*   **German Banks:** German banks, including the Bank of Germany and those in the Far East and South America, have resources including deposits, notes, and mortgage bonds amounting to fully £414,000,000.
