# Colonial Administration and Imperial Relations

The history of colonial organization involves a transition from early settlement patterns to complex systems of governance, legal harmonization, and economic integration. This evolution is evident in the differing structures of colonial administration, the movement toward imperial federation, and the legislative efforts to align laws across territories.

## Structures of Colonial Governance
Colonial administration is categorized by the level of local control over finance. In crown colonies, the imperial government maintains direct control over finances. Representative colonies are not directly controlled, though the imperial government typically influences important financial departures. In contrast, responsible colonies exercise entire local control over their finances, and the imperial government holds no moral or material responsibility for their debts.

During the period from 1845 to 1875, the "Manchester school" exerted significant influence on public affairs. During this era, colonial self-government reached its fullest development, as it was generally accepted that such institutions served as a preliminary to inevitable separation. However, a general inclination to withdraw from imperial responsibilities provided foreign nations with opportunities to profit, eventually leading to a reaction exemplified by Great Britain's role in the scramble for Africa.

## Imperial Federation and Consolidation
By the late 19th century, the view of self-government as a path to separation was challenged. In 1872, Lord Beaconsfield described colonial constitutions as part of a "great policy of imperial consolidation." In 1875, W. E. Forster advocated for imperial federation to replace dependence with association. This movement progressed with the 1884 foundation of the Imperial Federation League, with Forster as its first president, followed by Lord Rosebery.

Earlier efforts toward federation had appeared in New Zealand in 1852 and were officially suggested by the general association of the Australian colonies in London in 1857. The Royal Colonial Institution was also founded in 1868 under the motto "United Empire." To facilitate organization and defense, Colonial Conferences were held starting in 1887; these were renamed Imperial Conferences in 1907. These meetings served as forums where colonial opinion was sought and accepted regarding imperial organization.

## Legal and Social Harmonization
A significant point of legal contention between the United Kingdom and its colonies concerned marriages with a deceased wife's sister. While such restrictions had ceased to exist in all self-governing colonies except Newfoundland, they remained in England for many years. South Australia passed the first act legalizing such marriages, though royal assent was only granted after the state parliament passed the bill five times. Similar statutes followed in New Zealand, Canada, Barbados, Mauritius, Natal, Cape Colony, and several Australian states (Victoria, Tasmania, New South Wales, Queensland, and West Australia). In the Channel Islands, these marriages became legal in 1899, and in the Isle of Man in 1907.

In the United Kingdom, Lord Strathcona introduced bills on the subject to the House of Lords in 1898 and 1900. Although the House of Commons blocked attempts to move the measure to the committee stage in 1901 and 1902 due to High Church opposition, the Colonial Marriages (Deceased Wife's Sister) Act was adopted in 1906. This act legalized such marriages in all respects, including honors, dignities, and succession to real property. This led to the Deceased Wife's Sister Marriage Act of 1907 in England, which included a proviso allowing clergymen to refuse to solemnize such marriages and preserved the status of the wife's sister under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857 regarding incestuous adultery.

## Economic Policy and Tariff Reform
The imperialist idea evolved into a movement for tariff reform, initiated by Mr. Chamberlain in 1903. This movement sought to protect imperial trade and provide preference to colonial goods through retaliative duties on foreign goods. This shift was driven by trade statistics showing that trade with the colonies constituted an increasingly large proportion of United Kingdom trade. Despite the 1906 defeat of the Unionist party by a Liberal government committed to free trade, the movement for colonial preference continued. At the Imperial Conference of 1907, all colonial premiers advocated for this preference, noting the progress their own states had made in granting tariff preferences to British goods and to one another.

## Regional Colonial Examples
Colonial history extends across various eras and regions:
*   **Ancient Greece:** Corinth established colonies along the coast of Epirus, most of which acknowledged Corinthian hegemony. A notable exception was Korkyra, which maintained hostile relations with its mother-city. Korkyra founded the colony of Epidamnus (later Dyrrachium) on the coast of Illyria; however, due to Grecian custom, the founder-in-chief (œkist), Phalius, had to be a citizen of Corinth.
*   **West Africa:** Ashanti is a British possession bounded by the French Ivory Coast colony to the west, the river Volta (separating it from the German colony of Togoland) to the east, and the British Gold Coast colony to the south. As of 1907, it had an estimated population of 500,000 and an area of 23,000 square miles.
*   **Cartography:** By 1907, various colonial maps were produced, including an official map of the Congo State, a map of the French Congo by J. Hansen, and maps of the Ivory Coast and Dahomey.
