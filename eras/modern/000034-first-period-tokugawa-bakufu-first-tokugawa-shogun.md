# First Period of the Tokugawa Bakufu

The first period of the Tokugawa Bakufu spanned from the establishment of the shogunate under the first shogun, Ieyasu, until the tenure of the fourth shogun, Ietsuna, concluding in 1680. This era was characterized by the consolidation of hereditary power, the implementation of strict administrative controls over both the nobility and the feudatories, and a persistent governmental struggle to instill frugality within a society trending toward luxury.

## Administrative Control and the Imperial Court
During this period, the Tokugawa Bakufu established a system of comprehensive oversight over the Imperial Court, reducing the sovereign's functions to formal roles. These included the appointment of the shogun, the ordering of the calendar, fixing the name of year-periods, conferring ranks, nominating officials and priests, taking part in ceremonials, and sanctioning the construction of temples. While the regent (*kwampaku*) was appointed by the sovereign from the five *Go-sekke* families (Konoe, Takatsukasa, Kujo, Nijo, and Ichijo), the regent possessed practically no power.

Actual control was maintained through Bakufu nominees. The *denso*, chosen from seventeen families related to the Tokugawa, had direct access to the Throne but was required to swear an oath to report all knowledge to the Bakufu. The *giso* administered Court affairs and held offices such as *dai-nagon*, *chu-nagon*, or *sho-nagon*, which were reserved for the Tokugawa family. Most significantly, the *shoshidai* acted as a governor-general over the eight provinces surrounding Kyoto. The *shoshidai* managed the guarding of Court nobles and financial business, ensuring that all disturbances in Kyoto were reported to Yedo and that the Bakufu exercised complete control over the Imperial capital.

## Governance of the Feudatories
The Bakufu maintained a rigorous approach to the loyalty and conduct of the *kokushu* (masters of provinces). In a directive dated September 23, 1615 (the 20th year of Keicho), the government admonished these lords to select capable men for office, noting that the flourish or ruin of a domain depended on the capacity of its administration.

The shoguns extended little mercy to wrongdoers, including their own relatives and high-ranking officials. In 1614, Okubo Tadachika was deprived of his castle at Odawara and confined for contracting a private marriage. In 1622, the prime minister Honda Masazumi was exiled to Dawe and lost a fief of 150,000 *koku* for killing a Bakufu soldier and rebuilding his castle without permission. Such severity was viewed by officials like Doi Toshikatsu as necessary to ensure the permanence of the Bakufu.

## The Challenge of Osaka
The city of Osaka remained a point of contention and a sanctuary for those defying Tokugawa authority. Because the "Osaka party" and figures such as Harunaga and Lady Yodo had refused to sign the oath of fealty, the city became a refuge for men who had broken Bakufu laws, including Nambu Saemon, Goto Matabei, and the son of Hosokawa Tadaoki. 

Osaka also attracted a steady stream of *ronin* (free-lances) and Christian feudatories who openly preached their faith in defiance of Bakufu edicts. Furthermore, Shinto and Buddhist priests in the region remained independent of the Bakufu, and Tokugawa officials often declined to judge boundary disputes in the area because they lacked the power to enforce their decisions. Ieyasu viewed these conditions as obnoxious and waited for a pretext to assert total supremacy.

## Social Values and Economy
A central tenet of the Bakufu's governance was the promotion of frugality. Successive shoguns made strenuous efforts to encourage this virtue to counter a prevailing tendency toward increasing luxury brought about by the country's long period of peace. While the first three shoguns achieved some success in enforcing official injunctions of economy, the era was generally characterized by a steady growth of extravagance.
