# Army

The term "army" encompasses various national military structures, ranging from the organized forces of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy to the smaller military establishments of republics and the legal frameworks governing service in the United Kingdom.

## Military Forces of the Republic (1905)
In 1905, the military forces of the republic consisted of 2,890 regulars and an enrolled force of 80,000 men. This enrolled force was further divided into a second reserve of 40,000, a first reserve of 30,000, and 10,000 territorial guards; however, this group was unarmed and unorganized. 

The army's strength is determined by the annual budget. For example, the 1903 budget provided for 2,933 officers and men, which included 1,906 rank and file, 558 non-commissioned officers, 275 commissioned officers, and 181 musicians. While a conscription law from 1894 mandated compulsory service for those between the ages of twenty-one and fifty—specifically requiring two years of actual service for those aged twenty-one to twenty-five—the law is considered practically a dead letter. The republic maintains an arsenal at La Paz and a military school with 60 cadets.

## The Austro-Hungarian Military System
Since 1868, the military system of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy has been based on the principle of personal and universal obligation for citizens to bear arms. The force is composed of the *Landsturm* (levy-in-mass), the common army (*K. und K.*), and special national armies: the Hungarian *Honveds* and the Austrian *Landwehr*. The joint minister of war administers the common army, while the respective ministries of national defence manage the special armies.

Recruit contingents are fixed by military bills voted on by the Hungarian and Austrian parliaments, generally based on census population data. In 1905, the total recruits amounted to 103,100 men (59,211 from Austria and 43,889 from Hungary). Additionally, 12,500 men were allotted annually to the Hungarian *Honveds* and 10,000 to the Austrian *Landwehr*. Service terms typically involve two years with the colours (three years for cavalry), two years in the *Landwehr*, and seven or eight years in the reserve.

### Strength and Expenditure (1905)
On a peace footing in 1905, the total strength of the Austro-Hungarian army was 20,564 officers and 336,818 men, with 64,887 horses and 1,048 guns. The breakdown by branch was as follows:
*   **Common Army:** 15,863 officers and 285,733 men (including 376 officers and 6,372 men stationed in Herzegovina and Bosnia).
*   **Austrian Landwehr:** 2,053 officers and 25,766 men.
*   **Hungarian Honveds:** 2,648 officers and 25,319 men.

Other specialized units included Field Artillery (1,630 officers, 27,612 men, and 1,048 guns), Fortress Artillery (408 officers and 7,722 men), Technical troops such as the Railway and Telegraph Regiment and Pioneers (588 officers and 9,935 men), the Transport Service (461 officers and 4,312 men), and the Sanitary Service (85 officers and 3,062 men).

Regarding finances, the joint budget for the Ministry of War (covering both Army and Navy) rose from 9,005.4 thousand sterling in 1875 to 18,087.7 thousand sterling in 1905.

## Military Law and Desertion in the United Kingdom
In the United Kingdom, military or naval desertion occurs when a person absents themselves with the intent to escape important service (such as service in aid of civil power or embarkation for foreign service) or with the intention of not returning. Until 1827, this was a felony punishable by death under civil law. It was later managed by Mutiny Acts and eventually the Army Act 1881.

Under the Army Act 1881, those subject to military law who desert or attempt to desert—or procure others to do so—face death if the offence occurs during active service or under orders for active service. In other circumstances, the first offence results in imprisonment, while subsequent offences lead to penal servitude. Those who aid, conceal, or persuade a soldier to desert can be imprisoned for up to six months. 

The law distinguishes between actual desertion and "fraudulent enlistment," which is the act of quitting one regiment to enlist in another. Furthermore, soldiers who have served in an exemplary manner for at least three years in the regular forces are not punished for desertion that occurred before that three-year period began. Desertion from regular forces is tried by military courts, whereas desertion from reserve forces or the militia may be tried in civil courts.
