# Bonds Given to Secure the Doing of Anything Contrary to the Law

In English law, a bond is defined as an obligation by deed. Its primary purpose is to ensure that the obligor (the person providing the bond) either pays a specific sum of money or performs—or refrains from performing—a particular act. To achieve this, the obligor binds themselves to a penalty in favor of the obligee. A condition, known as the "defeasance," is typically added; if the obligor fulfills the specified act or pays the secured sum (which is often half of the penalty), the bond becomes void. If the condition is not met, the bond remains in full force and becomes absolute at law, charging the obligor and their estate.

## Legality and Void Bonds
Bonds that are given to secure the performance of any act contrary to the policy of the law are considered void. Examples of such void bonds include those given to a woman for future cohabitation (though this is distinguished from past cohabitation) or marriage brocage bonds, which are bonds intended to procure a marriage between parties. The archive notes that while such a bond might be the subject of a suit in a Venetian court—as seen in the fictional case of Shylock and Antonio—it would not be upheld in an English court.

## Types of Specialized Bonds
Bonds are utilized for a wide variety of purposes beyond the simple payment of money at a fixed date. These include:

*   **Performance and Fidelity Bonds:** Bonds may be used to guarantee the fidelity of a rent collector, a clerk, or an individual in an office of public trust. They are also used to ensure that building contracts are carried out or to restrict a rival business from operating within certain limits of time and space.
*   **Matrimonial and Personal Bonds:** Bonds can secure that an intended husband will settle a sum on his wife should she survive him.
*   **Post Obit Bonds:** These are given by a legatee or expectant heir and are payable on or after the death of the person from whom the obligor expects an inheritance. Such bonds may be set aside if the obligee has exacted unconscionable terms.
*   **Bottomry Bonds:** This is a contract of hypothecation where a ship's owner or master borrows money for ship emergencies, such as necessary repairs. The ship (or its keel/bottom) is pledged as security. If the voyage is completed safely, the obligee is repaid with interest; if the ship is totally lost, the obligee loses the money.
*   **Corporate and Financial Bonds:** Lloyd's bonds are instruments used by railway companies to admit indebtedness for goods supplied or work done without technically violating their charter. Debenture bonds are secured only by a company's covenant without a fixed or floating charge on assets. In the United States, national banks have used government bonds to secure note issues.

## Legal Remedies and Penalties
The remedy for a breach of a bond with special conditions is prescribed by an act of 1696 and preserved by the Judicature Act. The obligee identifies the specific breaches, damages are assessed, and the court enters a stay of execution once the obligor pays those damages into court.

A recurring legal difficulty is determining whether the sum in a bond of special conditions is a "true penalty" or "liquidated damages." Courts will not interfere if the parties have agreed upon the damages for a breach, as established in the leading case of *Kemble v. Farren* (1829).

In the case of common money bonds, the court of chancery historically intervened to prevent oppression, viewing the penalty as a form of pledge rather than the substance of the debt. This led to statutory recognition in an act of 1705, which allows the payment of the lesser sum, plus interest and costs, to satisfy the bond.

## Other Applications of Bonds
The concept of "bonding" also extends to the state's revenue systems. Historically, importers of goods in England had to provide a bond with security for the future payment of duties. This system was replaced in 1803 by "bonded warehouses," where goods were stored in approved warehouses and importers gave bonds for payment upon the removal of the goods. This system was later modified by the Customs Consolidation Act of 1853 and subsequent legislation.
