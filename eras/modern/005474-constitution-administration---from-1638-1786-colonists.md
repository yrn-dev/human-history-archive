# Constitution and Administration of British Honduras

The governance and administrative structure of British Honduras underwent a significant transition from a period of complete colonial independence to a formalized system of crown control and structured bureaucracy.

## Early Independence and Burnaby's Laws
From 1638 to 1786, the colonists maintained complete independence. During this era, the colonists elected their own magistrates, who were responsible for performing all executive and judicial functions. The precedents and customs established during this period of self-governance were eventually codified. Following a visit by Admiral Sir W. Burnaby in 1756, these regulations were published as "Burnaby's Laws," which the crown recognized as valid.

## Transition to Crown Administration
The administrative nature of the settlement changed in 1786 with the appointment of a superintendent by the home government. Although the office of the superintendent remained vacant between 1790 and 1797, the position was revived and continued until 1862. 

Further institutional developments occurred in the mid-19th century. An executive council was established in 1839, followed by the creation of a legislative assembly in 1853. This assembly consisted of eighteen elected members and three nominated members.

## Colonial Status and Legislative Evolution
In 1862, British Honduras was officially declared a colony. Its chief magistrate was a lieutenant governor, who was subject to the governor of Jamaica. The legislative assembly was abolished in 1870 and replaced by a legislative council. The constitution of this council was fixed in 1892, consisting of five unofficial members and three official members.

A significant shift in status occurred in 1884, when the lieutenant governor was elevated to the position of governor and commander-in-chief. This change rendered the office independent of Jamaica. The governor is assisted by an executive council composed of three unofficial and three official members.

## Administrative Divisions and Legal System
For the purposes of administration, the colony is divided into six districts:
* Belize
* Corosal
* Orange Walk
* The Cayo
* Stann Creek
* Toledo

While the other districts are named after their chief towns, the capital of the Toledo district is Punta Gorda.

The legal framework of British Honduras is based on English common law, which remains valid throughout the colony. This law is subject to the operation of the *Consolidated Laws of British Honduras*—a collection of customs and ordinances officially published and revised between 1884 and 1888—as well as modifications made by local enactments. Legal appeals may be directed to the supreme court of Jamaica or the privy council.
