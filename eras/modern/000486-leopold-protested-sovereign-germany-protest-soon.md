# Leopold and the Conflict with Revolutionary France

## Imperial Role and the French Revolution
Leopold was crowned Roman emperor at Frankfort on October 6, 1700. In his capacity as emperor, rather than as a member of the Habsburg family, he entered into direct antagonism with the France of the Revolution. Although his sister, Marie Antoinette, was the wife of Louis XVI, this familial connection did little to strengthen the Franco-Austrian alliance, which had been practically non-existent since 1763.

The conflict was precipitated by the decree of August 4, issued by the Constituent Assembly. This decree abolished feudal rights, which included the possessions of many German princes that existed as enclaves within Lorraine and Alsace. Viewing this as a move against the established European system, Leopold protested in his role as the sovereign of Germany. This protest was subsequently expanded into a broader appeal on behalf of Europe.

## The Concert of Europe and the Declaration of Pillnitz
On July 6, 1791, Count Kaunitz issued a circular letter calling upon sovereigns to unite against the Revolution. This letter marked the beginning of the Concert of Europe and served as a final manifesto for the Holy Roman Empire as a center of political unity. Following this, the declaration of Pillnitz was issued on August 27, proclaiming a common policy.

However, the goals of the Pillnitz declaration were soon undermined by the specific interests of the involved powers. Both Prussia and Austria were preoccupied with the Polish question, particularly after a new constitution was proclaimed in Poland on May 3. These powers feared that a crusade against France would leave Poland vulnerable to Russia.

## Escalation to War
Initially, Leopold maintained a studiously moderate position regarding the developments in France. However, the crisis intensified due to two primary factors:
1. Leopold's refusal to comply with the French government's demand to disperse the corps of émigrés who had gathered on the French frontier under the protection of German princes.
2. His insistence on the rights of the princes who had been dispossessed in Alsace and Lorraine.

In response, the French Assembly adopted a decree on January 25, 1792, stating that war would be declared if no satisfactory reply was received from the emperor by March 1. Subsequently, on February 7, Austria and Prussia signed an agreement.

## Other Historical Figures Named Leopold
The archive contains records of other individuals named Leopold within the Habsburg and Austrian contexts:

**Archduke Leopold of Austria:** In 1611, the Bishop of Passau, Archduke Leopold, invaded Bohemia, likely acting with the connivance of his cousin, King Rudolph. While he managed to seize part of the town of Prague, his army was defeated by troops raised by the Bohemian estates, forcing his departure from Bohemia. This raid led King Matthias to re-occupy Bohemia, as Matthias viewed his hereditary rights as being menaced by Leopold's actions.

**Leopold I:** Succeeding his father Ferdinand in 1657, Leopold I was crowned emperor the following year and reigned for 48 years. His reign was marked by significant external conflicts:
* **Sweden:** A war from 1657 to 1660 ended with the peace of Oliva, which safeguarded the frontier of Hungary and secured Polish independence.
* **The Ottoman Empire:** Leopold engaged in campaigns against the Turks from 1662–64 and 1683–99. A victory at St Gothard on the Raab on August 1, 1664, ended the first war, though the peace of Vasvar (August 10) left the Turks in possession of the fortress of Ersekujvar and Nagyvarad, while recognizing Transylvania as an independent principality.
* **The Siege of Vienna:** In 1683, spurred by Louis XIV and invited by Hungarian malcontents, the Turks invaded Hungary and reached the walls of Vienna. The city was saved on September 12 by a victory gained by John Sobieski.

Internally, Leopold I's policy in Hungary involved the suppression of the constitution in 1658 and the persecution of Protestants, which led to widespread conspiracy. Although the constitution and palatinate were restored with some concessions to Protestants after a period of arbitrary government (1672–1679), discontent persisted.
