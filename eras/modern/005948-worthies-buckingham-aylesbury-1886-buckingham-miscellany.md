# Worthies of Buckingham and Buckingham Miscellany

The *Worthies of Buckingham* (Aylesbury, 1886) and the *Buckingham Miscellany* (Aylesbury, 1891) are listed as historical authorities regarding the town and county of Buckingham. They are part of a broader body of literature on the region, which includes the standard history by G. Lipscomb, *History and Antiquities of the County of Buckingham* (1831-1847), as well as works by Browne Willis, R. Gibbs, G.S. Roscoe, and P.H. Ditchfield.

## Geography and Administration of Buckinghamshire
The shire of Buckingham was created during the reign of Edward the Elder following the division of Mercia. It was likely formed by aggregating pre-existing hundreds around the county town, resulting in irregular boundary lines. While the Domesday survey recorded eighteen hundreds, these were eventually reduced to eight. Three of these—Stoke, Burnham, and Desborough—are known as the "Chiltern Hundreds" and have remained unaltered in name and extent. Aylesbury is still referred to as "the three hundreds of Aylesbury."

The ancient county covers an area of 475,682 acres, while the administrative county covers 479,358 acres. As of 1901, the population was 195,764. The county town is Buckingham (population 3,152), and other municipal boroughs include Chepping Wycombe (also known as High or Chipping Wycombe), which had a population of 15,542. Other urban districts include Aylesbury, Slough, Chesham, Fenny Stratford, Newport Pagnell, Marlow, Eton, Linslade, and Beaconsfield.

## Local Governance and Institutions
Buckinghamshire is divided into three parliamentary divisions: Northern (Buckingham), Mid (Aylesbury), and Southern (Wycombe), each returning one member. The county also contains a small portion of the parliamentary borough of Windsor. Legally, the county is in the midland circuit, with assizes held at Aylesbury. Historically, Buckinghamshire and Bedfordshire shared a common sheriff until the time of Queen Elizabeth, and the shire court was held at Aylesbury.

The county is almost entirely within the diocese of Oxford, containing 215 ecclesiastical parishes. A notable institution in the region is Eton College, a famous public school founded by Henry VI.

## The Town of Aylesbury
Aylesbury is a market town located 38 miles northwest by west of London in the Vale of Aylesbury. It serves as the assize town for the county. The town is characterized by picturesque, narrow, and irregular streets. Its principal public buildings are the town-hall and the county-hall, which overlook a broad market-place. A grammar school was established there in 1611.

The church of St Mary is a large cruciform building primarily of Early English style, featuring a pre-Norman crypt and a transitional Norman font. The church also contains 15th-century chancel stalls and an ornate clock-turret from the second half of the 17th century. Economically, Aylesbury is noted for printing works, the manufacture of condensed milk, straw-plaiting, and a large agricultural trade, specifically the rearing of ducks.

## Transport and Communications
The region is served by several railway systems. The London & North-Western railway's main line crosses the northeast, with Bletchley serving as a major junction with branches to Oxford, Banbury, Bedford, Cambridge, and Fenny Stratford. A branch also connects Cheddington to Aylesbury. The Metropolitan-Great Central joint line serves Aylesbury, Chesham, and Amersham. Additionally, the Great Central and Great Western companies jointly own a line passing through Prince's Risborough, High Wycombe, and Beaconsfield.

Water communications include the Grand Junction Canal, which has branches reaching both Buckingham and Aylesbury. Aside from the Thames, no rivers in the county are continuously navigable.

## Peerages of Buckingham
The titles associated with Buckingham have a complex history involving different families:

*   **The Early Earldoms:** Evidence suggests the earldom existed under William Rufus. Walter Giffard was described as "Comes Bucchingehamensis" between 1097 and 1102. The title later passed to Richard de Clare (Strongbow) via his wife Rohais and died with him in 1176.
*   **The House of Woodstock and Stafford:** In 1377, Thomas of "Woodstock" was created earl of Buckingham. The title eventually passed to his sister Anne and her son Humphrey Stafford, who was created duke of Buckingham in 1444. This line ended with the execution of Edward, 3rd duke, in 1521.
*   **The Villiers Line:** King James I created George Villiers earl (1617), marquess (1618), and duke (1623) of Buckingham. This dukedom became extinct in 1687. A collateral line claimed the earldom until the death of George Villiers, a clergyman, in 1774.
*   **Later Titles:** In 1703, John Sheffield was created duke of the county of Buckingham and of Normanby; these titles became extinct in 1735. The later marquessate and dukedom of Buckingham (and Chandos) originated in 1784 with the Grenville family, derived from the town rather than the county. Richard Grenville, 3rd duke of Buckingham and Chandos (1823-1889), served as governor of Madras and secretary for the colonies. Upon his death, the dukedom became extinct.
