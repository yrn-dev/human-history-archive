# Populists, the Constitutional Landed Proprietors and the Christian

In the political landscape of the Austrian Empire during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, various parties represented distinct racial, social, and economic interests. Among these were the German Populists, the Constitutional Landed Proprietors, and the Christian Socialists. These groups often clashed over the extension of popular rights and the maintenance of traditional privileges, though they occasionally formed alliances to protect broader racial interests.

## The Constitutional Landed Proprietors
The Constitutional Landed Proprietors were a dominant force in Austrian politics from the 1860s onward, holding the leadership of the German element in the country and within parliament for a generation. Their political power was rooted in the *curia* system of election. Under the February Constitution of 1861, the *Reichsrath* (parliament) included a Chamber of Deputies whose members were chosen by diets. These diets were elected via four electoral bodies, or *curiae*; the first *curia* consisted of the "great proprietors," defined as owners of estates that had enjoyed certain feudal privileges prior to 1848.

Because of this system, the German nominees of the large landed proprietors fought more tenaciously than any other representatives of the *curia* system to preserve their privileges. They viewed the movement toward the extension of popular rights as a threat that would doom their influence and shift the leadership of the Germans to the Christian Socialists. Despite their efforts, their opposition to such reforms proved unavailing, as the emperor repeatedly frowned upon their attempts to block them.

## The German Populists
The German Populists (also referred to as the German Popular Party) operated within the broader category of German Liberals. Unlike other German Liberals who were bitterly antagonistic toward the extension of popular rights, the Populists felt that a "Liberal" party could not logically oppose such an expansion.

The party's strength fluctuated over time. In 1897, the German Popular Party held 42 seats, increasing to 51 by 1901. In other parliamentary records, the Populists are listed as having 29 seats among the German Liberals.

## The Christian Socialists
The Christian Socialists, also known as the Lueger party, were generally favorable toward the extension of popular rights. Their support for such measures was driven by the hope of extending their own organization throughout the empire and gaining parliamentary seats at the expense of the German Populists and German Progressives. In one parliamentary tally, the Christian Socialists are listed as the largest group among the German Conservatives with 96 seats; in another record, they are listed as having 28 seats in 1897 and 23 in 1901.

## Inter-Party Dynamics and Racial Interests
Despite their internal differences regarding social privilege and popular rights, the German parties—including the Christian Socialists, the German Populists, the Constitutional Landed Proprietors, and the German Progressists—were capable of cooperation when faced with external pressures. 

During the administration of Dr. von Koerber (who governed practically without parliament from 1902 until December 31, 1904), the Czechs blocked legislative business through noisy obstruction and "urgency motions," including one sitting that lasted 57 hours. In response to this Czech aggressiveness, the four German parties created a supreme committee of four members and a joint executive committee specifically to watch over German racial interests.

## Comparison with Other Proprietor Classes

In ancient Greece, city-communities typically included considerable land proprietors with rustic dependents. In the seventh and sixth centuries B.C., these great proprietors held the maximum ascendency, and the military force of most cities—consisting of cavalry and retainers—was in their hands. Over time, this oligarchical militia was replaced by heavy-armed infantry as small proprietors and town-artisans rose in importance. In Thessaly, a class of rich proprietors possessed most of the soil and formed separate oligarchies, supported by a class of serfs known as Penestæ.

In Denmark, the relationship between proprietors and the peasantry evolved under different monarchs. Under Christian IV (1588–1648), the breach between the gentry and the yeomanry widened as the number of peasant-proprietors diminished. Later, under Frederick V (1746–1766), the *Stavnsbaand* regulation bound males to the soil from age four. However, after 1755, some great landed proprietors began to abolish servile tenure and introduce hereditary leaseholds.
