# Cartagena

The name Cartagena (or Carthagena) refers to two distinct cities of historical and strategic importance: a seaport in south-eastern Spain and a Caribbean port city in Colombia.

## Cartagena, Spain

Located in the province of Murcia on the Mediterranean Sea at 37 deg. 36' N. and 0 deg. 58' W., this Cartagena is a fortified city with an episcopal see. As of 1900, its population was 99,871. It serves as one of Spain's three great naval stations, alongside San Fernando and Ferrol, and is governed by an admiral holding the title of captain-general. The city possesses naval dockyards and an arsenal.

### Geography and Infrastructure
The city is situated on a hill separated from its harbour by a small plain. It is bordered by high mountains to the south and west, and a fertile valley to the north and east. Its architecture is characterized by grey houses made of friable stone, which often appear dilapidated. Aside from the remnants of the town walls and a ruined citadel, there are few buildings of striking architectural beauty or antiquarian interest.

The harbour is the finest on Spain's east coast and the largest after Vigo. It is a spacious bay with a narrow entrance guarded by forts on both sides. The Arsenal Basin is considered the original harbour used by the Romans and Carthaginians. To the south, the island of La Escombrera protects the city from waves and wind. Modern infrastructure includes a system of tramways and a canal that allows small vessels to enter the town without using the port.

### History
Founded around 243 B.C. by the Carthaginian Hasdrubal as *Carthago Nova* (New Carthage), the city was a treasure city and headquarters for the Punic army until it was taken by P. Scipio in 210 B.C. Under Roman rule, it became the colony *Colonia Victrix Julia Nova Carthago*. The city was pillaged by Goths in A.D. 425 and served as a bishopric until 1289. Following a period as an independent Moorish principality, it was destroyed by Ferdinand II of Castile in 1243 and finally conquered by James I of Aragon in 1276.

Philip II rebuilt the city for its harbour. In later centuries, it was sacked by Sir Francis Drake (1585), occupied by Sir John Leake (1706), and retaken by the duke of Berwick (1707). It capitulated to the French on November 5, 1823. In the 19th century, the city was a center of insurrection; it was bombarded by the Spanish fleet under Admiral Lobos on August 23, 1873, and occupied by government troops on January 12, 1874, following a blockade led by Lopez Dominguez.

### Economy
The region is rich in minerals, with thousands of men and animals transporting sulphur, zinc, copper, iron, and lead to the coast. While epidemic diseases and the abandonment of the arsenal hindered progress in the first half of the 19th century, the city advanced rapidly after 1870. The introduction of the railway allowed it to compete with the port of Alicante and revived metallurgical and mining industries.

## Cartagena, Colombia

Cartagena is a seaport and the capital of the department of Bolivar, Colombia, located on the Caribbean coast at 10 deg. 25' 48" N., 75 deg. 34' W. According to a 1905 official estimate, its population was 14,000, consisting largely of mixed races and blacks.

### Geography and Climate
The city is situated on a low sandy peninsula or island. The port is considered very unhealthful, particularly for foreigners who are not acclimatized, with a mean annual temperature of 82 deg. The harbour is the best on the north coast of South America, covering approximately 62.5 sq. m. and providing secure, deep anchorages. Access to the harbour was originally available through the Boca Grande and Boca Chica; however, the Boca Grande was filled with stone after the city was captured three times to make the narrower Boca Chica the primary, more defensible entrance.

### History and Military Significance
Founded in 1533 by Pedro de Heredia, the city was a vital Spanish colonial naval station. It suffered several attacks: pirates plundered the town in 1544, Sir Francis Drake captured it in 1585, and the French captured it in 1697, taking over L1,000,000. In 1741, Admiral Vernon attempted an unsuccessful siege.

During the wars for independence, Bolivar took the city in 1815, though it surrendered to royalists that same year. Republicans recaptured it on September 25, 1821. It remained a site of political agitation and underwent a siege during the civil war of 1885.

### Commerce and Transport
The city's commercial importance declined following the war for independence but later revived. A canal known as "El Dique," originally opened during the reign of Philip II to provide access to the Magdalena river at Calamar, was reopened for steam navigation in 1881. A 65 m railway was also constructed between Cartagena and Calamar toward the end of the 19th century.

Exports include gold, silver, platinum, rubber, coffee, tobacco, sugar, cattle, and various forest products and dyes. Imports consist of machinery, hardware, kerosene, glass, and various fabrics (woollen, linen, and cotton). In 1906, the aggregate value of exports was $3,788,094 U.S. gold.
