# Predestination and the Concept of Dogma

The theological dogma of predestination is a subject addressed within the context of historical analysis and the broader study of religious doctrine. In the works of the historian Buckle, specifically his *History of Civilization in England*, the dogma of predestination is characterized as a "barren hypothesis" that exists beyond the province of human knowledge. Buckle contrasts this theological position and the metaphysical dogma of free will—which he asserts rests on an erroneous belief in the infallibility of consciousness—with the findings of science and statistics. According to Buckle, science proves that human actions are governed by laws that are as regular and fixed as those ruling the physical world.

## The System of Predestination in Church History
Within the history of the early church, the system of predestination is associated with Augustin, who is described as the "great doctor" of this system. The influence of such theological frameworks is noted in the broader context of early Christian disputes. While some conflicts, such as the schism of the Donatists in Africa, were viewed as accidental quarrels resulting from the abuse of freedom, other disputes, such as the Trinitarian controversy, were regarded as high and mysterious arguments derived from the abuse of philosophy. These theological disputes, including those surrounding Arianism, deeply involved the temporal interests of both Barbarians and Romans from the age of Constantine to that of Clovis and Theodoric.

## Definition and Evolution of Dogma
The term "dogma" originates from the Greek *dogma* (from *dokein*, meaning "to seem"), literally translating to "that which seems good or true or useful" to someone. Its usage has evolved through several stages:

*   **Greek Constitutional History:** A *dogma* was a decree, representing a decision or body of opinions officially adopted by an assembly and regarded as possessing authority.
*   **Early Christian Usage:** While the New Testament used the term in a political sense, the Greek Fathers shifted its usage toward doctrine. For the Greek Fathers, all doctrines were considered "dogmas" provided they were held to be of authority. For example, Cyril of Jerusalem referred to "Ten Dogmas" (or Ten Great Doctrines).
*   **Theological Technicality:** In modern theology, "dogma" is used broadly to describe all theological doctrines, or more narrowly to refer to fundamental beliefs whose confession is required for church communion.

## Perspectives on Dogmatic Authority
The nature of dogma is viewed differently across various theological and philosophical schools:

*   **Sceptics:** Sceptics often use the term "dogma" contemptuously to describe an opinion based on assertion rather than evidence. From an empirical standpoint, this is justified by the fact that theological dogmas deal with subjects that cannot be demonstrated using the methods of physical science.
*   **Roman Catholic Church:** The Roman Catholic tradition views dogma as that which is definitely formulated by authority. The Vatican decrees of 1870 affirmed the personal infallibility of the pope as *divinitus revelatum dogma*. Additionally, the church distinguishes between dogma and "faith," the latter of which may be revealed in Scripture or unwritten tradition and taught through formulated decrees or the ordinary *magisterium*.
*   **Protestant and Academic Views:** Scholars such as A. Harnack have viewed dogma as obsolete, suggesting that Protestants undermined it through a deeper conception of faith. Other historians, such as Arthur Cushman McGiffert, have focused their published work on the history of dogma and church history, sometimes departing from traditionalism.

## Conflict and Toleration
The application of disputed dogmas has historically led to significant social and political tension. In Holland, the conflict between the Remonstrants (Arminians) and the Anti-remonstrants (Gomarists) reached a point where an appeal to force seemed likely. To mitigate this, Grotius proposed a form of edict recommending mutual toleration and forbidding ministers from discussing disputed dogmas from the pulpit. However, orthodox Calvinists found the concept of "toleration" to be insupportable, a position supported by the stadtholder Maurice of Nassau.
