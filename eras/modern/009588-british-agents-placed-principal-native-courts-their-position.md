# British Agents at Principal Native Courts

In the administration of British India, specifically within regions such as the Bombay Presidency and Baluchistan, the British government utilized agents to manage political relations and maintain control over native states. These agents served as the primary link between the paramount British power and the various principalities.

## Position and Role in the Bombay Presidency
In the Bombay Presidency, the political administration of native states was conducted under the superintendence of British agents stationed at the principal native courts. The specific position and influence of these agents varied across different states, depending on the nature of the relations between the individual principality and the paramount power.

The native states within the Bombay Presidency consisted of 353 separate units. These were administered through two primary channels:
*   **Political Agents:** Used for the administration of various states.
*   **District Collectors:** Used to administer the smaller states located within their respective districts.

These states were organized into several chief groups, including North Gujarat (which comprised the Palanpur, Mahi Kantha, and Rewa Kantha agencies, as well as Cutch, Kathiawar, and Cambay), South Gujarat (including Sachin, Bansda, and Dharampur), and North Konkan, Nasik, and Khandesh (including the Khandesh political agency, Jawhar, and Surgana). Other groups included South Konkan and Dharwar, the Deccan Satara Jagirs, and the southern Mahratta states.

## Administration in Baluchistan
In Baluchistan, the political status of the province was described as being almost identical to that of the native states of the Indian peninsula. The region was under the superintendence of the agent to the governor-general, who, supported by a staff of political assistants, exercised practical supreme control.

The territory was divided into British Baluchistan and foreign territories. The latter included native states—specifically Las Bela and Kalat (which included Kharan and Makran)—as well as tribal areas and directly administered districts. In some instances, the British government appointed specific officers to reside as political agents within a state to provide assistance. For example, in 1857, Major Henry Green was deputed to reside as a political agent with the Khan at Kalat to provide advice and help the Khan maintain control over turbulent tribes.

## Other Political Agents and Residents
The use of agents and residents extended beyond the Indian peninsula to other areas of British influence:

*   **Bagdad:** The British consul-general in Bagdad also served as the political agent to the Indian government. This position was of high status, second only to the British ambassador at Constantinople. The agent in Bagdad maintained a residency with an Indian post-office and a guard of sepoys.
*   **Burma:** Following military conflict, peace conditions stipulated that residents from each court be allowed, accompanied by an escort of fifty men.
*   **Ashanti:** In 1895, the British government dispatched a mission to King Prempeh of Ashanti, calling upon him to accept a British protectorate and receive a resident at Kumasi.
