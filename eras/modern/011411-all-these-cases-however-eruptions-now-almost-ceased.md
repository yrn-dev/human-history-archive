# Volcanic Activity and Geological Transitions

## Continental Volcanism in Asia
The geological history of the Asian continent reveals three distinct regions that have experienced significant volcanic eruptions during or since the Cretaceous period. In the southern region, volcanic activity is evidenced by the "harras" lavas of Arabia and the extensive flows of the Deccan Trap in India. The central folded belt contains great volcanoes in Persia, Armenia, Baluchistan, and Asia Minor, though these are now mostly extinct. Additionally, Burma possesses at least one extinct volcano.

In the northern unfolded region, basic lava flows lie directly upon Ordovician and Cambrian beds in Siberia, with some being of Tertiary age. Smaller flows of a similar nature are found in northern China, Korea, and Manchuria. While eruptions in these specific continental regions have almost ceased, active volcanoes continue to exist on the islands off the south eastern and eastern coasts.

## Island Archipelagos and Volcanic Formations
Volcanic activity is a defining characteristic of various island groups, often resulting in high, precipitous coastlines and undulating outlines. In one such archipelago, the islands rise into peaks ranging from 350 ft. at Corvo to 7,612 ft. at Pico. The primary erupted materials in this region are scoria and basalt. 

Activity in this archipelago has been concentrated largely on St Michael's, which suffered successive eruptions and earthquakes in 1630, 1652, 1656, 1755, and 1852. Other notable events include a 1522 convulsion that buried the capital town of Villa Franca and its 6,000 inhabitants, a 1572 eruption in Pico, and numerous outbursts in St George in 1580. Subterranean eruptions have also occurred (such as in 1638, 1720, 1811, and 1867), occasionally creating temporary islands. A notable example was the island of Sabrina, thrown up in June 1811 near the western extremity of St Michael's. In contrast, islands such as St Mary, Corvo, Graciosa, and Flores have remained largely or entirely exempt from such activity, while Fayal experienced only one eruption in 1672.

## The Case of Krakatoa
Krakatoa, located in the Sunda Strait between Sumatra and Java, is known for one of the most stupendous eruptions ever recorded in 1883. Originally, a large volcano existed in the center of the tract, but a prehistoric explosion blew the mountain away, leaving only a broken ring of islands consisting of the outer base. Over time, new small cones formed within this crater ring, the most significant reaching 2,623 ft. above sea level.

Following a period of relative obscurity and a suspected eruption in 1680, earthquakes returned to the Sunda Strait around 1877. In May 1883, Krakatoa erupted, initially discharging dust and pumice. This culminated in a series of paroxysmal explosions between August 26 and August 28, 1883. The four most violent explosions occurred on the morning of the 27th, destroying the northern and lower portions of the island and the northern part of the Rakata cone, leaving a vertical cliff that exposed the volcano's inner structure.

## Regional Volcanic Belts and Global Patterns
In other regions, such as a belt of volcanic rocks covering over 150,000 sq. m., activity is divided into older and newer sets. The older group featured fissure eruptions that created the Laikipia and Kapte plateaus, with eruptions also occurring at Chibcharagnani, Elgon, Kimawenzi, and Kenya. These outbursts are post-Jurassic and likely extended through the Cretaceous into early Tertiary times. This was followed by eruptions of the larger rift-valley volcanoes and Kibo, the latter producing leucite and nepheline basanite lavas rich in soda felspars. More recent activity includes the Kyulu mountains, Donyo Longonot, and various rift-valley craters. While some have recently become extinct or are dormant, Donyo Buru still emits steam, and Mount Teleki was in eruption at the close of the 19th century.

On a global scale, the earth's history is characterized by phases of internal activity where accumulated upthrusts break out in lines of upheaval and volcanic eruption. These periods rearrange continental and mountain outlines, increasing mountain height and sea depth. Such active phases are followed by vast ages of comparative quiescence, where rain, river, and frost wear down heights and fill sea bottoms with silt.
