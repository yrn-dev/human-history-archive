# Lob, Ehr' und Weisheit (Blessing, Glory, and Wisdom)

The terms "blessing," "glory," and "wisdom" appear across various contexts within the archive, ranging from musical misattributions and philological studies to sacred texts and geographical explorations.

## Musical Misattribution
In the study of the works of J.S. Bach, the 8-part motet titled *Lob, Ehr' und Weisheit* (translated as "blessing and glory and wisdom") serves as a notable example of archival error. While the piece was once associated with Bach, a closer examination revealed it to be of poor quality. The attribution was eventually proven absurd when it was discovered that the actual composer was a contemporary of Bach named Wagner. This case highlights the difficulties editors of the *Bach-Gesellschaft* faced in identifying Bach's autographs, as the composer typically only placed his name on the outside sheet of his manuscripts.

## Philology and Indian Wisdom
In the field of German philology, the concept of wisdom is linked to the study of Eastern languages. Franz Bopp (1791–1867), a German philologist, was influenced by the lectures of Karl J. Windischmann, who, along with G.F. Creuzer, J.J. Gorres, and the Schlegel brothers, expressed enthusiasm for Indian philosophy and wisdom. Specifically, Fr. Schlegel's 1808 book, *Uber die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier*, exerted a powerful influence on German historians and philosophers, stimulating Bopp's interest in Sanskrit, the sacred language of the Hindus.

## Geographical Contexts of "Lob"
The term "Lob" also appears in geographical records concerning Central Asia, specifically in relation to the region of Lob-nor. 

### Etymology and Description
According to traditions cited by Prince Henri d’Orléans, "Lob" is a local name meaning "wild animals," assigned to the country when it was crossed by Kalmuk caravans. The Mongol word "Nor" (meaning Great Lake) was added to this name. However, travelers noted that "Lob-nor" does not refer strictly to a lake, but to the entire marshy region watered by the Tarim, extending from the village of Lob to the end of the river. The village of Lob itself was described by Bonvalot in 1889 as being home to miserable, hungry inhabitants who sold smoked fish and ducks.

### Exploration and Debate
The identification of the "true" Lob-nor has been a subject of debate among explorers:
*   **Sven Hedin:** Discovered a mass of water in a valley marked by Chinese topographers, consisting of Avullu Kul, Kara Kul, Tayek Kul, and Arka Kul. He argued that the lake found by Prjevalsky was of recent formation.
*   **Prjevalsky:** Found an immense lake in 1885, though subsequent visitors, such as Prince Henri d’Orléans, found it greatly reduced in size.
*   **N. M. Prjevalsky's Teacher:** Identified the Kara-Koshun-Kul as the historical and true Lob-nor of Chinese geographers.
*   **Marco Polo:** It is noted that Marco Polo did not mention the Lob-nor; some scholars suggest this is because he did not see it, instead traveling between various lakes on the route from Charchan to Shachau.
