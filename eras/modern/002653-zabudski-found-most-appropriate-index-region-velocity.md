# Colonel Zabudski and the Index m

Colonel Zabudski conducted an analysis of the Krupp experiments to determine the most appropriate index $m$ within specific regions of velocity. This index is utilized in contexts where it is possible to represent $p$ with sufficient accuracy using an empirical formula consisting of a single power of velocity, expressed as $v^m$.

## Application in Ballistics
The determination of the index $m$ allows for the effect of integration to replace the summation processes used in ballistic calculations. This is part of a broader effort to gain accurate experimental knowledge of air resistance to projectiles at all velocities useful in artillery. 

While ancient theories, such as those attributed to Galileo, ignored air resistance and viewed trajectories as parabolas, such theories lack practical value for gunnery. Similarly, theoretical assumptions by Euler and Newton—which suggested resistance varies as a simple power of velocity, such as the quadratic (square) or cubic (cube) laws—are noted as being of great analytical complexity. These are generally used only for provisional extrapolation at high or low velocities until further experiments can be conducted.

## Velocity Regions and Values of m
Based on the analysis of the Krupp experiments, Colonel Zabudski identified specific values for the index $m$ corresponding to different velocities ($v$). These values are associated with $gp$, which is denoted as $f(v)$, $v^m/k$, or its equivalent $Cr$ (where $r$ represents retardation). 

The following values were identified:
*   At a velocity of 3600, $m$ is 1.55.
*   At a velocity of 2600, $m$ is 1.7.
*   At a velocity of 1800, $m$ is 2.
*   At a velocity of 1370, $m$ is 3.
*   At a velocity of 1230, $m$ is 5.
*   At a velocity of 970, $m$ is 3.
*   At a velocity of 790, $m$ is 2.

These figures were later converted from kilogramme-metre to pound-foot units by Colonel Ingalls for use in calculating an extended ballistic table.

## Mathematical Integration
In each region of velocity, calculations can be carried out using specific formulae involving the index $m$ and the constant $k$:
*   $T(V) - T(v) = k \int_{v}^{V} v^{-m} dv$
*   $S(V) - S(v) = k \int_{v}^{V} v^{m+1} dv$
*   $I(V) - I(v) = gk \int_{v}^{V} v^{-m-1} dv$

For practical arithmetical operations, the use of a slide rule is recommended to achieve the accuracy obtainable in practice.
