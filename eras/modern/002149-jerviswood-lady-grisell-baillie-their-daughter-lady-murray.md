# Jerviswood and Lady Grisell Baillie

The history of the Baillie family of Jerviswood is characterized by political turmoil, persecution under the Stuart administration in Scotland, and a subsequent legacy of literary and musical contributions. Central to this history are Robert Baillie of Jerviswood, his son George, and George's wife, Lady Grizel Baillie.

## Robert Baillie of Jerviswood
Robert Baillie, the son of George Baillie of St. John's Kirk in Lanarkshire, was a Scottish conspirator who came into conflict with the Scottish government. In June 1676, Baillie rescued his brother-in-law, a Presbyterian minister named Kirkton, who had been illegally confined in a house by an informer named Carstairs. As a result, Baillie was imprisoned for four months and fined £500, half of which he paid to Carstairs to secure his liberation.

By 1683, distressed by the state of Scotland, Baillie considered emigrating to South Carolina, though this plan failed. That same year, he traveled to London to communicate with Russell, Monmouth, and their party to seek redress. Following the discovery of the Rye House Plot, Baillie was arrested. During questioning by the king, he denied knowledge of the conspiracy but admitted he had been consulted regarding a potential insurrection in Scotland. Despite a lack of evidence connecting him to the plot, he was kept in close confinement and fined £6,000. On December 23, 1684, while in a languishing state, he was charged with treason. He was found guilty the following day and hanged at the market cross in Edinburgh on December 24, 1684. His treatment is remembered as one of the worst crimes of the Stuart administration in Scotland. Bishop Burnet, a cousin of Baillie, described him as a man of great virtue, piety, and learning in mathematics, languages, and the law, holding Presbyterian principles.

## George Baillie and Lady Grizel Baillie
Robert Baillie left a son, George, who sought refuge in Holland. George eventually returned to Scotland with William III and was restored to his estates. In 1692, George married Lady Grizel Baillie.

Lady Grizel (1665–1746) was a Scottish song-writer and the eldest daughter of Sir Patrick Hume (or Home) of Polwarth, who later became the earl of Marchmont. Born on December 25, 1665, at Redbraes Castle in Berwickshire, Grizel was involved in the political struggles of her father's era. At age twelve, she carried letters to Robert Baillie of Jerviswood while he was imprisoned. Because of his friendship with Baillie, Sir Patrick Hume became a suspected man, leading king's troops to occupy Redbraes Castle. During a period of hiding in a churchyard, Grizel supplied her father with food. Following the execution of Robert Baillie in 1684, Sir Patrick fled to Holland, where his family later joined him. They returned to Scotland during the Revolution.

## Literary and Musical Legacy
Lady Grizel Baillie is recognized for her contributions to song and prose. Some of her songs were included in Allan Ramsay's *Tea-Table Miscellany*, and her most famous work, "And werena my heart light I wad dee," first appeared in *Orpheus Caledonius* in 1725.

The couple had two daughters: Rachel, Lady Binning, and Grizel, who married Sir Alexander Murray of Stanhope. Lady Murray of Stanhope preserved a manuscript of her mother's prose and verse. In 1822, Lady Murray published *Memoirs of the Lives and Characters of the Right Hon. George Baillie of Jerviswood and Lady Grisell Baillie*. Additionally, George Baillie's *Correspondence* (covering 1702–1708) was edited by Lord Minto for the Bannatyne Club in 1842. The legacy of Lady Grizel was further extended into poetry through Joanna Baillie's *Metrical Legends of Exalted Character*, which includes "The Legend of Lady Grizelda Baillie."
