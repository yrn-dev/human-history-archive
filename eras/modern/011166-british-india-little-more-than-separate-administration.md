# British India

British India was a vast territory characterized by a diverse collection of states acquired through various methods. Geographically, northern India is defined by the great rivers—the Indus, the Ganges, and the Brahmaputra—which derive their waters from the Tibetan mountain mass, with the northern water-parting located in central Tibet. The region is densely populated, with most land highly cultivated and featuring numerous large cities. Forests are primarily restricted to mountain flanks where rainfall is most abundant. The climate generally supports the population's needs without severe labor, though extremes of heat and drought make the land unsuitable for pasture, leading the population to rely on commerce and soil cultivation in settled towns and villages.

The island of Ceylon was part of this broader context, distinguished from British India primarily by its separate administration and the Buddhistic religion of its people. Ceylon's highest point reaches approximately 9,000 feet, with densely forested mountain slopes and lower levels similar in cultivation and climate to the Malay peninsula.

## Administrative Structure

For administrative purposes, the Indian empire was divided into four minor commissionerships and nine great provinces. These provinces were presided over by various officials: two governors (Madras and Bombay), five lieutenant-governors (Burma, the Punjab, United Provinces [Agra and Oudh], Eastern Bengal and Assam, and Bengal), a chief commissioner (the Central Provinces), and an agent to the governor-general (the N.-W. Frontier Province). Each of the four minor commissionerships was led by a chief commissioner.

The supreme executive authority within India was vested in the viceroy in council, which included the commander-in-chief and six ordinary members. For legislative functions, the governor-general's council included fifteen crown-nominated members. This body held the power, under certain restrictions, to create laws for British India, for native Indian subjects of the crown globally, and for British subjects within native states. In England, the administration was managed by a secretary of state for India and a council of at least ten members, with the secretary in council controlling revenue expenditure.

## Historical Transitions and Governance

The British became the successors to the Mogul emperors, adopting the title *Kaisar-i-Hind* for the British monarch. Much of the consolidation and organization established by the Mogul emperor Akbar—who is credited with giving India's ruling classes a sense of common interest and preventing the region from remaining a "rag-bag of incoherent states"—was taken over and continued by the British.

Between 1800 and 1858, the rule of the British company initially interfered little with the domestic life of Indian states, and British officials often became integrated into the local system. However, the mechanical revolution altered this dynamic. The introduction of the railway and telegraph, along with easier travel to Europe, meant officials ceased to be "Indianized" and remained conspicuously foreign. This period saw increased interference in Indian customs, the activity of Christian missions, and the "Europeanization" of young men in towns.

## Frontier Relations and Afghanistan

The north-west frontier was a point of critical strategic concern, particularly regarding the advance of Russia in Central Asia. In 1875, Lord Lytton was nominated governor-general of India. Following efforts to conciliate the amir of Afghanistan, Shere Ali, war broke out in November 1878 after a Russian agent was established at Kabul and a British mission was denied entry. 

Following the flight and death of Shere Ali, the treaty of Gandamak (May 1879) recognized Yakub Khan as amir. Under this treaty, the Khyber and other passes, as well as the districts of Sibi, Pishin, and Kuram, were assigned to British administration. A permanent British Resident was established at Kabul. To maintain stability, a belt of independent or neutral territory was established between British India and Afghanistan, including Bajour, Swat, and part of the Mohmand territory. Baluchistan also served as an intervening state between the two powers.

## Social Unrest and the British Officialdom

By the early 20th century, Indian life moved forward with an increase in educated people influenced by Western ideas and a growing service of newspapers. This led to an increasing sense of common grievance against the government. In contrast, the British official system was described as unimaginative and inflexible. The British military caste, in particular, was noted for intellectual stagnation.

This tension resulted in "spasmodic violence" by military men apprehensive of ridicule from a more educated Indian population. This culminated in an "official's Terror," including floggings, humiliating outrages, and the massacre of an unarmed political gathering at Amritzar, where nearly two thousand people were killed or wounded. These events were brought to the British public's attention by the Hunter Commission of 1919, causing dismay among liberal-minded Englishmen.

## International Standing

In the context of the League of Nations established by the Covenant of April 28, 1919, India appeared as a separate sovereign state alongside the four dominions (Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa). However, the Indian representative was a British nominee.
