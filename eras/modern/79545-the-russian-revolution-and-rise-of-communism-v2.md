# The Silent Engine: The Role of the Peasantry in the Russian Revolution

While most historical narratives of the 1917 Russian Revolution focus on the urban proletariat, the intellectual debates of the Bolsheviks, and the collapse of the Romanov dynasty in Petrograd, the revolution was not merely a city-born phenomenon. To understand the rise of communism in Russia, one must look beyond the factories and into the vast, agrarian heartland. The peasantry, comprising over 80% of the population, provided the essential—though often volatile—foundation upon which the Soviet state was built.

For the peasants, the revolution was not initially about the abstract theories of Marxism or the establishment of a "dictatorship of the proletariat." Instead, it was a visceral struggle for *zemlya* (land). The legacy of serfdom, abolished only in 1861, had left a deep-seated resentment toward the landed gentry and a desperate hunger for land ownership.

## The Land Decree and the Tactical Alliance
Vladimir Lenin recognized a critical flaw in orthodox Marxism: Russia lacked a massive industrial working class. To seize power, the Bolsheviks performed a strategic pivot, adopting the Socialist Revolutionary (SR) party's platform of land redistribution. 

Upon taking power in October 1917, the Bolsheviks issued the "Decree on Land," which effectively legalized the spontaneous seizures of estates already being carried out by peasants. This was not a move born of ideological purity—Marxism envisioned collective farming—but a tactical necessity to neutralize the peasantry and prevent them from siding with the White Army during the ensuing Civil War.

## From Cooperation to Coercion
The honeymoon between the urban communists and the rural peasants was short-lived. During the Russian Civil War, the Bolsheviks implemented "War Communism," which included *prodrazvyorstka* (forced grain requisitioning). Armed detachments were sent into villages to seize crops to feed the starving cities and the Red Army.

This led to widespread peasant uprisings, most notably the Tambov Rebellion (1920–1921). The state's violent suppression of these revolts highlighted a fundamental tension: the communist dream of a classless society required the total eradication of the "Kulaks" (wealthier peasants) and the forced collectivization of land. This tension would later culminate in the catastrophic famines of the 1930s under Stalin.

## Scholarly Perspectives
Historians continue to debate the agency of the peasantry. Some argue that the peasants were "passive" actors who were simply manipulated by Bolshevik rhetoric. Others suggest that the peasants were "rational actors" who supported the revolution only as long as it served their immediate goal of land ownership, viewing the Soviet state as just another iteration of the imperial bureaucracy.

### Key Facts
* **Demographics:** Approximately 80-85% of the Russian Empire's population were peasants in 1917.
* **The Decree on Land:** Issued in October 1917, it abolished private ownership of land and handed it over to those who worked it.
* **War Communism:** The policy of forced requisitioning that alienated the rural population between 1918 and 1921.
* **The NEP:** The New Economic Policy (1921), introduced by Lenin to appease peasants by allowing them to sell surplus grain on the open market.
* **Collectivization:** The later Stalinist policy of merging individual farms into state-run *kolkhozy*.

### Did You Know?
* **The Green Armies:** During the Civil War, "Green Armies" emerged—peasant militias who fought against both the Red (Bolshevik) and White (Tsarist/Liberal) forces to protect their local villages.
* **The SR Party:** The Socialist Revolutionaries, who focused on peasant rights, were actually more popular than the Bolsheviks in the 1917 Constituent Assembly elections.
* **Village Soviets:** In the early days of the revolution, many peasants formed their own local councils (Soviets) to manage land distribution independently of the central government in Petrograd.
