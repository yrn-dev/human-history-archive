# Japan and the Europeo-Asiatic Group

## Biological Classifications
Japan is categorized as part of the Europeo-Asiatic group. This classification is evident across various biological domains, particularly in the distribution of insects and birds. In the study of Coleopterous insects, nearly one-third of those known to inhabit east Siberia are also found in western Europe. While Indo-Malayan types are generally encountered south of 30 deg. N., the European forms extend to this latitude, with Japan belonging to the Europeo-Asiatic group. These northern forms generally extend from the Levant to the Caspian and along the south coast of the Mediterranean up to the border of the great desert.

The ornithology of northern Asia shows an even closer alliance with Europe than the mammal fauna. Approximately three-fourths of the well-known bird species of Europe extend through Siberia into the islands of the Japanese empire. This region is characterized by a lack of tropical forms and a significant development of groups suited to temperate and cold regions.

## Mammalian Fauna
The mammals of northern Asia, including Japan, are largely shared with Europe, though the Palaearctic fauna in Asia is enriched by additional species. These groups are mostly restricted to temperate and cold regions. Consequently, certain animals are poorly represented; for instance, the Quadrumana (monkeys) are nearly unrepresented, with only a single species occurring in Japan. Similarly, the frugivorous division of insectivorous bats is represented by only one species in Japan.

Other numerous mammals in the region include Carnivora—specifically foxes, wolves, weasels, and bears—as well as various forms of hedgehogs, shrews, and moles from the Insectivora order. Rodents such as hares, mice, and squirrels are also well represented.

## Botanical Relations
The flora of north China is akin to that of Japan. This botanical group shows a manifest relation to the neighboring American continent, with many temperate forms extending from there to the Himalaya, reaching almost as far as Kashmir. In contrast, the number of species common to northern Asia, Japan, and southern China is small.

## Geological and Geographical Connections
Geological evidence indicates that the islands of Japan were once part of the Asiatic continent. This is supported by the discovery of Tertiary mammals in gravel, including various unnamed varieties of horse, ox, bison, deer, bear, rhinoceros, and whale, as well as *elephas primigenius*, *elephas Namadicus*, and *stegodon Clifti*.

Japan possesses six primary routes of communication with the Asian continent:
*   **North:** One route runs from the northwest of Yezo across the Soya strait to Saghalien and the Amur region of Manchuria; a secondary route runs from the northeast of Yezo through the Kuriles to Kamchatka.
*   **Southwest:** One route leads from the northwest of Kyushu via Tsushima and Iki to southeast Korea; another runs from the south of the Izumo promontory.
*   **South:** One route extends from the southwest of Kyushu via the Goto Islands to southeastern China; the final route runs from the south of Kyushu through the Philippines, Formosa, and the Ryukyu Islands to Polynesia and Malaysia.

These avenues likely provided access for immigrants from China, Siberia, Polynesia, and Malaysia.

## Prehistoric and Political History
During the New Stone Age, dolmen-builders occupied Japan and Korea. It is suggested that from these advanced Asiatic lands, they may have spread across the Polynesian world.

In more recent history, the Ryukyu Islands became a point of contention between Japan and China. Although Ryukyu had sent gift-bearing envoys to Peking, the islands were subdued by Satsuma and remained part of the Satsuma fief for two centuries. Following a dispute over the islands, a conference in Peking was held in 1880. While a compromise was suggested to divide the islands between the two nations, China eventually drew back from the convention, resulting in Japan retaining the islands.
