# Russell, Crimes and Misdemeanours; Stephen, Criminal Law

The works of Russell and Sir James Stephen are cited as primary authorities in the study of English criminal law, particularly regarding the classification of crimes, the history of legal codes, and the definitions of specific offences. Their texts serve as foundational references for understanding the distinctions between various criminal acts and the evolution of statutory law.

## Legal Codification and Homicide
Significant effort has been expended by jurists to create scientific classifications for unlawful homicide to properly define punishable cases and their appropriate penalties. While most states—including Japan and France—have established statutory definitions for these crimes, the United Kingdom has not. 

Sir James Stephen played a central role in attempts to codify English law. In 1878, a code bill based on his labors was submitted to Parliament. Subsequently, in 1879, a draft code was prepared by Barry, Lush, and Blackburn; this draft was a revision of Stephen's digest of criminal law and was founded upon his work. 

Regarding the specific application of homicide laws, Stephen's *Commentaries* (Book vi. c. iv.) emphasizes the strict requirements for justifiable homicide during the execution of a criminal. Stephen notes that killing even the "greatest of malefactors" is considered murder if it is done deliberately, extrajudicially, and uncompelled. Furthermore, if a judge not authorized by lawful commission gives a judgment of death and the execution is carried out, that judge is guilty of murder. The execution must be performed by the proper officer or deputy, and the specific method of death judicially condemned must be strictly pursued; any deviation is classified as murder.

## Definitions of Property and Peace Crimes
The archive's sources utilize the works of Russell and Stephen to distinguish between various offences against property and public order.

### Housebreaking and Burglary
Under the Larceny Act 1861, English law distinguishes housebreaking from burglary. Housebreaking does not require the act to be committed in a dwelling-house or at night; it may occur in a counting-house, warehouse, shop, or school-house. While every burglary involves housebreaking, not every instance of housebreaking is burglary. Penalties for housebreaking include imprisonment for up to two years (with or without hard labour) or penal servitude ranging from three to fourteen years. In the United States, many state statutes have modified the common-law definition of burglary to encompass what England defines as housebreaking, with maximum punishments not exceeding twenty years of imprisonment.

### Common Barratry
Common barratry is defined in English criminal law as the act of constantly stirring up and inciting quarrels that disturb the peace, whether in courts or other locations. This is a cumulative offence, meaning at least three commissions of the act must be proven. It is punishable by imprisonment and fine. A 1726 statute further stipulated that any person of the legal profession found guilty of common barratry was disabled from practicing law in the future. Although indictments for this offence were rare for nearly two centuries, a case occurred at the Guildford summer assizes in 1889 (*R. v. Bellgrove*), though the charge was not proceeded upon because the defendant was convicted of another offence.

## Influence on International Law
The influence of English criminal law and the work of Sir James Stephen extended to the Indian penal code. Following a commission appointed under the India Act 1833 and presided over by Macaulay, a penal code was developed. Sir James Stephen described the Indian penal code as a "concise and even beautiful" reproduction of the spirit of English law, comparing it to a "manufactured article ready for use" when compared to the raw materials of English criminal law. He further asserted that it was simpler and better expressed than Livingston's code for Louisiana and superior to the German code of 1871 or the French *code pénal*.

## Other Legal Applications
The works of Stephen and Russell are also referenced in the context of other criminal matters:
* **Bigamy:** In the United States, bigamy law is largely based on the English statute of 1604, though US states generally substituted fines and imprisonment for the felony classification.
* **Incest:** While the Punishment of Incest Act 1908 eventually criminalized specific sexual relations in England, Stephen's *History of Criminal Law* is cited as an authority on the broader legal treatment of the subject.
