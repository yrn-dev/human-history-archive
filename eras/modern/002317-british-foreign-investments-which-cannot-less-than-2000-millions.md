# British Foreign Investments

## Overview of British Trade and Capital
The United Kingdom is characterized by a largely preponderating foreign trade. The nation's annual exports and imports, when excluding bullion, exceed 800 millions sterling. Additionally, bullion accounts for another 100 millions sterling in some years. Between the middle and the end of the 19th century, the United Kingdom experienced a gradual increase in its excess of imports, which rose from a small figure to 180 millions sterling annually.

## The "Adverse" Balance of Trade
The rise in the excess of imports led to popular discussions regarding an "adverse" balance of trade. These discussions were driven by a belief in many quarters that the nation was living on its capital. This perception prompted a new investigation into the subject to clarify the economic position of the country.

## Magnitude of Foreign Investments
The investigation into the balance of trade sought to highlight the credits the United Kingdom is entitled to in its capacity as a commission merchant and shipowner. Central to this economic picture is the scale of British foreign investments. These investments are estimated to be no less than 2000 millions sterling, a sum that must generate an enormous annual income for the country.

## International Economic Comparisons
The United Kingdom is grouped with other creditor countries, including Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Holland, Belgium, Germany, and France. While these nations are in a similar economic condition, their foreign trade does not operate on the same scale as that of the United Kingdom.

In contrast, other nations have historically occupied the position of borrowers. In the first decade of the 20th century, the United States was a conspicuous example of a borrower, maintaining a large excess of exports, although signs of a change in this direction were appearing. New countries, including the South American countries, Australia, and Canada, generally resemble the position of the United States.
