# Siege of Kumasi

The Siege of Kumasi was a central event of a serious rebellion that broke out in 1900 against British rule in the Ashanti confederation. The conflict involved the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes, while other tribes within the confederation remained loyal to the British.

## Origins of the Rebellion
Following the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was appointed to the Ashanti throne. Instead, the affairs of the Kumasi were administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart. During this period, a fort was constructed in Kumasi and garrisoned by the Gold Coast constabulary. While the Kumasi chiefs appeared submissive, they remained unreconciled to British authority and sought to erase the dishonor of having allowed Prempeh to be deported without a fight.

Tensions escalated on March 28, 1900, during a public palaver. Sir F. Hodgson, the governor of the Gold Coast, announced that the Ashanti chiefs were required to pay the British government 4,000 oz. of gold annually. Additionally, he reproached the chiefs for hiding the golden stool, which had been concealed since 1896. Although a secret attempt to seize the stool had already failed in January of that year, the situation worsened three days after the palaver when Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa who had been sent specifically to discover the location of the stool.

## The Siege and Defense
Following the attack on the Hausa party, the Kumasi warriors threatened the fort at Kumasi. Those seeking refuge within the fort included Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, as well as the Basel missionaries, including Mr. and Mrs. Ramseyer. The Ramseyers had previously been imprisoned by King Kofi Karikari for four and a half years before returning to Kumasi.

The defenders urgently requested reinforcements. On April 18, 100 members of the Gold Coast constabulary arrived. A major attack by the Kumasi occurred on April 29, but the forces were repulsed. On that same day, 250 Lagos constabulary reached the city; however, they had been forced to fight their way to Kumasi and arrived with little ammunition. Further reinforcements arrived on May 15, when Major A. Morris brought 250 men from the British territory north of Ashanti. This brought the total garrison to 700 men. Among the 29 Europeans stationed in the fort were four women.

## Relief and Suppression of the Revolt
The rebellion was eventually crushed by a force led by Colonel Willcocks. His command was augmented by Ashanti levies and 3,368 natives, which included Sikhs from Central Africa and Yaos, supported by 35 British non-commissioned officers and 134 British officers.

The Kumasi forces suffered a complete defeat at Obassa on September 30. This victory led many rebel chiefs to surrender. The final two chiefs remaining in the field were captured on December 28, and peace was restored by the beginning of 1901. The total casualties for the campaign, including those who died of disease, numbered 1,007. British losses included nine officers killed in action, forty-three wounded, and six who died of disease. For his leadership, Colonel Willcocks was promoted and created a K.C.M.G.

## Aftermath and Infrastructure
Following the suppression of the revolt, measures were implemented to improve trade between Cape Coast and Kumasi, as some Ashanti trade had previously been diverted to the French port of Assini. Kumasi serves as the distributing center for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti. 

Communication and transport in the region were historically difficult due to dense forest land, estimated at fully 12,000 sq. m., where travel relied on tortuous paths too narrow for wheeled vehicles or palanquins borne by four carriers. To improve this, a 141 m. wide road was cut through the bush from Cape Coast to Kumasi. Additionally, a railway from Sekondi to Kumasi was completed in 1903, passing through the auriferous region. The city is also connected to the Northern Territories and coast towns via telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service.
