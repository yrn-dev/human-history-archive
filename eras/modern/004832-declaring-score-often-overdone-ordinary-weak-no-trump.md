# Declaring to the Score and No-Trump Declarations

In the game of bridge, the process of declaration involves selecting a trump suit or opting to play without one. The strategy for these declarations varies based on the strength of the hand, the current score, and the specific variety of bridge being played.

## Declarations to the Score
The current score can influence the frequency and risk level of specific declarations. When a player's score exceeds twenty, the dealer should make club declarations more frequently. The requirements for declaring spades change as the score increases: they should be declared with six cards at a score of twenty-six, and with five cards at a score of twenty-eight.

In situations where a player is significantly behind in the score, they may opt for a risky no-trump declaration. An example of such a risky hand is one with an established suit of seven or eight cards that lacks a card of entry. However, the practice of declaring to the score is often overdone. An ordinary weak no-trump declaration typically offers small chances of winning three by tricks, unless the dummy holds a no-trump hand.

## General Declaration Strategies
The dealer and their partner select the trump suit without consultation, with the dealer holding the first option. They may also choose to play without a trump suit. When the dealer's partner must make a declaration because the call has been passed, they must assume the dealer possesses less than average strength regarding card rank and potentially more black cards. Consequently, the partner should be hesitant to make a high declaration if a sound declaration of less value is possible.

However, the partner may be forced to declare on less strength if the only alternative is a short suit of spades. For instance, a hand with two spades, three clubs, and several diamonds may prefer a diamond declaration over spades, as the chances favor the dealer with five trumps but are against them with only two. In cases of extreme weakness, five clubs or six diamonds are preferable to declaring spades with two or fewer.

## Suit-Specific Declaration Guidelines
Different suits require different strengths for declaration:
*   **Hearts:** Six hearts should be made trumps. Five hearts should be declared if they include two honours, unless the hand is very weak. If the hand is nearly strong enough for no trumps, or is very irregular (such as missing one suit or having five of a black suit), four hearts with three honours or five hearts with one honour should be declared.
*   **Diamonds:** Six diamonds with one honour, five with three honours, or four all honours should be declared. Weaker diamonds are declared if suits are irregular, particularly if the hand is blank in hearts.
*   **Clubs:** Six clubs with three honours or five with four honours should be declared.
*   **Spades:** These are primarily declared with weak hands. A suit of five spades with only a king may be declared as a defensive measure. With nothing above a ten, two or three spades can be declared, though five clubs or six red cards are often less expensive.

## Calculating Hand Strength
To determine the strength of a hand, specific values are assigned to cards:
*   A knave is worth two tens.
*   A queen is worth two knaves.
*   A king is worth a queen and knave together.
*   An ace is worth a king and queen together.

The value of these cards is affected by their support. An ace loses value if it is sole, and a king unguarded is worth less than a guarded queen. A queen is not considered fully guarded unless accompanied by three additional cards; if it has only one small card, it is worth a guarded knave. An average hand contains an ace, king, queen, knave, and ten, or equivalent strength. Hands that vary from this by the value of a king or less are termed "featureless."

## Variations of Bridge
The rules of declaration differ across bridge varieties:
*   **Ordinary Bridge:** The dealer or partner selects the trump suit or no-trump. The partner of the dealer serves as the dummy, with their cards exposed after the first lead.
*   **Three-handed Bridge:** The dealer declares without seeing the dummy's cards. If the dealer passes, the right-hand adversary declares. In this version, declaration is forced: if there are three or four aces, no trumps (*sans atout*) must be declared; otherwise, the longest suit is chosen.
*   **Auction Bridge:** Introduced around 1907, this version allows any player to make a declaration. The dealer must first declare to win at least one "odd" trick. Other players may then pass, double, redouble, or overcall with a declaration of higher value.
