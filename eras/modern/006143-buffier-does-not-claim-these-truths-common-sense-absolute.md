# Claude Buffier and the Concept of Common Sense

Claude Buffier (1661–1737) was a French educationalist, historian, and philosopher. Born in Poland on May 25, 1661, to French parents, he moved to France shortly after birth and settled in Rouen. His early education took place at a Jesuit college in Rouen, and at the age of nineteen, he was received into the Jesuit order. Following a dispute with the archbishop, Buffier left Rouen and spent a brief period in Rome before returning to Paris to join the Jesuit college, where he remained for the rest of his life. He was regarded as an admirable teacher known for his ability to provide lucid expositions.

## The Search for the Ultimate Principle of Knowledge

In his best-known work, *Traité des vérités premières* (1717), Buffier sought to discover the ultimate principle of knowledge. He concluded that this principle is found in the sense of one's own existence and the internal feelings one perceives within oneself. While this position is substantially the same as that of Descartes, Buffier rejected the use of the *a priori* method.

## Definition and Role of Common Sense

Buffier argued that "common sense" is necessary to understand what exists distinct from the self. He defined common sense as a "disposition which nature has placed in all or most men." This disposition enables individuals, once they have reached the age and use of reason, to form a common and uniform judgment regarding objects that differ from the internal sentiment of their own perception. Crucially, Buffier noted that this judgment is not the result of any prior judgment.

The truths that this natural disposition obliges humans to accept cannot be disproved or proved. Buffier observed that these truths are followed in practice even by individuals who reject them from a speculative standpoint.

## Certainty and Probability

Buffier distinguished between different levels of certainty. He did not claim that the truths of "common sense" possess the absolute certainty found in the knowledge of one's own existence or in logical deductions derived from one's thoughts. Instead, these common-sense truths possess only the highest probability. Consequently, while a person who rejects these truths is not guilty of a contradiction, Buffier considered such a person to be a fool.

## Critical Reception and Legacy

Buffier's writings have been characterized by some as having an appearance of shallowness or a lack of metaphysical insight, a result of his aversion to scholastic refinements. Critics have noted that he failed to indicate the nature of the necessity and universality he attributed to his "eternal verities." Despite this, he is recognized as one of the earliest thinkers to recognize the psychological aspects of knowledge.
