# Armenia, Persia, and Baluchistan

## Geological Characteristics and Volcanism
The regions of Armenia, Persia, and Baluchistan are situated within the central folded belt of the Asian continent. This area has been the site of significant volcanic eruptions during or since the Cretaceous period, though the great volcanoes in these specific regions are now mostly extinct. A line of recent volcanic cones extends from western Baluchistan into eastern Persia. While the volcanoes in Baluchistan appear extinct, the Koh-i-Tafdan, located beyond the Persian frontier, continues to emit vapours at frequent intervals. The lavas and ashes forming these cones are primarily andesitic.

In Baluchistan, the mountain ranges consist mainly of Tertiary and Cretaceous beds, arranged in folds that run approximately parallel to the mountain ridges. This system forms a "festoon" hanging southwards between Mount Ararat and Peshawar. Within this region, Jurassic rocks contribute to the formation of the hills in British Baluchistan, and Triassic beds, including *Fusulina* limestone, are found along the south side of the upper Zhob. Most deposits, excluding later Tertiary beds, are marine. However, north-western Baluchistan shows an extensive development of conglomerates and volcanic tuffs in the lower Tertiary and upper Cretaceous, which are likely contemporaneous with the Deccan Traps of India. Additionally, syenite and diorite masses were intruded during the Tertiary period. Along the Makran coast, "mud volcanoes" occur, though it is uncertain if they are linked to true volcanic agencies.

## Geography and Demographics of Baluchistan
Baluchistan is a sparsely populated tract, larger in area than any Indian province except for Bengal, Madras, and Burma. It is divided into two distinct parts by a railway line running from the Indus to Quetta. 

The northern region is hedged between the plains of the Indus and Afghanistan. It features long ridges of picturesque highlands and the central ranges of the Suliman system, the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander. This area is characterized by a bracing climate with no great temperature extremes and occasionally alpine vegetation. Drainage in the north either flows toward the Gomal or bursts eastwards through limestone hills to the Indus.

The southern region is a square block of territory roughly 300 miles by 300 miles, primarily the home of the Baluch and the Brahui. This area encompasses diverse climates and various Asian races. The Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau feature drainage that cuts deep gorges and flows east into the plains of Sind. Other streams either run to the Arabian Sea north of Karachi or disappear into inland swamps.

## Persian Baluchistan
Persian Baluchistan is a province of Persia covering approximately 60,000 square miles along the northern shore of the Arabian Sea. It is bounded by Kerman to the west, Seistan and the central Persian desert to the north, and independent and British Baluchistan to the east. The landscape is dominated by barren mountains, waterless plains, and deserts of stony ground or moving sands. Its principal rivers, the Bampur and the Mashkid, flow away from the sea and are lost in depressions known as *hamuns*.

Administratively, it is a division of the province of Kerman, subdivided into twenty districts, including Bampur, Serhad, Dizek, Jalk, Sib, Irafshan, Magas, Serbaz, Lashar, Champ, Fannuj, Bazman, Aptar, Daman, Aprandagan, Asfehgeh, Surmij, Meskutan, Pushteh, and Makran (the country of the Ichthyophagi). Makran further includes the subdistricts of Bahu, Kucheh, Dasht, Bint, Geh, and Kasrkand. The total population is under 200,000. The province remained practically independent until Persian troops occupied Bampur in 1849, with supremacy over some eastern districts not recognized until 1872.

## Historical and Political Developments
In the 4th century, Armenia was a point of contention between Rome and Persia. King Diran of Armenia was betrayed and blinded by the Persian governor of Aderbidjan (or Atropatene) and his chamberlain in A.D. 337. This event led Constantine to declare war against the Persians. Despite the Persian king Shahpour attempting to seize Armenia, he was forced to peace terms following the resistance of the Armenian people, the failure at Nisibis, and a defeat at Oskha. Consequently, Diran and his son Arsaces were released; Arsaces subsequently became king of Armenia, though he pursued a vacillating policy between Persia and Rome, leading to renewed war in 345.

In more recent history, the period starting around 1880 saw extensive boundary-making and the definition of politico-geographical limits. Russia, Afghanistan, India, China, Persia, and Baluchistan all revised their borders. Systematic surveys of Baluchistan since 1880, along with the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1885 and the settlement of Afghanistan's southern boundaries, allowed for the creation of geographical maps extending from the Euphrates to the Indus and from the Indus to the Oxus. These efforts, supported by geographers such as Major Percy M. Sykes in Persia, helped clarify the ancient connections between the east and west and reopened old trade routes between India and Seistan.
