# The Great Mountain Mass of Asia

The great mass of mountain that constitutes a complete natural line of division across a large part of the Asian continent serves as a primary basis for understanding the principal countries of the region. This mountain mass includes the Himalaya and all elevated areas with a true physical connection to that range.

## Geography and Dimensions
The mountain system occupies an area approximately 2,000 miles in length, with a width varying between 100 and 500 miles. It is situated between 65 and 100 degrees east and between 28 and 35 degrees north. This range contains the highest peaks in the world, with summits that rise far above the line of perpetual snow along its entire length. Peaks reaching 20,000 feet are abundant throughout the chain, and numerous points exceed this elevation. Due to these extreme heights, few passes across the main ridges are located at an altitude lower than 15,000 or 16,000 feet above sea level.

## Tibet
The summit of this great mountain mass is occupied by Tibet, a country referred to by its inhabitants as *Bodyul* or *Bod*. Physically, Tibet is a rugged table-land that is narrow in proportion to its length. It is characterized by a succession of mountain ranges that generally follow the direction of the table-land's length and frequently rise into regions of perpetual snow. 

Between the flanks of these ranges are narrow, closely hemmed-in valleys with moderate inclinations. At intervals, these valleys open into wide plains. These areas are occupied either by rivers or by salt lakes that have no outflow. The boundaries of the Tibetan table-land are as follows:
*   **East:** The eastern termination is marked by a line of snowy mountains flanking China on the west, located about 103 degrees east and between the 27th and 35th parallels of latitude.
*   **West:** The table-land extends beyond the political limits of Tibet to approximately 70 degrees east. The ranges covered in perpetual snow extend as far west as Samarkand, after which they diminish rapidly in height and end in low hills north of Bokhara.

## Atmospheric and Biological Impact
The scale of the mountain mass significantly influences the physical conditions of Asia and the history of its population. Because the range penetrates the stratum of the atmosphere in contact with the earth's surface, it creates a complete separation of atmospheric conditions between its opposite flanks. 

The highest summits obstruct nearly three-fourths of the mass of air resting on this part of the earth, as well as nearly all of its moisture. The average height of the entire chain acts as an almost absolute barrier to one-half of the air and three-fourths of the moisture. Even the lower ranges produce atmospheric effects, as one-fourth of the air and one-half of the watery vapour lie below 9,000 feet.

Biologically, the range constitutes an almost impassable barrier to all forms of living creatures. This is due to the altitudes reaching levels where vegetable life ceases and the support of animal life becomes extremely difficult.

## Related Systems: The Arka-tagh
The Arka-tagh, also known to Russian geographers as the Przhevalsky Range, is a lofty parallel range that serves as the actual border-range of the Tibetan plateau. It is considered the true backbone of the Kuen-lun system. In Central Asia, the Arka-tagh is exceeded in elevation only by the Tang-la, which is likely an eastern wing of the Karakorum Mountains of the Pamirs region.

The Arka-tagh is a mountain system rather than a single range, featuring various configurations including sharply defined main crests with lower flanking ranges or numerous parallel crests of uniform altitude. It consists of four predominant ranges; the second from the north is likely the principal range, while the fourth is the highest. The crests of these ranges are characterized by broad, rounded, flattened domes rather than jagged peaks. The system is superficially composed of powdery, finely sifted disintegrated material and sand, with hard rock appearing only where it is excessively weathered.

Passes across the Arka-tagh ranges are situated at high altitudes:
*   **First range (north):** 15,675 to 18,300 feet.
*   **Second range:** 16,830 to 17,220 feet.
*   **Third range:** 16,660 to 17,880 feet.
*   **Fourth range:** 16,540 to 18,110 feet.

The valleys separating these ranges run at altitudes between 14,940 and 16,700 feet, typically only 500 to 1,000 feet lower than the accompanying crests.
