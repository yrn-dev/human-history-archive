# Sesamum, Poppy, Crotolaria, Cannabis, Cotton, and Indigo

## Cultivation in the Indian Region
The Indian region supports a diverse array of cultivated plants, combining harvests from both temperate and tropical zones. Among the miscellaneous crops grown in this region are *Sesamum*, poppy, *Crotolaria*, and *Cannabis*. Other significant agricultural products include cotton, indigo, and sugar, alongside various cereals such as wheat, barley, rice, and maize, as well as various millets (*Sorghum*, *Penicillaria*, *Panicum*, and *Eleusine*).

In the Indian region, agricultural operations are generally limited to the rainy months when water is available, although artificial irrigation allows some cereals to be grown in all seasons. In areas north of the tropic, winter cold permits the cultivation of European vegetables and cereals, with wheat typically sown in November and reaped in early April. Conversely, the summer heat and rain facilitate the growth of tropical cereals like rice, which are generally sown in early July and reaped in September or October. In southern India and similar Asian climates, the lack of a significant difference between winter and summer prevents the proper cultivation of barley or wheat.

## Regional Production and Distribution
Specific regions within and around India exhibit specialized agricultural outputs:

*   **Bengal:** The valley of the Ganges, divided between Bengal and Eastern Bengal and Assam, is one of the world's most fertile and densely populated tracts. It produces indigo, cotton, and "white fields of the opium-poppy," as well as tea, turmeric, and various spices and drugs.
*   **Kotah:** In this native state of the Rajputana agency, poppy and cotton are cultivated alongside wheat and a high-quality tobacco. Opium and grain serve as the chief articles of export.
*   **The Deccan and Khandesh:** In these areas, *Sesamum orientale* (til) is a principal oil-seed. Fibres produced here include cotton and *Crotalaria juncea* (sunn or tag), as well as Deccan hemp (*Hibiscus cannabinus*). In the Dharwar collectorate and southern Mahratta country, American cotton varieties have been introduced. In Khandesh, the superior Hinganghat variety of cotton has largely replaced the indigenous plant.
*   **Lower Burma:** In the Bassein district, the delta of the Irrawaddy is primarily used for rice, with sesamum, cotton, and tobacco grown as subsidiary crops.
*   **Hong Kong:** In the leased territory behind the Kowloon peninsula, the fertile alluvial soil of the valleys yields sesamum, indigo, and hemp, among other crops.

## Cotton and Indigo Processing
Cotton and indigo are central to both agriculture and industry, serving as primary materials for dyeing and textile manufacture.

### Indigo Dyeing Processes
Indigo is used in various "vats" to dye cotton and other vegetable fibres. The "lime and copperas vat" utilizes quicklime, ferrous sulphate, and finely ground indigo. The "zinc powder vat" employs zinc powder, lime, and indigo. A third method, the "hydrosulphite vat," uses hydrosulphite of soda, lime, and indigo; this method is widely used because it is easily prepared, produces little sediment, and can be used for both wool and cotton. 

For wool, a "woad vat" is typically used, which relies on fermentation rather than chemical agents. This vat contains indigo, lime, madder, bran, and woad, the latter of which is prepared from the fermented and partially dried leaves of the woad plant (*Isatis tinctoria*).

### Synthetic Alternatives
Artificial colouring matters have been developed within the indigo series. *Thioindigo red* is a paste that yields shades from dull pink to full claret red. *Indanthrene*, an anthracene derivative, is reduced by hydrosulphite of soda to create a blue vat. When applied to cotton, Indanthrene produces reddish-blue shades that are extremely fast and cannot be removed from the fibre by any known means. Other related vat colours include flavanthrene (yellow), viridanthrene (green), fuscanthrene (grey-brown), violanthrene (dull violet), and melanthrene (grey to black).

## Trade and Commerce
Cotton and indigo are significant commodities in international trade:
*   **Central Asia:** Raw cotton and silk are exported to Russia, while cottons and indigo are imported from India.
*   **Egypt:** The commerce of Cairo involves the transit of cotton and sugar from Upper Egypt, as well as indigo and shawls from Persia and India. In Bulak, factories were established by Mehemet Ali for the printing, weaving, and spinning of cotton.
*   **India:** In Kotah, cotton fabrics are woven locally, though they are being rapidly superseded by cheaper products from Manchester and Bombay.
