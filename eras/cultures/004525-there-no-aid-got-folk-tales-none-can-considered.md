# Folk-Tales and Traditional Literature

## Basque Literary Traditions
In the study of Basque culture, folk-tales are not considered a source of aid, as none can be identified as exclusively Basque. Furthermore, the existing literature is viewed as being altogether too modern. The earliest printed book in the Basque language is the *Linguae Vasconum Primitiae*, featuring the poems of Bernard d'Echepare, which is dated 1545. 

The standard for the Basque language is recognized as the Protestant translation of the New Testament. This work was printed at La Rochelle in 1571 and was produced by Jean de Licarrague under the auspices of Jeanne d'Albret. Other literary forms include *pastorales*, which are open-air dramas similar to the mysteries and moralities of the middle ages. While these are derived from French materials, certain elements of the *mise-en-scene* and the invariable introduction of a dancing chorus suggest the possibility of earlier traditions. However, no discovered manuscript predates the 18th century. Much of the remaining literature consists of translations and religious texts; successful attempts at a real Basque literature have only occurred recently.

## Breton Folk-Literature
In contrast to the Basque experience, Brittany is noted for maintaining a very "green" taste for marvelous legends. An entire folk-literature continues to flourish there, evidenced by a large number of collected folk-songs and folk-tales. The Bretons are described as conservative by nature, clinging to their local customs and religious superstitions. In some regions, paganism was not nominally abolished until the 17th century, and popular Christianity in this area has assimilated more from earlier creeds than perhaps anywhere else in Europe. Belief in the influence of fairies and witchcraft remains common.

## The Folk-Ballad
The folk-ballad, along with the carol and song, is attributed to immemorial antiquity. It is considered a mistake to view any preserved ballad as a purely communal product created by subconscious processes of a community. Similarly, it is a mistake to argue that ballads are essentially metrical romances in a state of decay. 

By the 15th century, communal processes of composition had ceased, and traditional techniques and formulae became conventional aids for individual singers. Despite this, these elements provided a vibrant framework for emotional expression, conveying "old, unhappy, far-off things," including sudden griefs, treachery, and battles. Most ballads are not considered works of art, as they often contain trivialities, dullness, and excrescences; however, their best moments possess a universal appeal.

## Religious Perspectives on Folk and Nations
Sacred texts provide various accounts of the interactions between messengers and the folk they were sent to. The Qur'an teaches that messengers were sent in succession, but whenever a messenger came to a people, they accused him of falsehood. Consequently, these people were made to follow one another in punishment and were made "bywords" or "stories."

Specific examples include:
* **The People of Noah:** Along with other Confederates of Evil, they denied their messengers and plotted to seize them, arguing by means of vanities to condemn the Truth.
* **Pharaoh and his Chiefs:** When Moses (Musa) and Aaron (Haroun) were sent to them with clear authority and signs, they behaved insolently and haughtily. They questioned why they should believe in two mortals like themselves, noting that the folk of those two men were subject to them.

The texts further teach that Allah does not change the condition or lot of a people unless they first change what is in their own hearts. When Allah intends evil or punishment for a folk, there is no protector besides Him and no way to avert it.

## Cultural and National Contrasts
Historical records highlight stark contrasts between national characters, such as those between the Athenians and their antagonists. The Athenians are characterized by innovation, swiftness in execution and conception, and a daring nature that sometimes exceeds their judgment. They are described as being "never at home," seeking to extend their acquisitions through absence, and viewing an unexecuted scheme as a positive loss.

Conversely, their antagonists are described as having a genius for keeping what they have, a total lack of invention, and a tendency to procrastinate. They are characterized by a mistrust of their own judgment and a fear that advancing would endanger what they have left behind.
