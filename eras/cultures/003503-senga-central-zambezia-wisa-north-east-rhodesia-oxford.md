# Senga of Central Zambezia and Wisa of North-East Rhodesia

The linguistic and regional landscape of South Central Africa is characterized by a diverse array of Bantu languages. Among these are the Senga language of Central Zambezia and the Wisa (or Bisa) family of North-East Rhodesia, both of which are situated within the broader geographical context of the Zambezi river basin and the protectorates of British Central Africa.

## The Senga Language and the Nyanja Group
The Senga language is a distinct but closely allied member of the Nyanja group, which is considered perhaps the most extensive group of cognate languages within the Bantu field. The Nyanja domain is primarily associated with the east and west shores of the southern half of Lake Nyasa and encompasses the valley of the Shire down to its junction with the Zambezi.

The Senga language specifically extends the Nyanja domain west of Lake Nyasa, reaching the Central Zambezi and the river Luangwa. The Luangwa regions are noted as being occupied by the Senga language. Other dialects within the Nyanja genus include Cinyanja of Eastern Nyasaland, Ci-peta and Ci-maravi of South-West Nyasaland, Ci-mananja of the Shire highlands, and Ci-sena or Ci-nyungwe of Sena and Tete. In the delta of the Zambezi, the very distinct language Ci-podzo is also found within the Nyanja genus.

## The Wisa (Bisa) Family
The Wisa, also referred to as the Bisa family, comprises a separate group of Bantu languages. This family includes the languages of Bausi, Lukinga, and Iramba, located in the southernmost projection of the Belgian Congo. 

Within the region of North-East Rhodesia, the Wisa family includes the dialects of Ilala and Lubisa. These are situated between the Luangwa river to the east and south, and the Chambezi river and Lake Bangweulu to the north. Additionally, some of the languages found along the course of the Upper Luapula river may also belong to this family.

## Regional Geography and Administration
The areas inhabited by these linguistic groups fall under the broad designation of British Central Africa. This term refers to British protectorates north of the Zambezi river, specifically a territory bounded by Lake Tanganyika to the north (8° 25' S.) and the river Shiré near the Zambezi to the south (17° 6' S.).

Administratively, the region was divided into several entities. While "British Central Africa" was officially confined in 1893 to the protectorate on the Shiré and Lake Nyasa (later renamed the Nyasaland Protectorate in 1907), the territories of North Eastern Rhodesia and North Western Rhodesia (Barotseland) were designated as divisions of the British South Africa Company's territory north of the Zambezi. The western boundary of this general region is defined by a line from the southern boundary of the Belgian Congo near the source of the Lualaba to the western source of the Luanga river, following the Luanga to its junction with the Luengwe-Kafukwe.

## Natural History of the Region
The environment of these regions, particularly the Luangwa valley, supports a rich variety of fauna. The giraffe is found in the Luangwa valley and the extreme north-east of the country. The region is home to the African elephant, as well as abundant leopards and lions. 

Among the antelopes found in these areas are the sable, the roan, and five species of waterbuck (Cobus), which include the Senga puku, the lechwe, the puku, Crawshay's waterbuck, and the common waterbuck. Other present species include the kudu, Livingstone's eland, and various duykers. The rivers and lakes are inhabited by hippopotamuses and the common Nile crocodile.

## Human Population and Linguistic Affinities
The indigenous population of British Central Africa is of the Negro type, belonging exclusively to the Bantu family linguistically. In the plateau country between the river Luapula, the south end of Lake Tanganyika, and the north end of Lake Nyasa, the Bantu dialects are of an archaic type. 

Linguistic connections exist between the languages of northern Nyasaland, North Eastern Rhodesia, and the Kafukwe basin with the Bantu languages of Uganda via dialects spoken to the north and west of Tanganyika. There is also a slight resemblance to Zulu-Kaffir. In the south, the Subiya language of southern Barotseland is noted as being one of the most archaic Bantu languages, potentially more so than Tonga.
