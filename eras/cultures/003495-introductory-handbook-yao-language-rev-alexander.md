# Introductory Handbook of the Yao Language

The *Introductory Handbook of the Yao Language* is a linguistic work authored by the Rev. Alexander Hetherwick. It is categorized among the important resources for students of the languages of the East African coast-lands, alongside the handbooks and vocabularies published by Bishop Steere.

## The Yao Language and its Distribution
The Yao language belongs to an extensive genus of languages. Its geographical range extends from the area just behind the coast of the Lindi settlements in German East Africa (referred to as *Ki-mwera*), moving south-westward across the Ruvuma river to the north-east shores of Lake Nyasa (referred to as *Ki-kese*). From there, the language extends back to the valley of the Lujenda-Ruvuma (*Cingindo*).

Various dialects of the Yao language are found further southwards, reaching the south-east corner of Lake Nyasa and the region east of the Shire river, situated between the Shire highlands, Mt. Mlanje, and Lake Nyasa. While the language has existed in these regions, it is only since the middle of the 19th century that the Yao language has conquered territory located to the south of Lake Nyasa. Within the domain of the Yao language, there remain colonies of people who speak Nyanja.

## Regional Linguistic Context
The Yao domain is bordered to the east by the Makua genus, a well-marked group of languages. The Makua genus is bounded to the north by the separate group of *Ki-makonde* and by the range of the Yao language within the Ruvuma valley. Together, these languages occupy a significant portion of Portuguese East Africa, excluding the watershed of Lake Nyasa.

The Makua language is likely divided into several dialects, including *I-lomwe*, *I-medo*, *I-tugulu*, and *Anguru*. Additionally, there are unnamed dialects in the Angoji coast-region, an area where strong colonies of Swahili-speaking people are settled. The southern portion of the Makua domain is occupied by the *Ci-cuambo* of the Quelimane district.

## Related Linguistic Scholarship
The study of the Yao language and other Bantu speeches is supported by a variety of comparative and descriptive works. These include:

*   **Comparative Grammars:** Dr. W. I. Bleek authored *A Comparative Grammar of South African Languages* (1869), though it remained unfinished. Father J. Torrend produced the *Comparative Grammar of the South African Bantu Languages* (1894), which focused on the languages of the Central Zambezi. Carl Meinhof contributed extensive information on the original structure of the Bantu mother-speech and languages in South-East and East Africa through his essays in the *Mittheilungen des Seminaers fuer Orientalische Sprachen* (1899) and his *Grundzuege einer vergleichenden Grammatik der Bantusprachen* (1906).
*   **Vocabularies and Dictionaries:** Illustrative vocabularies can be found in the works of Sir H. H. Johnston, specifically *The Kilimanjaro Expedition* (1884), *British Central Africa* (1898), and *The Uganda Protectorate* (1902-1904). Other significant works include the *Swahili-English Dictionary* by Dr. L. Krapf (1882), which contains information on the Swahili language as spoken on the East African coast, and the *An Encyclopaedic Dictionary of the Mananja (Mang'anja) Language of British Central Africa* by the Rev. D. C. Scott (1891).
*   **Other Bantu Studies:** The Congo groups of Bantu speech, as well as the Cameroon and Fernandian groups, were classified in *George Grenfell and the Congo* (1908). Further contributions include the *Dictionary of the Congo Language* by the Rev. Holman Bentley (1891), and Dr. Heli Chatelain's *Grammar of Kimbundu* and *The Folklore of Angola*.
