# Jewish Populations and Religious Distributions in Various Regions

The presence and demographic proportions of Jewish populations vary significantly across different historical and geographical contexts, ranging from concentrated urban centers to minority groups within larger imperial or national territories.

## Central and Eastern European Distributions

In the duchy and crownland of Bukovina, a territory of Austria bounded by Russia, Rumania, Transylvania, and Hungary, Jews constituted 13% of the population as of 1900, which totaled 729,921 inhabitants. In this region, commerce is primarily managed by Jews and Armenians, focusing on raw products such as wood, cattle, wool, and agricultural produce.

In the city of Budapest, the population grew rapidly during the 19th century, reaching a total of 732,322 (including civil and military populations) by 1900. Within this population, 168,985 were identified as Jews. Other religious groups in the city included 445,023 Roman Catholics, 67,319 Protestants of the Helvetic Confession, 38,811 Protestants of the Augsburg Confession, 5,806 Greek Catholics, and 4,422 Greek Orthodox, with the remainder belonging to various other creeds.

In Baden, the population reached 2,009,320 by 1905. The religious distribution in this area consisted of approximately 60% Roman Catholics, 37% Protestants, and 1.5% Jews, with the remainder consisting of other confessions.

## Middle Eastern and Mediterranean Populations

In Bagdad, where the population is estimated between 70,000 and 200,000, there are approximately 34,000 Jews. This group occupies its own quarter in the north-western part of the city. Notably, Jews in Bagdad are the only segment of the population provided with schools, including an apprentice school for industrial training and schools for boys (established in 1865 by the Alliance Israelite) and girls (established in 1899).

In Macedonia, the estimated population of 2,200,000 includes about 75,000 Jews. Under the Turkish system of classification, which uses religion rather than nationality, Jews are categorized under the *Musevi* millet. Other millets include the *Katolik* (Catholics), *Ermeni* (Gregorian Armenians), *Prodesdan* (Protestants), the *Rum* (Roman/Greek), and the nation of Islam.

Historically, Alexandria became the largest Jewish city in the world, containing more Jews than Jerusalem. This population included those brought from Palestine and those of the Diaspora or Dispersion—Jews who had not shared the Babylonian Captivity but remained in possession of the Bible. Due to their numbers and environment, they translated their scriptures into Greek.

## Historical Conflicts and Governance

In Cesarea, tensions arose between Jews and Syrians. The Jews claimed ownership of the city, asserting that its builder, King Herod, was a Jew. While the Syrians agreed that the builder was Jewish, they argued the city was Grecian because it contained temples and statues. This conflict escalated into armed struggle, as the elders of the Jews were unable to restrain those disposed to tumult. The Syrian faction received assistance from the Roman garrison, which was largely composed of Syrians. During this sedition, the governor Felix ordered soldiers to slay a great many Jews after they had beaten the Syrians, and their possessions were plundered. Eventually, eminent men from both sides were chosen as ambassadors to Nero to argue regarding their privileges.

In Alexandria, the Jewish community was represented in diplomatic efforts; for instance, Philo served as a principal ambassador from the Jews to Caius. Additionally, the city had an alabarch, or governor of the Jews, named Alexander, who was the brother of Philo.

## Cultural and Religious Characteristics

Historical accounts describe the posterity of Abraham as worshippers of the true God, noting that interpositions of Providence were more frequent among them than among idolatrous nations. It is noted that during certain periods, the Jews were not idolaters, and divine deliverances were provided to prevent a relapse into idolatry. In the context of the intellectual environment of Alexandria, the "moral fervour and monotheism of the Semitic Jew" existed alongside the criticism of the Aryan Greek and the Mediterranean traditions of the native Egyptians.
