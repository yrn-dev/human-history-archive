# Revival of the Shinto Cult

The revival of the Shinto cult in Japan was a process of differentiation and rehabilitation of the traditional faith, which had for centuries been intertwined with Buddhism and Confucianism. This movement sought to establish "Pure Shinto" by stripping away foreign influences and asserting the divine right of the Mikado.

## Early Integration and Ryobu Shinto
The original Shinto cult was centered on ancestor worship, where great families made offerings and supplications to their own *uji no Kami*. Following the rise of Buddhism, these traditional deities were not replaced but were instead supplemented and grafted onto a Buddhist stem. This led to the creation of *uji-tera* (uji temples), where the temple (*tera*) took precedence over the shrine (*yashiro*). When referred to together, they were known as *ji-sha*.

In the early ninth century, the Buddhist priest Kukai (also known as Kobo Daishi) formalized this synthesis into a system of doctrine called Ryobu Shinto, or mixed Shinto. The central theory of this creed was that Shinto deities were transmigrations or avatars (incarnations) of Buddhist divinities. Under this system, Buddhism became the national religion.

## The Doctrine of Yuiitsu-shinto
The first explicit attempt to differentiate Shinto from other creeds occurred during the reign of Go-Tsuchimikado (1465-1500). Urabe Kanetomo enunciated the doctrine of Yuiitsu-shinto (unique Shinto), which proposed a hierarchical relationship between three creeds: Shinto was the root, Confucianism represented the branches, and Buddhism was the fruit. This doctrine was further strengthened by Kanetomo's son, Yoshida.

Yoshida is regarded as a forerunner to later philosophers who advocated for the revival of "Pure Shinto." Despite these efforts, some Japanese annalists argue that the religious character of Shinto was derived solely from Buddhist suggestions, noting that the cult had historically lacked a great exponent or propagandist. Furthermore, Shinto never served as a vehicle for transmitting useful knowledge, nor did any Japanese sovereign ever abandon the Throne for the quiet of a Shinto shrine.

## The Tokugawa Period and the Reaction to Confucianism
During the era of the Tokugawa shoguns, Buddhism was supplanted among the educated classes by the moral philosophy of Confucius, as interpreted by figures such as Wang Yang-ming and Chutsz. The intolerance and enthusiasm displayed by the disciples of this Chinese philosophy eventually triggered a reaction.

This reaction culminated in the revival of Shinto. While Ieyasu, the founder of the Tokugawa shogunate, originally turned national thought into this new channel, he did not foresee that this shift would eventually contribute to the downfall of the Tokugawa house by demonstrating the anomalous position of the shogun in relation to the sovereign.

## The Four Great Masters of Rehabilitation
The rehabilitation of the Shinto cult and the clarification of the Mikado's divine right were achieved by four primary scholars:

*   **Kada Azumamaro (1668-1736):** He focused on restoring pure Japanese literature. Because five centuries of warfare had destroyed many books and altered the Japanese language, ancient texts like the *Manyo-shu* and *Kojiki* required special study. Kada's work enabled his successors to access these ancient treasures.
*   **Kamo Mabuchi (1697-1769):** Mabuchi championed Japanese ethical systems over Chinese ones, providing a lucid understanding of the Shinto cult as viewed by the Japanese.
*   **Motoori Norinaga (1730-1801):** One of the key philosophers who contended for the revival of "Pure Shinto."
*   **Hirata Atsutane (1776-1834/1843):** A major figure in the Shinto revival and a commentator on the Japanese government.
