# The Ashanti

## Origins and National Character
The most probable tradition regarding the origin of the Ashanti describes them as bands of fugitives driven before Moslem tribes migrating southward from the countries of the Senegal and the Niger during the 16th or 17th century. After securing a region of impenetrable forest, the Ashanti defended themselves with a valour that became a defining part of their national character, eventually elevating them to the status of a powerful and conquering nation.

The Ashanti are of the pure negro type and are believed to be of the same race and language as the Fanti people located nearer the coast. Tradition attributes the separation of the two groups to a famine; the Fanti moved south and lived on herbs (*fan*), while the Ashanti subsisted on Indian corn (*san*), leading to the names Fanti and Santi.

## Social and Political Organization
The Ashanti are divided into numerous tribes, twelve of which are distinguished: the Adansi, Bekwai, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Juabin, Kokofu, Kumasi, Mampon, Nkwanta, Nsuta, Ofinsu, and Adjisu. While each tribe possesses its own king, the king of Kumasi was recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti from the beginning of the 18th century. The symbol of this paramount authority is the "golden stool." Following the deposition of Prempeh in 1896, no king of Kumasi was chosen, and Prempeh himself was never "enstooled."

The government was historically a combination of military aristocracy and monarchy. The state's social fabric was based on an organization of six great divisions or clans originally formed for war. The King's Council consisted of the chiefs of these clans and a few sub-chiefs with hereditary rights. Unless the king possessed an exceptionally strong character, the council of chiefs often exercised more power than the monarch.

Socially, polygamy is practiced by all who can afford it. Land is held in common by the tribes, and unallotted lands, known as "stool lands," are attached to the king or head chief.

## History and Expansion
Osai Tutu is considered the real founder of Ashanti power. He extended the empire through conquests to the east and west, subdued the Mahommedan countries of Banna and Gaman (Jaman), and either built or expanded the capital, Kumasi. He also subdued the neighbouring state of Denkera in 1719 before being defeated and slain in 1731. His successor, Osai Apoko, continued to make acquisitions toward the coast.

In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina became king. In 1807, after the Fanti refused to deliver two fugitive Assin chiefs, Osai Tutu Quamina invaded Fantiland. This campaign reached the coast near Anamabo, resulting in the destruction of the town and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants, though the Ashanti failed to storm the British fort. A subsequent agreement with Governor Colonel G. Torrane acknowledged the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland and involved the payment of rent to the Ashanti for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

Later conflicts occurred in 1852 and 1863, the latter caused by the British refusal to surrender a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief. Under the peace-loving King Kwaka Dua I (who reigned from 1838 to 1867), trade was encouraged. However, his successor, Kofi Karikari, declared upon being enstooled that his business would be war, leading to the conflict of 1873-74. This war was triggered by the British transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch on April 2, 1872—territory the Ashanti considered their own—and the captivity of four Europeans (including Mr. J. Kuhne and Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer) in Kumasi.

## Economy and Culture
The Ashanti are skilled in pottery, gold work, and weaving cotton. Artistic works found in the king's palace in 1874 included masks of beaten gold and silver-plate, showing the influence of Moorish art.

The economy is based on several key products:
*   **Agriculture:** The rubber tree (*Funtumia elastica*) is the most important commercial vegetable product. Kola nuts are in great demand, and cocoa plantations were established in eastern districts after 1905. Tobacco is grown in the north.
*   **Mining:** Gold is found in western districts, particularly the Obuassi mines. In 1905, gold output reached 68,259 oz, valued at £254,790.
*   **Trade:** Kumasi serves as the distributing centre for the hinterland. Trade extends to the Niger countries, as well as French and German neighbours. Revenue is generated from liquor licences, caravan taxes, government land rents, and gold-mining company contributions.

The majority of the population are spirit-worshippers and generally show a repugnance to Islam, though some traders are Mahommedans and some converts to Christianity exist via Wesleyan and Basel missions.

## Administration and Annexation
Ashanti was formally annexed to the British dominions by an order in council on September 26, 1901. It was placed under the control of the governor of the Gold Coast, represented by a chief commissioner assisted by four commissioners and four assistant commissioners. A battalion of the Gold Coast regiment is headquartered at Kumasi.

The British administration provided that native laws regulating the civil relations of tribes and chiefs would be respected, provided they were not clearly injurious to the natives or incompatible with British sovereignty. By 1906, reports indicated that suspicion of British motives was decreasing, as natives showed a preference for the impartial treatment of British civil and criminal courts.

## The Capital: Kumasi
Kumasi is situated on a low rocky eminence in a clearing of dense forest. Its name, more correctly *Kum-ase*, means "under the okum tree." As of 1906, the population was 6,280, exceeding 12,000 when including suburbs. The town features native houses built of red clay, while principal chiefs own European-style houses leased to strangers. The most imposing structure is the fort built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner.
