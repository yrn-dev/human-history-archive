# Philosophical Systems of Aristotle and Spinoza

## The Philosophy of Aristotle

Aristotle's intellectual contributions span logic, ethics, politics, and metaphysics. His logical treatises, collectively known as the *Organon*, were known in part to the West and in their entirety after approximately 1130. His other major works, including the *Ethics* and *Politics*, became known in the thirteenth century through direct translations from Greek into Latin, such as the translation of the *Politics* by William of Moerbeke around 1260. Other works, specifically the *Physics* and *Metaphysics*, first reached the West via indirect translations of Arabic paraphrases or commentaries by scholars such as Avicenna and Averroes. These indirect versions often underwent significant changes—sometimes translated from Greek to Syriac, then to Arabic, and finally into Hebrew or Latin—resulting in "transmogrified" views and unintelligible transliterations.

In his logical treatises, Aristotle exhibits an idealistic bent, asserting that the universal is the real and provides coherence and individuality to the particulars of sense. This perspective is further evident in his *Ethics* and *Politics*, where he posits that the universal or common will provides reality and substance to the individual. However, as his work moves from logic to metaphysics and ontology, his concepts function as "categories" or predicates of a reality that exists outside of them. This creates an ultimate division between the thinking or moving principle and the world as the matter or object of thought.

Aristotle's contributions to idealistic philosophy advanced in two primary directions. First, he argued that any satisfactory explanation of the world must be based on the notion of continuity, where the reality of a lower order is found in how it expresses the potentialities of its own nature and the whole of which it is a part. Second, he concluded that oppositions such as matter and mind, the particular and the universal, and sense and thought cannot be absolute in nature.

## The Ethics and Metaphysics of Spinoza

Benedict de Spinoza's philosophical system is characterized by a rigorous application of the geometrical method to metaphysics. Following the manner of Euclid, Spinoza structures his *Ethics* by placing definitions, axioms, and postulates at the beginning of each book. These are intended to be intuitively certain and serve as the basis for the subsequent system; in total, there are eight postulates, twenty axioms, and twenty-seven definitions.

In his metaphysics, Spinoza emphasizes the unity of substance, defining substance as that which is in itself and conceived through itself. An attribute is the same thing, but described in relation to an intelligence that assigns a specific nature to the substance. This focus on unity creates a logical difficulty: the contradiction between attributes and substance becomes explicit. Spinoza accounts for the difference in attributes not through the nature of the substance itself, but through the nature of the intelligence to which it is revealed.

Spinoza's ethical philosophy is determined by these same metaphysical principles. He teaches a morality of self-assertion and self-seeking rather than self-denial or asceticism. The supreme principle of virtue in his system is the *conatus sese conservandi*. Under the guidance of reason, this self-seeking is intended to identify with the love of God and the love of man, finding blessedness within virtue itself. Spinoza rejects the necessity of a "dead self" or the element of sacrifice and mortification in morality, as he views the negative as nothing and the only form of evil. Consequently, the higher seeking of oneself, which is identical to the love of God, is viewed as a direct extension of the natural self-seeking common to all beings, including animals.

## Intellectual Influences and Context

Spinoza's work is often analyzed in relation to Cartesianism. While he carries on a continual polemic against Descartes in his *Ethics*, he strives to show that his doctrines are legitimately derived from Cartesian principles. Some scholars suggest that Spinoza's introduction of the idea of an absolute unity—where the difference between the finite and infinite is lost—was influenced by his Jewish origin. Others point to similarities, including verbal coincidences, with Jewish writers such as Hasdai Crescas, Gersonides, and Maimonides. Spinoza himself referred to Jewish philosophy as containing an "obscure and unreasoned anticipation" of his doctrines, specifically regarding the unity of God, the intellect of God, and the things understood by it.

In a broader historical context, the systematic metaphysics of Plato and Aristotle were viewed by later philosophers like Epicurus as tendencies that subordinated the practical man to the philosopher. Epicurus reacted against this by moving away from logic and metaphysics toward sensation as the only touchstone of truth.

Additionally, the works of Aristotle and Spinoza remained subjects of study for later thinkers. For example, Boole, in his reflections on philosophical questions, frequently studied the ethics of Spinoza and the metaphysics of Aristotle, alongside the philosophical works of Cicero.
