# BAJOUR

Bajour, also referred to as Bajaur, is a small district located in the North-West Frontier Province of India. It is characterized as a region peopled by Pathan races of Afghan origin.

## Geography and Topography
The district measures approximately 45 miles in length and 20 miles in breadth. It is situated at a high elevation to the east of the Kunar valley. A continuous line of rugged frontier hills separates Bajour from the Kunar valley, creating a barrier that is easily passable at only one or two points. 

The terrain of the district is characterized by a gradual downward slope that begins at the Kunar ridge and terminates at the Panjkora river. Consequently, the drainage of Bajour flows eastwards, originating from the eastern slopes of the dividing ridge that overlooks the Kunar.

## Population and Tribal Composition
The population of Bajour consists almost exclusively of Tarkani (Tarkalanri) Pathans. These are further sub-divided into the Ismailzai, Isazai, and Mamunds. Together with a small number of Utmauzais and Mohmands, the total population is approximately 100,000. The Tarkanis are listed as part of the "Bar Durani," a name sometimes applied to independent Pathan tribes inhabiting various hill districts south of the Hindu Kush, including the Salt Range and the range of Suliman.

## Borders and Neighboring Regions
Bajour is surrounded by several distinct territories and ethnic groups:
*   **South:** The district borders the wild mountain district of the Mohmands, another Pathan race.
*   **East:** Beyond the Panjkora river lie the hills of Swat, which are dominated by a different Pathan race.
*   **North:** An intervening watershed separates Bajour from the small state of Dir. This watershed and the valley of Dir serve as the route for the new road from the Punjab and Malakand to Chitral.
*   **West:** The district is bordered by the Kunar valley.

## Political History and Administration
Nawagai serves as the chief town of the district. The khan of Nawagai is under British protection, a status maintained to safeguard the road to Chitral. 

Historically, the valley of Jandol in northern Bajour held political significance, though it has since ceased to be important. This decline followed the failure of its chief, Umra Khan, in his attempts to appropriate the Kunar valley, Dir, and Bajour. Umra Khan's ambitions led to two major geopolitical events:
1.  The demarcation agreement of 1893, which fixed the boundary of Afghanistan in Kunar, resulting from the hostility between Umra Khan and the amir of Kabul, who claimed sovereignty over those same districts.
2.  The 1895 siege of the Chitral fort, following an invasion of Chitral by Umra Khan, who was not a party to the 1893 boundary settlement.

In the broader geopolitical context, Bajour is part of an independent belt of territory—which also includes Swat and part of the Mohmand territory north of the Kabul river—that serves as a neutral width between Afghanistan and the boundaries of British India.

## Infrastructure and Transport
Before the Khyber Pass was adopted as the primary route, the old road from Kabul to India ran across the rugged frontier hills that separate Bajour from the Kunar valley.
