# Gilgit and Chitral: Boundary Extensions and Regional Geography

The regions of Gilgit and Chitral are situated on the north-west frontier of India, forming part of a complex geographical zone where the boundaries of British India, Afghanistan, and other territories meet. In recent history, these areas have been the subject of significant scientific geographical mapping and boundary extensions, moving beyond simple exploration to professional surveying.

## Geographical Mapping and Boundary Extensions
The extension of boundaries east and north of Afghanistan, along with the occupation of Gilgit and Chitral, contributed to the creation of a series of geographical maps spanning from the Indus to the Euphrates and from the Indus to the Oxus. These efforts were supported by the Afghan war of 1878-80, the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission of 1884-1885, and the systematic survey of Baluchistan beginning in 1880. Key figures in these professional labors included scientific geographers such as General Sir A. Houtum Schindler, Captain H.B. Vaughan, and Major Percy M. Sykes in Persia, as well as Cockerill and Sir George Robertson in the Hindu Kush and Kafiristan.

## Dardistan
Scientists use the conventional name "Dardistan" to describe a tract of country on India's north-west frontier. While the inhabitants do not use this name for their own country, those living on the right bank of the Indus (from Batera to the Kandia river) use the term to refer to the dwellers on the left bank. 

Scientifically, Dardistan encompasses several areas:
* The Gilgit valley and the Gilgit agency.
* Chitral, Yasin, and the Astor valley.
* Panyal, Hunza, and Nagar.
* The Indus valley from Batera to Bunji.
* The Kohistan of Swat and the Kohistan-Malazai (the upper reaches of the Panjkora river).

The "Dard races" are believed to be of Aryan origin, having ascended the Indus valley from the Punjab plains to Chitral, where they dispossessed the Khos. Their presence is noted in the Shina, Burishki, and Khoswar dialects spoken in the Gilgit agency. Dr. Grierson has suggested that the dialects of Dardistan, Gilgit, Chitral, and Kafiristan form a separate group descended from Aryan, independent of Iranian or Sanskrit.

## Regional Boundaries and Political Geography
The boundaries of Afghanistan to the north-east extend to nearly 75 deg. E., following the water-divide to the head of the Taghdumbash Pamir and the water-parting of the Hindu Kush. This line leaves the Hindu Kush near the Dorah Pass, which connects the Lutku valley leading to Chitral with Zebak and Ishkashim.

A principle of maintaining neutral, independent territory between British India and Afghanistan was established along the eastern borders of Afghanistan. This independent belt includes Bajour, Swat, and portions of Mohmand territory north of the Kabul river. The boundary between Afghanistan and British India is demarcated to the point where it touches the northern limits of Baluchistan on the Gomal river.

In the Kunar valley, the Afghan boundary crosses the river at Arnawai; above this point, the valley belongs to Chitral. To the north of Bajour lies a watershed and the valley of Dir, which serves as the route for the new road from the Punjab and Malakand to Chitral.

## Historical and Ethnic Context
Historically, the region was referred to as *Bolor* (or *Balaur*), a name appearing in 7th-century Chinese works and the travels of Hiuen Tsang. According to the *Táríkh Rashídí* (16th century), *Malaur* was a country bordering Kashmir to the south, Kabul to the west, Badakhshan to the north, and Kashgar and Yarkand to the east. This region likely included Chitral, Gilgit, Yasin, Balti, and possibly Kafiristan.

The chiefs of several regions, including Gilgit, Chitral, Swat, and Badakhshan, have claimed a pedigree descending from Alexander. In terms of population, the mountainous tracts of the neighboring Badakhshan province are inhabited by Tajiks of the Shiá creed, speaking various dialects such as *Wakhi* in Wakhan and *Sanglichì* in Zebak.

## Conflict and Political Influence
The political landscape was influenced by the hostility between the amir of Kabul and Umra Khan, the chief of Jandol. Umra Khan attempted to appropriate Bajour, Dir, and much of the Kunar valley. This conflict led to the 1893 demarcation agreement fixing the Afghan boundary in Kunar. Additionally, Umra Khan's actions led to the invasion of Chitral and the subsequent siege of the Chitral fort in 1895. To safeguard the road to Chitral, the khan of Nawagai in Bajour remains under British protection.
