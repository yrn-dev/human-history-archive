# Bedouins

The Bedouins, who refer to themselves as *Ahl Bedu* ("dwellers in the open land") or *Ahl el beit* ("people of the tent"), constitute the most important and well-known division of the Arab race. Descendants of the Arabs of North Arabia, they claim Ishmael as their ancestor. While their earliest home is believed to be the deserts of North Arabia, they migrated in ancient times to the lowlands of Syria and Egypt. Following the Arab conquest of northern Africa in the 7th century A.D., they were widely dispersed, leaving a strong Arab element among the Nubian, Saharan, and Nile Valley peoples.

## Lifestyle and Environment
The Arabian Bedouin are shepherds and herdsmen who lead a roving, open-air existence. This nomadic life is necessitated by the nature of their occupation and the geography of the Arabian territory, where much of the land is suited only for pasture. Prolonged summer droughts frequently render large areas unfit even for grazing, forcing herdsmen to migrate constantly in search of water and herbage for their beasts.

These environmental pressures often lead to extreme want and frequent quarrels between groups over the use of specific pasture-grounds or wells. Consequently, Bedouins may become plunderers of others for self-support. While they are professionally herdsmen, they occasionally engage in the robbery of caravans and travelers, which they justify as a substitute for custom dues, arguing that trespassers on their land must pay a forfeit.

## Social Structure and Governance
The total pastoral population of Arabia is estimated not to exceed 1.5 million people, roughly one-fifth of the total population. The primary tribal authority is the "sheik" or "elder." This title is granted based on qualities such as wealth, courage, liberality, birth, or other leadership traits, rather than necessarily advanced age. While descent influences rank, most tribal members consider themselves equal, and distinctions of poverty or wealth are not heavily weighted.

The sheik is consulted on general tribal affairs and serves as the sole magistrate for disputes, though he is not always obeyed. In private and personal matters, individuals generally act as they see fit. Social ties are based strictly on race and blood. Because the Bedouins are unacquainted with books and writing, they rely on memory and imagination for their genealogies and histories, making their assertions regarding the strength and antiquity of their clans unreliable.

## Cultural Characteristics and Morality
Bedouin morality is governed by public feeling and custom rather than a fixed code. This leads to contradictions: while deliberate perjury, lying, and the violation of solemn engagements are frequent, there are also instances of romantic fidelity and the strict observance of promises. Common oaths include "the wood," "the wind," and "the honour of the Arabs."

Publicity is a matter of great sensitivity for Bedouins. In domestic life, wives and daughters perform all household tasks—including baking bread, fetching water, working the loom, and grinding wheat—while the men smoke pipes before the tent. While Bedouins are jealous of their women, they generally do not prevent them from talking or laughing with strangers. A maiden's honor is strictly guarded, and open courtship is frowned upon.

Marriage and divorce are simple processes requiring only the consent of the parties and the oral testimony of witnesses if available. Polygamy is not common, and mutual constancy is rarely expected or observed by either gender.

## Military Tradition and Equipment
The characteristic weapon of the Arab nomad is the light, sharp-pointed lance made of African or Persian cane; the way a Bedouin carries or trails this lance often identifies his tribe. A complete military outfit may also include a helmet (an iron head-piece without a crest or visor), a coat of mail from Bagdad or Yemen, an ornamented knife or dagger, an out-of-date musket, and an antiquated sword.

## Related Groups and Influence
The influence of the Arab type extends beyond the Arabian peninsula. In the Suliman Mountains, the Marri and Bugti tribes are Rind Baluchis of almost certain Arab extraction. These individuals possess Semitic features resembling those of the Bedouin and carry themselves as loftily and straight as any Arab gentleman. Like the Bedouin, the true Baluch of the Derajat values courage as the highest virtue and is an expert rider, with horse-racing serving as a national amusement.
