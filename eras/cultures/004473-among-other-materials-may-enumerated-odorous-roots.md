# Natural Materials and Biological Roots

The use of various botanical and biological materials for construction, art, and survival has been a consistent feature of human activity across different regions and eras. These materials range from the structural use of grasses and palms in basketry to the complex biological interactions of fungal roots in forest ecosystems.

## Materials for Basketry and Weaving
Across the globe, diverse plant species have been utilized to manufacture baskets and other wicker-work. In China and Japan, various species of bamboo are used to create baskets of high finish and beauty. Japanese artisans specifically use bamboo wicker-work to encase delicate egg-shell porcelain and apply it to furniture. 

In other regions, different flora are employed:
*   **India and the East:** The leaves of various screw pine species and the odorous roots of the khus-khus grass (*Anatherum muricatum*) are commonly used. In India, "Cajan" baskets are made from the fronds of the Palmyra palm (*Borassus flabelliformis*), a manufacture that has also been established as a characteristic staple in Germany's Black Forest.
*   **Island Nations:** The Seychelles utilize the fronds of the palm *Lodoicea sechellarum* for delicate basket-work, while New Zealand employs strips of the flax plant *Phormium tenax*.
*   **Africa and Europe:** In Algeria and Spain, esparto fibre is used to produce rude fruit baskets.
*   **The Americas:** In South America and the West Indies, various species of *Maranta* provide materials. In Trinidad, the *Tirite* (a species of *Calathea* and member of the order *Zingiberaceae*) is used similarly.

Additionally, baskets are frequently constructed from straw, various sedges (*Cyperus*), and splints or shavings of many types of wood.

## Biological Roots and Symbiosis
In the natural world, the concept of symbiosis describes a "life in common" where two organisms unite to form a compound organism. This is observed in lichens, where an alga provides carbohydrates and a fungus ensures the supply of moisture, shade, and mineral matters.

A similar symbiotic relationship exists between fungi and the roots of many trees and plants, known as mycorhiza (fungus-root). This relationship is necessary for the well-being of various plants found in woodlands and moors, including Orchidaceae, Gentianaceae, Pyrolaceae, Ericaceae, and ferns. For example, orchid seeds require a specific fungus to be present to infect the young seedling immediately after emergence to germinate successfully. Similarly, the failure of heaths and rhododendrons in ordinary garden soils is attributed to their need for fungus-infected peat for their roots. The fungus provides materials from the surrounding leaf-mould that ordinary root-hairs cannot provide, while the plant supports the fungus using its reserve materials.

## Fungal Storage and Sclerotia
Fungi store reserve materials—such as carbohydrates, glycogen, and oil—within their vegetative tissues to be used during the formation of large fructifications. These reserves can be stored in swollen cells or single hyphae, which may gather into mycelial strands or thick cords. 

Some of these strands differentiate into rhizomorphs, which possess a core of ordinary hyphae protected by an outer layer of blackened, hardened cell-walls. These can extend through the soil like roots or remain dormant. In other instances, hyphae form dense, interwoven masses called sclerotia. These can range in size from a pea to a man's head, weighing 10 to 25 lb. or more. Sclerotia, such as those found in Agaricini, Pezizae, and Polyporei, function similarly to a potato tuber, giving rise to fructifications when kept moist.

## Early Human Settlement and Materials
The transition of Neolithic men from temporary settlements to permanent habitation depended on a reliable year-round supply of water, food, and building materials. These conditions were found on a significant scale in Egypt and Mesopotamia (the region between the Persian Gulf and the upper waters of the Tigris and Euphrates). 

In these areas, the availability of specific materials allowed populations to multiply and settle. In Egypt, builders utilized easily worked stone and clay. In Mesopotamia, a specific type of clay was used that transforms into brick when exposed to sunshine. These environmental advantages led to a denser human population and increased security, allowing people to "take root" and live in peaceful communities without the need for weapons.

## Early Use of Roots in Dyeing
The practice of dyeing, which dates back to prehistoric times, likely began with the use of fugitive stains. These early dyes were obtained through the decoctions of roots, barks, leaves, flowers, and the juices of fruits. Over time, the art of dyeing evolved as humans discovered that certain muds and earths containing iron or alumina could render these stains permanent. In its early history, this was primarily a home industry managed by women alongside weaving and spinning.
