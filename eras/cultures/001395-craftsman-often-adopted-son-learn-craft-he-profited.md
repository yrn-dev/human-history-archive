# Adoption and the Transmission of Crafts

In various historical and social contexts, the practice of adoption has served as a mechanism for ensuring the continuity of familial care, the preservation of estates, and the transmission of professional skills. While often used to provide for parents in their old age, adoption also functioned as a structured method for vocational training.

## Adoption for Care and Estate Management
Adoption was a common practice, particularly for parents who were childless or whose children had grown and married. These arrangements were typically formalized by contract, which detailed the maintenance expected by the parents and the assets the parent would leave to the adopted child. In cases where biological children existed, they generally consented to these contracts, as the arrangement relieved them of the burden of care, though it simultaneously cut off their expectations regarding the estate.

The legal nature of these contracts meant they could be annulled in law courts if the adopted child failed to perform their filial duties. This applied even to the adoption of slaves; if a slave was adopted but proved unfilial, they could be reduced to slavery once again. Similarly, Vestals frequently adopted other Vestals to ensure care during their old age.

In instances where a man adopted a son but later married and started his own family, the contract could be dissolved. In such cases, the adopted child was entitled to one-third of a child's share in goods, though they were excluded from receiving real estate, which remained within the original family.

## Adoption as Apprenticeship
Beyond familial care, adoption was utilized by craftsmen as a means of training successors. A craftsman would adopt a son specifically to teach him the craft, thereby profiting from the son's labor during the process. This arrangement functioned as a form of apprenticeship, although it remains unclear if the apprentice maintained a true filial relationship with the craftsman.

These contracts carried specific obligations for the teacher. If a craftsman failed to teach the adopted son the craft, the son had the legal right to prosecute him and seek the annulment of the contract.

## Professional Organizations and Hereditary Employment
The transmission of crafts was also influenced by broader social and legal structures. In Britain during the Roman period, "colleges of operatives" existed. Some interpretations suggest these were practically castes because the Theodosian code compelled sons to follow the employment of their fathers, and marriage into a family necessitated the adoption of the family's employment. 

However, these *collegia opificum* are viewed as precursors to later craft-gilds and frith-gilds. While sons certainly held great advantages as apprentices within these voluntary associations, the gilds admitted qualified strangers and viewed intermarriage as a matter of social feeling rather than a strict caste restriction.

## Specialized Craftsmanship and Training
The mastery of various crafts required different forms of training and aptitude. For example, basket-making in England involves no machinery and relies on the craftsman's natural aptitude and a "perfect conception of form" to impress upon the material. The Basketmakers' Company of London stands as one of the city's oldest craft gilds.

Other crafts were transmitted through international influence. Following the accession of Empress Suiko in 593, Japan cultivated numerous crafts—including ceramics, tile-making, sculpture, architecture, and the casting of metal—under the instruction of Koreans and Chinese. In architecture, the Japanese adopted a style for temples that blended Indian and Chinese influences, though they frequently modified these originals to fit their own ideals.

## Individual Mastery and Professional Standards
History records individuals of exceptional skill within their crafts, such as Sigmund Schnitzer, a *Pfeifenmacher und Stadtpfeifer* of Nuremberg. He was renowned for producing *fagotte* of pure intonation and extraordinary size, which were highly valued across Germany, Italy, and France.

Professional reputation also depended on adherence to ethical standards. In bricklaying, for instance, the use of "snapped headers" (cutting heading bricks in half to save material) is described as a pernicious practice that destroys the proper bonding of a wall. Such methods are considered unworthy of adoption by any craftsman of repute.
