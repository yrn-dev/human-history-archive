# Bamboo Industries in China and Japan

The use of bamboo is extensive in China and Japan, where the plant is utilized for a vast array of domestic, industrial, and artistic purposes. From the construction of large-scale structures to the creation of delicate ornaments, the manipulation of bamboo—particularly through the use of splits and wicker-work—is a hallmark of these regions.

## Basketry and Fine Arts
The Japanese and Chinese are noted for manufacturing baskets of "unequalled beauty and finish" using splits from various species of bamboo. The outer cuticle of Oriental bamboo species is particularly valued for this work because it is hard, durable, and provides a sharp cutting edge. 

In Japan, this skill is demonstrated in the creation of bamboo wicker-work used to encase delicate egg-shell porcelain, an application described as a "marvellous example of manipulation." Both the Chinese and Japanese excel in applying bamboo wicker-work to furniture, weaving strips into items such as chairs, beds, and cages. These works are regarded as unequalled in their neatness of workmanship and beauty.

## Construction and Domestic Utility
Bamboo serves as a primary building material in these regions. It is used as a timber wood to construct entire houses, where complete sections of the stem function as columns or posts. When split, the material is used for rafters and floors, while interwoven lattice-work is employed for the sides of rooms to allow for the entry of air and light. Roofs may be constructed solely of bamboo, and the plant can be easily split into planks or laths.

Beyond housing, bamboo is used for a wide variety of domestic and agricultural implements. In China, it is used for:
*   **Water Management:** Bored-out septa joined together to transmit water to gardens or reservoirs, similar to leaden pipes.
*   **Storage and Transport:** Joints are used as holders for pens or papers. Historically, a bamboo joint was used to transport silk-worm eggs from China to Constantinople.
*   **Paper Production:** The interior portions of the bamboo stem are beaten into a pulp to manufacture finer varieties of paper.
*   **General Utility:** The plant is used for oil-cans, water-stoups, dinner-trays, bellows, and clothes-boxes.

## Maritime and Military Applications
Bamboo is employed extensively in shipping. For boats of moderate size, the strongest plants are selected for masts. In larger vessels, masts are sometimes constructed by joining several bamboos together. Other maritime uses include the creation of oars, yards, cables, and caulking.

In military applications, bamboo is used to create bows—formed by joining two pieces with many bands—and shafts for arrows. It is also used to manufacture helmets, spears, and quivers.

## Recreation and Other Uses
Bamboo is a central component in the traditional art of kite-flying, a national pastime in China and Japan. Kites, which can reach up to 7 feet in height or breadth and take the shapes of beasts, fishes, dragons, or birds, are constructed using bamboo strips covered in thin silk or rice paper.

Additionally, bamboo is used to create melodious musical instruments, torches, and footballs. In China, the plant is also associated with medicine and "works of fantastic art."

## Botany and Cultivation
Many species of bamboo native to China and Japan are hardy enough to be cultivated in the British Isles. These include genera such as *Arundinaria*, *Bambusa*, and *Phyllostachys*. Notable species include:
*   *Phyllostachys mitis*: Capable of growing to 60 feet in its native China and Japan.
*   *Arundinaria japonica*: A tall plant often grown in gardens as *Bambusa metake*.
*   *Bambusa quadrangularis*: Distinguished by its square stems.

## Food Sources
In China, the grains of the bamboo are used for food. A Chinese proverb suggests that bamboo produces seed more abundantly during years when rice crops fail, indicating that the population relies on it as a food source during times of dearth.
