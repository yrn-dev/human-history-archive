# Bokhara, Khiva, and Turkestan

The region encompassing Bokhara, Khiva, and Turkestan proper is situated between the Caspian and Aral Seas and the northern border of the Persian highlands. This area consists of a nearly desert, low-lying plain that extends to the foot of the north-western extremity of the Tibeto-Himalayan mountains. It is prolonged eastward through the valleys of the Jaxartes (Syr-Darya) and the Oxus (Amu Darya), and northward across the country of the Kirghiz to the south-western border of Siberia.

## Geography and Climate
The region is characterized by rainfall that is insufficient for agriculture, making farming possible only through irrigation. Consequently, the fixed population is relatively small and restricted to towns and districts near the rivers. 

In Bokhara, the climate is extreme. Lowlands experience very hot summers and short, cold winters where frosts may reach -20 degrees Fahrenheit and the Oxus may freeze for two weeks. The highlands feature hot, dry summers and four-month winters. In regions above 8,000 feet, there are glaciers and snowfields that bury mountain passes. The lowlands are also subject to terrible sand-storms from the west that can kill cotton trees and exhaust people. Malaria is widely prevalent, becoming malignant in some years following a wet spring.

## Population and Society
The Uzbeg Turks are the dominant race in Bokhara, Khiva, and Turkestan proper. In Bokhara, the Uzbegs are fanatical Moslem Sunnites who maintain old divisions of clans and tribes; they are noted for scorning work and despising their Iranian subjects. The population of Bokhara is estimated at 1,250,000, with approximately 20% being nomads and 15% semi-nomads. Other groups in Bokhara include Iranian Tajiks (the bulk of the rural population), Sarts (a mixture of Turks and Iranians who make up the bulk of the town populations), nomad Kirghiz and Turkomans of Turkish origin, as well as Persians, Arabs, Armenians, Afghans, and Jews. Trade is largely handled by a colony of Hindus from Shikarpur.

In the oasis of Khiva, the population is divided into four main groups:
* **Uzbegs (150,000 to 200,000):** The dominating race among settled inhabitants and the source of government officials.
* **Sarts and Tajiks:** Tradespeople and agriculturists of mixed race.
* **Turkomans (c. 170,000):** Steppe dwellers to the south and west who previously raided settled inhabitants.
* **Kara-kalpaks (c. 50,000):** A Turki tribe living south of Lake Aral, prevailing in the towns of Kipchak, Khodsheili, and Kungrad. They breed cattle, cultivate soil, and produce carpets.

Additionally, there are about 10,000 Kirghiz. In the broader region, nomadic tribes are described as marauders. However, those to the north under Russian subjecthood are more peaceable and disciplined.

## The Khanate of Khiva
Khiva is the capital of its namesake khanate in Western Asia, located 25 miles west of the Amu-darya and 240 miles west-north-west of Bokhara. The town has a population of about 10,000 and is enclosed by a low earthen wall. It features a citadel for the khan and high officials, four large *madrasas* (Mahommedan colleges), and approximately twenty mosques, the most notable being the one containing the tomb of the patron saint, Polvan.

The economy of Khiva involves the production of silks, cottons, and carpets. It exports silk cocoons, wool, rough woollen cloth, and cotton to Russia, and animal products to Bokhara. Imports from Russia include hardware, pepper, velveteen, and cottons, while tea, china, cotton, and silks are imported from Bokhara. Khivan merchants frequently attend the Nizhniy-Novgorod and Orenburg fairs.

## Historical Development
### Bokhara
Known to ancients as Sogdiana, Bokhara was never under Roman dominion. It was part of the Seleucidae empire after the conquests of Alexander the Great. Around A.D. 45, the Yue-chi king Kadphises I extended dominions from Bokhara to the Indus. The region was later reconquered by the Sassanian dynasty of Persia; Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand by the 6th century. Islam became the dominant faith following the Arab conquest of Persia (640-642). 

During the early middle ages, the region was called Sughd or Transoxiana (*Ma wara 'l Nahr*). Bokhara gained a reputation as a center of learning and religion, while Samarkand served as the seat of government. The region was subdued by Malik Shah of the Seljuk dynasty in the 11th century, and later by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm in 1216. In 1220, Jenghiz Khan conquered Bokhara, wasting the country, though it recovered some prosperity under his son, Ogdai Khan. In 1369, Timur (Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand.

### Khiva
The khanate is a relic of the kingdom of Chorasmia (also known as Kharezm or Urgenj). Mentioned by Herodotus as a Persian province under Darius, it was seized by Arabs in A.D. 680. The first king known to history was Mamun-ibn-Mahommed in 995. The region fell to Mahmud of Ghazni in 1017 and later to the Seljuk Turks. In 1097, Kutb-ud-din became king; his descendant 'Ala-ud-din-Mahommed eventually conquered Persia. After being conquered by Jenghiz Khan in 1219 and Timur in 1379, the region fell under Uzbeg rule in 1512.

## Russian Relations and Annexation
Russia established relations with Khiva in the 17th century. In 1717, Peter the Great sent a military force to release Russian slaves and seek auriferous sand in the Oxus, but the force was annihilated by the Khivans. A 1839 expedition under General Perovsky also ended in disaster. 

In 1847, Russia founded a fort at the mouth of the Jaxartes, and in 1865, they captured Tashkent. Following the founding of Krasnovodsk in 1869 and extensive surveys, a large-scale expedition of 10,000 men under General Kaufmann occupied Khiva in 1873. This resulted in the annexation of 35,700 square miles and 110,000 souls on the right bank of the Oxus. Upon taking Khiva, the Russians abolished the slave trade and liberated 29,300 Persian slaves (stolen by Turkomans) and over 6,500 other slaves, mostly Kizil-bashes.
