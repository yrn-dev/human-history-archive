# Buddhist Perspectives on the Soul and Divinity

## The Rejection of the Soul
In Buddhist doctrine, the concept of the "soul" is categorically and systematically rejected. This position is of such central importance that it is placed at the forefront of Buddhist expositions. The Buddha is stated to have devoted his very first discourse to the first converts to this subject. This rejection is further emphasized in the *Dialogues of Gotama* and the *Kathavatthu*—the latest book in the canon—where the question of the soul is placed at the head of all discussed points, occupying a space that overshadows all other topics. Similarly, in the conversations between the Buddhist teacher Nagasena and the Greek king Menander, Nagasena convinces the king that the "soul," in the ordinary sense, does not exist.

## Contrast with Other Thought Systems
The Buddhist view stands in stark contrast to other Indian and European thought. In most Indian thought (excluding Buddhism) and in Europe, souls and the gods who are made in imitation of souls are viewed as exceptions. These spirits are attributed with an individuality without change, a beginning without an end, and a "Being without Becoming." According to the doctrine of the Noble (or Aryan) Path, holding such views is considered erroneous, and this error is believed to block the entrance to the Path.

## The Concept of Non-Soul and Becoming
Within the details of the Path, "Right Views" include the recognition of the "Three Signs." Two of these signs are Impermanence and Non-soul (the absence of a soul), both of which are declared to be signs of every individual, whether they are a man, an animal, or a god. 

Buddhist teaching posits that there is no "Being," only "Becoming." Every individual is a compound—a putting together of form, material qualities, and, in higher classes, a rising series of mental qualities. Because the relation of these component parts is ever-changing and never the same for two consecutive moments, the state of every individual is unstable and temporary. Consequently, the moment individuality begins, dissolution and disintegration also begin, as there can be no individuality without a putting together, and no putting together without a becoming and subsequent passing away.

## Transmigration and Karma
While Buddhism rejects the soul-theory of the Jains and the pantheistic speculations of the pre-Buddhistic *Upanishads*, it retains a belief in transmigration. This differs significantly from popular belief and brahmin theology. In those systems, the soul is viewed as a minute, subtle entity that leaves one body at death through a hole at the top of the head to enter a new, ready body.

In the Buddhist adaptation, no soul, memory, or consciousness passes from one body to another. Instead, it is the "grasping" and "craving" existing at the time of death that causes a new set of *Skandhas* (the new body along with its capacities and mental tendencies) to arise. This is linked to the theory of *Karma*, which explains the mysteries of inherited character and the connection between all forms of life. The Buddhist adaptation of *Karma* offers a mode of escape from the miseries of transmigration: the Arahat, having conquered the cravings that produce a new body, performs actions that are *Kiriya* rather than *Karma*, leading to no further rebirth.

## Misinterpretations of Nirvana
European writers, writing before the publication or translation of canonical texts, often misinterpreted Buddhist concepts through the lens of their own beliefs. Because they viewed the world as hopeless and sought salvation in the next life, they incorrectly interpreted Nirvana as a state reached after death. This led to the erroneous conclusion that the "dying out" associated with Nirvana referred to the annihilation or eternal trance of a "soul." However, canonical texts support the belief in salvation during this life, a concept known in Indian religious language as *Jivanmukti*.
