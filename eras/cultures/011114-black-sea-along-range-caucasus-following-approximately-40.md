# The Black Sea and the Caucasus Region

The region spanning from the Black Sea along the range of the Caucasus serves as a primary geographical boundary and a transition zone between various high plateaus and coastal lowlands. This area follows approximately the 40th parallel north, extending from the Black Sea to the Caspian Sea.

## Geographical Boundaries and Topography
The boundary of Asia on its western side passes through the Black Sea and follows the Caucasus range approximately along 40 deg. N. until it reaches the Caspian Sea. To the west of this region lies the Mediterranean and the Sea of Marmora, while to the north, the high land follows nearly 36 deg. N. to the southern shore of the Caspian.

The Caucasus range is described as the loftiest and greatest of all mountain ranges in extent. It separates the Black Sea to the west from the Caspian Sea to the east. In the region of Russian Transcaucasia, the government of Kutais is situated between the Caucasus range to the north and the Black Sea to the west. This area includes the southern slopes of the main Caucasus range and the slopes of the Armenian highlands. Further east, the government of Baku stretches along the west coast of the Caspian Sea, encompassing the eastern extremity of the Caucasus range—including peaks such as the Bazardyuz (14,727 ft.) and the Shad-dagh (13,960 ft.)—where the range terminates at the Apsheron peninsula.

## Regional Provinces and Districts
Several administrative and geographical divisions characterize this territory:

*   **Kutais:** A government of Russian Transcaucasia comprising the districts of Svanetia, Abkhasia, Imeretia, Mingrelia, and Guria. Its terrain varies from the lowlands drained by the Rion to the western slopes of the Suram mountains and the highlands drained by the Ajaris-tskhali and Chorokh. The south-western districts of Artvin and Batum are characterized by the Pontic range, with heights reaching 9,000 to 11,240 ft.
*   **Kubañ:** A province of Russian Caucasia bounded by the Sea of Azov to the west and the Black Sea district to the south and south-west. It contains the northern slopes of the Caucasus range up to the Elbruz and the Black Mountains (Kara-dagh), which reach 3,000 to 6,000 ft. The region features fertile steppes and a wide, low delta formed by the Kubañ river.
*   **Baku:** A government of Russian Transcaucasia that includes the Kuba plain on the north-east slope of the Caucasus and the steppes of the lower Aras and Kura.

## Geology and Natural Resources
Geological theories suggest that the Caspian Sea was once part of the Sarmatian Ocean, which extended from the middle Danube through South Russia and along both flanks of the Caucasus. During the Ice Age, the Caspian may have remained connected to the Black Sea before a period of general desiccation severed these connections.

The region is noted for its diverse natural resources. The Apsheron peninsula, though dry and bare, contains famous petroleum wells in the town of Baku. These wells, reaching depths of 700 to 1,700 ft., produce crude naphtha used to distill kerosene and produce *mazut* for fuel and lubrication. This oil is transported via pipes to Batum on the Black Sea for export, or via tank railway-cars to Poti and Novorossiysk. Additionally, the slopes of the Caucasus are highly fertile, supporting gardens, rice, cereals, vines, and mulberry trees.

## Historical and Ethnic Occupations
Ancient accounts identify the mountainous region between the Black and Caspian Seas as Iberia, occupied by the Iberi and the Albani. The Albani resided between the Caspian Sea and the Iberi, with the river Cyrus (Kur) flowing through Albania into the Caspian. This region was later invaded by the Romans under Pompeius Magnus and L. Lucullus.

The Caucasus has been home to various races of men who lived primarily on the wild produce of forests. Some of these inhabitants were known for using pounded leaves mixed with water to paint permanent figures on woollen garments.

In ancient migrations, the Cimmerians fled across the Cimmerian Bosphorus, coasting along the east of the Euxine (Black) sea and passing west of Mount Caucasus to enter Kolchis and Asia Minor. Their pursuers, the Scythians, took a more circuitous route east of Mount Caucasus near the Caspian sea, which led them into Media.
