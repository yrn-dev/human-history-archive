# War-Dances and Religious Dances

## War-Dances and Mimic Combat
War-dances and mimic fights are described as nearly universal among tribes where war is a primary interest of life. These dances often take the form of "Bravery dances," such as those performed by the Dahomans, or the *Hoolee* of the Bhil tribe in the Vindhya Hills. In the case of the Bhil, the dance has evolved into a professional amusement where performers travel between villages; in these displays, women use long poles on one side while men use short cudgels on the other, introducing an element of comedy. Similarly, the Fiji club-dance, though originally suggested by war, is characterized by a present element of comedy.

Among the Basques, war-dances are preserved as a survival of primitive race traditions, with the sword-dance cited as a specific example. In ancient Rome, the *Salii*, an ancient order of priests, performed a Pyrrhic dance in which they sang *assamenta* and struck their shields. Some accounts suggest the song of the *Salii* mentions the name Mamurius as a reward for his workmanship, while others claim the phrase used is *Veterem Memoriam*, meaning "ancient memory."

## Religious Dances of Antiquity and Early Culture
Sacred dances were a prominent feature of ancient worship and were adapted for various purposes, including humiliation, supplication, praise, and thanksgiving. A Pythagorean philosophy suggested these dances resembled the courses of the stars, an idea later expanded upon by Sir John Davies in his 1596 poem *Orchestra*. In ancient Greece, religious dances may have been rooted in a world-wide ancient heliolithic culture. One such tradition involved chanting, dressing-up, and dancing associated with the worship of the god Dionysus, who is killed and lives again. Over time, the vocal element in these proceedings grew stronger, leading to the development of the chorus and eventually the arts of tragedy and comedy.

Other notable ancient examples include the Delian dance of the Labyrinth (attributed to Theseus) among Athenian stage dances, as well as the *Bacchanalia*, the *Hymenaea*, and the *Trichoria* associated with Lycurgus. In Japan, prehistoric records mention a pantominic dance performed by the "monkey female" (*Sarume*) before the rock cave of the Sun goddess. Later, in 734, the religious fanatic Shomu witnessed a performance of song and dance (*utagaki*) involving 240 people, while 230 members of six great *uji* performed in blue garments with red cords and tassels.

## Religious Dancing in the Christian Tradition
The practice of combining song and dance, known as carolling, moved from pagan ritual into the Christian church. The term "carol" may be derived from *chorus* or *corolla* (meaning a ring or circle), referring to a ring dance. Early examples include the "Giants' Dance" at Stonehenge, also known as the Carol. In early church history, bishops were called *Praesules* because they led dances on feast days, and some church fathers believed that the apostles and angels are constantly dancing.

However, religious dancing faced significant opposition. The Albigenses of Languedoc and the Waldenses characterized dancing as the "devil's procession," and St Augustine stated that it is better to dig than to dance. Various councils sought to limit the practice: the third council of Toledo (589) forbade dancing in churches on the vigils of saints' days, the council of Auxerre (590) forbade secular dances in church, and the council of Avignon (1209) forbade secular songs and theatrical dances in churches.

Despite these prohibitions, religious dances persisted. In the cathedral of Seville, choristers are permitted to perform a castanet dance round the lectern three times a year. Traces of religious dancing remained in the Mozarabic Mass of Toledo and in the cathedrals of Roussillon, Portugal, and Spain after the mid-18th century. In Aragon, the *Jota*—a dignified national dance derived from the 16th-century *Passacaille*—is still utilized as a religious dance.

## Regional and Primitive Survivals
The Basques have preserved specimens of almost every class of primitive dance. Their religious dances are performed in procession before the altar and before the Host. These dances were defended vehemently by Father Larramendi, S.J., in his *Corografia de Guipuzcoa*.

In other primitive cultures, dances often serve as imitative representations of nature rather than formal religion. These include the Ostyak tribes and Kamchadales imitating bears and wolves; the *Kru* representing hunting scenes; the Damara mimicking oxen and sheep; and the Australians and Tasmanians (*corrobories*) imitating the kangaroo and frog.

In modern civilized countries, dancing has transitioned into a pastime or entertainment. Consequently, the direct application of dance to arouse religious feeling or emotion tends to be dropped or obscured.
