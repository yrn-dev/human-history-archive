# The Deccan and Khandesh

The Deccan and Khandesh are regions located within the Bombay Presidency of British India. The presidency is divided into four commissionerships, with the central division designated as the Deccan. Khandesh, which was partitioned into two districts in 1906, is situated in the north of the presidency.

## Geography and Hydrography
The plains of the Deccan and Khandesh are watered by large rivers. However, because rainfall is uncertain, these plains generally remain bleak and devoid of vegetation for the greater part of the year. 

In Khandesh, the principal natural feature is the Tapti river, which flows from east to west and divides the districts into two unequal parts. The larger portions lie to the south and are drained by the Panjhra, Bori, and Girna rivers. While the district is fairly well supplied with surface water, the Tapti flows in a bed too deep to be useful for irrigation, and none of the rivers in the region are navigable. The Tapti receives thirteen principal tributaries as it passes through Khandesh. To the north, beyond the alluvial plains, the land rises toward the Satpura hills, which separate the valley of the Tapti from the valley of the Nerbudda and divide the district of Khandesh from other territories.

The Deccan plateau is bordered to the west by the Sahyadri or Western Ghats. This range of hills, with an average elevation of 1800 ft., runs parallel to the ocean. In some areas, the crest of the range is only slightly raised above the level of the Deccan plateau. The spurs of the Sahyadri hills run eastward and serve as watersheds for the great rivers of the Deccan.

## Agriculture and Natural Resources
The staple food grains of the Deccan and Khandesh are bajra (*Holcus spicatus*) and joar (*Sorghum vulgare*). Other principal crops grown in Khandesh include pulses, oilseeds, wheat, and millets. Cotton is also a major crop; in Khandesh, the indigenous cotton plant has been largely superseded by the superior Hinganghat variety. The region supports various factories for pressing and ginning cotton, including a cotton-mill located at Jalgaon.

The landscape of Khandesh varies from level ground in the center and east—which often appears arid and unfertile—to a rugged, thickly wooded country in the north and west. This wooded area is inhabited by wild tribes of Bhils who support themselves through wood-cutting and the fruits of the forests. While a large area is under forest, most valuable timber has been denuded from the jungles. The region is not rich in minerals.

## Population and Society
In the Deccan, the Mahrattas are the home population, constituting 30% of the region's inhabitants. In Khandesh, the languages spoken are Mahratti and Gujarati.

The Bhils are the most important of the aboriginal tribes in Khandesh, numbering 167,000 as of 1901. Formerly described as a lawless robber tribe, many Bhils have been won over through British rule and the offer of employment, now serving as village watchmen and in police duties. In 1901, the population of the old single district of Khandesh was 1,427,382.

## History
The Deccan was first invaded by the emperor Ala-ud-din in 1294. By 1312, Mahommedan arms were triumphant throughout the Mahratta country. During the 14th century, the Bahmani kingdom was established in the Deccan, embracing part of the Bombay presidency. 

Khandesh was the site of a kingdom founded by the Farukhi dynasty, which established the town of Burhanpur around A.D. 1400. Burhanpur served as the chief seat of the Mogul empire's Deccan provinces until 1635, when Shah Jahan moved the capital to Aurangabad. The empire of Akbar annexed the Farukhi kingdom and effected the reconquest of Khandesh in 1599. 

During the late 17th century, the Mahrattas rose to power and brought almost every part of the current Bombay presidency under their sway. Burhanpur was plundered by the Mahrattas in 1685, and the town eventually passed through various hands—including the peshwa and Sindhia—before being ceded to the British government between 1860 and 1861.

## Infrastructure
The eastern district of Khandesh is traversed by the Great Indian Peninsula railway, which has an important trade center at Bhusawal, branching toward Nagpur and Jubbulpore. Additionally, both districts are crossed by the Tapti Valley line from Surat.
