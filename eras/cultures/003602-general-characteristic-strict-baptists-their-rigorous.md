# Strict Baptists

The Strict Baptists constitute a separate denomination of Baptist churches located throughout England. They are primarily characterized by their rigorous adherence to a form of Calvinistic theology that is now generally considered obsolete, as well as their insistence that baptism is a necessary condition for Christian communion.

## Organization and Divisions
The Strict Baptists lack a national Union and are characterized by a loose organization, which makes the collection of accurate statistics regarding their membership impossible, though the number of adherents is small. Instead of a centralized national body, they are generally linked together through associations based on geographical distribution. Examples of such organizations include the "Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches" and the "Suffolk and Norfolk Association of Particular Baptist Churches." In the latter association, the term "Particular" is preferred, though the group remains aloof from other Baptist churches due to its "strict" principles.

Internally, the denomination is divided into two rival groups: the "Standard" party and the "Vessel" party. These names are derived from the respective organs of the two groups, the "Gospel Standard" and the "Earthen Vessel."

## Theological Context and Origins
The distinction of the Strict Baptists is rooted in the broader history of Baptist divisions between General and Particular Baptists. General Baptists held Arminian views and the doctrine of "General Redemption," while Particular Baptists adhered to Calvinistic theory and the doctrine of "Particular Redemption." Over time, the Calvinism of many Particular Baptist churches became more rigid, in some cases approaching or becoming Antinomianism.

While many Particular Baptist churches became more moderate in their Calvinism toward the end of the 18th century—a shift largely attributed to the writings of Andrew Fuller—the Strict Baptists maintained their rigorous stance. Furthermore, while the practice of "mixed communion" (admitting those not baptized to communion) gradually spread through other Baptist denominations, the Strict Baptists continued to insist upon baptism as a requirement for communion.

## Education and Missions
The Strict Baptists have sought to provide an educated ministry for their congregations. In 1866, the Baptist College at Manchester was instituted at Bury specifically to serve the interests of "Strict" Baptist views. This exists alongside various other voluntary Baptist colleges, such as the Bristol Baptist College and the Pastors' College in connection with the Metropolitan Tabernacle.

The denomination also engages in international outreach through the Strict Baptist Missionary Society. Founded in 1860 and refounded in 1897, this society conducts mission work in South India. As of 1905, the society's income was L1146, and its membership included 730 church members and 72 workers and pastors.
