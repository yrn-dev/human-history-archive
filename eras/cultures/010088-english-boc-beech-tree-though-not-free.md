# The Beech Tree and the Etymology of "Book"

## Botanical Characteristics and Distribution
The beech (_Fagus sylvatica_) is a member of the order Fagaceae, which also includes the oak and sweet-chestnut. It is one of the largest trees in Britain, particularly when growing in sandy or chalky soils. It is native to England from Yorkshire southwards and has been planted in Ireland and Scotland. Across temperate Europe, the beech spreads from the Mediterranean to southern Sweden and Norway. Its range extends to southern Russia, Asia Minor, and the northern provinces of Persia, reaching altitudes of approximately 5,000 feet in the Swiss Alps.

The tree is characterized by a smooth olive-grey bark and a sturdy, pillar-like stem that often reaches 15 to 20 feet in girth. Its structure consists of vertical main branches and outward-spreading subsidiary branches, creating a rounded outline. In April, slender brown pointed buds produce clear green leaves fringed with silky hairs. The inconspicuous flowers appear in May; male flowers hang in long-stalked globular clusters from the axils of lower leaves, while female flowers stand erect near the top of the shoot in tiny cups. The resulting fruit, known as mast, consists of two or three three-sided chestnut-brown fruits enclosed in a brown, tough, four-sided cupule.

## Global Species and Varieties
The genus _Fagus_ is widely distributed across temperate regions and contains about 15 species in addition to the native beech. In eastern North America, _Fagus americana_ is a widely distributed species distinguished from _F. sylvatica_ by its lighter green, more sharply-toothed leaves and paler bark. In Japan, _F. sylvatica_ var. _Sieboldi_ is an abundant deciduous forest tree. Other species are found in New Zealand, Australia, and southern Chile. In Tierra del Fuego and along the Straits of Magellan, forests consist largely of _Fagus betuloides_ (evergreen) and _F. antarctica_ (deciduous).

Cultivated varieties include the copper-beech, which possesses red colouring-matter in its sap, as well as weeping or pendulous-branched varieties and those with cut leaves.

## Ecological Impact and Uses
The beech has a strong ability to hold ground in congenial soil, and its deep shade prevents other trees from growing. While its leaves enrich the soil, the tree is more injurious to plants under its drip than other species; shade-bearing trees such as thuja, yew, and holly suffer. However, the beech is often usefully mixed with Scotch fir and oak. The ash tree is also frequently associated with the beech; ash terminal shoots can pierce through beech thickets, and the two grow well together, allowing the ash to reach great girth and height.

The timber of the beech is not noted for durability or strength. While it was previously used in turnery and mill-work, its current primary use is in the manufacture of bedsteads, chairs, and other minor articles. It is also used for charcoal and fuel.

Beech-mast serves as food for pigs, park-deer, and other game in autumn, and is used in France to feed domestic poultry and pheasants. In times of famine and distress, humans have used beech-mast as food. Well-ripened mast contains 17 to 20% non-drying oil, which is used for illumination and, in some European countries and parts of France, as a butter substitute or in cooking.

## Etymology and the Origin of "Book"
The English word "book" is linked to the old English _boc_, meaning a beech tree. This connection is found in the Anglo-Saxon _boc, bece_, or _beoce_ (similar to the Swedish _bok_ and German _Buche_), words that simultaneously meant a beech-tree and a book. This linguistic link is attributed to the ancient practice of forming Runic tablets from thin boards of beech-wood.

Philologically, the original form of "book" is postulated as a strong feminine _Boks_ in Teutonic languages, used to describe a writing-tablet. Prior, in _Popular Names of British Plants_, notes that this is identical to the Sanskrit _bokos_ (writings) and _boko_ (letter). This correspondence suggests that the Brahmins possessed the art of writing before separating from the common Indo-European stock in Upper Asia, and that Germans and English received alphabetic signs via a northern route rather than the Mediterranean.

## Regional Associations
In England, the county of Buckinghamshire is named for its fame as a beech-growing country. William Camden, writing around 1585, noted that the beech tree is predominant in the woods of the county, specifically citing the Anglo-Saxon _boc_. A remnant of ancient forest, known as Burnham Beeches, is preserved as public ground in the south of the county. Additionally, the name of buckwheat (_Bucheweizen_) is derived from the similarity of its angular seeds to beech-mast.

In Denmark, the forest is almost exclusively composed of beech, where the tree thrives better than in any other European country. This predominance is not ancient; in the first half of the 17th century, the oak was the characteristic tree of Denmark.
