# The Marri and Bugti Tribes

The Marri and Bugti are two cognate Baluch tribes inhabiting the southern buttresses of the Suliman Mountains in Baluchistan. Their combined territory is classified as a tribal area covering 7129 square miles. While this region is politically controlled from Sibi, the tribes maintain a large measure of autonomy under their own chieftains. As of 1901, the total population of this area was 38,919, divided almost equally between the two tribes.

## Origins and Characteristics
The Marri and Bugti are Rind Baluchis, who are almost certainly of Arab extraction. It is believed they arrived in Sind either alongside or following the Arab conquerors, where they intermixed with the original Hindu inhabitants. This Arab type of Baluch is described as including the finest and best of Baluch humanity.

Physically, both the Marris and Bugtis are described as a magnificent race of people. They possess Semitic features similar to those of the Bedouin and maintain a straight and lofty carriage. While the general Baluch frame is shorter, more spare, and wiry than that of the Pathan to the north, generations have provided them with a bold and manly bearing. The chiefs of the Marri and Bugti clans, in particular, are noted for their imposing presence and stately dignity.

In terms of character, the Rind Baluch is characterized as being easier to control than the Pathan due to a lack of blind allegiance to priests or bigoted fanaticism. They are described as frank, open, fairly truthful, temperate, and enduring. Courage is regarded as their highest virtue. However, they are noted as poor husbandmen due to a lack of industry and a pride that views manual labor as degrading.

## Skills and Traditions
The Marris and Bugtis are recognized as fine horsemen and good swordsmen. Horse-racing is considered a national amusement, and the Baluch breed of horses is celebrated throughout northern India. By tradition and descent, they are bandits and hereditary robbers, making them first-rate fighting men, though they rarely enlist in the Indian army. Nominally, they are Mahommedans.

## History and British Relations
The Bugti tribe, numbering approximately 15,500, occupies the hills east of the Sind-Peshin railway between Sibi and Jacobabad. They remained beyond control until the rule of Sir Robert Sandeman in Baluchistan, although an expedition against them had been organized by Sir C. Napier in 1845. Since the construction of the railway, which outflanks their country, the Bugtis have been fairly orderly.

The Marri tribe, numbering 19,161 in the 1901 census with a fighting strength of about 3,000, is located on the Dera Ghazi Khan border of Baluchistan. Their relations with the British began in 1840 with attacks on the communications of Sir John Keane's army. An attempt to punish the tribe resulted in a disastrous failure for the British; Major Clibborn was repulsed at the Naffusak Pass, where 179 men were killed and 92 wounded out of a force of 650, with many others dying from thirst and heat. During this conflict, the fort of Kahan was forced to capitulate.

Despite these early conflicts, the Marris joined the British in 1845 to fight against the Bugtis. Following the 1843 annexation of Sind, the Marris continued to cause trouble until they were pacified by the policies of Sir Robert Sandeman and General John Jacob. In 1880, during the second Afghan War, the Marris raided British communications and plundered a treasure convoy. This led to an intervention by Brigadier-General Macgregor, who led 3,070 British troops through the country. Consequently, the tribe submitted, provided hostages for future behavior, and paid 1¼ lakh (£12,500) from a fine of 2 lakhs (£20,000). Since that event, they have caused little trouble.
