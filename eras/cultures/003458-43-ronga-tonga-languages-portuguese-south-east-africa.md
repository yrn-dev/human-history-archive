# The Ronga (Tonga) Languages of Portuguese South-East Africa

The Ronga (Tonga) languages constitute a specific group of Bantu speech located within Portuguese South-East Africa. This linguistic group is primarily distributed across several key regions, including Gazaland, the Lower Limpopo valley, and Delagoa Bay. Additionally, the group extends into patches of the North Transvaal, where the dialect *Shi-gwamba* is spoken.

## Linguistic Affiliations and Influences
The Ronga (Tonga) languages are characterized by a dual relationship with other Bantu language groups. They are almost equally related to the Nyanja group (Group 41) and to Zulu. It is probable that the Ronga languages represent a mingling of these two distinct influences, although the influence of Zulu is the predominating factor.

This relationship is further reflected in the linguistic landscape of the Beira and Inhambane coast-lands and Manika. In these areas, dialects such as *Ci-shangane*, *Chopi*, or *Shi-lenge* have been significantly influenced by Zulu dialects, specifically those of the Ronga and Tebele.

## Distinctions from Other Tonga Groups
It is necessary to distinguish the Ronga (Tonga) languages of Portuguese South-East Africa from other Bantu groups that share the "Tonga" name. For instance, there is a separate group styled as the *Subiya-Tonga-Ila*. The Tonga subgroup of that family includes the dialects of Central Zambezia and the *Ba-tetela* and *Ba-ila* (*Mashukulumbwe*). 

Unlike the Ronga languages, the *Subiya-Tonga-Ila* group exhibits only a slight relationship with the Zulu group and possesses no strong affinity to *Oci-herero*.

## Geographic and Cultural Context
The Ronga languages exist within a broader region of Portuguese East Africa where other Bantu genera are present. To the north and east, the *Makua* genus occupies a large portion of Portuguese East Africa away from the Lake Nyasa watershed, divided into dialects such as *I-medo*, *I-lomwe*, *I-tugulu*, and *Anguru*. The southern part of the Makua domain is occupied by the *Ci-cuambo* of the Quelimane district.

Furthermore, the coast-lands of Portuguese East Africa, extending as far south as Angoche, host colonies of Swahili-speaking people. Swahili serves as a commercial *lingua franca* across Eastern and Central Africa, though it remains distinct from the local languages of the mainland.
