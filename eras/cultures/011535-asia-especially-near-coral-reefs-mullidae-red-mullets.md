# Marine Life and Coral Formations of Asia

## Sea Fishes of Asia
The waters of Asia host a diverse array of fish, particularly among the spiny-rayed fishes known as Acanthopterygii. Within this group, the perches (_Percidae_) are largely represented. The genus _Serranus_, which contains only one species in Europe, is very numerous in Asia and consists of very large forms. Other allied genera are abundant and extend from the Indian seas to eastern Africa.

The scaly-finned fishes, or Squamipennes, are primarily found in the seas of southern Asia, with a particular prevalence near coral reefs. This group includes the _Mullidae_, or red mullets, which are largely represented by genera that differ from those found in Europe. Other notable fish in the region include:

*   **Polynemidae**: Ranging from the Atlantic through the Indian Ocean to the Pacific. This group includes the mango fish, which is considered a great delicacy and inhabits the seas from Siam to the Bay of Bengal. Animals from this family are used to prepare isinglass.
*   **Sciaenidae**: These extend from China to the Bay of Bengal, though they are not known to the westward.
*   **Stromateidae**: Known as pomfrets, these resemble the Mediterranean dory and extend into the Pacific and China.
*   **Other Groups**: The seas of south Asia are also distributed with sword fishes (_Xiphidae_), scabbard fishes (_Trichuridae_), and lancet fishes (_Acanthuridae_). Additionally, gobies, blennies, mullets, and various genera of mackerels abound.

## Nature and Composition of Coral
Coral consists of the hard skeletons of various marine organisms, primarily composed of carbonate of lime. This material is secreted from sea-water and deposited in the tissues of certain organisms, most notably Anthozoan polyps, which are the principal source of the world's coral reefs. Other contributors include Hydroids—which were extremely abundant in Palaeozoic times but are less important for modern reef-building—and certain Algae. While the skeletons of Mollusca and Polyzoa contribute to coral masses, they are not included in the term "coral."

## Classification of Coral Reefs
Coral formations are categorized into three main types:

*   **Fringing Reefs (Shore Reefs)**: These are platforms of coral rock that extend a short distance from the shores of an island or continent. The outer wall is steep, while the inner part is lower and often intersected by channels and creeks, particularly where fresh water flows from the land. Because fresh water, mud, and sand kill corals, the most active growth occurs on the outer edge.
*   **Barrier Reefs**: These are essentially fringing reefs on a larger scale. The outer edge is much further from the shore than a fringing reef, and the channel between the reef and the land is significantly deeper. An example is the great Australian barrier reef, which extends 1,250 miles from Lady Elliot island (24 deg. S. lat.) to Torres Strait (9.5 deg. S. lat.). Opposite Cape York, the seaward edge is nearly 90 miles from the coast, with a channel depth of nearly 20 fathoms.
*   **Atolls**: These are encircling reefs with a central lagoon. They often feature narrow strips of land covered in vegetation, such as coco-nut palms, rising only a few feet above the ocean.

## Distribution in the Indian and Pacific Oceans
In the Indian Ocean, large groups of atolls are found in the Maldive and Laccadive islands off the south-west and west of India. Further south lies the Chagos group. Other reefs are located at the Seychelles, Bourbon, Mauritius, and along the north coast of Madagascar. The Cocos-Keeling Islands (12 deg. S. lat., 96 deg. E. long.) serve as typical atolls in the eastern Indian Ocean.

In the Pacific Ocean, a vast area of coral formations extends from 130 deg. E. long. to 145 deg. W. long., and from 5 deg. N. lat. to 25 deg. S. lat. Additionally, extensive reefs exist in the westernmost Hawaiian islands at approximately 25 deg. N. lat.

## Theories of Reef Formation
Two primary theories have been proposed to explain the origin of atolls and barrier reefs:

1.  **The Theory of Subsidence**: Proposed by C. Darwin, this theory suggests that reef-building corals (which do not flourish deeper than 20 fathoms) form around land that is slowly sinking. As the land subsides, the coral continues to grow upward, eventually leaving a ring of reef around a central lagoon. This was supported by J. D. Dana and later tested by a Royal Society of London expedition to Funafuti. A boring at Funafuti reached 1,114 feet, revealing a mass of pure organic limestone without any other rock, supporting the subsidence model.
2.  **The Theory of Submarine Banks**: A. von Chamisso initially proposed that corals grow on submerged banks. While Darwin rejected this due to a perceived lack of such banks, J. Murray later used soundings from the "Challenger" and "Tuscarusa" to prove the existence of submarine elevations rising from 2,000 fathoms to within a few hundred fathoms of the surface. Murray argued that these banks are gradually raised by the accumulation of skeletons from molluscs, crustacea, echinoderms, and deep-sea corals until reef-building corals can establish themselves.
