# The Native Races of Australia and the Polynesian Islands

The native populations of Australia and the Polynesian island groups are categorized into distinct racial and physical types, characterized by varying levels of civilization, physical traits, and cultural practices.

## Racial Classifications and Types
The native races of the Polynesian groups and Australia are divided into two primary types: the dark Polynesian and the light Polynesian. The dark type is black and described as being of a very low order; in certain islands, this group still retains cannibalistic habits. The white Polynesian races are described as a superior type, with the Maoris of New Zealand exhibiting characteristics of a high order.

The aboriginal tribes of Australia are classified as a low-class black race, though they are generally noted for being inoffensive and peaceful in their habits. In broader racial groupings, the populations of Australia and New Guinea are referred to as the black, primitive Australoids.

## Physical Characteristics
A distinctive cranial feature shared by Australians, Polynesians, and Papuans is dolichocephaly, or being long-headed. This means the transverse diameter of the skull is small compared to the longitudinal diameter. Among Australians and Papuans, this dolichocephaly is often occipital, meaning it is confined to the back of the head.

Specific groups within the region exhibit unique physical traits. The natives of Samoa, a pure Polynesian race, are described as the physically finest of all South Sea Islanders, possessing a healthy bronze complexion.

## Population and Distribution
Population estimates for the region vary. In Australia, there are approximately 200,000 black natives, alongside 50,000 who are Chinese and half castes. In New Zealand, the population includes Maoris (brown) and 53,000 Chinese and half castes. Other island groups include Fiji, with 121,000 Polynesian people of black and brown types, and Papua, with 400,000 Polynesian people of black and brown types. The natives of Papua (New Guinea) are described as being in a very low state of civilization.

In the case of Samoa, the population was 20,000 at the time of French annexation in 1842, but it later reduced to just over 4,000.

## Culture and Social Practices
Cultural practices vary significantly across the islands. In Samoa, traditions identify the islands as the colonizing center for their race. Historically, Samoans were known for elaborate tattooing before the introduction of civilization. While they practiced cannibalism, it was attributed to taste rather than religion; their religious sacrifices consisted of fowls and swine. Their native drink is known as *kava*, and their houses are uniquely built on platforms raised from the ground. Samoans are described as friendly, hospitable, and brave, though also bloodthirsty, morose, and naturally indolent, despite being keen traders and industrious. Similar to the Tahitians, the Samoans had organized institutions of infanticide and systematized debauchery.

In the Loyalty Islands, the inhabitants are Melanesians. While they previously had a reputation as dangerous cannibals, they are now considered among the most civilized Melanesians.

## Language and Origins
The distribution of these peoples is a subject of ethnographic study. Many inhabitants of the South Seas, including Hawaii and New Zealand, speak languages that are either Malay or have been strongly influenced by Malay. One theory suggests the Malays were a seafaring race that dispersed from an island center, possibly Java. Another theory proposes a larger linguistic group comprising Polynesian, Micronesian, Malay, Monkhmer, and Munda languages, originating from the extreme west.

## Prehistoric Remains and Subsidence
In the Pacific, specifically in Kusaie and Ponape, massive stone structures and cyclopean ramparts built of basaltic blocks exist, including artificial harbors and canals. These colossal structures, which extend as far as Easter Island, are not believed to have been built by current Polynesian or Melanesian peoples. Instead, they are attributed to a prehistoric race of Neolithic man—possibly represented by the more regular-featured and light-colored Polynesian groups—who possessed an advanced general culture. 

Evidence from the Funafuti borings of 1897 indicates that Polynesia is an area of recent subsidence. It is suggested that land connections were once more continuous, allowing dolmen-builders from advanced Asiatic lands like Japan and Korea to spread into the Polynesian world. As these land areas subsided, the culture degenerated, though oral traditions and folklore still contain echoes of philosophies and theogonies.
