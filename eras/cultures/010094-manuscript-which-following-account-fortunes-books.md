# MANUSCRIPT

The history of the manuscript is defined by the evolution of materials, the transition of physical forms, and the eventual intersection with the invention of printing.

## Evolution of Form and Material
In the period when papyrus was the predominant material for writing, books typically took the form of the *volumen*, or roll, which was wound around one or more sticks. The *volumen* featured a series of narrow writing columns. As parchment began to supersede papyrus, the *codex*—the modern form of the book—gained popularity. The term *codex* originated from the Latin word for a block of wood or a tree stump, referring to the three-leaved tablets created by sawing such a block.

The influence of the *volumen* persisted in the layout of early codices. For example, the 4th-century Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus of the Bible contain three and four columns per page, respectively. By the 5th century, the Codex Alexandrinus utilized only two columns, and by the 6th century, the Codex Bezae used only one. From the 6th century until the invention of printing, the arrangement of books remained relatively conservative, with scribes using single or double columns based on convenience.

External forms also exhibited long-term consistency. A miniature in the 8th-century Codex Amiatinus, written in England, depicts a book with a red leather cover; the pattern of this cover closely resembles the 15th-century red leather bindings found in the Biblioteca Laurenziana at Florence. Similarly, certain small stamps used in 15th-century Oxford bindings are nearly indistinguishable from those used in England three centuries prior.

## Transition to Printing
During the early stages of printing, the visual difference between a manuscript written in a formal book-hand and an early printed copy of the same work from the same district was very slight. Printers typically based their type on handwriting that scribes considered appropriate for that specific class of work. Many elements were still added by hand to mimic manuscript decorations, including paragraph marks, initial letters, headlines, illustrations, and chapter headings.

Early printed books generally lacked title-pages and often contained no statement regarding who produced the book, where, or when. Such information, if included, was placed in a paragraph at the end of the book known as a colophon. Printers often attached a device to the colophon, such as a fanciful design or the arms of the printer or the town.

The first known dated title-page appeared in 1470, printed by Arnold ther Hoernen at Cologne; it consisted of a nine-line paragraph on a blank page for the work *Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie Semper Virginis*. In 1476, Erhard Ratdolt and partners in Venice printed a Latin calendar with a title-page featuring their names, the date, and descriptive verses surrounded by a four-piece border. For the following twenty years, title-pages remained simple, often consisting only of a short title and a woodcut or device.

## Notable Collections and Figures
Manuscripts have been central to the libraries of royalty and scholars. King George III accumulated a library containing 440 manuscripts and over 80,000 pamphlets and books, which passed to the British Museum in 1823. 

Francis Douce (1757–1834), an English antiquary and former keeper of manuscripts at the British Museum, left a diverse legacy upon his death. By his will, he bequeathed his illuminated manuscripts and printed books to the Bodleian library. His own manuscript works were left to the British Museum with the specific instruction that the chest containing them remain closed until January 1, 1900.

## Historical and Literary Manuscripts
Manuscripts have served as primary sources for historical narratives. Major Stewart translated a manuscript provided by Major Davy regarding the life of Timour, covering his birth to his forty-first year, though the final thirty years of his conquests were missing. Stewart noted that two other manuscripts containing the complete work existed. Additionally, the historian Nicephorus Gregoras left last books that remained in manuscript form.

In the realm of medicine and science, manuscripts have been used to preserve posthumous works. For instance, the books and manuscripts of a physician who retired to Carcassonne were bequeathed to J. Lordat, leading to the 1810 publication of two volumes of *Consultations de medecine*.
