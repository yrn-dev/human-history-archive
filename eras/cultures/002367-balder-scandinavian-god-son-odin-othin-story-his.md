# BALDER

Balder is recognized as a Scandinavian god and is the son of Odin (also referred to as Othin). 

## Literary Accounts of Balder
The narrative regarding the death of Balder is preserved in two distinct forms within the archive's records. One version is provided by Saxo in the *Gesta Danorum*, while a different account is found in the prose Edda, specifically within the section titled *Gylfaginning*, chapter 49.

## Context of Scandinavian Mythology
Balder belongs to a wider pantheon of gods whose influence extended across the Scandinavian and Germanic peoples. According to old heathen faith, the royal pedigrees of the Swedish, Norwegian, Danish, Anglian, and Saxon kings—and likely those of Scandinavian and German kings generally—were traced back to Odin or his heroic sons and immediate companions.

The records indicate that after the worship of Odin was extinguished, these mythical genealogies were altered. In some instances, the line was extended to include Noah or Japhet, and Odin was degraded from his position at the top of the lineage to a simple human member. Despite the introduction of Christianity, the poems and tales of the Edda do not present the ancient gods in a manner that is intentionally degrading or odious.

## Related Deities and Figures
Balder's father, Odin, is described as the "Scandinavian Jove." Odin's own lineage traces back to his father Bor and his grandfather Buri. Buri was born from rocks and licked by the cow Andhumla (darkness). Bor was the son of Buri and Bestla, who was the daughter of the giant Bolthorn (evil).

Within the divine sphere, other figures associated with Odin's court include Brage, who serves as Odin's counsellor and is the god of poets. The archive also mentions the valkyries, such as Gondul and Skogul, who serve as "angels of the dead," tasked with bringing brave warriors to Valhal.

## The Hall of Odin
The divine environment associated with Balder's father includes Valhal, a hall prepared for a host of the slain and the chosen. In the accounts of the gods, it is noted that an empty place stands in Odin's hall for a king of Yngve's race. The arrival of noble chiefs and brave kings, such as Hakon, is celebrated in this abode, where they are welcomed by the council of the gods to drain the cup or repeat their fights.
