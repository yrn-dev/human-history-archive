# Ariya- and the Term Aryan

## Etymology and Linguistic Connections
The term *ariya-* (Old Persian) is closely related to the Sanskrit *arya-* and *arya-*, as well as the Zend *airya-*. From the earliest recorded occurrences, it is evident that the word served as a national name used not only in India but also in Persia and Bactria. 

The precise origin of the word remains a subject of scholarly debate. Max Muller asserted a connection between the term and the Sanskrit word for earth, *ira*, though this claim is considered far from certain. Spiegel noted that while the word can be linked to a root *ar-*, there are several roots of that form with differing meanings, and no certain criterion exists to determine which specific root is related. Other proposed connections are similarly doubted. For instance, an attempt by Pictet to establish a connection with *Erin* (Ireland) is regarded as impossible. However, it is possible that the word shares an origin with the *Ario-* found in Celtic and Germanic names, such as *Ariovistus*. Additionally, the name of Armenia (Old Persian *Armina-*) has been connected to the term, but its origin remains uncertain.

## Usage in Sanskrit and Other Languages
Within the Sanskrit language, a distinction is likely necessary between two different words: *arya*, which is the origin of "Aryan" and the source of the usual term *arya*, and *arya*, which frequently appears in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet for deities. In certain passages, *aryas* may also be interpreted as the genitive of *ari*, a word explained as "pious, devoted, active."

In other contexts, the word *ariya* is translated as "noble," as seen in the terms "Noble Path" and "Noble Truth." In these instances, *ariya* also means Aryan. The negative form, "un-Aryan," is used to describe low aims. While the term retains a tribal meaning, the ethical meaning of the word had likely become predominant in the language of that period.

## Geographic and Cultural Distribution
The Aryans of India are considered among the most settled and civilized of the Asiatic races, with the type appearing in its purest form in the north and north-west of the region. The spoken languages of northern India are thoroughly Sanskritic in their vocables, though they vary significantly from one another. In contrast, the languages of southern India are Dravidian rather than Sanskritic.

Beyond India, the linguistic landscape shifts. West of the Indus, dialects move closer to Persian. Persian, in turn, meets Turki and Arabic west of the Tigris and along the Caspian and Turkoman desert. While northern Indian languages use modifications of ancient Pali written from left to right, the Persian character is used for writing Urdu (or Hindostani), the mixed language of the Mahommedan conquerors of northern India, which is written from right to left.

## Related Linguistic Classifications
The term is associated with the broader category of Indo-Aryan languages. These languages often feature *tatsamas*, which are words borrowed in modern times from Sanskrit. For example, literary Bengali is heavily influenced by *tatsamas*, sometimes to the point where they replace genuine Bengali (*tadbhava*) words in writing. In contrast, languages like Assamese are more free from such borrowings, and the Bihari dialects contain few *tatsamas*. In Marathi, the use of *tatsamas* was encouraged by Brahmins to lend dignity to the vernacular, although the language also possesses a large proportion of *desyas*—words descended from old Primary Prakrits spoken outside the Midland that are strange to Sanskrit.
