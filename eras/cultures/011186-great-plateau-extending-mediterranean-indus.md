# The Great Plateau of Western and Central Asia

The great plateau extending from the Mediterranean to the Indus is an extensive high region characterized by significant variations in climate, productivity, and geography. Spanning approximately 2,500 miles from east to west, the plateau has a breadth of over 600 miles in the west and is nowhere less than 250 miles wide. Its general altitude ranges between 2,000 and 8,000 feet above sea level.

## Regional Divisions and Characteristics
The plateau is divided into two distinct halves with contrasting environmental and social conditions:

*   **The Eastern Half:** Comprising Baluchistan, Afghanistan, and Persia, this region is described as poor and unproductive. It is characterized by a severe winter and extremely hot summers. Rainfall is very scanty, and running waters are rarely found except among the mountains that form the scarps of the elevated country. Consequently, the population is sparse, frequently nomadic, and inclined toward plunder, with limited progress in civilization and the arts.
*   **The Western Half:** This area falls within the Turkish empire. Compared to the east, its climate is less hot, its natural productiveness is much greater, and its population is more settled and generally more advanced.

## Geographical Boundaries and Topography
The portion of Asia west of British India (excluding Syria and Arabia) forms a plateau of an area comparable to that of Tibet, though at a lower altitude. Its boundaries are defined as follows:

*   **Southern Border:** The border runs along the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Sea, and the Tigris, extending westward to the north-east angle of the Levant.
*   **Northern Border:** The highland follows nearly 36 deg. N. to the southern shore of the Caspian Sea, continuing to the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmora.
*   **Eastern Flank:** This edge follows a line of hills located a short distance from the Indus between the Himalaya and the mouth of the river, approximately on the 72nd meridian. While these hills generally do not exceed 5,000 or 4,000 feet, some summits reach 10,000 feet or more.
*   **Southern and South-Western Face:** This face closely follows the coast of the Persian Gulf from the mouth of the Indus. Further west, it is formed by a mountain scarp that flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris, rising in many points to 10,000 feet. This scarp extends through Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian before turning along the Mediterranean's north-eastern angle and the Taurus range.

## Mountain Ranges and Water Systems
The north-eastern portion of the Afghan table-land abuts on Tibet and the Himalaya, forming a continuous mountain mass between the 71st and 72nd meridians and 34 deg. and 36 deg. N. From the intersection of the 36th parallel and the 71st meridian, an unbroken range stretches northeast toward the Tibetan plateau and west toward the Caspian. 

The high-altitude north-eastern section of this range separates the headwaters of the Indus and its Kabul tributary (which flow south through Peshawar into the Arabian Sea) from the headwaters of the Oxus, which flow toward the Aral Sea. The western part of this range was known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons and decreases in height west of the 65th meridian.

In terms of hydrography, the Iranian (or Perso-Afghan) uplands are categorized as part of the interior or inland basins of Asia. These inland basins, which also include the Tibetan plateau, the Gobi depression, and the basins of Asia Minor and Syro-Arabia, total approximately 3,141,500 square miles.

## Related Regional Highlands
The archive notes other significant high regions in the vicinity:
*   **British India:** The Indian peninsula south of 25 deg. N. is a table-land with a general level between 1,000 and 3,000 feet, though western points rise above 8,000 feet.
*   **Baluchistan:** The central Quetta-Pishin plateau serves as the dominant water-divide of Baluchistan and the base of the Kandahar highway. To its south lies the Kach Gandava, an alluvial bay extending 130 miles from the Indus to Sibi, known for extreme heat and dust-storms.
*   **Tibet:** A great elevated table-land that has been the subject of extensive modern scientific exploration by figures such as Nain Singh, Krishna, and Sven Hedin.
