# Pronominal Prefixes and Concord in the Guinea Coast and Bantu Languages

The linguistic landscape of the Guinea coast and Central Africa is characterized by specific grammatical structures, most notably the use of pronominal prefixes and systems of concord. These features are prominently found in the Bantu family of languages, as well as in certain languages of the north Guinea coast and northern Togoland.

## Pronominal Prefixes and Concord in the North Guinea Coast
The Timne language, along with cognate languages located in Sierra Leone and the north Guinea coast, employs pronominal prefixes and a system of concord. The application of concord in these languages is described as being precisely similar to the practice found in Bantu languages. However, despite this structural similarity in the use of prefixes and concord, there is absolutely no resemblance between the north Guinea group and Bantu languages regarding their word-roots, which include verbs, pronouns, numerals, and substantives.

Other languages in the region, such as those of Ashanti, the lower Niger, the eastern Niger delta, Benue, and northern Togoland, also utilize simple prefixes. These are used to indicate the quality of a noun and to express the difference between singular and plural. While these prefixes sometimes fall into "classes," they do not correspond to the prefixes of the Bantu type.

## The Bantu System of Concord
In Bantu languages, substantives are divided into genders or classes, which are indicated by a pronominal particle prefixed to the root. These prefixes generally function as either singular or plural. With the exception of the "abstract" prefix *Bu* (No. 14), singular prefixes cannot be used as plurals, and vice versa. There is often a correspondence between the two; for example, prefix No. 8 serves as a plural to No. 7, and No. 2 serves as a plural to No. 3.

The "concord" is the system wherein the pronominal particle or prefix of the governing noun is attached as a prefix to the roots of other words in the sentence connected to that noun, including verbs, pronouns, prepositions, and adjectives. While these particles may evolve to differ in form from the original prefix of the substantive, they share a common origin. These particles must always precede the verbal, adjectival, pronominal, and nominal roots, although they may follow the roots of some prepositions and auxiliary prefix-participles used in verb conjugation.

## Bantu Prefix Classes and Variations
There are approximately sixteen prefixes common to the entire Bantu group. Some specific examples and their variations include:
*   **Prefix 2 (*Ba-* or *Aba-*):** In archaic speech (such as the languages of Mt. Elgon), this appears as *Baba-*. In less corrupt tongues, the concord is *-ba-*, though it may degenerate into *Va-*, *Wa-*, *Ma-*, or *A-*.
*   **Prefix 4 (*Mi-*):** Its concord is *-gi-*, *-i-*, *-ji-*, or sometimes *-mi-*.
*   **Prefix 5 (*Li-* or older *Di-*):** The concord is the same, though the prefix may be dropped or become *Ri-*, *I-*, *Ji-*, or *Ni-*.
*   **Prefix 7 (*Ki-*):** This may transition through *Ci-* into *Si-* (or *Shi*), with a resembling concord.
*   **Prefix 8:** The plural of the 7th prefix, originally *Ibi-* or *Mbibi-*. It is highly variable across dialects, appearing as *Vi-*, *Pi-*, *Fi-*, *Fy-*, *By-*, *Zwi-*, *Zi-*, and others.
*   **Prefix 10:** Originally *Ti-*, *Tin-*, *Zi-*, or *Zin-*. In some languages, such as the Swahili of Zanzibar, the prefix may disappear as a definite article for nouns but persist in the concord.

Additional prefixes exist in specific regions. Prefix No. 17 (*Fi-* or *Vi-*) has a diminutive sense and is found in Cameroon and the northern Congo basin. Prefixes No. 18 (*Ogu-*) and No. 19 (*Aga-*) are used in an augmentative sense and appear to be confined to the Masaba and Luganda dialects and some branches of Unyoro. Prefix No. 20 (*Mu-*) functions as a preposition meaning "in" or "into" and lacks a complete concord.

## Linguistic Construction and Phonology
Bantu languages are agglutinative, meaning syntax is formed by adding suffixes and prefixes to a root, without the use of infixes. The root remains practically unchanging, except for its terminal vowel, though preceding prefixes or succeeding suffixes may modify the pronunciation of the first or penultimate vowel or consonant.

Regarding gender, Bantu pronouns and concord do not recognize sexual gender. Instead, sexual gender is indicated by specific prefixes—such as a male prefix often identical to the word for "father," or a feminine prefix (*na* or *nya*) connected to the root for "mother," or a suffix (*ka* or *kazi*) indicating "female" or "wife." The first and second prefixes are usually restricted to humanity and living beings.

Phonologically, the Western group of Bantu languages is akin to the Negro languages of West-Central and Western Africa. However, most Bantu groups do not adopt the *kp* and *gb* combinations characteristic of African speech between the Guinea coast and the Upper Nile.
