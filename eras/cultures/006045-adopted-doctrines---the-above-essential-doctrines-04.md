# Adopted Doctrines

The archive contains records of various adopted doctrines across religious, philosophical, and scientific traditions, ranging from ancient Indian and Greek thought to early modern physics and political economy.

## Buddhist and Indian Doctrines
Original Buddhism is characterized by distinctive essential doctrines that separated it from previous Indian teachings. In establishing these, the Buddha rejected several existing systems, including the ritualistic magic and sacrifices of the brahmin schools, the soul-theory and asceticism of the Jains, the animistic superstitions of the general population, and the pantheistic speculations found in the pre-Buddhistic *Upanishads*.

Despite these rejections, Buddhism retained the belief in transmigration—the concept that the soul, following the death of the body, enters other bodies, which may be those of gods, beasts, or men. This belief was part of a widely held animistic creed. In India, this was integrated into an ethical framework through the doctrine of *Karma*. Under this doctrine, a person's physical advantages or social position in life are viewed as the result of their actions in a previous birth. For example, a person born blind is explained as having had "lust of the eye" in a previous birth, while someone with unusual hearing is explained as having loved to listen to the preaching of the law in a prior existence.

## Greek Philosophical and Mystery Doctrines
In ancient Greece, various doctrines emerged regarding the nature of truth and divinity. Protagoras proposed the doctrine that "man is the measure of all things," which Aristotle interpreted as the claim that perception or knowledge is the measure of all things. While Aristotle dismissed this as trivial, other perspectives suggest that insisting on the relative nature of cognizable truth was a significant doctrine, especially compared to the unmeasured conceptions of scientific research common during Protagoras's time.

Additionally, the Grecian mysteries contained "concealed treasure[s] of doctrine." According to Warburton, the interior doctrine communicated within these mysteries involved the existence of one Supreme Divinity combined with the Euemeristic creed, which posited that pagan gods were originally mere men. Other Greek thought touched upon metempsychosis; for instance, Xenophanês presented this doctrine in a manner that enforced a practical lesson against cruelty, highlighting the sympathy between humans and specific animals.

## Theological and Physical Doctrines
The archive records several doctrines concerning the nature of God and the universe. The "immanence doctrine" in philosophy and theology describes God as existing in and throughout the created world, as opposed to Deism, which views God as separate from and above the universe. This is distinct from pantheism, which identifies the universe as a mode or form of deity, or naturalism, which regards the deity as the sum of the forces of nature. Some views within Christendom have used this to postulate a divine element in human beings to bridge the gap between the finite and the infinite.

In the realm of physical science, René Descartes developed a physical doctrine of the universe titled *The World*. This work included fundamental views on the rotation of the earth around the sun and the infinitude of the universe. Due to the hostility of the church—evidenced by the condemnation of Galileo, who adopted the Copernican theory as a hypothesis—Descartes abandoned the publication of *The World* to avoid open conflict with established doctrines.

## Political and Legal Doctrines
In the field of political economy, the work of Cairnes, specifically *Some Leading Principles of Political Economy, newly Expounded* (1874), serves as a commentary on the principal doctrines of the English school. These include doctrines regarding capital, labour, wages, cost of production, value, and international values. While Cairnes remained in fundamental harmony with Mill regarding the general conception of the science, he differed from Mill on nearly all cardinal doctrines.

Regarding legal and religious adherence in Badakshan, the population is divided by adopted creeds. The Tajiks, Turks, and Arabs of Badakshan proper profess the orthodox doctrines of the Mahommedan law as adopted by the Sunnite sect, while the Tajiks in the mountainous districts profess the Shi'ite creed.
