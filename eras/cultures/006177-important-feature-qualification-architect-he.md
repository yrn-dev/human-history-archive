# Qualifications and Role of the Architect

## Professional Qualifications
An essential feature in the qualification of an architect is a thorough conversance with the by-laws of various districts or towns. This knowledge must encompass the requirements for different classes of buildings as well as the special features associated with various portions of those buildings. 

Such expertise is particularly critical when designing factories, places for the slaughter of beasts intended for human consumption, or facilities for the making and storage of different patent foods. In most countries, these specific types of buildings are subject to stringent by-laws enforced by public health authorities. For example, in England, borough or district authorities carry out the Public Health Acts and By-laws. These authorities appoint inspectors who study public health regarding sanitary arrangements and possess special powers to address defects in buildings that might affect the cleanly preparation of food, or to deal with improper or defective food.

## Scope of Design and Supervision
The architect is responsible for the design and superintendence of a wide array of structures. These include:
*   **Residential and Domestic:** Dwelling-houses, domestic buildings, and dwellings for the working class.
*   **Public and Institutional:** Schools, hospitals, libraries, hotels, and churches.
*   **Commercial and Industrial:** Shops, factories of all general trades, studios, and electric power stations.
*   **Specialized Facilities:** Cold storage buildings, slaughterhouses, and stables.

## Administrative Duties and Documentation
Once an architect's drawings have been approved by the client or owner, as well as by local authorities or the district surveyor and adjoining owners, specific copies must be deposited. In London, one copy made on linen is typically deposited with the London County Council or the district surveyor. If a lease of the land is granted, a second copy is prepared for the freeholder, and a third is provided to the builder. In complex scenarios, such as in the city of London where land may have four or five distinct owners, the architect may be required to prepare numerous complete copies for all interested parties.

## Distinction from the Builder and Clerk of Works
While the duties of a builder are similar to those of an architect, there are distinct differences in their qualifications and roles. The builder is not expected to plan or design; rather, their role is to execute the architect's plans and designs through the actual work of building. The builder's qualifications must emphasize constructive knowledge, including a thorough understanding of materials, their brands, and the distinguishing features of good and bad quality in each class. Additionally, the builder must know acts relating to the pulling down of old buildings, as well as the erection of gantries, hoardings, shoring, and scaffoldings.

On large or important projects, an architect may utilize a delegate or clerk of works. This individual is a practical man—distinguished from an architectural draughtsman—who remains on-site during progress to ensure the use of the best construction and materials. The clerk of works reports any difficulties to the architect for instruction. The salary for this role is paid by the client and is separate from the architect's remuneration.

## Historical and Stylistic Influence
Architectural qualifications and styles have evolved over time. During the Renaissance, the doorway was the principal characteristic of the style. Italian architects often relied on the doorway for effect, while in France and Germany, doors were elaborately carved, sometimes featuring architectural elements like niches, pediments, and entablatures with columns. In the Italian cinquecento period, side pilasters were often enriched with elaborate carving, a method seen in the museums of Rome and the doorways of Venice.

In England, architects were often influenced by pattern books that suggested designs of a rococo character. However, through Inigo Jones and the influence of Palladio, English architects eventually returned to a simpler and purer Italian style.

## Notable Practitioners
Charles Bulfinch (1763–1844) was a prominent American architect born in Boston. Educated at Harvard and the Boston Latin school, and having studied in Europe, he became the first professional architect to practice in Boston in 1787. His work, characterized by refinement of taste, simplicity, and sincerity, influenced early American architecture. His projects included the "new" State House (1798) and the old Federal Street theatre (1793). Bulfinch also served as the architect of the National Capitol in Washington starting in 1818, where he designed the new portico and western approach, and constructed the rotunda based on his own plans and suggestions from his predecessor, B.H. Latrobe.
