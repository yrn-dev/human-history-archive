# The Concept of Salvation in Human History

The idea of salvation has appeared across various cultures and religious systems as a response to human suffering, mental distress, and the perception that earthly existence is unsatisfactory or wrong. It has manifested as a desire for emancipation from the self, a means of escaping temporal or eternal torment, or a method of restoring a perceived cosmic order.

## Early Religious and Philosophical Perspectives

In the context of early Indian thought, the idea of emancipation from the self was often difficult for the general population to grasp. Consequently, the teachings of the Buddha were sometimes interpreted by his followers not as emancipation from the self, but as salvation from sufferings and misfortunes both in the present life and hereafter. These teachers utilized existing superstitions, particularly the belief in the transmigration of the soul, to urge virtue. They warned that a lack of virtue could lead to rebirth in miserable forms or descent into the numerous hells of torment described by Brahminical teachers, presenting Buddha as the savior from such unlimited torment.

Among the Hebrews, the realization of the disorders and miseries of life—often attributed to the lawless acts of men—led to the conclusion that salvation could only be achieved by subduing oneself to the service of the one God who rules heaven and earth. This perspective crystallized into a belief in the Promise of the One True God and the eventual coming of a Messiah or Savior.

In contrast, the Greeks approached the problem of "right living" through inquiry rather than through a patriarchal deity. Living in a world of multiple gods or a cold, impersonal Fate, the Greek perspective lacked a definite correlation between the right-living man and the will of a single God.

## Gnostic and Early Christian Interpretations

In the system of Basilides, salvation is introduced as a response to the extreme pitch of confusion in the world. To address this, the supreme God sends his *Nous*, also called Christ, into the world. Within this framework, salvation is defined as the process where that which was combined for evil is once more separated. This process is linked to the mission of the Savior, which initiates the restoration of all things and the great separation of the sexes. 

Regarding the identity of the Savior, Basilides recognized an earthly Jesus but denied his sinlessness. Other accounts vary: Irenaeus suggests the Savior appeared only as a phantasm, while the *Excerpta ex Theodoto* claims the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus during his baptism in the form of a dove.

Other early Christian perspectives linked salvation to baptism. Tertullian compared believers to "little fishes" who, following the example of Jesus Christ, are born in the water and remain in a state of salvation only by abiding in the water. Similarly, Hilary wrote that Jesus was "born again" through baptism to become a perfect son of God.

## Medieval and Institutional Developments

By the 13th century, the Catholic Church developed a complex system regarding penance and the removal of punishment. It was held that while Absolution removed guilt and freed a person from eternal punishment, "satisfactions" were required to free the penitent from temporal punishment in this life or in purgatory.

This system introduced the "treasury of merits" (*thesaurus meritorum*), which consisted of the inexhaustible merits of Christ and the good deeds of saints or living persons that exceeded what was necessary for their own salvation. The pope could draw from this treasury to grant Indulgences to the faithful, transferring merits to those who required them to reduce their temporal pains.

## Linguistic and Political Contexts

The concept of salvation also intersected with linguistic evolution. In the Koran, Mahomet used the word *forqan* to mean "revelation," although the word originally meant "redemption." This shift may have been influenced by Aramaic terms for "salvation," which were central to the faith of Christians and those Israelites who looked for a Messiah.

In a political context, the term has been used to describe ideological retreats. Following the overthrow of the French Revolution, parties of reaction sought "salvation" through the "union of altar and throne," a move that contributed to the gulf between Catholicism and Liberalism.
