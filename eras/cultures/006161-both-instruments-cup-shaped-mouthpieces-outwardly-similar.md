# Cup-Shaped Mouthpieces in Wind Instruments

The cup-shaped mouthpiece is a common structural feature across various brass and woodwind instruments, serving as the interface where the performer's lips are stretched to act as double reeds, set in vibration by the breath. While many of these mouthpieces appear outwardly similar, subtle differences in their internal geometry and the bore of the instrument significantly influence the resulting timbre and tone quality.

## The Trumpet and Bugle
The trumpet and bugle both utilize cup-shaped mouthpieces that are outwardly similar, but they differ fundamentally in their internal construction and the bore of their main tubes. The trumpet features a cylindrical tube that widens only at the bell, while the bugle possesses a conical bore of rapidly increasing diameter, a characteristic preserved from its origin as a bull's horn.

The distinction in tone—the trumpet's brilliant blaring tone versus the bugle's more veiled but penetrating quality—is attributed to the shape of the mouthpiece basins where they open into the tube. In the trumpet, this area is angular, whereas in the bugle, it is bevelled.

## The Cornet Family
The cornet family demonstrates a wide variety of mouthpiece applications across different historical iterations:

*   **Modern Cornet a Pistons:** This brass instrument uses a cup-shaped mouthpiece that is larger than that of the trumpet. The timbre is determined by the shape of the cup, the bore proportions, and the length of the tube. Specifically, the cornet's loose, coarse tone quality results from the absence of angles at the bottom of the cup, which instead curves into the bore. In contrast, the angles found in trumpet mouthpieces cause the column of air to break, producing a brilliant tone.
*   **Obsolete Woodwind Cornets:** These instruments, now obsolete, also featured cup-shaped mouthpieces and conical bores without bells. They existed in straight and curved forms. Some straight cornets used a detachable cup-shaped mouthpiece similar to the trumpet, which produced a shrill tone due to the "throat"—the sharp angle of incidence where the mouthpiece communicates with the tube. Other straight cornets, known in Germany as *stiller* or *sanfter Zinck* and in Italy as *cornetto muto*, lacked a cup-shaped mouthpiece entirely. Instead, they featured a funnel-shaped mouthpiece cut directly into the wood, which merged gradually into the bore to produce a softer, sweeter tone.

## The Horn and Saxhorns
The mouthpiece of the horn differs substantially from that of the trumpet. Strictly speaking, it does not have a cup; the interior is shaped like a truncated cone or funnel. This funnel-shaped bore converges into the main conical bore of the instrument without the sharp edges or breaks found in cup-shaped mouthpieces used for the trumpet or bombardon.

In orchestral settings, the choice of mouthpiece is a test of skill. First horns typically use a smaller and shallower mouthpiece to more easily obtain higher harmonics, while second horns use a different mouthpiece to emphasize lower harmonics.

In contrast, the saxhorns—a family of hybrid instruments designed by Adolphe Sax—were created by adapting valves and a cup-shaped mouthpiece to instruments of the calibre of the bugle. The German equivalent of the saxhorns is the Flugelhorn family.

## Ancient Roman Instruments
Evidence from the Roman era indicates that the ancestors of modern brass instruments utilized similar mouthpiece designs. The *tuba*, *buccina*, and *cornu* all possessed well-formed mouthpieces that differed little from the modern cup-shaped form used for the trumpet, tubas, and trombone. 

The *buccina*, a brass instrument used extensively in the Roman army, consisted of a narrow cylindrical bore tube approximately 11 to 12 feet long, played with a cup-shaped mouthpiece. Archaeological evidence, including Pompeian buccinas and bronze Etruscan cornua found in the British Museum, confirms the use of well-preserved cup-shaped mouthpieces during this period. Additionally, a carved specimen on an ivory *capsa* or *pyxis* suggests that even the short horn of the 4th century was provided with a mouthpiece.
