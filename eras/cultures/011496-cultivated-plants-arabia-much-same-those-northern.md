# Cultivated Plants of Arabia

The cultivated flora of Arabia shares significant similarities with the plants found in northern India. Primary crops grown in the region include barley, wheat, and the common *Sorghum*, as well as indigo, cotton, and lemons. Coffee is also cultivated, though its growth is restricted to the slopes of the western hills.

## Regional Variations and Natural Vegetation

The south-western region of Arabia, referred to as Arabia Felix, possesses mountainous areas with summits reaching 6000 or 7000 feet. In these elevations, rainfall is sufficient to support a more luxuriant vegetation. The flora of the valleys in this region resembles that of the less elevated parts of Afghanistan and Baluchistan, as well as similarly situated areas of southern Persia, and it shares characteristics with the hotter Mediterranean region.

In these south-western countries, aromatic shrubs are found in abundance. Trees are rare and are almost entirely restricted to *Dodonaea*, *Celtis*, *Pistacia*, the date palm, and poplars. The dry hills are covered by *Astragalus* and prickly forms of *Statice*. During the spring, there is an abundance of herbaceous vegetation and many bulbous plants; some of these are identical in species or genera to those of the Syrian region, and some extend as far as the Himalaya.

## The Date Palm

The date palm (*Phoenix dactylifera*) is a beautiful tree that grows to heights between 60 and 80 feet. It features a stem marked with old leaf-scars and a crown of shining, graceful pinnate leaves. Because the trees are unisexual, artificial means are required to fertilize the female flowers, which spring in branching spadices from the axils of the leaves. The resulting fruit is fleshy and oblong, containing a single, deeply furrowed hard seed. Under cultivation, the fruit varies in quality, color, and size.

In Arabia, the date palm is the staple article of food and serves as the chief source of national wealth. Fresh dates in Central Arabia are described as delicious, healthy, and pleasant, lacking the "heating" quality found in preserved fruit.

The species ranges from Northern Africa and the Canary Islands through south-east Asia to India, where it has been highly prized since remotest antiquity. Its use and cultivation were recorded on the mural tablets of ancient Assyrians. While introduced to the Mediterranean shores of Europe, the fruit does not ripen in the north; consequently, European plants are used primarily for the celebration of the Passover by Jews and for the Christian festival of Palm Sunday. Spanish missionaries introduced the tree to the new world, where it is now grown in Mexico and the dry districts of the south-western United States.

## Comparative Geography

The extremely hot and dry tracts extending from Arabia through Baluchistan and south Persia to Sind are characterized by a uniformity of life types that closely resemble those of neighboring hot and dry regions of Africa. In contrast, the region of high temperature and heavy periodical summer rains—including southern China, India, and the Indo-Chinese peninsula—exhibits a different similarity in plants.

Further north, the flora of Asia Minor and northern Persia differs little from that of southern Europe. In areas of abundant rain, these mountains are clothed in forests of *Corylus*, *Carpinus*, *Acer*, *Ulmus*, *Fagus*, and *Quercus*, along with various Coniferae.
