# Bag-pipes of Calabria, Greece, and Rome

The bag-pipe is a complex reed instrument of great antiquity that serves as a link between the primitive organ and the syrinx. Its primary function is to provide a reservoir for wind supply, allowing the performer to regulate sound-producing pressure using their arm. The instrument generally consists of an air-tight leather bag with three to five apertures containing fixed stocks or short tubes. These stocks serve as air-chambers to protect the reeds and as sockets for three types of pipes: a blow-pipe for insufflation, a melody-pipe known as a "chaunter" (or chanter), and "drones," which are cylindrical pipes that produce a single fixed note.

## The Calabrian Bag-pipe
The Calabrian bag-pipe is categorized as a Class I instrument, meaning it is inflated by the mouth via a blow-pipe. Its bag is constructed from goatskin with the hair left on. The instrument features two chaunters and two drones, all of which are fixed into a single stock. Both the drones and the chaunters utilize double reeds. Each chaunter is equipped with three or four finger-holes; specifically, the right-hand pipe has its fourth hole covered by a key that is enclosed within a perforated box.

## The Askaulus and Tibia Utricularis
The ancient Greek bag-pipe is referred to as the *askaulos* (likely derived from *askos*, meaning bag, and *aulos*, meaning pipe), while the Roman version is known as the *tibia utricularis* (or *utricularium*). Like the Calabrian variety, these instruments were inflated by the mouth and belonged to the same class of instrument. However, it is not certain whether these ancient versions possessed drones.

The existence of the *askaulos* in classical Greece is a matter of some scholarly debate because the term does not appear in the writings of classical Greek authors, nor is it mentioned in the lists of pipes compiled by Athenaeus or Pollux. However, J.J. Reiske notes a description in the works of Dio Chrysostom (who flourished around A.D. 100) regarding a person skilled at playing a pipe with the mouth on a bag placed under the arm-pits. This is corroborated by the use of the term *ascaules* for the Latin *utricularius* in the writings of Martial.

## Historical Context and Development
It is believed that the Greeks received a form of the bag-pipe from Chaldaea or Egypt (where it existed as the *as-it*). In Greece, the instrument remained a rustic tool used primarily by peasants and shepherds. 

Following the fall of the Roman Empire, the *tibia utricularis* disappeared from the record. The bag-pipe likely persisted for a time among jugglers, actors, and itinerant musicians before reappearing in the early middle ages under the name *chorus*. During this early medieval period, the instrument often appeared in a primitive guise. Some depictions from the 9th to 13th centuries show bag-pipes without drones, featuring only a long chaunter and a stock, though the insufflation tube is sometimes absent in these representations.

## Technical Characteristics
The defining feature of the bag-pipe is the drone ground bass, which sounds without intermission. Drones use a "beating-reed"—a piece of cane or reed with a cut and a raised tongue or flap—fixed into a socket. Air forced from the bag by the performer's arm causes this tongue to vibrate, setting the air column in the cylindrical drone-pipe into vibration. Because they are cylindrical tubes with reed mouthpieces, drones produce a note corresponding to a pipe twice their length and are tuned using sliding-joints.
