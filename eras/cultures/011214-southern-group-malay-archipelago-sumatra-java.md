# The Southern Group of the Malay Archipelago

The southern group of the Malay Archipelago, extending from Sumatra to Java and Timor, forms an arc of a circle located between 95 and 127 degrees East and 5 to 10 degrees South. This region serves as a geographical link between the Asian continent, Australia, and the islands of the Pacific.

## Geography and Environment
The central portion of this island group is a volcanic region featuring many active volcanoes, with summits frequently reaching or exceeding 10,000 feet. Java possesses the highest volcanic peaks, which rise to 12,000 feet above the sea. 

The islands vary significantly in their development and population:
* **Java:** This is the most advanced, best cultivated, and most thickly peopled island in the entire Eastern archipelago. Under the Dutch government, it has achieved a high degree of prosperity and wealth. Its population is characterized as industrious and peaceful, primarily engaged in agriculture.
* **Sumatra:** The largest island in the group, Sumatra is thinly peopled. Much of its surface is covered by dense forest, and cultivation is largely confined to the low lands and the volcanic regions near the center of the island.
* **Eastern Islands:** The islands to the east of this group are described as less advanced and less productive.

## Natural History and Biology
The region is defined by distinct biological boundaries. Modern science recognizes "Wallace's line," named after naturalist A.R. Wallace, which follows the Straits of Macassar (approximately 120 degrees East) between Borneo and Celebes. To the west of this line, flora and fauna are essentially Asiatic; to the south and east, the Australian element becomes distinct and eventually predominant. Consequently, while the south-eastern parts of the archipelago have affinities with the Australian continent, their primary affinities remain Indian.

Specific fauna found in the region include:
* **The Binturong:** An arboreal animal with a prehensile tail, long harsh fur, and tufted ears, inhabiting southern Asia through the Malay Peninsula to Java and Sumatra.
* **The Slow Loris:** A larger, heavier-built primate ranging from eastern Bengal to the Malay Peninsula, Java, and Sumatra. The Sumatran race (*N. t. hilleri*) is noted for its reddish color.

## Ethnography and Culture
The region is inhabited by the Malays, a widely scattered seafaring race with exceptional powers of dispersal. While they lack political union, they occupy Java, Sumatra, Borneo, the Philippines, the extremity of the Malay Peninsula, and Madagascar. Some theories suggest they spread from an island center, possibly Java, while other linguistic theories suggest a broader group—including the Munda, Monkhmer, Polynesian, and Micronesian languages—that originated in the extreme west.

The history of the Asiatic Malays is divided into three periods:
1. A semi-barbarous period, exemplified by tribes such as the Dyaks.
2. A period of Hindu civilization, which reached Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula. This influence is evidenced by Indian customs, words, and the presence of Hindu ruins.
3. The spread of Islam, which superseded Hindu influence before the 16th century.

Cultural influences from India also include Buddhism, which brought Indian art, literature, and alphabets to Java. While the Arabic alphabet is used on the mainland, Indian alphabets persist in Sumatra and Java. In terms of performing arts, the drama of Java and Sumatra resembles Indian drama. The Javanese distinguish between the *panton* (a short fable or simile) and the *tcharita* (a dialogue sung or recited by actors). The *tcharita* led to the development of Javanese drama, which in its higher forms depicts kings and gods and utilizes wooden masks. Comic drama in Sumatra and Java is often performed by strolling troupes consisting of a woman and two men.

## Colonial History and the Dutch East India Company
The Dutch East India Company, formed in 1602 and dissolved in 1798, established its headquarters at Batavia in Java, founded in 1619 on the ruins of the native town of Jacatra. The company sought a total monopoly of trade with the East Indies, a policy that led to violent conflicts with the English. Between 1613 and 1632, the Dutch nearly entirely drove the English from the Malay Archipelago and the Spice Islands, leaving the English in a precarious position at Bantam in Java. One notable conflict involved the judicial murder and torture of English factors at Amboyna in 1623.

The company's power in Sumatra was established via a treaty with native princes in 1667. At its peak in 1669, the company possessed 40 ships of war, 150 trading ships, and 10,000 soldiers, paying a dividend of 40%. However, its fortunes declined in the late 17th century due to the anger of rivals provoked by its rigid monopoly.
