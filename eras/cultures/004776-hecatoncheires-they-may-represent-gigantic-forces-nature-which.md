# Hecatoncheires

The Hecatoncheires are figures from Greek mythology characterized as monstrous natures that are ultra-human and extra-human. While they possess attributes such as conscious agency, free-will, and the capacity for pleasure and pain, they are not properly classified as gods. They are described as having fifty heads and one hundred arms.

## Members and Origins
There are three Hecatoncheires: Briareus (also known as Aegaeon), Cottus, and Gyges (or Gyes). They are the sons of Gaea and either Uranus or Poseidon. 

The figure of Briareus is noted for having different names depending on who is speaking; according to Homer in the *Iliad*, he was called Aegaeon by men but Briareus by the gods.

## Role in Mythological Conflict
The legends surrounding the Hecatoncheires are various and sometimes contradictory. In the most widely spread myth, Zeus called upon Briareus and his brothers for assistance during the war between the Olympians and the Titans. Following the defeat of the Titans, the three brothers were stationed at the gates of Tartarus, where the defeated enemies were consigned.

Other traditions present different roles for Briareus:
* **As an adversary:** Some accounts, such as those by Callimachus, describe Briareus as one of the assailants of Olympus who was buried under Mount Aetna after his defeat.
* **As an ally to Zeus:** Homer mentions Briareus assisting Zeus during a period when other Olympian deities were plotting against the king of gods and men.
* **As a maritime ruler:** Another tradition identifies him as a giant of the sea and the inventor of warships, ruling over the fabulous Aegaea in Euboea and acting as an enemy of Poseidon.

## Symbolic Interpretation
The Hecatoncheires are part of a broader mythical cycle involving the collision of terrific and overboiling forces. These gigantic agents were eventually extinguished, chained, or reduced to obedience under the more orderly government of Zeus.

It is difficult to determine the exact natural phenomena symbolized by the Hecatoncheires. However, they may represent the multitudinous motion of sea waves or the gigantic forces of nature associated with other convulsions and earthquakes.
