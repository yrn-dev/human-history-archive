# Scientific and Historical Advances in Natural and Human Studies

## The Role of Bacteria in Carbon Circulation
Recent advances in scientific knowledge have clarified the part played by bacteria in the circulation of carbon within nature. The earth's surface receives enormous masses of cellulose annually, primarily produced by chlorophyll acting on atmospheric carbon dioxide using energy from the sun. While other processes of carbon-assimilation, such as those by nitrifying bacteria, exist, they are likely comparatively small in magnitude.

Research by figures such as van Senus, van Tieghem, Omeliansky, and Fribes has demonstrated the specific roles of different bacteria in breaking down organic matter like wood, leaves, straw, rags, and paper. Certain anaerobic bacteria decompose pectin compounds in the middle lamella, which isolates cellulose fibres (a process seen when flax is "retted"), but these bacteria cannot attack the cellulose itself. However, other anaerobic bacteria found in rivers, cloacae, and marsh mud can decompose cellulose, likely by hydrolysing it and splitting the products into marsh gas and carbon dioxide. In environments where calcium sulphate is present, the resulting nascent methane leads to the formation of water, sulphuretted hydrogen, and calcium carbonate. This process explains the presence of sulphuretted hydrogen and marsh gas in bogs, and likely accounts for similar putrefactive changes in the undigested cellulose within the intestines of herbivorous animals.

## Archaeology and the History of the Israelites
Archaeology and historical criticism have significantly increased the understanding of the nations surrounding the Israelites and the political powers that periodically held Palestine in subjection. This has helped relate the history of the Israelites to that of the ancient East and raised questions regarding the origins of Hebrew culture. For instance, the discovery of the Code of Khammurabi reveals remarkable resemblances to Pentateuchal codes, prompting inquiries into the relationship between Babylonian and Hebrew law.

Assyrian inscriptions have provided independent evidence regarding the relations between the Assyrians and certain Hebrew kings, specifically Ahaz, Jehu, and Ahab, thereby supporting evidence found in the Old Testament and helping to clarify the confused chronology of the Hebrews in the books of Kings. 

Despite these gains, some historical questions remain unanswered by archaeology. While the conditions in Palestine before the Hebrew invasion are better understood, there is little knowledge of the Hebrew nation prior to that time. The stele of Merenptah (Mineptah), discovered in 1896 and dating to approximately 1270 B.C., states, "Ysirael is desolated, its seed is not," and contains some vague allusions to particular tribes. However, archaeology has not provided knowledge of any specific Hebrew individual from this period. Consequently, the historical elements underlying the stories of the Hebrew patriarchs in Genesis must be determined through critical study of the Old Testament. While archaeology has made the historic reality of Chedorlaomer probable, it has not proved the historical reality of the patriarch Abraham or his specific role.

## Development of Hebrew Tradition and Society
The Hebrew people evolved their tradition and organized their history while in Babylon. Those who returned to Jerusalem following the command of Cyrus were different in knowledge and spirit from those who had originally entered captivity, as they had learned civilization. A significant part in the development of their peculiar character was played by a new type of man: the Prophets, who represented new and remarkable forces in the steady development of human society.

## Astronomy and the Universe
Astronomy is the most ancient of sciences, as it could be systematized before the era of experiment and was necessary for social life due to its relation to seasons, times, and day and night. It is a science of observation because the matter of the heavens cannot be subjected to the same experimental scrutiny as matter on earth.

The universe is divided into the solar system—consisting of the sun and the bodies moving around it—and the remainder of the universe. While the solar system is insignificant in extent compared to the universe, it is of great practical importance and easier to study. Incandescent bodies in the universe are categorized as either stars or nebulae. Stars, including the sun, are generally spherical with well-defined boundaries. Nebulae are generally irregular in outline without well-defined limits, though it is probable that the two classes blend through imperceptible gradations.
