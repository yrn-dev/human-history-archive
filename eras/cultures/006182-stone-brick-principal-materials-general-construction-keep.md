# Stone and Brick in General Construction

Stone and brick serve as the primary materials for general construction, maintaining their positions within a structure through gravity. While these materials may be simply packed together, they are typically compacted using cement or mortar. Although the individual constituent materials are wholly incompressible, the combined masses in a structure remain compressible until the setting medium has indurated to a state of hardness.

## Selection and Application of Stone
The suitability of stone for general construction depends on its moisture absorption and its ability to work. Stone that is least absorbent of moisture and free to work is considered best for general purposes. Stone that absorbs moisture tends to disintegrate rapidly when exposed to weather, whereas non-absorbent stone is often so hard that it cannot be used economically. 

To balance these qualities, builders may use harder stone for the exterior or for areas exposed to actions that softer stone cannot resist, while using the softer stone for the main body of the structure. These different types of stone must be bonded and coursed together by the mason's art, whether the stone is wrought into gauged blocks or consists of unshaped rubble compacted with mortar.

## Brickwork and Tooling
Brickwork refers to constructions made of bricks. The process requires a specific set of tools for layout, leveling, and shaping:
*   **Layout and Leveling:** Bricklayers use a trowel for spreading mortar, a plumb-rule for perpendicularity or regular batter, a spirit-level and straight-edge for horizontals, and a gauge-rod to mark brick courses. Line and line pins are used to keep intermediate work regular, while quoins or angles are built first using the gauge-rod.
*   **Pointing:** Tools used for pointing joints include the raker, jointer, pointing rule, and Frenchman, with the pointing staff held on a small board known as a hawk.
*   **Cutting and Shaping:** Large trowels are used for rough cutting, while bolsters and club-hammers are used for neater work like facings. Cold chisels are used for holes, chases, and general cutting. For shaping hard bricks, a tin saw is used for incisions on a V-shaped cutting block, followed by a bolster, club-hammer, and a scutch (a small axe) to hack off rough parts. Soft bricks, such as malms and rubbers, are cut with a frame saw using a soft iron wire blade and finished on a rubbing stone made of Yorkshire stone.

## Combined Masonry and Structural Integrity
Certain construction methods combine both materials, such as "brick-ashlar," where walls are faced with ashlar stonework and backed with brickwork. This method is prone to unequal settling because the outer stone face is unyielding with very thin joints, while the brick backing contains mortar joints (comprising about one-eighth of its height) that shrink during drying and yield to pressure. To prevent the wall from failing or the face from bulging, builders should use a stiff mortar composed of sand and a large proportion of Portland cement, and ensure the stones are equal in height to an exact number of brick courses.

Similarly, when using Kentish rag rubble for facing, bricks are often used for backing. Due to the irregularity of the stones, stability is ensured by using cement mortar and frequently building in headers or bonding stones.

## Specialized Construction and Materials
### Furnace Construction
Materials for furnaces are divided into ordinary and refractory (fire-resisting) classes. Ordinary materials, including granite, most building stones, and red or yellow bricks, are used for pillars, walls, and casing. Refractory materials are reserved for areas in direct contact with flame and fuel, such as flues, arches, the roof, the fireplace lining, and the internal walls of blast furnaces. These include firebricks, fireclay, certain sandstones, silica (ganister), Dinas stone and bricks, alumina, ferric oxide, magnesia, lime, chromium oxide, and carbon (graphite and coke).

### Flooring and Aviaries
While residential floors were traditionally timber, fire-resisting floors have been introduced to prevent serious fires. These consist of steel joists and girders filled with concrete or patented brick materials. In other specialized settings, such as aviaries for large parrots—which may destroy woodwork and vegetation—the only suitable construction materials are iron, stone, or brick, paired with stout gauge wire-netting.

### Ornamental and Architectural Work
Mosaic work utilizes small pieces of pottery, glass, stone, or marble laid in a float of cement (half to three-quarters of an inch thick) over a concrete bed. "Terazzo mosaic" is a less costly version created by pressing irregularly shaped marble chips into the cement with a hand float and rolling the surface with an iron roller.

In large-scale architecture, such as the Renaissance domes of Florence and Rome, combined materials were used for structural support. The dome of Santa Maria del Fiore uses stone for its lower portion and brick for the upper part. Similarly, the dome of St Peter's is constructed of brick, though its ribs are made of stone from Tivoli.

## Maintenance and Damp Prevention
Dampness in walls can occur via rising moisture from the earth, water soaking down from the top, or wind-driven rain. These are prevented through:
1.  **Damp-proof courses** or dry areas for rising moisture.
2.  **Copings** of cement, stone, or other non-porous materials for water soaking from the top.
3.  **Hollow walls** or impervious exterior coverings for wind-driven rain.
