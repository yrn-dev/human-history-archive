# Books of Imaginative Appeal to Collectors

Book-collecting is defined as the gathering of books that possess an element of permanent interest in their form, contents, or the history of the individual copy, and which are rare—meaning they are difficult to procure. While rarity is often emphasized in popular views, it is subordinate to interest; a rare book that lacks interest is of no concern to the collector. Conversely, the anticipation that a book will always be easily available typically restrains collecting.

## The Appeal to Imagination
Among the various ways a book can attract a collector—appealing to the eye, the mind, or the imagination—those that appeal to the imagination are often the most obvious. These are primarily books associated with famous events or persons. Examples include volumes that belonged to great poets, soldiers, statesmen, or royalty, such as Mary, Queen of Scots. Interest is heightened if the book bears marks of being valued by the owner or is linked to a striking incident in their life.

Collectors also value the "pedigree" of a work, showing a tenderness for predecessors when a book's ownership can be traced through a long series of book-loving owners. This interest has led to the publication of works such as *English Book-Collectors* by W.Y. Fletcher (1902), *Great Book-Collectors* by Charles and Mary Elton (1893), and Guigard's *Nouvel armorial du bibliophile* (1890).

Other imaginative interests include books that caused the persecution of their authors or those condemned to be burnt, though this specific appeal has been more prominent among writers than collectors.

## First Editions and Value
The desire for first editions is driven by a combination of factors: their positive superiority for study purposes and the associations—either proven or imagined—that they possess. In some cases, the value placed on these editions is fanciful. For example, collectors during the reign of George III paid high prices for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics despite their lack of value as texts and the absence of associations with the authors' personalities. Today, such books are primarily collected as specimens of printing.

In contrast, the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces has grown significantly. A notable example is the First Folio Shakespeare (1623), which was sold in 1906.

## Intellectual and Aesthetic Appeals
Beyond the imagination, books may appeal to the collector's mind or eye:

*   **Intellectual Appeal:** This includes works of historical value that have not been reprinted or original editions that are more authentic than modern versions. Examples of this work include the philological library of Prince Lucien Bonaparte and the Foxwell collection of early books on political economy. Lord Crawford's collections of papal bulls and English, Scottish, and Irish proclamations further demonstrate how collecting can promote historical research.
*   **Aesthetic Appeal:** A book may be beautiful due to its paper, print, illustrations, or binding. In France, high prices for French first editions and Elzevirs are often attributed to their 18th and 17th-century jackets. In England, appreciation for old bindings increased following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club, leading to a higher demand for embroidered bindings, English blind stamped bindings, and those attributed to Samuel Mearne.

## History and Evolution of Collecting
Modern book-collecting is a relatively recent growth. While early examples existed under the Roman Empire and late Middle Ages, early texts focused on the delights of literature rather than collecting. For instance, the *Philobiblon* (1345), attributed to Richard de Bury, deals with literary delights, while Sebastian Brant later argued that possessing books was a poor substitute for learning.

Even after the invention of printing reduced book costs by approximately 80%, modern collecting did not immediately emerge. Early owners like Jean Grolier (1479-1565) bought books to encourage printers or provide friends with Renaissance scholarship rather than out of antiquarian instinct. Other figures who assembled great libraries in handsome leather jackets included Archbishop Cranmer, Lords Lumley and Arundel, Henry, prince of Wales, and the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou.

## Notable Collectors and Theories
Various approaches to collecting have emerged over time:

*   **Specialized Collecting:** During George III's reign, James West (who owned 34 Caxtons) and John Ratcliffe (who owned 48) focused on early English presses.
*   **The Cabinet Theory:** Popularized by Henry Perkins (d. 1855), this theory favors small, compact collections that epitomize the owner's tastes over miscellaneous libraries. This approach was also adopted by a group of French amateurs described by Henri Beraldi (1892), who preferred treasures that could fit in a neat cabinet or bookcase, such as dainty 18th-century French illustrated books.
*   **Distinctive Bindings:** Some collectors are remembered for specific styles. Demetrio Canevari used dark red or green morocco with a cameo stamp of Apollo and Pegasus. Jean Grolier and Tommaso Maioli used specific legends on their bindings, while Geoffrey Tory designed fine bindings for himself and possibly Grolier.
