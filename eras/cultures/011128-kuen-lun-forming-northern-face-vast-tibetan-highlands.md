# Kuen-lun Mountains

The Kuen-lun (or Kwen-lun) is a vast system of mountain ranges located in Central Asia, forming the northern edge of the great Tibetan plateau. In a broad geographical sense, these ranges extend from the Pamirs in the west to 113° E, where they merge with the steep escarpments of the southeastern flank of the Mongolian plateau. In a narrower application, the term refers specifically to the ranges that separate the Tibetan plateau to the south from the Takla-makan desert to the north, extending from the Pamirs to the transverse glen of the Kara-muren, near the town of Cherchen (approximately 85½° E).

## Tectonic Structure and Geography
The Kuen-lun is described as the "backbone" of the Asian continent. It is significantly older than the Tian-shan to the north and the Himalayas to the south. In its widest meaning, the system stretches in a wavy line for nearly 2,500 miles from east to west. The geography of the system varies by region: in the west, lateral compression has squeezed the ranges into a breadth of 150–200 miles, forcing summits to higher altitudes. In the east, the ranges are less folded, flatter, and lower, spreading out to a breadth of some 600 miles.

A distinctive feature of the northern edge of the Tibetan plateau is the "twinning" of its outermost border-ranges and its intermont lake-basins. The crests of the component ranges generally reach altitudes between 21,500 and 22,000 feet. Unlike the Himalayas, the Kuen-lun typically lacks individual towering peaks; instead, the ranges are relatively uniform in altitude, remaining only slightly higher than the lofty valleys that separate them.

## Regional Divisions

### Western Kuen-lun
The western extremity of the system is a rugged region that continues the double border-ridge of the Pamirs. This system consists of twin ranges running from the west-northwest to the east-southeast. The northerly range includes the Kilian (or Kiliang), Khotan, and Keriya Mountains, while the southerly range comprises the Raskem (or Raskan), Sughet, and Ullugh-tagh Mountains. 

These ranges are separated by deep glens: the Raskem (or Yarkand-darya) gorge separates the southerly range from the Karakorum Mountains, while the Kara-kash (or Khotan-darya) glen lies between the upper Sughet and lower Kilian ranges. While altitudes decrease from west to east, they still reach 19,000 feet, with some peaks ascending an additional 2,000–2,500 feet. Passes in this region are very high; for example, the Sughet-davan reaches 17,825 feet. Geologically, these ranges contain Devonian epoch fossils, including corals and *Bryozoa*.

### Arka-tagh
The Arka-tagh (known to Russian geographers as the Przhevalsky Range) is considered the true backbone of the Kuen-lun system and the actual border-range of the Tibetan plateau. It is exceeded in elevation in Central Asia only by the Tang-la. The Arka-tagh is a complex system rather than a single range, sometimes appearing as a defined main crest and other times as numerous parallel crests of uniform altitude. 

The system includes four predominant ranges in its middle section, with the fourth being the highest. Passes across these ranges vary from 15,675 to 18,300 feet. A defining characteristic of the Arka-tagh is that its summits are broad, rounded, flattened domes rather than jagged peaks. These mountains are superficially composed of sand and finely sifted disintegrated material, with any exposed hard rock being excessively weathered.

### Astin-tagh and Eastern Extensions
The Astin-tagh forms the outermost border-ridge on the north of the Tibetan plateau. While some consider it a continuation of the Kuen-lun, others view it as a subsidiary branch or the westward continuation of the Nan-shan (Southern Mountains). The Astin-tagh consists of two parallel main ranges in the west, increasing to three in the east and four northeast of Tsaidam. 

Unlike the rounded domes of the Arka-tagh, the Astin-tagh mountains consist of hard, bare rock piled into rugged, pinnacled crests. The general elevation of this twin border ridge decreases toward the east; passes in the Lower Astin-tagh range from 10,150 to 10,700 feet, while those in the Upper Astin-tagh range from 11,770 to 15,680 feet.

## Hydrographic and Strategic Importance
The Kuen-lun serves as a major hydrographic barrier. To its north lies the Tarim Basin (roughly Eastern Turkestan), an area of rivers that end in intermittent lakes and swamps rather than reaching the sea. To its south lies the Tibetan plateau, with a mean elevation of 15,000 feet.

Historically, the Kuen-lun provided a natural meeting place for Aryan and Mongolian peoples. Despite the high barriers, practicable routes exist along the northern foothills of the Kuen-lun or through the Tarim valley, allowing travel from China to Kashgar, and subsequently over the mountains to Bokhara, Samarkand, and Kokand.
