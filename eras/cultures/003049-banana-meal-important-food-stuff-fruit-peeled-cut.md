# Banana

The banana belongs to the genus *Musa*, which comprises approximately 40 species. These plants are widely distributed throughout the tropics of the Old World and have been introduced into the New World. In many tropical regions, they are as essential to the inhabitants as grain plants are to those in cooler climates.

## Botany and Cultivation
Bananas are most successfully grown in hot, damp, tropical climates. Their cultivation reaches its northern limit in Egypt, the Canary Islands, south Japan, and Florida (south of 29 degrees latitude), while the southern limits are found in south Brazil and Natal. 

The genus includes several species used for fruit. *Musa paradisiaca* is the source of a vast number of cultivated varieties. Its sub-species *sapientum* provides the fruits generally known in England as bananas and eaten raw. Forms of *M. paradisiaca* itself are known as plantains and require cooking. Other fruit-bearing species include *M. acuminata* in the Malay Archipelago, *M. Fehi* in Tahiti, and *M. Cavendishii* (the Chinese banana), which is grown in cooler countries and is characterized by a delicate, fragrant flesh and a thinner rind.

The fruit is rich in starch when unripe, which converts to sugar during ripening. Species that require cooking are of greater importance as food and can reach significant sizes; in East Africa, some forms are nearly 2 feet long and as thick as a man's arm. In the Malay Archipelago and Cochin China, a form of *M. corniculata* produces a single fruit capable of providing a meal for three men.

## Nutritional Use and Processing
Banana-meal is an important food-stuff produced by peeling the fruit and cutting it into strips, which are then pounded in a mortar after being dried. According to Hutchison, the composition of this flour is 80% carbohydrates, 13% water, 4% proteid, 2.5% salts, and 0.5% fat. Because of this composition, an average person would need about eighty average-sized bananas daily to meet energy requirements, and double that amount to meet proteid requirements. Hutchison notes that those who rely mainly on this diet may experience undue abdominal development.

Beyond the fruit, the root-stock of the Abyssinian species *M. Ensete* is sometimes used as food because it is soft and full of starch just before the flowering period. Additionally, an intoxicating drink is prepared from the fruit in East Africa and other regions.

## Industrial and Material Uses
Various parts of the banana plant are used for non-food purposes:
* **Leaves:** Cut into strips and plaited, the leaves are used to make bags and mats. They are also used for packing, and the finer leaves are used for cigarette papers.
* **Fibre:** Several species yield valuable fibre. The best is "Manila hemp," derived from *M. textilis*. In the Luchu islands, a banana-cloth known as *basho-fu* (or *aka-basho*) is woven from the fibre of a banana species.

## Global Distribution and Commerce
Bananas are grown in diverse regions, including southern China, the Philippine Islands (specifically the fertile volcanic soil of the Batangas province in Luzon), and various vilayets where they are grown alongside dates and pomegranates.

In terms of international trade, the banana industry saw significant development in Jamaica for the English and American markets. In another region, the banana trade began in 1881 with the export of 3,500 bunches to New Orleans. By 1904, exports exceeded 6,000,000 bunches, with regular steamship services running from Limon to the United States, Germany, and Great Britain (with weekly sailings to Bristol and Manchester). The economic importance of the crop is reflected in labour wages; in banana districts, labourers earn nearly 3s. daily, compared to 1s. 8d. in coffee plantations.

In the United Kingdom, bananas are a major import. In 1908, the outlay for imported bananas amounted to £1,769,249, as they cannot be grown on an industrial scale within the British Isles.
