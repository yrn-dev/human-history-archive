# Bosnians

## Origins and Language
The Bosnians are identified as descending from the original stock of the Slavonian (or Sclavonian) race. They are grouped with kindred bands such as the Servians, Croatians, Rascians, and Walachians, who followed the example or standard of the leading Bulgarian tribe. Evidence of language supports this descent, and the identity of the Bosnian language is noted as being the same as that of the Bulgarians, Servians, Poles, and Dalmatians.

## History and the Turkish Conquest
The fate of the Bosnians, along with the Croats and Servians, was decided in 1389 following a rout on the field of Kossovo. This event occurred during a period of Turkish expansion in the Peninsula, which had seen the capture of Sofia in 1382 and Philippopolis in 1362. Following the Turkish conquest, numerous conversions to Islam took place. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, 35% of the inhabitants adopted the creed of the conquering race. These Bosnian Moslems are compared to indigenous Albanian Moslems and the Pomaks (Mahommedan Bulgarians), who retained their national usages and speech.

## Religious Composition
The religious landscape of the Bosnian population is divided among several faiths:
*   **Islam:** A significant portion of the population converted to Islam following the Turkish conquest.
*   **Roman Catholicism:** Approximately 21% of Bosnians belong to the Roman Catholic Church.
*   **Orthodox Christianity:** While the archive notes that the great bulk of the Christian population in the broader Peninsula belongs to the Orthodox Church, specific percentages for Bosnians are not provided beyond the Catholic and Muslim figures.

## Geographic Distribution and Migration
Bosnia is located in the north and northeast of Dalmatia. Beyond their native peninsula, Bosnians were introduced by the Turks in the 16th century to the region between Assuan and Hannek. In this area, the descendants of these Bosnians ruled the district, maintaining only a nominal allegiance to the Porte. During their time there, they constructed castles along the islands and banks of the Nile, including at Mahass and Ibrim, though these structures are now in ruins.
