# The Temborari and Parallel Zones of British East Africa

The geography of British East Africa is characterized by an arrangement of parallel zones that run in relation to the coast, affecting the region's topography, geology, and climate.

## The Coast Plain (Temborari)
The first of these parallel zones is the coast plain, known as the "Temborari." This zone is generally of insignificant width, typically varying between 2 and 10 meters, although it widens in the valleys of the main rivers. The shoreline of the Temborari is broken by branching creeks and bays, which frequently cut off islands from the mainland. Examples of such islands include Mvita or Mombasa (located at 4° 4' S) and the Lamu archipelago, which consists of the larger islands of Patta, Manda, and Lamu between 2° S and 2° 20' S. As the coast extends further north, it becomes straighter, though it remains skirted seawards by a row of small islands and features one indentation at Port Durnford (1° 10' S).

## The Nyika and Interior Uplands
Beyond the coast plain, the land rises in one or more well-defined steps to an altitude of approximately 800 feet. This area forms a wide level plain called the "Nyika" (uplands), which is largely composed of quartz. The Nyika contains large areas without water, such as the Taru desert located in the Mombasa district.

The ascent continues into an intermittent line of schistose or gneissose mountains that generally run north-north-west. These mountains, which represent the primitive axis of the continent, reach heights between 5,000 and 8,000 feet and sometimes appear in parallel chains.

Further inland, the region consists of grassy uplands extending to the eastern edge of the rift-valley. These uplands, with surfaces largely composed of lava, vary in altitude from 5,000 to 8,000 feet. This zone is varied with forest (particularly between 0° and 0° 40' S) and cultivated ground, specifically in Kikuyu. The most extensive grassy plains are found in Athi and Kapte (or Kapote) between 1° and 2° S. This zone contains the highest elevations in British East Africa, including:
* Kenya: 17,007 ft.
* Sattima: 13,214 ft.
* Nandarua: approximately 12,900 ft.
* The Aberdare Mountains (Sattima range): a general elevation of fully 10,000 ft.

The transition to the rift-valley to the west is marked by cliffs, most notably the Laikipia escarpment on the equator and the Kikuyu escarpment (8,000 ft.) just south of 1° S. A main watershed of East Africa runs close to the eastern wall of the rift-valley, separating rivers of the east coast from the basins of inland drainage.

## Climate and Vegetation
The climate and vegetation of British East Africa follow the same parallel zonal arrangement as the topography.

### The Coast Region
The coast is tropical and hot, though it is generally healthier than the coast lands of other tropical countries due to the dryness of the soil and a constant breeze from the Indian Ocean. Annual rainfall in this region is approximately 35 inches.

### The Plateaus and Highlands
The outer plateaus and succeeding plains are more arid than the coast. However, the highlands—defined as all districts over 5,000 feet high—are very healthy, and fever is almost unknown there. In the highlands, the average temperature is 66° F during the cool season and 73° F during the hot season. Once the altitude exceeds 7,000 feet, the climate becomes distinctly colder, and frosts occur. Average rainfall in the highlands ranges between 40 and 50 inches.

### Other Regions
The region bordering Victoria Nyanza is typically tropical with annual rainfall exceeding 60 inches, making it quite unsuitable for Europeans.

## Seasonal Patterns
Across the protectorate, the hottest period occurs from December to April, while the coolest period is from July to September. Rainfall is divided into two periods: the "smaller rains" in November and December, and the "greater rains" from March to June. Rainfall is not as regular as is typical for countries within the tropics, and the region occasionally experiences severe droughts.
