# Umbrians

The Umbrians were an ancient people of Italy whose history is documented through linguistic monuments and archaeological records. While the term "Umbrian" is frequently used by archaeologists in a broad sense to describe all pre-Etruscan Italic inhabitants of upper Italy—including those associated with the Early Iron Age Villanova culture—linguistic evidence suggests a more restricted historical area.

## Geography and Territorial Shifts
Strictly defined by the language found in ancient liturgies, the Umbrian area extended primarily across the eastern half of the Tiber valley, ranging from Urvinum Mataurense to Interamna Nahartium. However, the Umbrians' hold on their territory was challenged by migrating groups. The Boii, a Celtic people originally from Gallia Transalpina, crossed the Pennine Alps into the Po valley. After finding that district occupied, the Boii drove out the Umbrians and Etruscans, establishing their own presence as far as the Apennines in the region of modern Romagna.

## Language and Dialect
The Umbrian language is the only surviving long-form monument of the tongue spoken in the Umbrian district before it was latinized. It is categorized as part of the Osco-Umbrian group, which is distinct from Latin.

### Linguistic Characteristics
While Umbrian morphology and syntax are largely similar to Oscan, there are specific divergences:
*   **Phonetic Changes:** Umbrian exhibits the palatalization of *k* and *g* before *i* or *e* (e.g., *tiçit*). It also experienced the loss of final *-d* (e.g., *tota* compared to the Oscan *toutad*) and the change of *-s-* to *-r-* between vowels (e.g., *erom* compared to the Oscan *ezum*).
*   **Consonant Shifts:** A distinctive feature is the change of *d* between vowels to a sound similar to *r*.
*   **Morphology:** The language contains two peculiar perfect-formations utilizing *-l-* and *-nçi-*.

### Writing and Alphabets
The records of the Umbrian dialect, specifically the Tables, reveal a chronological evolution in writing. The earliest records utilize a "normal" Umbrian alphabet written from right to left. This alphabet included specific signs for sounds akin to *r* (derived from *d*) and a voiceless palatal consonant. Over time, the alphabet evolved, with later forms showing an angular, undivided *k* and an abbreviated *m*. Eventually, the Latin alphabet was adopted for certain inscriptions, such as those found in the *Claverniur* paragraph and later Tables.

## Regional Variations and Inscriptions
The language was not entirely uniform across the region. Inscriptions from Fulginia and Assisium align closely with the dialect of Iguvium. However, an inscription from Tuder, likely dating to the 3rd century B.C., demonstrates the preservation of a medial *-d-* and a final *-s-*, sounds that had undergone changes in the Iguvine dialect.
