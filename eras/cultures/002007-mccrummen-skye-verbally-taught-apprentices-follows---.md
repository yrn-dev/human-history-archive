# McCrummen of Skye

The McCrummen pipers of the Isle of Skye were practitioners of the Highland bag-pipe who utilized a system of verbal equivalents to transmit musical tunes to their apprentices. This method of instruction predates the recent innovation of using musical notation for Highland pipe tunes.

## Verbal Instruction and the McCrummen Method
The process of teaching by verbal equivalents involved the use of specific phonetic sounds to represent notes. An example of this method was documented by Capt. Niel MacLeod, who took down the piobaireachd *Coghiegh nha Shie* ("War of peace") from the piper John McCrummen (also referred to as Crimmon) of Skye. 

The opening of the tune, as verbally taught to apprentices, is recorded as: "Hodroho, hodroho, haninin, hiechin, Hodroha, hodroho, hodroho, hachin, Hiodroho, hodroho, haninin, hiechin," &c. The conclusion of the same tune is expressed through the verbal sequence: "Hiundratatateriri, hiendatatateriri, hiundratatateriri, hiundratatateriri."

## Technical Context of the Highland Bag-pipe
The necessity for complex fingering and the specific nature of pipe tunes arise from a technical peculiarity of the Highland bag-pipe. The instrument makes the repetition of a single note difficult without the use of "warblers" or "cuts." These are grace notes fingered with great rapidity to relieve the constant discord created by the drone bass.

These warblers can consist of single notes or groups of three to seven notes that move in leaps rather than consecutively. Highly skilled pipers are capable of introducing up to eleven warblers between two beats in a single bar.

## Cultural Role of the Bag-pipe in Skye
The bag-pipe is a military instrument held in high esteem by Celtic races and became deeply identified with the life of the people in Ireland and Caledonia. It was employed during festivals, funerals, and weddings. In Skye, the instrument was also used to lighten physical labor; for example, in 1786, each party of laborers engaged in roadmaking had its own bag-piper.
