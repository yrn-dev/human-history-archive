# Climate of Bolivia

Bolivia is located entirely within the torrid zone. Because of this position, variations in temperature across the country are primarily driven by prevailing winds, mountain barriers, and elevation. This results in a vertical arrangement of climatic zones that encompasses every gradation of temperature, ranging from the heat of tropical lowlands to the Arctic cold found on snow-capped peaks.

## Factors Influencing Climate
The vertical climatic structure is modified by prevailing winds and the high mountain ranges that cross one corner of the republic, which govern rainfall conditions. In Southern Bolivia, the trade winds create a cycle of wet and dry seasons. In the regions further north and east of the Cordillera Oriental, rain falls throughout the year, although the months from November to March are typically identified as the rainy season.

The Cordillera extracts moisture from the prevailing easterly winds, leaving the elevated plateaus on the west side with limited rainfall in the north. This rainfall diminishes toward the south until the land becomes absolutely barren. While electrical wind storms are frequent at these high altitudes, brief and furious rain-storms occur only during a short season and are not frequent on the northern plateau.

## Native Climatic Zones
Due to the wide range of temperature between locations of the same latitude, natives categorize the Bolivian climate into five distinct zones:

*   **Yungas:** This zone consists of mountain valleys and lowlands up to 5000 ft. It is characterized by an exceedingly humid atmosphere and tropical temperatures where winter is unknown. Official estimates place the mean temperature at 70 deg. F., though this likely averages the low country and higher elevations.
*   **Valle (or medio yungas):** Including deep valleys from 5000 to 9500 ft., this sub-tropical region is sometimes described as a region of perpetual summer. It features a warm climate with moderate temperature variations and no cold weather.
*   **Cabezera de valle:** This zone comprises the heads of deep valleys above the valle zone, with elevations between 9500 and 11,000 ft. The climate is temperate and divided into regular seasons.
*   **Puna:** Located between 11,000 and 12,500 ft., this zone includes the great central plateau. It has only two seasons: winter and a cold summer or autumn. The air is dry and cold, with an official mean temperature of 54 deg. F. The warmer season is too short to support the production of most crops, except for barley and potatoes.
*   **Puna brava:** Extending from 12,500 ft. up to the snow limit at approximately 17,500 ft., this is a bleak and inhospitable territory inhabited only by miners and shepherds.

Above the puna brava lies the region of eternal snow, which is described as an Arctic zone within the tropics.

## Health and Environment
The temperate (cabezera de valle) and sub-tropical (valle) regions are considered agreeable and healthy, characterized by moderate temperatures in the shade and plentiful rainfall. In contrast, the tropical yungas are prone to fevers and malaria, and the ground is covered in decaying vegetation. While some exposed elevations and open country offer healthy conditions, there is a high rate of mortality among natives; however, this is attributed to diet and unsanitary habits rather than the climate itself.

## Flora and Vegetation
The diversity of altitude allows Bolivia to support flora from every climatic zone, from luxuriant tropical forests in the Amazon basin to the scanty Arctic vegetation of the lofty Cordilleras. 

In the puna region, the flora is characterized by quinoa, oca, the potato, and some coarse grasses, while barley is grown for fodder. Indian corn was cultivated in warm and temperate regions before Europeans introduced oats, rye, wheat, beans, pease, and various Old World fruits and vegetables. 

The tropical and sub-tropical zones feature indigenous plants including:
*   **Hardwoods:** Mahogany, cedar, rosewood, jacaranda, walnut, quebracho, and colo.
*   **Other Plants:** Cotton, rubber-producing trees, vanilla, sarsaparilla, ipecacuanha, copaiba, tolu, peanuts, cassava, pineapple, guava, pawpaw, chirimoya, and false jalap.
*   **Fruits:** Banana, aguacate (alligator pear), and caju (cashew) have disputed origins, while others like the date, grape, fig, lemon, lime, and orange are exotics.

Two significant natural products of the eastern Andes are Coca, cultivated between 5000 and 6000 ft. where frosts are unknown and temperatures are uniform, and Quina (calisaya), found between 3000 and 9000 ft. The bark of the calisaya tree is a major item of foreign trade, though destructive collection methods are reducing natural sources.
