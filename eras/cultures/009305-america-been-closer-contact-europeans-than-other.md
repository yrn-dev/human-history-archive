# America and European Contact

The relationship between America and Europe has been characterized by waves of migration, economic exploitation, political conflict, and biological exchange. From the initial discovery of the continent to the establishment of independent states, the interaction between these two regions has fundamentally reshaped their respective populations and political landscapes.

## Early Exploration and Settlement
The primary motivation for European sailors, such as Columbus, to travel to America was trade. While European settlements in the East remained largely trading posts, the discovery of America presented a different opportunity. Europeans encountered communities with lower levels of productive activity and were driven by the search for natural products, gold, and silver. This was particularly evident in Spanish America, where silver mines were abundant.

These economic pursuits necessitated the transition from armed merchants to prospectors, miners, and planters, leading to the establishment of permanent overseas homes. In northern regions, the search for furs drove settlement. Other migrations were fueled by social or religious needs; for example, English Puritans moved to New England in the early seventeenth century to escape religious persecution, and in the eighteenth century, Oglethorpe sent people from English debtors' prisons to Georgia. By the nineteenth century, the advent of the steamship facilitated a great migration of Europeans to the "new empty lands" of America.

## Colonial Governance and Independence
The discovery of America was viewed by European powers as a "splendid legacy" and an opportunity for exploitation. However, this led to frantic claiming of realms and exhausting conflicts. Spain, which at one time controlled two-thirds of America, suffered significant internal depletion due to its possessions.

Over time, American colonies began to revolt against the European Great Power System. Following the example of the United States, Spanish colonies revolted when Napoleon placed his brother Joseph on the Spanish throne in 1810, a movement led by General Bolivar. To prevent a monarchist restoration supported by the Holy Alliance, President Monroe issued the Monroe Doctrine in 1823. This policy warned that the United States would view any extension of the European system in the Western Hemisphere as a hostile act, effectively keeping the Great Power system out of America for nearly a century.

## Demographic and Social Impacts
The contact between Europeans and the indigenous peoples of America resulted in complex demographic mixtures. In Bolivia, for instance, the population consists of Indians, Caucasians of European origin, and a mixture of the two known as *mestizos*, along with a small percentage of descendants of African slaves introduced during colonial times. 

Within Bolivia, certain groups, such as the Aymaras south of Lake Titicaca—who had attained a degree of civilization before the discovery of America—had closer contact with Europeans than other tribes. Other groups, like the Mojos and Chiquitos, were organized into industrial communities by Jesuits in the 17th century, though their prosperity declined following the expulsion of the Jesuits. Additionally, European emigrants, such as the Basques from France and Spain, settled in South America, where they remained a distinct racial group.

## Biological and Environmental Exchange
The biological relationship between America and Europe is evident in the flora and fauna of transitional regions. In Greenland, land mammals are decidedly more American than European; the reindeer, dog, Eskimo, white polar wolf, banded lemming (*Cuniculus torquatus*), and musk-ox all originated from America. Marine life, including fishes and species of seals and whales, also shows a stronger American influence. For example, the bladder-nose seal (*Cystophora cristata*) is considered a Greenland-American species.

The flora of Greenland shows a near equivalence between American and European-Asiatic elements. Of approximately 310 species of vascular plants, about forty are American and forty-four are European-Asiatic. In contrast, other archipelagos show a much stronger European bias; in one such region, 400 of 478 indigenous species are European, while only four are found in America.

## Modern Political Relations
In the twentieth century, the relationship shifted toward global military and political cooperation, though tensions remained regarding international outlooks. The United States joined the Allies in April 1917 during the Great War, a move that was vital for victory as it counterbalanced the collapse of Russia.

Following the war, however, a "violent revulsion" toward European policy emerged in the United States. This was manifest in the Senate's refusal to ratify the covenant of the League of Nations, which Americans viewed as a league of allied imperialisms. By 1919 and 1920, a phase of "Americanism" arose, characterized by a distrust of European socialism and imperialism, as the United States sought to distance itself from the moral responsibilities and political systems of the "Old World."
