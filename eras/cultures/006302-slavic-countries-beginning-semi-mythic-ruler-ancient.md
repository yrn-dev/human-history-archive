# Slavic Countries

The history of the Slavic peoples is characterized by early migrations, the establishment of regional principalities, and the eventual adoption of Christianity. While some early records are obscure or based on legend, archaeological and historical evidence provides a framework for their development in Central and Eastern Europe.

## Early Origins and Settlements
Evidence from archaeological research indicates that Slavic inhabitants were present in Bohemia as early as the beginning of the Christian era. In other regions, scholars such as Paul Joseph Schafarik have argued that the Budini and Neuri tribes—described by Herodotus—were of Slavic family. This conclusion is supported by the fact that the names Budini and Neuri are traceable to Slavic roots. Furthermore, the wooden towns described by Herodotus among the Budini closely parallel primitive Slavic towns existing as late as the twelfth century. The geography of the Budini territory, characterized by marshes and woods containing otters and beavers, aligns more closely with Russia and southern Poland than with the Ural mountains.

## The Early History of Bohemia
The land of Bohemia derives its name from the Boii, a Celtic tribe whose capital was Boiohemum. The Boii were weakened by warfare with neighboring tribes and subdued around 12 B.C. by the Teutonic Marcomanni. After the Marcomanni were expelled by other Teutonic tribes, Slavic tribes conquered the region, with the Cechs becoming the most significant group. The Cechs are believed to have become the masters of the country during the 5th century.

The first ruler of the Cechs mentioned in historical records is Samo. He is noted for defeating the Franks in a great battle at Wogatisburg in 630 (likely near the present town of Eger) and for defeating the Avars, a Turanian tribe that had previously held overlordship over Bohemia.

## Mythic Foundations and the Premyslide Dynasty
Following the death of Samo, Bohemian history remains obscure for approximately 130 years. Later chronicles, including those by Cosmas, describe the foundation of a Bohemian principality by Krok (or Crocus) and his daughter Libussa. According to legend, Libussa married a peasant named Premysl who was discovered ploughing his field; this specific narrative is a common legend found across most Slavic countries. Ancient chroniclers used this semi-mythic ruler, Premysl, as the starting point for a continuous list of Premyslide princes, though the specific dates of their reigns and their attributed deeds are not considered historical.

## Christianization and the Bohemian State
The introduction of Christianity into Bohemia initially faced difficulties when attempts were made by Germany, as the Bohemians harbored an innate distrust of their western neighbors. Success was achieved when the faith arrived from Moravia via Cyrillus and Methodius, Greek monks from Thessalonica. Around the year 873, the Bohemian prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius, leading to a rapid adoption of the faith by the population.

One of the most prominent rulers of this era was Wenceslas, known as Wenceslas the Holy. In 935, he was murdered by his brother Boleslav, an act that led to war with Germany because Wenceslas had been a German ally. Boleslav, who succeeded Wenceslas, was generally successful in this conflict.

## Expansion and Political Instability
Under Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its borders. Boleslav II established rule over Moravia, Bohemia, a large portion of Silesia, and the part of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. However, like many Slavic states of the period, this empire was short-lived. Boleslav III, the son of Boleslav II, lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great, the king of Poland.

The reign of Boleslav III was marked by constant civil war resulting from conflicts between him and his brothers, Ulrick and Jaromir. Although Boleslav III eventually expelled his brothers, his cruelty led the Bohemians to dethrone him. They instead chose the Polish prince Vladivoj as their ruler. Vladivoj was the son of the Bohemian princess Dubravka and the brother of Boleslav the Great. To secure his power, Vladivoj sought assistance from Germany and agreed to continue paying tribute.
