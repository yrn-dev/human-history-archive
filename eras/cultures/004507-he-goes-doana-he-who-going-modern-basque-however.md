# Basques

The Basques are a sturdy and independent-spirited people inhabiting Navarre and the three Basque Provinces of Spain—Alava, Biscay, and Guipuzcoa—as well as the arrondissement of Mauleon and Bayonne in France. In Europe, their population is estimated at approximately 600,000, with an additional 100,000 emigrants residing in the Americas, particularly in the region of La Plata in South America. The term "Basques" is historically derived from *Vascones* (or *Wascones*), a term that also gave rise to the name *Gascons*, though the latter refers to a very different race. The Basques refer to themselves as *Eskualdunak*, meaning "those who possess the *Eskuara*," and they call their country *Eskual-Herria*.

## Language and Philology

The Basque language, known as *Eskuara* (also *euskara* or *uskara*), likely corresponds to the idea of "clearly speaking." It is a fully developed, agglutinative, incorporating, and polysynthetic system of speech that remains absolutely isolated from other European tongues. Grammatically, it recalls Finnic and Magyar languages, occupying an intermediate position between Ugro-Altaic or Ugrian languages and American tongues. Some philologists, such as A. H. Keane, have suggested a remote link to the Hamitic languages via the Berber language of North Africa, while others believe it is a specialized survivor of a widely extended group of pre-Hamitic languages, similar to vestiges found in the Caucasian Mountains.

The language's structure includes a vicesimal numeral system; for example, the number 34 is expressed as *hogoi ta hamalaur* ("twenty and fourteen"). The numbers one through ten are *bat, bi, hiru, lau, bortz* (or *bost*), *sei, zazpi, zortzi, bederatzi,* and *hamar*. The number 20 is *hogoi* (or *hogei*), 40 is *berrogoi* ("twice twenty"), and 100 is *ehun*; there is no genuine word for one thousand.

Personal pronouns include *ni* (I), *hi* (thou), *gu* (we), and *zu* (you). In modern usage, *zu* has become a polite form of "thou," while a true plural "you" is formed by adding the suffix *k--zuek*. Third-person pronouns are demonstratives: *hura* or *kura* (that), *hau* or *kau* (this), and *ori* or *kori* (this or that). The reflective "self" is expressed by *buru* ("head"). The language lacks a relative pronoun; historically, a verbal participle with the ending *n* was used (e.g., *doa*, "he goes"; *doana*, "he who is going"). However, modern Basque uses the interrogatives *zein* or *zoin* as relatives, imitating Spanish or French.

## Culture and Traditions

The Basques exhibit strong conservatism. Historically, they used the *laya*—a two-pronged short-handled steel digging fork—instead of the plough. Their traditional week consisted of only three days, and all cutting instruments were made of stone. 

The people have preserved a wide array of primitive dances, including:
* Animal or totem dances (personating horses, foxes, or bears).
* Agricultural and vintage dances performed with wine-skins.
* Simple arts dances, such as weaving, where dancers form patterns with colored ribbons around a pole.
* War-dances, including the sword-dance.
* Religious procession dances before the altar and the Host.
* Ceremonial dances for festivals and welcoming distinguished guests.

## Religion and Beliefs

Prior to Christianity, Basque religion involved the worship of the sun, the moon, the morning star, and the elements. There was a belief in the immortality of the body if it remained unburied and unburnt. The Romans assimilated local deities into their own pantheon, as seen in Latin inscriptions for *Herauscorrtsehe* and Deo *Baicorrixo*. 

The Basques refer to the Deity as *Jaincoa* or *Jaungoikoa*, which may mean "Lord of the high" or "master." In the Roncal dialect, *Goikoa* means "the moon," suggesting *Jaungoikokoa* could mean "Lord of the moon." The term *Jaun* (lord or master) is also used for householders, as in *Etcheko Jauna* (the lord of the house).

## Literature and History

Basque literature is relatively modern. The first printed book, *Linguae Vasconum Primitiae* (poems by Bernard d'Echepare), dates to 1545. The standard for the language is considered to be the Protestant translation of the New Testament by Jean de Licarrague, printed in 1571. Other literary forms include *pastorales*, which are open-air dramas derived from French materials.

Ancient remains and Iberian inscriptions, such as those on the *toros de guisando* (stone bulls or boars) and at *Cerro de los Santos* in Murcia, suggest the Basque people were once distributed more widely across Spain. In the Americas, the earliest "French" settlers in Canada were Basque, and the language continues to be used in newspapers in the United States and Argentina.
