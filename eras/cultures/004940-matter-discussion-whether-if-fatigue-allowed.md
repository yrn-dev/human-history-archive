# Fatigue and Allowances in Engineering and History

The concept of fatigue appears across various disciplines in the archive, ranging from the structural calculations of bridge engineering to the physical limitations of historical figures and the causes of disease.

## Engineering and the Weyrauch Method
In the field of bridge engineering, there is an ongoing discussion regarding the application of the Weyrauch method and whether an additional allowance for impact should be made if fatigue is already allowed for. This debate stems from the fact that Wöhler's experiments did not include impact. While it may seem rational to add an impact allowance to the fatigue allowance, doing so results in bridge sections that are larger than experience indicates are necessary.

To resolve this, some engineers argue that Wöhler's results are not applicable to bridge work. These engineers reject the allowance for fatigue—defined as the effect of repetition—and instead design bridge members based on the total live and dead load, adding a large impact allowance based on empirical rules.

When applying Wöhler's law, the maximum stress ($f_{max.}$) for a bridge member is determined using the maximum possible live load. Because this maximum load is not the usual load the bridge is subjected to, the resulting range of stress ($f_{max.}-f_{min.}$) is not the ordinary range repeated an infinite number of times, but rather a range occurring at long intervals. Consequently, it appears probable that the allowance for fatigue provided in standard tables is sufficient to cover ordinary effects of impact as well.

## Physical Fatigue in Historical Figures
The archive records instances where physical fatigue influenced the leadership and health of historical individuals.

### Marius
During a war characterized by many reverses, Marius experienced a decline in reputation and influence compared to Sulla. Marius was perceived as tardy and over-cautious. He attributed this to diseased nerves and a body incapable of supporting fatigue, though he claimed to endure the hardships of the campaign due to a feeling of honor. Despite these limitations, Marius won a significant battle in which six thousand enemy soldiers were slaughtered. He remained intrenched in his camp, refusing to be irritated by challenges to fight, once telling Publius Silo that if Silo were a great general, he would compel Marius to fight against his will. Eventually, Marius relinquished command, citing that his weakness made him unable to endure the fatigue of the campaign.

### Socrates
In the records of the Roman Empire, a specific disease is discussed. Socrates ascribed this disease to the fatigues of war, although Philostorgius represented it as the result of intemperance and sloth. Photius characterized Philostorgius's claim as that of an impudent liar.

## Travel and Environmental Fatigue
Physical fatigue is also documented in the context of difficult travel. In the region near Gumisch-Khana, the road is described as extremely difficult. Mr. Kinneir reached Gumisch-Khana after three days of travel from Trebizond, noting that the final two days were very long and fatiguing. Mr. Hamilton reached the same location in two long days. 

The route through Gumisch-Khana is identified as the "winter-road" from Erzerum to Trebizond, which is the longest route because it follows the longer portion of the valley. Other routes cross the mountains at various points to the east of the road by the mines. The distance from Trebizond to Gumisch-Khana is recorded by Mr. Hamilton as eighteen hours, or fifty-four calculated post miles (approximately forty English miles).
