# Right Effort and Right Mindfulness

In the context of the Aryan Path established by Gautama, Right Effort is the sixth element of an eight-part system. It is characterized by a rejection of slovenly application and good intentions that lack rigorous execution. Instead, the disciple is required to maintain a keenly critical eye upon their activities and a state of constant intellectual alertness.

## Connection to Right Mindfulness
Right Effort is closely connected with the seventh stage of the path, Right Mindfulness. While Right Effort focuses on the application of critical alertness to one's activities, Right Mindfulness serves as a constant guard against lapsing into personal feeling or seeking glory for actions performed or omitted.

The practice of these stages requires the disciple to maintain a clear mental awareness regardless of their current activity. Whether the disciple is speaking or silent, eating or drinking, standing or walking, or going forth or coming back, they must keep the following concepts in mind:
* The temporary character of the act.
* The ethical significance of the act.
* The understanding that there is no eternally persistent unity—no actor, speaker, eater, seer, or goer—behind the act.

## Context within the Aryan Path
Right Effort and Right Mindfulness exist within a broader framework of eight elements. The path begins with Right Views, which emphasizes the stern examination of ideas and the pursuit of truth, specifically condemning tawdry superstitions and the belief in the transmigration of souls. This is followed by Right Aspirations, which seeks to replace base cravings with desires for justice and the service of others.

Other elements of the path include Right Speech, Right Conduct, and Right Livelihood. The final stage is Right Rapture, which is intended to oppose witless gloryings and pointless ecstasies.

## Philosophical Foundations
The insistence on intellectual alertness in Right Effort is tied to the Buddhist identification of three cardinal sins: *raga* (sensuality), *dosa* (ill-will), and *moha*. Of these three, *moha*—defined as dullness or stupidity—is considered the worst. The Buddhist approach to these stages is described as an analogue to the Christian precept that all actions, including eating and drinking, should be done to the glory of God.
