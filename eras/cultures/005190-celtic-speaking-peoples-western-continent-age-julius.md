# Celtic-Speaking Peoples of the Western Continent

By the age of Julius Caesar, the Celtic-speaking peoples occupied a vast expanse of the western continent, extending from the British Isles through Gaul and into the Iberian Peninsula. These populations shared commonalities in speech, customs, and artistic traditions, though they were often divided into separate, warring tribal entities.

## Distribution and Migration
The presence of Celtic peoples on the western continent was the result of various migrations. In Britain, real knowledge of these movements begins with two distinct invasions: the Goidels during the later part of the Bronze Age, and the Brythons and Belgae during the Iron Age. These groups brought Celtic dialects and civilization to the island. By the time of Julius Caesar, nearly all inhabitants of Britain—with the possible exception of some tribes in the far north—were Celts in their customs and speech.

On the continent, kindred Celtic peoples pressed southward into Spain, where they encountered the Semitic Phoenician colonies of the coast and the heliolithic Basque people. In Gaul (modern France and Belgium), the chief tribes belonged to the same Celtic people as those who had previously occupied northern Italy and those who had settled in Asia Minor as Galatians.

## Social and Political Organization
Politically, the Celtic peoples were not a unified empire but were divided into separate tribes, each governed by its own princes. In Britain, these tribes were generally engaged in warfare with one another. 

Their settlements varied by environment and region. Many dwelt in hill forts protected by walls of rude stone or earth. Others lived in villages consisting of round huts sunk into the ground—a style similar to those found in parts of northern Gaul—or in subterranean chambered houses. In marshy areas, they constructed hamlets of pile-dwellings. However, in the south of Britain, town life and market centers began to emerge, accompanied by the use of improved housing.

## Economy and Trade
The southern Celtic tribes in Britain developed a system of currency. This included a gold coinage as well as a currency consisting of iron ingots or bars. These iron bars, as attested by surviving examples and the writings of Caesar, typically weighed 1-1/3 lb, though some weighed two-thirds of a pound or 2-2/3 lb.

## Religion and Culture
The priesthood of the Druids was the central feature of Celtic religion. The Druids wielded significant influence and taught a secret lore. They were known to practice magical arts and "barbarous rites of human sacrifice." Despite their social power, Druids ordinarily did not participate in political affairs.

In the realm of art, these tribes practiced a native Late Celtic fashion. This style was connected to the La-Tène culture of the continental Celts and descended from Mediterranean antecedents. The art is characterized as flamboyant.

## Contact with Rome
The expansion of the Roman state brought the Celtic peoples into direct conflict with Rome, most notably through the campaigns of Julius Caesar. Caesar established his reputation as a military leader in Gaul, where he drove back a German invasion and annexed the region to the Roman Empire. 

Caesar also crossed the Straits of Dover into Britain twice, in 55 and 54 B.C. During the second expedition, he encountered the native tribes led by Cassivelaunus (or Cassivelaunus), a chieftain who ruled the country north of the Thames. After several indecisive engagements, Caesar captured the camp of Cassivelaunus. The chieftain was forced to make peace, agreeing to provide hostages and pay tribute, although records indicate the tribute was never actually paid. Despite these incursions, Caesar made no permanent conquest of Britain.

## Later Transitions
Over time, the Celtic-speaking populations of the continent were supplanted. In regions such as Brabant and Limburg, the Celtic-speaking population was eventually subdued by the Franks. Consequently, the Celtic languages in these areas were entirely replaced by Frankish dialects.
