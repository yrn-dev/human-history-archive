# Western Africa and the Timne Language

The linguistic landscape of Western Africa includes a variety of speech groups, some of which exhibit specific grammatical structures such as pronominal prefixes and systems of concord. Among these, the Timne language of Sierra Leone and its cognate languages found along the north Guinea coast are notable for their use of pronominal prefixes.

## The Timne Language and Concord
The Timne language employs a system of concord and pronominal prefixes. The employment of this concord system is described as being precisely similar to the practice found in Bantu languages. This similar concord is also present in the suffixes of the Kiama (Tem) and Fula languages of Western Africa.

Despite these structural similarities in the use of prefixes and concord, there is absolutely no resemblance between the Timne group and Bantu languages regarding their word-roots. This lack of similarity extends to the language's substantives, pronouns, verbs, and numerals.

## Related Linguistic Groups in Western Africa
Other languages in the Western African region utilize prefixes, though they differ from the Bantu type. In the tongues of the eastern Niger delta, lower Niger, Benue, Cross River, and Northern Togoland, prefixes of a simple kind are used to express the quality of a noun and the difference between singular and plural. While these prefixes sometimes fall into "classes," they do not correspond to those of the Bantu type.

In Northern Togoland, the Benue river, the lower Niger, Calabar, and the Gold Coast, there is a distinct affinity with Bantu languages specifically in a few verbal roots and in the numerals 2, 3, 4, and occasionally 5. Similarly, Fula shows some resemblance in its grammatical construction, where suffixes take the place of prefixes, and possesses a concord form considerably like that of the Bantu. Fula also shares affinities in a few verbal, pronominal, and nominal roots, as well as in the numerals 3 and 4.

## Comparison with Bantu Structures
The Bantu type of language is characterized as a pronominal-prefix system. It is agglutinative in construction, meaning syntax is formed by adding suffixes and principally prefixes to a root. In these languages, the root remains practically unchanging except for its terminal vowel, although the preceding prefix or succeeding suffix may modify the pronunciation of the first or penultimate vowel or consonant.

The Bantu languages are distinguished from other West African speech by their phonology. While the Western group of Bantu is akin to the Negro languages of West-Central and Western Africa, the Bantu groups generally do not adopt the peculiar West African combinations of *gb* and *kp*. These specific combinations are characteristic of African speech located between the Guinea coast and the Upper Nile.

## Origins and Distribution
The Bantu languages and peoples are believed to have spread from a center of origin in west central Africa in comparatively recent times. It has been suggested that ancient Libyan, Nilotic, or Hamite conquerors may have taken up a form of speech—such as Kposo of northern Togoland, Fula, Kiama (Tern), or languages of the Benue or lower Niger—and cast it into the Bantu type. However, this division of sexless Negro speech shows no obvious traces of Hamitic (Caucasian) influence. The exact birthplace and the specific group of dialects from which the Bantu sprang remain a puzzle.
