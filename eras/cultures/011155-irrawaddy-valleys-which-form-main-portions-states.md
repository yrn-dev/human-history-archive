# Irrawaddy

The Irrawaddy is the most important river of Burma and one of the great rivers of the Indo-Chinese peninsula. Along with the Mekong, the Menam, and the Salween, the Irrawaddy's valleys form the main portions of the states of the region, specifically those of Burma, Siam (including Laos), and Cochin-China (including Cambodia and Tongking). These countries are characterized as populous, fertile, and well-watered, possessing a climate very similar to that of eastern Bengal.

## Geography and Course
The Irrawaddy rises in unexplored regions where China, Tibet, and India meet, appearing to be formed by the junction of several considerable streams. Two primary rivers, the Mali and the N'mai, meet at approximately latitude 25° 45', about 150 meters north of Bhamo, contributing chiefly to the river's volume. 

The river flows south through a landscape of varied terrain. In its upper reaches, it passes through a highland tract characterized by rugged hills and narrow gorges. This area includes the districts of Bhamo, Myitkyina, Katha, and the Upper Chindwin, as well as the Kachin hills and a large portion of the Northern Shan states. Further south, the river passes through the "dry zone" of Burma, consisting of undulating lowlands between the western fringe of the Southern Shan States and the Arakan Yomas. This zone extends from the north of Mandalay to Thayetmyo and encompasses several districts, including Sagaing, Mandalay, Meiktila, and Magwe.

South of Thayetmyo, the valley opens again. The influence of the tide begins at Yegin Mingyi near Myanaung, marking the start of the delta.

## The Delta and Tributaries
The Irrawaddy discharges its waters into the sea through a vast delta. The delta is a close network of creeks where boats frequently replace carts for transportation. The larger mouths of the Irrawaddy are often referred to as separate rivers, including the Pyamalaw, Pyinzalu, Pantanaw, Ngawun, and Panmawaddy. The Bassein river is one of the main arteries of this discharge. Wherever the delta land is cultivable, it forms a vast sheet of rice, with subsidiary crops of tobacco, sesamum, and cotton.

The river's chief tributary in Burma is the Chindwin (known as the Tanai in its upper reaches). The Chindwin rises southwest of Thama, flows north into the Hukawng valley, and eventually turns south to join the Irrawaddy at Pakôkku. Another tributary, the Mogaung river, flows for 180 meters from the watershed dividing the Irrawaddy and Chindwin drainages before joining the Irrawaddy.

## Natural Resources and Geology
The valley of the Irrawaddy contains Tertiary deposits where coal is found. Petroleum occurs in Miocene beds, with a well-known field located at Yenangyaung. The region also features "mud volcanoes" at Minbu; these are craters formed by the accretion of soft oily mud that overflows during gas discharges. In the Bhamo neighborhood, jadeite is associated with Tertiary intrusions of serpentine-like rock.

## Navigation and Communication
The Irrawaddy is a vital artery for internal communication. It is navigable for steamers throughout the year up to Bhamo, a distance of 900 meters from the sea. During the dry weather, navigation extends further north to the confluence of the Mali and N'mai rivers and as far as Myitkyina. The Chindwin is navigable for steamers for 300 meters from its junction with the Irrawaddy. The Mogaung river is navigable as far as Kamaing for approximately four months of the year.

The Irrawaddy Flotilla Company operates vessels between Rangoon and Bassein, as well as to Bhamo and other points along the river. To complement water transport, railway lines have been constructed, including the Mu Valley railway from Sagaing to Myitkyina and lines connecting Rangoon to Prome and Mandalay.

## Historical Significance
The Irrawaddy valley has been a focal point of military and political conflict. During the Second Burmese War in 1852, British forces seized Bassein on May 19 and occupied Prome on October 9. Following these actions, the British government and the East India Company's court of directors approved the annexation of the lower portion of the Irrawaddy Valley, including Prome. This annexation was officially proclaimed on January 20, 1853, ending the war.
