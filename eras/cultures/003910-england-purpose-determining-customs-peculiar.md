# England: Customs and Legal Traditions

The historical and legal landscape of England is characterized by a variety of specialized customs, ranging from ancient industrial courts and linguistic developments to specific regulations governing commerce and religious organization.

## Industrial and Local Customs
In the lead-mining districts of Derbyshire, England, specialized courts known as Barmote courts (also referred to as Berghmote, Barghmote, Bargemote, or Barmoot) were established. These courts are of very ancient origin, with records of their existence dating back to the reign of Edward I. The primary purpose of the Barmote courts is to determine customs peculiar to the lead-mining industry and to settle disputes arising from it. 

The jurisdiction of these courts is divided into seven clearly defined districts, covering both individual ownership lands and crown lands within the duchy of Lancaster. While many of their procedures and customs became obsolete due to the progress of modern mining, their powers were regulated by the High Peak Mining Customs and Mineral Courts Act 1851. Legal appeals from these courts are conducted via *certiorari*.

## Legal Customs of Carriage and Commerce
English law maintains a specific distinction between private carriers and common carriers. A common carrier is defined as someone who holds themselves out to the public as ready to carry goods for hire from place to place. Examples include railway companies, owners of stagecoaches, wharfingers carrying goods on their own lighters, and masters of general ships. This status applies regardless of whether one of the journey's termini is located outside of England.

Common carriers are subject to peculiar liabilities under the law. They are generally bound to carry the goods of any person offering payment unless a valid reason exists for refusal, such as the carriage being full or the item not being something the carrier habitually conveys. They must avoid unnecessary delay or deviation and charge reasonable rates without giving undue preference to specific customers—a principle of great importance in the law of railway companies. 

Unless a special contract limits liability, the common carrier acts as an insurer for entrusted goods, meaning the warranty is to carry them securely and safely, regardless of negligence. However, the law exempts carriers from liability for injuries or losses caused immediately by "the king's enemies" or "the act of God." Liability is also relieved if the customer commits fraud or deceit, such as misrepresenting the value of the goods.

## Linguistic and Writing Customs
Before the adoption of Christianity and the invasion of Britain, the Anglo-Saxons utilized the Runic alphabet. In its earliest form, as seen in Scandinavian countries and Schleswig, it consisted of twenty-four letters, all of which are found in abecedaria in England. Over time, two letters became obsolete while others were added; some of these additions were peculiar to specific parts of England.

While originally used for writing on wooden boards, surviving Runic inscriptions are found on metal objects (such as brooches and swords) and memorial stones, particularly in the north of England. Notable examples include the cross at Ruthwell in Dumfriesshire and the column at Bewcastle in Cumberland, the latter believed to commemorate Alhfrith, son of Oswio, who died around 670. 

The introduction of Christianity brought the Roman alphabet, though Runic writing persisted; coins with Runic legends were issued until at least the middle of the 8th century, and some memorial stones date to the 9th century. The Roman alphabet was adapted for the native language, as seen in the laws of Æthelberht. Early manuscripts are believed to be written in a character type of Celtic origin, likely introduced by Irish missionaries in the north of England, with possible Welsh influence. By the end of the 7th century, the alphabet was expanded to include two Runic letters for *w* and *th*.

## Religious Organization and Customs
Within the Baptist denomination in England, there is a general agreement with Congregationalists that each separate church is complete in itself, possessing the power to make its own regulations and choose its own ministers to advance the kingdom of Christ. However, a distinctive peculiarity exists among "Scotch Baptists" in the north of England and Scotland: they maintain that a "plurality of pastors" or elders is required for a church's complete organization.

Most of these churches are voluntarily united in associations based on geographical limits. These associations, along with independent churches, are part of the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland, formed in 1813 by the Particular Baptists. The Union does not exert authoritative action over separate churches but focuses on collecting information. As of 1909, the United Kingdom contained 3,046 Baptist churches, 4,124 chapels, and 424,008 members.
