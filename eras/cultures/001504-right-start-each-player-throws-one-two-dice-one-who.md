# Backgammon

Backgammon is a game played by two persons using a special board, draughtsmen (referred to as "men"), and a pair of dice. It is said to have been invented around the 10th century, though the Romans knew of a similar game called *Ludus duodecim scriptorum* ("the twelve-line game"), and Plato alluded to a game involving the placement of men after throwing dice. The etymology of the name is disputed; it may be Saxon (*baec* meaning back and *gamen* meaning game), Danish (*bakke* meaning tray and *gammen* meaning game), or Welsh (*bach* meaning little and *cammaun* meaning battle). In French, the game is called *trictrac*, a name that imitates the rattle of the dice.

## Equipment and Board Layout
The board is divided into four "tables," and each table is marked with six "points" of different colors. A projecting bar separates the inner tables from the outer tables. In the ordinary form of the game, the board is furnished with fifteen black and fifteen white men. It is customary to position the inner table nearest to the light. Each player requires a dice-box, and both players share a pair of dice with numbers one through six. These numbers have specific names: one is the "ace," two is the "deuce," and three is the "trey." Historically, four was called "quatre," five was "cinque," and six was "six."

## Gameplay and Movement
Players move their men from point to point alternately based on the throw of the dice. White men move from the black player's inner table to the black outer table, then to the white outer table, and finally to the white inner table. Black men move in the opposite direction.

A player may move a man a number of points corresponding to the dice thrown, provided the destination point is not "blocked" by two or more of the adversary's men. A player can use the whole throw for one man or move two separate men according to the numbers on each die. If a player is blocked and cannot move the whole throw, they must still move as much of the throw as possible. If moving one number first allows the other number to be played, the player is required to do so.

## Dice Rules and Doublets
To determine who starts, each player throws one or two dice; the player with the higher number plays first. This player may either use the numbers thrown or throw again using both dice.

When a player throws doublets (two of the same number), they may make four moves of that number. Some rules require the player to move the number thrown and then the number corresponding to the opposite side of the dice (for example, throwing sixes requires moving sixes and then aces). A player who throws doublets is generally granted another throw. If doublets are thrown a second time, the player moves and throws again. Certain variations restrict this advantage for the first doublets thrown by each player, though this restriction does not apply to a "deuce-ace" throw. A throw of deuce-ace may sometimes allow the player to choose any doublets on the opposite side of the dice and throw again.

Crucially, a player must be able to complete the doublets thrown to take the corresponding doublets or receive another throw. If a player cannot move the whole throw, they are not entitled to another throw.

## Strategy and Probability
Effective play requires knowledge of the thirty-six possible throws of two dice. Strategy involves making points to obstruct the opponent and placing "blots" (single men) where they are least likely to be hit. The most advantageous points to secure are the five-point in both the player's own and the adversary's inner tables, followed by the player's own bar-point and the four-point in their own inner table.

Probability tables indicate that the odds against being hit on an ace are 25 to 11 (approximately 9 to 4). Generally, if a blot must be left within the reach of one die, placing it nearer to the adversary's man decreases the probability of it being hit. If a blot can only be hit with double dice, it is considered "long odds" against being hit.

## Laws and Variations
According to the laws provided by Hoyle:
1. A man touched by the caster must be played if possible.
2. A man is not considered played until it is placed on a point and quitted.
3. There is no penalty for omitting a man from the board.
4. If men are borne before a taken-up man is entered, those borne men must be entered again.
5. Mistaken plays cannot be altered once the adversary has thrown, unless both players agree.
6. If dice are "cocked" (not lying squarely), a fresh throw is required.

A variation known as *Russian Backgammon* differs in that men are entered into the same table via dice throws, and both players move in the same direction toward the opposite table. In some versions of this game, a player is not required to enter all men before moving any, but if a man is "up," it must be entered before any other moves are made.
