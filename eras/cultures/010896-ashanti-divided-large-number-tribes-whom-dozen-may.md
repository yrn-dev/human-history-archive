# Ashanti

The Ashanti are a powerful African nation of the pure negro type, residing in a region characterized by dense, impenetrable forest. Tradition suggests they originated from bands of fugitives driven before Moslem tribes migrating southward from the Senegal and Niger countries during the 16th or 17th century. Their national character was forged through the valour they displayed while defending their forest territory. They are believed to be of the same race and language as the Fanti people located nearer the coast. According to tradition, the two groups separated due to a famine; the Fanti moved south and subsisted on herbs (*fan*), while the Ashanti subsisted on Indian corn (*san*), leading to their respective names.

## Social and Political Organization
The Ashanti are divided into a large number of tribes, twelve of which are distinguished: the Bekwai, Adansi, Juabin, Kokofu, Kumasi, Mampon, Nsuta, Nkwanta, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Ofinsu, and Adjisu. While each tribe possesses its own king, the king of Kumasi has been recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti since the beginning of the 18th century. The symbol of this paramount authority is the "golden stool."

Historically, the government was a blend of military aristocracy and monarchy. For the purposes of war, the confederate tribes were organized into six great clans, which eventually became the primary social fabric of the state. The King's Council consisted of the chiefs of these clans and a few sub-chiefs with hereditary rights. Unless the king possessed an exceptionally strong character, the council often exercised more power than the monarch. Socially, polygamy is practiced by those who can afford it. Land is held in common by the tribes, and any unallotted land is referred to as "stool lands," attached to the office of the king or head chief.

## History and Expansion
Osai Tutu is regarded as the real founder of Ashanti power. He extended the empire through conquests to the east and west, subdued the Mahommedan countries of Banna and Gaman (Jaman), and subdued the state of Denkera in 1719. He is also credited with either building or greatly extending the capital, Kumasi. Following his death in 1731, his successor, Osai Apoko, continued acquisitions toward the coast.

In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina became king. In 1807, after the Fanti refused to surrender two defeated Assin chiefs, Osai Tutu invaded Fantiland. This campaign resulted in the destruction of a town near Anamabo and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants. A subsequent agreement with Colonel G. Torrane, the governor of Cape Coast, acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over Fantiland and required the British to pay rent for the Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

The 19th century saw fluctuating relations with the British. In 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy at Essamako, killing the governor and eight Europeans; the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. However, a signal defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826, caused Ashanti power over coast tribes to wane. By 1831, the king purchased peace from administrator George Maclean for 600 oz. of gold and sent his son as a hostage.

Later monarchs included the peace-loving Kwaka Dua I (succeeded 1838), who encouraged trade, and Kofi Karikari, who succeeded him after a period of civil war and declared that his business would be war. This led to the war of 1873-74.

## British Annexation and Administration
In 1896, King Prempeh was deported. Following this, no successor was appointed to the throne, and Prempeh was never "enstooled." The affairs of Kumasi were administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart. In 1900, a rebellion broke out involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes. The rebels, numbering 40,000, were motivated by the dishonour of Prempeh's deportation and the British demand for 4,000 oz. of gold yearly, as well as Governor Sir F. Hodgson's demand for the golden stool, which the Kumasi had kept hidden.

On September 26, 1901, Ashanti was formally annexed to the British dominions. It was given a separate administration under the governor of the Gold Coast, represented by a chief commissioner and a staff of commissioners. The 1901 order in council stipulated that British ordinances regarding revenue and justice should respect native laws unless they were injurious to the natives or incompatible with British sovereignty.

## Culture, Economy, and Geography
The capital, Kumasi, is situated on a low rocky eminence in a forest clearing. Its name derives from the okum tree. The town features houses built of red clay, though principal chiefs own European-style houses. The most imposing structure is the fort built in 1896.

The Ashanti are skilled in gold work, pottery, and weaving cotton. In 1874, masks of beaten gold and elaborate goldsmith work were discovered in the royal palace, showing the influence of Moorish art. Economically, Obuassi is the center of gold mining, while Kintampo serves as a depot for government stores and a center for the kola-nut commerce, having formerly been a major mart for ivory and slaves.

Regarding religion, the majority of the Ashanti are spirit-worshippers and generally show a repugnance toward the doctrines of Islam, though some traders are Mahommedans and some converts to Christianity exist through Basel and Wesleyan missions.
