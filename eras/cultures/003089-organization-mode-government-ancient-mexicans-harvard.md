# Organization and Mode of Government of the Ancient Mexicans

## Academic Study of Ancient Mexican Governance
The study of the social and political structures of ancient Mexico has been advanced through the work of American archaeologists and ethnologists. Specifically, Adolph Francis Alphonse Bandelier, a Swiss-born researcher who emigrated to the United States, conducted extensive archaeological and ethnological investigations in Mexico, as well as in the south-western United States and South America. 

Bandelier's research contributed to the understanding of prehistoric civilizations in these regions. His specific scholarly contributions regarding the ancient Mexicans include a series of studies published in the *Annual Reports* of the Peabody Museum of American Archaeology and Ethnology at Harvard University between 1877 and 1879. These works focused on three primary areas of societal structure:
* The social organization and mode of government.
* The distribution and tenure of lands, including customs related to inheritance.
* The art of war and the mode of warfare.

## Cultural and Cosmological Context
While the specific administrative details of government are noted as subjects of Bandelier's research, the archive provides information on the broader Mexican cosmology, which informs the cultural worldview of the people. A central feature of Mexican cosmology is the theory of the ages of the world. Unlike other ancient civilizations such as Greece, Persia, or Babylon—which recognized four ages—the Mexicans assumed the existence of five ages, referred to as "suns."

These five ages are categorized as follows:
1. The sun of earth.
2. The sun of fire.
3. The sun of air.
4. The sun of water.
5. The fifth sun, which is the present age and remained unnamed.

According to this belief system, each of these ages concluded with a physical catastrophe.

## Regional Isolation and Legal History
The ancient Mexicans developed their societal systems in isolation from other major American civilizations. At the time of the arrival of the Spaniards, there was no intercourse between the Mexicans and the Peruvians; the two groups had no knowledge of one another, and any previous links between them had been lost or forgotten. This isolation is evidenced by the fact that the Mexicans had never heard of the potato, which served as a primary article of diet for the Peruvians.

In later historical contexts, the legal reach of the Mexican state extended into territories such as Texas. Under Mexican law, slavery was forbidden in Texas before the region seceded from the republic of Mexico in 1835 and was subsequently annexed by the United States in 1844.
