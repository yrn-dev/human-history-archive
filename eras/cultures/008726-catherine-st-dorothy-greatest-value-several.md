# Catherine and St. Dorothy

The names Catherine and Dorothy appear across various contexts in the archive, ranging from early Bohemian literature and royal history to military fortifications and the etymology of children's toys.

## Early Bohemian Literature
In the study of the Bohemian or Czech language, the earliest genuine documents consist of legends and hymns. Among these legends, those concerning St. Catherine and St. Dorothy are noted as having the greatest value. Other early Bohemian writings include ancient epic fragments, such as *Tandarias a Floribella* and the *Alexandreis*. While the MSS. of Kralodvur (Koniginhof) and Zelena Hora (Gruneberg) are considered forgeries from the early 19th century, the genuine early works have been printed since the 19th-century revival of Bohemian literature.

## Historical and Royal Figures
The name Catherine is associated with several prominent historical figures:
* **Catherine de' Medici**: The queen of Henry II of France. Decorative bookbindings created for Henry II feature the initials of the king and Catherine de' Medici, accompanied by emblems of bows and crescents.
* **Catherine II**: The ruler who invited the French painter Gabriel Francois Doyen to St. Petersburg after the beginning of the Revolution. Doyen, who had previously been a professor at the Academy of Painting and known for works such as the "Death of St Louis," settled in St. Petersburg under her invitation and received rewards and honours before his death in 1806.

## Military Fortifications
Fort St. Catherine was one of the strongest forts of Antwerp, designed by Engineer-General Brialmont. The fort was equipped with nine modern armoured turrets. During conflict, these turrets were targeted by heavy German artillery, specifically 42-cm mortars nicknamed "black Maria" (*die schwarze Marie*) and "fat Bertha" (*die dicke Bertha*). The impact of these shells caused severe destruction; one shell flung a 40-ton cupola ten metres to the side, while another penetrated several metres of concrete, causing appalling havoc.

## Etymology of the Doll
The term "doll," referring to a child's plaything in the shape of a human figure, was not in common use during the middle ages, when terms such as "children's babies" were used instead. One commonly accepted view is that the word "doll" is an abbreviation of the name Dorothy, a connection also seen in the Scottish "Doroty." Other theories suggest a derivation from the Norse *daul* (woman) or a connection to the word "idol."
