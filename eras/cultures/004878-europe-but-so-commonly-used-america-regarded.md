# Europe

## Prehistoric and Early History
Geological evidence indicates that during the Pliocene era—prior to the First Glacial Age—and throughout the First Interglacial period, crude stone implements known as "Eoliths" were used. These roughly chipped flints and stones served as hand-axes for hammering or fighting. However, there are no known bones or other remains of the quasi-human beings who utilized these tools in Europe or America from half a million years ago.

In terms of technological development, the use of iron weapons became customary in Europe, Egypt, and Assyria several centuries before such practices became common in China; specifically, iron weapons were used in Europe two or three hundred years or more before 500 B.C.

## Natural History and Biology
The geological record shows that several extinct animal types once had a wide distribution across Europe. The *Ceratodontidae*, currently restricted to a single species in the rivers of Queensland, left remains in the Jurassic, Rhaetic, Cretaceous, and Triassic rocks of Europe. Similarly, the *Osteoglossidae* occurred in Europe during the Eocene, and the *Amiidae* and *Lepidosteidae* were abundant during the Miocene and Eocene epochs.

In the modern era, certain non-native species have become common in Europe. The Sandwich island goose (*Bernicla sandvicensis*) is now frequently domesticated. Additionally, the budgerigar (*Melopsittacus undulatus*), a small parrot native to Australia, has become more familiar in Europe than most native birds, as it is bred in aviaries and used in the streets to "tell fortunes."

Regarding botany, the plant *Medicago sativa* (known as lucerne, purple medick, or alfalfa) is native to the eastern Mediterranean. It was introduced to Italy in the 1st century A.D. and subsequently became naturalized across Europe. In Britain, it was first cultivated around 1650 and can be found wild in fields and hedges. Lucerne is heavily cultivated as a forage crop in France and other continental European regions.

## Engineering and Infrastructure
European bridge construction has seen a shift in connection methods for girders. Early English bridges, such as those at Saltash, Newark Dyke, and Chepstow, utilized a pin system of connection. While this system saves weight and simplifies erection, early versions often worked loose due to insufficient bearing area, sometimes requiring the substitution of riveted covers. Consequently, the pin system is now rarely used in Europe, where the tendency is to prefer riveted connections or to limit pins to tension members only. This contrasts with America, where the pin system remains common enough to be considered a distinctive American feature.

## Political History and the Great Power System
During the early 19th century, Spain exhibited a strong monarchist tendency to return to past conditions, which included the restoration of the Inquisition. In 1810, Napoleon placed his brother Joseph on the Spanish throne, leading Spanish colonies in the Americas to revolt against the European Great Power system. 

When Spain struggled to suppress these revolts, Austria suggested that European monarchs provide assistance to Spain, a move aligned with the spirit of the Holy Alliance. While Britain opposed this within Europe, the United States issued the Monroe Doctrine in 1823, warning that any extension of the European system into the Western Hemisphere would be viewed as a hostile act.

Despite the loss of its colonies, Spain continued to operate under the protection of the Concert of Europe. In 1823, a French army, acting on a mandate from a European congress, crushed a popular insurrection in Spain. Simultaneously, the Austrian statesman Metternich, the driving force behind this conspiracy of governments, oversaw the suppression of a revolution in Naples by Austria.
