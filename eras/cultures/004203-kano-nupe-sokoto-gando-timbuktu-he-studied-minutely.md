# Sokoto, Kano, Nupe, Gando, and Timbuktu

The regions of Sokoto, Kano, Nupe, Gando, and Timbuktu are areas of significant historical and geographical importance in Africa, particularly within the central and western Sudan. These locations have been the subject of extensive study by European explorers and have transitioned through various forms of governance, including ancient sultanates, Fula emirates, and British administration.

## The Empire of Sokoto and Fula Influence
The Fula (also known as Fulbe, Peuls, or Fellatah) are a powerful and numerous people spread across a vast region from Senegal to nearly Darfur. While they have no single country of their own, they have frequently been the dominant native race in the areas they inhabit. In the early years of the 19th century, under the leadership of Othman Dan Fodio, the Fula extended their sway east of the Niger River. After subduing the Hausa states, they established the empire of Sokoto.

The Sokoto empire included several vassal emirates, among which were Kano, Gando, and Nupe. To the east of the Niger, Sokoto and its tributary emirates were ruled by Fula princes. In other regions, such as the town of Illorin—founded by Yoruba toward the end of the 18th century—the kingdom became an emirate of the Sokoto empire around 1825 after coming under Mahommedan influence. In Illorin, the Fula maintained the Yoruba system of government, which vests chief power in a council of elders.

## Nupe and the Province of Bida
Nupe is a region that became a Fula emirate, with the town of Bida serving as its capital. Bida was founded in 1859 upon the establishment of Fula rule in Nupe. The town is walled and characterized by streets planted with large shade-trees. Its inhabitants are mostly Hausa and are renowned for their glass factory and the production of indigo and dyed cloths. Bida is particularly noted for its elegant embossed copper and brass work, specifically "Bida goblets" that blend the two metals.

The political history of Nupe involved conflicts with British interests. In 1897, a two-day battle occurred outside the walls of Bida between the Fula army (consisting mostly of cavalry) and the Royal Niger Company. Although the Fula were defeated, the king returned to his throne after the company's troops withdrew. However, by 1901, British troops marched on Bida; the emir fled to Kano, and the province was placed under British administrative control.

## Timbuktu and Early Exploration
Timbuktu is a city in the west that has long been a goal for explorers seeking to penetrate the interior of Africa. René Auguste Caillié, a French explorer born in 1799, successfully reached Timbuktu in 1828. To achieve this, Caillié learned Arabic and the laws and customs of Islam, posing as a Mussulman and an Arab from Egypt. He arrived in Timbuktu by water via the city of Jenné, spending a fortnight there from April 20 to May 4. Caillié was the first traveler to accomplish the journey in safety, as a previous British officer, Major Gordon Laing, had been murdered upon leaving the city in 1826.

## European Exploration and British Administration
In 1850, a mission was launched from Tripoli involving James Richardson, Adolf Overweg, and Heinrich Barth. Following the deaths of Richardson and Overweg, Barth continued the mission alone until 1855. Barth conducted a fruitful expedition, traversing the country from Cameroon in the south and Bagirmi and Lake Chad in the east to Timbuktu in the west. During his travels, he made prolonged sojourns in Timbuktu and the emirates or sultanates of Gando, Nupe, Sokoto, and Kano, where he minutely studied their resources, civilizations, history, and topography. His findings were published in the five-volume work *Travels and Discoveries in North and Central Africa* (1857-1858).

Much of this region eventually came under British rule as part of Nigeria. In Northern Nigeria, the British established protectorates and organized provinces. For example, the province of Kontagora—which had been a Fula emirate established in 1864 after the conquest of the pagan kingdom of Yauri—was brought under British domination in 1901. This action was prompted by threats against the British military station of Jebba and slave-raiding within protected territory. By 1903, the emir of Kontagora, Ibrahim, was reinstated after taking an oath of allegiance to the British crown and agreeing to abolish the slave trade within the province.
