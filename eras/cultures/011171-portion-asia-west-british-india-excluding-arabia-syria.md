# The High Plateau of Western Asia

The region of Asia situated west of British India, excluding the territories of Arabia and Syria, is characterized as an extensive plateau. This high region is nearly conterminous with the provinces of Asia Minor, Armenia, Iran (or Persia), Baluchistan, and Afghanistan. In terms of size, this plateau is as large as Tibet, although it exists at a significantly lower altitude.

## Geography and Borders
The borders of this plateau are defined by a variety of maritime and terrestrial landmarks. To the south, the boundary runs along the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Sea, and the Tigris, extending westward to the north-east angle of the Levant. The northern border follows the high land nearly along the 36th parallel North to the southern shore of the Caspian Sea, continuing thence to the Sea of Marmora and the Black Sea.

The eastern flank is marked by a line of hills located a short distance from the Indus river, between the river's mouth and the Himalaya, approximately on the 72nd meridian. While most of these hills do not exceed 5,000 or 4,000 feet in elevation, some summits reach 10,000 feet or more. The south-western face follows the coast of the Persian Gulf from the mouth of the Indus. Further west, it is formed by a mountain scarp that flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris, rising in various points to 10,000 feet. This scarp extends through Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian before turning along the north-eastern angle of the Mediterranean and the Taurus range.

In the north-east, the Afghan table-land abuts on Tibet and the Himalaya, forming a continuous mountain mass between the 34th and 36th parallels North and the 71st and 72nd meridians. From the intersection of the 36th parallel and the 71st meridian, an unbroken mountain range stretches west toward the Caspian and north-east toward the crest of the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau. The north-eastern portion of this range is of great altitude and serves as a divide between the headwaters of the Oxus, which flow to the Aral Sea, and the headwaters of the Indus and its tributary, the Kabul, which discharge southward into the Arabian Sea. The western section of this range was known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons and decreases in height west of the 65th meridian.

## Ethnology and Human Populations
The populations of this region are categorized by various racial and linguistic classifications. According to Professor Huxley, the "Melanochroic" race—characterized by dark hair—extends through Persia and south-western Asia. This group is often intermixed with the "Xanthochroic" race, which consists of fair-haired people with blue eyes and fair skins. Together, these two groups were formerly referred to as the Caucasian races.

Linguistically, the region is home to the Aryans, who speak languages derived from or akin to ancient Sanskrit. They occupy the temperate zone extending from the Mediterranean across Afghanistan, Persia, and the highlands of Asia Minor to India. Both the Aryans and the Semitic peoples (who speak languages akin to ancient Assyrian) are considered branches of the Melanochroi, modified by admixture with other groups such as the Mongols, Xanthochroi, and Australoids.

## Natural History and Fauna
Zoologically, the region is divided among different provinces. The northern portion of Asia, extending south to the Himalaya, is not distinct from Europe and is part of the Palaearctic region. However, the extreme south-west—including southern Persia and reaching to Baluchistan—constitutes a separate zoological district belonging to the Ethiopian region, which also encompasses Africa.

In terms of domesticated animals, the horse is produced in high perfection within the hot and dry countries of western Asia. While the elephant is found in northern India, it is not found wild west of the Jumna river.

## Historical and Political Context
Historically, the countries west of India remained comparatively untouched by the maritime oriental conquests of European naval powers in the 16th century. Because these regions were less exposed to naval invasion than India or the extreme East, European power (excluding Russia) did not establish the same level of territorial acquisition through commerce and navigation in this specific area.

In terms of governance, the region has historically seen the consolidation of small aristocratic or feudal states into monarchies. Unlike in Europe, where royalty was sometimes replaced by popular government, revolutions in Asia have generally resulted in a change of dynasty rather than a change in the form of government.
