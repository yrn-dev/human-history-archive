# BARD

The term "bard" is a word of Celtic derivation, appearing as *baird* in Gaelic, *bardh* in Cymric, and *bard* in Irish. It is applied to the ancient poets of the Celtic peoples. While the name is sometimes used loosely as a synonym for poets in general, the specific title of "bard" and the particular privileges associated with this class of poets are found only among Celtic peoples.

## Historical Origins and Early Records
In his accounts of the customs and manners of Britain and Gaul, Caesar did not use the term "bard," though he attributed the functions of bards to a section of the Druids, suggesting a close connection between the two classes. However, later Latin authors recognized *Bardi* as the official title for the national minstrels or poets of the peoples of Britain and Gaul. This includes mentions by Ammianus Marcellinus, Festus, and Lucan.

The institution of the bard disappeared relatively quickly in Gaul as Latin and Teutonic conquests pushed purely Celtic peoples into Brittany, Ireland, Wales, and the north of Scotland. While evidence of bards exists in all these regions, the most distinctively national institutions and surviving relics are found in Ireland and Wales.

## The Bardic Society in Wales
In Wales, bards formed an organized society characterized by hereditary privileges and rights. They were held in the highest respect and were exempt from military service and taxes. Their primary responsibilities included singing hymns of praise to God and celebrating the victories of their people. Because they provided poetic expression for national and religious sentiments, bards exercised significant influence.

The society of bards was governed by laws. These were first distinctly formulated by Gruffydd ap Conan and later revised by Hywell Dha. To maintain their craft, bards gathered at stated intervals for great festivals known as *Eisteddfodau* (derived from the Welsh *eistedd*, meaning "to sit"). At these congresses, famous bards from different districts competed in song, with nobles and princes serving as umpires. These gatherings continued to be summoned by royal commission even after the conquest of Wales.

## Traditions and Historical Reliability
In Ireland, bards maintained unwritten poems that recorded traditional truths. Lord Lyttleton, commenting on these tales in his *History of Henry II*, noted that while some traditional truths may have been preserved in manuscripts written after the arrival of Roman letters via St. Patrick, these are often mixed with "fabulous stories." He argued that such traditions cannot always be separated from "specious fictions," and that a bard living in the sixth or seventh century after Christ cannot serve as a voucher for facts occurring before the incarnation, though their evidence may be useful for events within their own time or the memory of their contemporaries.

## Evolution of the Term
The usage of the word "bard" shifted in later centuries. Due in part to Lucan's use of the phrase *carmina bardi*, the term began to be used in the early 17th century to describe any serious poet, whether they wrote epics or lyrics. This usage is found in the works of Pope, Milton, and Shakespeare. Conversely, in Lowland Scots, the term evolved into a word of reproach and contempt, used to describe a class of "frenzied vagabonds."
