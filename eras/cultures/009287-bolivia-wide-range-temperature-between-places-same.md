# Climate and Geography of Bolivia

Bolivia is located entirely within the torrid zone. Because of this position, variations in temperature are primarily driven by prevailing winds, mountain barriers, and elevation. The country exhibits every gradation of temperature, ranging from the tropical heat of the lowlands to the Arctic cold found on snow-capped peaks.

## Climatic Zones
The natives of Bolivia categorize the climate into several distinct vertical zones based on elevation:

*   **Yungas:** This zone encompasses the mountain valleys and lowlands up to 5,000 feet. It is characterized by a tropical temperature, an exceedingly humid atmosphere, and the absence of winter. Official estimates place the mean temperature at 70°F, though this likely averages the low country with higher elevations. In these regions, malaria and fevers are common, and the ground is covered in decaying vegetation.
*   **Valle:** Extending from 5,000 to 9,500 feet, this sub-tropical region consists of deep valleys. It is described as a region of perpetual summer, featuring a warm climate with moderate temperature variations and no cold weather.
*   **Cabezera de Valle:** This temperate zone includes the heads of deep valleys from 9,500 to 11,000 feet. It is divided into regular seasons and is favorable for producing vegetables and cereals.
*   **Puna:** Located between 11,000 and 12,500 feet, the puna includes the great central plateau. The air is cold and dry, with only two seasons: winter and a cold summer or autumn. The warmer season is too short to support the production of anything other than barley and potatoes. The official mean temperature is estimated at 54°F.
*   **Puna Brava:** This bleak and inhospitable territory extends from 12,500 feet up to the snow limit at approximately 17,500 feet. It is inhabited only by miners and shepherds.
*   **Eternal Snow:** Above the puna brava lies an Arctic zone within the tropics.

Generally, the temperate (*cabezera de valle*) and sub-tropical (*valle*) regions are considered agreeable and healthy, with moderate temperatures in the shade and plentiful rainfall. While there is a high mortality rate among natives, this is attributed to diet and unsanitary habits rather than the climate.

## Rainfall and Winds
Rainfall patterns are governed by prevailing winds and the high mountain ranges. In southern Bolivia, trade winds create a wet and dry season similar to northern Argentina. In the north and east of the Cordillera Oriental, rain falls throughout the year, with the rainy season typically occurring from November to March. 

Conversely, the Cordillera extracts moisture from easterly winds, leaving the elevated plateaus on the west side with limited rainfall in the north. This rainfall diminishes toward the south until the surface becomes absolutely barren. The northern plateau occasionally experiences brief, furious rain-storms and frequent electrical wind storms.

## Geology and Terrain
The geology of Bolivia is divided by its mountain ranges and plateaus. The Western Cordillera consists mainly of Cretaceous and Jurassic beds, along with ashes and lavas from great volcanoes. The eastern ranges are formed of Palaeozoic rocks with other intrusions and granitic elements. The intervening plateau is covered by terrestrial and freshwater deposits, featuring ridges of Palaeozoic rock and gypsiferous marls and red sandstones.

Palaeozoic beds have provided fossils from the Carboniferous, Devonian, Ordovician, and Cambrian ages. In the north-eastern ranges and north, the Carboniferous and Devonian are extensively developed, while Cambrian and Ordovician beds dominate the eastern Andes in the south. The Chiquitos hills in eastern Bolivia are composed of ancient sedimentary rocks of unknown age.

Recent deposits in the depression between the Western and Eastern Cordilleras appear to be partly of torrential origin. Lakes Pampa Aullaguas (Poopo) and Titicaca were once more extensive than they are currently.

## Flora and Fauna
The diversity of altitude allows Bolivia to support flora from every climatic zone, from luxuriant tropical forests in the Amazon basin to scanty Arctic vegetation in the lofty Cordilleras. 

In the puna region, coarse grasses, quinoa, oca, and potatoes characterize the landscape, while barley is grown for fodder. The temperate and warm regions grew Indian corn before Europeans introduced beans, rye, oats, wheat, and various Old World fruits and vegetables. Tropical and sub-tropical indigenous plants include cassava, peanuts, pineapple, rubber-producing trees, vanilla, sarsaparilla, and hardwoods such as mahogany, cedar, and rosewood. Coca is cultivated on the eastern slopes of the Andes at 5,000 to 6,000 feet, where frosts are unknown and temperatures remain uniform. Quina (calisaya) is found between 3,000 and 9,000 feet; its bark is a significant export.

The fauna mirrors that of Peru, Brazil, and Argentina. Tropical forests are home to jaguars, pumas, monkeys, tapirs, and sloths. The wooded Andean foothills house the rare spectacled bear (*Ursus ornatus*). In the colder plateau regions, the chinchilla, viscacha, and South American otter are found. The peccary ranges from the Chaco to the northern wooded districts, and the large marsh deer is common in the Chaco. Lower tropical districts feature various amphibia, turtles, lizards, and snakes, including boa-constrictors and rattlesnakes.
