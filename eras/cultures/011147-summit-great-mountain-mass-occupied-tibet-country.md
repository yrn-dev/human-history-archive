# Tibet

Tibet is a country that occupies the summit of a great mountain mass in Asia. Known to its own inhabitants as *Bod* or *Bodyul*, it is characterized as a rugged table-land. While narrow in proportion to its length, the region is broken up by a series of mountain ranges that generally follow the direction of the table-land's length and frequently rise into regions of perpetual snow.

## Physical Geography and Topography
The landscape of Tibet consists of narrow, closely hemmed-in valleys with moderate inclinations located between the flanks of mountain ranges. At various intervals, these valleys open into wide plains. These plains are occupied either by rivers or by salt lakes that have no outflow.

The eastern termination of Tibet is marked by a line of snowy mountains that flank China on the west, situated between the 35th and 27th parallels of latitude and approximately 103 degrees east. To the west, the table-land extends beyond the political limits of Tibet to about 70 degrees east, maintaining similar physical features. The ranges covered in perpetual snow extend as far west as Samarkand, after which they diminish in height and end in low hills north of Bokhara.

## Regional Mountain Systems
Tibet is situated between several major mountain systems that radiate eastward from the central mountain masses of Asia. The Himalayas are located to the south-east of Tibet, while the Kuen Lun ranges lie to the north. 

The *Astin-tagh* forms the outermost border-ridge or escarpment on the north of the Tibetan plateau. Considered by judges such as Przhevalsky, Sven Hedin, Bogdanovich, and Grenard to be a subsidiary range of the main Kuen-lun, the *Astin-tagh* consists of several parallel main ranges—two in some areas, three in the east, and up to four to the northeast of Tsaidam. This system includes the *massif* of Anambaruin-ula and the Humboldt Range, the latter of which features a pass at 13,200 ft. The *Astin-tagh* eventually merges into the Nan-shan (Southern Mountains) or continues as the south Koko-nor Range.

Passes in the *Astin-tagh* vary in altitude: the Lower *Astin-tagh* ranges from 10,150 to 10,700 ft., while the Upper *Astin-tagh* ranges from 11,770 to 15,680 ft. (Tash-davan), though one pass by the Charkhlik-su is only 9,660 ft. high. Unlike the ranges of the Tibetan plateau, the mountains of the *Astin-tagh* consist from base to summit of bare, barren hard rock piled into rugged masses and pinnacled crests.

## Hydrology and Environmental Impact
The Tibetan mountain mass serves as the northern water-parting of India. The great rivers of northern India—the Indus, the Brahmaputra, and the Ganges—all derive their waters from this mass. Specifically, the Tsanpo, a great river of eastern Tibet, supplies the main stream of the Brahmaputra. Additionally, the two primary rivers of China originate on the eastern face of Tibet: the Hwang-ho (from the north-east angle) and the Yang-tsze-kiang (from the south-east). The main stream of the latter is known in Tibet as the Dichu, and its chief feeder, the Ya-lung-kiang, is regarded as the territorial boundary between Tibet and China.

The immense altitude of this mountain mass, which includes the highest peaks in the world, creates a significant atmospheric barrier. The range occupies an area roughly 2,000 miles long and between 100 to 500 miles wide, situated between 28 and 35 degrees north and 65 and 100 degrees east. Because these mountains rise far above the line of perpetual snow—with many peaks exceeding 20,000 ft. and few main passes below 15,000 or 16,000 ft.—they form an almost impassable barrier to animal and vegetable life. Furthermore, the mass obstructs nearly three-fourths of the air and moisture resting on this part of the earth, causing a complete separation of atmospheric conditions between its opposite flanks.

## Regional Connections
To the west, the north-eastern portion of the Afghan table-land abuts upon Tibet and the Himalaya, forming a continuous mountain mass between the 71st and 72nd meridians and 34 and 36 degrees north. From the intersection of the 36th parallel and the 71st meridian, an unbroken range stretches toward the crest of the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau.
