# The Perennially Humid Regions of the Malayan Peninsula and Western Archipelago

The perennially humid regions of the Malayan peninsula and the western portion of the archipelago are characterized by dense forests. These areas are often difficult to traverse due to the prevalence of thorny cane, a palm of the genus *Calamus*, which reaches its greatest development in this part of Asia.

## Flora and Vegetation
The vegetation in these regions is largely consistent with the hotter and wetter regions of India. However, the Malayan Islands exhibit a greater uniformity of humidity and temperature, which leads to the predominance of certain tropical forms that are less conspicuous in India, such as nutmeg and liquidambar. Liquidambar is particularly notable as one of the most conspicuous trees in Java.

The chief trees found in these humid regions belong to several orders, including:
*   Dipterocarpaceae, Meliaceae, and Sapindaceae
*   Terebinthaceae, Clusiaceae, and Ternstroemiaceae
*   Leguminosae, laurels, oaks, and figs
*   Sapotaceae, Dilleniaceae, and nutmegs

Additionally, palms, bamboos, *Dracaena*, and *Pandanus* are abundant. The genus *Nepenthes* is also specially developed in the Malayan area, extending from Madagascar to New Caledonia; while it is found in Ceylon and as far north as the Khasi hills, it is absent from the peninsula of India and the Himalaya.

## Regional Variations and Influences
The flora of the Malayan province—which includes the Malay peninsula, the Malay islands, and the Philippines—possesses an extraordinary number of interesting and peculiar genera. The proximity of the Australian continent has allowed for the partial diffusion of Australian types not seen in India. For example, the casuarina is abundant on the mountains of eastern Java, and temperate Australian forms appear on the higher mountains of Borneo. Australian genera also extend to the Philippines and southern China.

In Borneo and Sumatra, rhododendrons occur as low as sea level. Conversely, the mountains of Java lack a truly alpine flora, and *Saxifraga* is not found there.

## Broad Biological Context
The Malayan peninsula and islands are part of a larger south-eastern Asian region (extending from approximately the 65th to the 105th meridian) that includes India. Sir J.D. Hooker estimated the number of indigenous plant species in this broad region to be between 12,000 and 15,000. The principal orders in this region, arranged by numerical importance, are Leguminosae, Rubiaceae, Orchidaceae, Compositae, Gramineae, Euphorbiaceae, Acanthaceae, Cyperaceae, and Labiatae. Orchidaceae are present in specially large proportions.

In terms of fauna, the insects of the Malayan Islands, Siam, China, and India south of the Himalaya belong to a single group. Genera and species are often identical on opposite sides of the Bay of Bengal. Regarding avifauna, the Malayan province is distinguished by a high number of peculiar genera. The influence of the Australian realm is further evidenced by the presence of Megapodes in Borneo, Labuan, and Celebes. However, cockatoos are absent from this region, as they remain strictly on the other side of Wallace's line.

## Human Geography: The Bugis
Within the Malay Archipelago, the Bugis (or Bughis) are a people of Malayan stock. Originally from the kingdom of Boni in the south-western peninsula of Celebes, they spread across the island and established settlements throughout the archipelago. They are described as robust, active, and enterprising, with a complexion slightly lighter than the average Malay.

The Bugis are known for being industrious and ingenious, practicing agriculture more than neighboring tribes and manufacturing cotton-cloth for both local use and export. They maintain a considerable trade in vegetable and mineral products from Boni, including camphor, nutmeg, pearls, tortoise-shell, and gold-dust. Their affinity for the sea has allowed them to maintain an almost complete monopoly of trade around Celebes.

Socially, the Bugis have their own schools and well-built towns. Their political structure in Boni consists of a confederation of eight petty states. The king is elected for life from among their own number by the chiefs of these states and requires their consent for public measures. While some chiefships are hereditary, others are open to the privileged classes, and women occasionally govern the state. Since the 17th century, the Bugis have been Mahommedans, though they retain rites connected to the worship of Siva from an original form of nature-worship influenced by Hinduism.
