# Ashanti

The Ashanti were a powerful African kingdom that maintained complex and often volatile relations with British colonial interests on the Gold Coast during the 19th and early 20th centuries. Their history during this period is characterized by territorial disputes, diplomatic treaties, and several major military conflicts.

## Early 19th Century Diplomacy and Conflict
In an effort to cultivate intercourse with the kingdom, the British government appointed Mr. Joseph Dupuis, formerly the consul at Mogador, as consul to Kumasi. Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast in January 1819. At that time, tensions existed between the Ashanti and the coast natives, who received British support. Dupuis departed for Kumasi on February 9, 1820, arriving on the 28th. Following meetings with the king, a treaty was established that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over the territory of the Fanti and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies.

This treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, who viewed it as a betrayal of the natives under British protection. Supported by the government in London, the British assumed direct control of their settlements in 1821. The first crown-appointed governor, Sir Charles M'Carthy, supported the Fanti. However, on January 21, 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles at Essamako, a location beyond the Prah. The Ashanti force of 10,000 men overwhelmed Sir Charles's 500. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. It was claimed that the defeat was caused by an ordnance-keeper bringing kegs of vermicelli instead of ammunition, though records suggest the catastrophe was inevitable.

## Transition of Power and the Battle of Dodowah
The day of the defeat at Essamako coincided with the death of Osai Tutu Quamina, who was succeeded by Osai Okoto. A period of chronic warfare followed until August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at Dodowah. This loss led to a decline in Ashanti power over the coast tribes. By 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, paying 600 oz. of gold and providing his son as a hostage to Cape Coast. Following the battle of Dodowah, the payment of ground rent for British-held forts ceased.

## The War of 1873
Hostilities resumed in the 1870s due to two primary causes. The principal cause was the transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti considered the Elmina their subjects and the territory their own; the king of Ashanti had previously received an annual payment (a "custom-note") from the Dutch, which the Dutch viewed as a trade present but the Ashanti viewed as ground rent. A secondary cause was the Ashanti captivity of four Europeans: Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife and infant son (the child died of privation), and Mr. J. Kuhne—all members of the Basel mission—as well as a French trader, Monsieur M.J. Bonnat. These captives were taken to Kumasi.

On January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force crossed the Prah and invaded the British protectorate, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon near Cape Coast. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition led by Sir Garnet Wolseley. Upon landing at Cape Coast on October 2, Wolseley found the Ashanti forces had been decimated by fever and smallpox and were preparing to return home. Wolseley marched toward Kumasi, while Captain John Glover cooperated from the east with native levies.

## British Administration and the 1900 Rebellion
Following the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was named to the Ashanti throne. Captain Donald W. Stewart was installed as a British resident at Kumasi. While other states in the confederacy kept their tribal systems and kings, the affairs of the Kumasi were administered by chiefs under British guidance. A fort was established in Kumasi and garrisoned by Gold Coast constabulary.

Despite an outward appearance of submission, the Kumasi chiefs remained unreconciled. In 1900, a rebellion broke out involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes, though other confederacy tribes remained loyal. The rebels commanded a force reported at 40,000. Tensions escalated on March 28, when the governor of the Gold Coast, Sir F. Hodgson, demanded the chiefs pay 4000 oz. of gold annually and reproached them for hiding the golden stool since 1896. On April 1, Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa sent to find the stool.

The rebels threatened the Kumasi fort, where Sir F. and Lady Hodgson and Basel missionaries took refuge. The garrison was eventually reinforced by Gold Coast and Lagos constabulary, and later by Major A. Morris with 250 men, bringing the total garrison to 700. The rebellion was eventually crushed by Colonel Willcocks, whose force included 134 British officers, 35 non-commissioned officers, Ashanti levies, and 3,368 natives (including Sikhs from Central Africa and Yaos). The Kumasi were decisively beaten at Obassa on September 30. The final two rebel chiefs in the field were captured on December 28, and peace was restored by 1901. Total casualties for the campaign, including those who died of disease, numbered 1,007.
