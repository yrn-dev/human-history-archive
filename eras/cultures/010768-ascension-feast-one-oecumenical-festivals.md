# ASCENSION, FEAST OF THE

The Feast of the Ascension is one of the oecumenical festivals of the Christian Church. It ranks in solemnity alongside the festivals of Easter, Christmas, and Pentecost. The feast celebrates the ascension of Christ into heaven, which occurred forty days after the resurrection.

## Calendar and Timing
The festival is held forty days after Easter, or ten days before Whitsunday. It always falls on a Thursday, a day referred to as Holy Thursday or Ascension Day. In the broader schedule of church feasts depending on Easter, Ascension Day occurs 39 days after Easter and 5 weeks before Pentecost (Whitsunday).

## History and Origins
The festival is of great antiquity, although no discoverable traces of it exist prior to the middle of the 4th century. However, subsequent historical references suggest it was established long before that period. St Augustine noted that the feast had been kept from time immemorial and was likely instituted by the apostles. 

Early records of the celebration include:
*   A celebration in the church of Romanesia outside Antioch, mentioned by Chrysostom.
*   A celebration in a suburb of Constantinople in the year 390, which Socrates recorded as being based on "old custom" ([Greek: *ex ethous*]).
*   A celebration in the Church of the Nativity at Bethlehem, as noted in the *Peregrinatio* of Etheria (Silvia) dating from approximately A.D. 385.

## Regional Traditions and Rituals
### Eastern Traditions
In the East, the festival is known as *analaepsis* ("taking up") or *episozomenae*. The latter term, first used in the Cappadocian church, is thought to signify the feast "of completed salvation."

### Western Traditions
In the West, the term *ascensio* is used, which implies that Christ ascended by his own power. This is distinguished from *assumptio*, the taking up of the Virgin Mary by the power of God. 

During the middle ages in the West, processions featuring banners and candles were held outside the church to symbolize Christ's triumphant entry into heaven. In some medieval churches, an image of Christ was raised from the altar through a hole in the roof, followed immediately by the throwing down of a burning straw figure representing Satan.

In the Roman Catholic Church, a characteristic ritual is the solemn extinction of the paschal candle after the Gospel at high mass. This candle is lit at every mass for the forty days following Easter to symbolize Christ's presence with his disciples; its extinction symbolizes his parting from them. This custom began in 1263 with the Franciscans and was prescribed for the universal church by the Congregation of Rites on May 19, 1697. An obsolete custom formerly associated with the liturgy was the blessing of new beans after the Commemoration of the Dead in the canon of the mass.

### Pilgrimages
Early celebrations were often associated with professional pilgrimages to commemorate the passing of Christ and his apostles to the Mount of Olives. Adamnan, abbot of Iona, described such a procession taking place at Jerusalem in the 7th century, where the feast was celebrated in the church on Mount Olivet.

## Denominational Observances
The Feast of the Ascension is retained in Lutheran churches as warranted by Holy Scripture. In the Anglican Church, Ascension Day and its octave continue to be observed as a great festival. For this occasion, the Anglican communion service provides a special preface to the consecration prayer, similar to those provided for Trinity Sunday, Whitsunday, Easter, and Christmas.
