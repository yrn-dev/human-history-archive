# The Cornemuse and Biniou

The cornemuse and the biniou are varieties of the bag-pipe, a complex reed instrument of great antiquity. The bag-pipe serves as a link between the primitive organ and the syrinx by utilizing a reservoir for wind supply and a method of regulating sound pressure via the performer's arm. Generally, the instrument consists of an air-tight leather bag with three to five apertures containing stocks that hold the pipes. These pipes include a valved blow-pipe for inflation, a melody-pipe called a chaunter (which always uses a double-reed), and drones, which are cylindrical pipes producing a single fixed note.

## The Cornemuse
The cornemuse, along with the chalemie, was a bag-pipe used in the Netherlands, Italy, and France prior to the emergence of the musette. In France during the 16th and 17th centuries, two distinct types of cornemuses existed, differing primarily in their structural details and timbre.

The first variety was used as a solo instrument or by shepherds and was similar in construction to the Highland bag-pipe. It featured a leather bag inflated by a valved blow-pipe and two drones tuned to C: a "gros bourdon" (large drone) measuring 2-1/2 feet with a 2-1/2 inch beating-reed, and a "petit bourdon" (small drone) measuring 1 foot with a 2 inch beating-reed. Its chaunter had a conical bore and a double reed hidden in the stock. When played as part of the bag-pipe, the chaunter's eight holes (seven in front and one thumb-hole) provided a compass from C to C'. However, the chaunter could be removed and played separately; by applying pressure with the lips and breath to the reed, the compass could be increased by a third to E.

The second variety of cornemuse was played exclusively in concert with a family of instruments known as the Hautbois de Poitou. Unlike the shepherd's version, this instrument had only one drone, which utilized a double reed rather than a beating-reed. The chaunter of this variety featured eight holes, with the lowest hole covered by a key enclosed in a perforated box.

Over time, the cornemuse of rustic swains and shepherds became a fashionable instrument among the French courtly class. To avoid the facial distortion caused by inflating the bag with breath, bellows were substituted, and the instrument was refined in tone and appearance, evolving into the musette.

## The Biniou (Bignou)
The biniou, or bignou (a Gallicized form), is a species of cornemuse or bag-pipe still used in Brittany. The term is the Breton plural form of *benvek*, meaning tool or instrument. The word is also found in the phrase "*Sac'h ar biniou*," referring to a bag used by weavers for spindles and tools.

The biniou is a primitive bag-pipe consisting of a leather bag inflated by a short valved blow-pipe. It features a chaunter with a conical bore and a double reed concealed within the socket. Most biniou chaunters have seven holes, with the first hole duplicated to allow the instrument to be played by either left- or right-handed musicians. The drone is made of boxwood, often inlaid with tin, and contains a single or beating-reed hidden in the stock. The drone is typically tuned to the lower octave of the first hole.

In remote districts of Morbihan and Cornouailles, a more primitive version of the biniou is still occasionally found. This version possesses a chaunter with only five holes, providing part of the scale of D, with the drone also tuned to D.

The biniou remains a popular traditional instrument for Breton peasants in Morbihan and Cornouailles. It is almost always paired with the bombard—a survival of the medieval chalemie, hautbois, or musette. Together, the two musicians, known as *sonneurs*, perform ancient Breton melodies and *rondes* in shrill, penetrating tones during wedding feasts, threshing dances, and festivals known as *pardons*.
