# Christ

## Prophetic and Religious Status
According to the records of the Mahometans, Christ Jesus, the son of Mary, is recognized as an apostle of God. He is associated with a word conveyed unto Mary and a Spirit proceeding from him. Within the framework of a chain of inspiration extending from the fall of Adam to the promulgation of the Koran, Christ is listed as one of six legislators of "transcendent brightness" who announced successive revelations of a single immutable religion. In this gradation of authority and station, Christ is positioned alongside Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Mahomet. Along with Moses, Christ is noted as one of the few prophets who "lived and reigned," with the remnant of inspired writings being contained in the books of the Old and New Testaments.

## Doctrinal Controversies on the Person of Christ
The nature of Christ's person was the subject of intense debate and authoritative action during the early centuries of the Church, most notably at the Council of Ephesus in 431. While the councils of Nicaea and Constantinople had asserted that Christ possessed both full divinity and real humanity, they had not defined how these two natures were united.

Several conflicting theories emerged to explain this union:
*   **Apollinarianism:** This view substituted the human *nous* (mind) or *pneuma* (spirit) of Jesus with the divine Logos, which critics argued detracted from the completeness of his humanity.
*   **Nestorianism:** This doctrine suggested that the divine Logos dwelt within the man Jesus as if in a temple. Nestorius argued that the union was one of dignity rather than a perfect union of person. Consequently, he claimed it was blasphemous to call Mary *theotokos* ("Godbearer"), as the Logos could not have been born.
*   **The Alexandrian View:** Led by Cyril, this group advocated for the perfect union of two complete natures in one person, adopting *theotokos* as the mark of orthodoxy.

Following these disputes, the Catholic Church established a doctrine of a "substantial, indissoluble, and everlasting union" between a perfect God (the second person of the trinity) and a perfect man possessing human flesh and a reasonable soul.

## The Epiphany and Early Festivals
In early Christian tradition, the festival of Epiphany originally signified that God was seen upon earth and consorted with men. Over time, this concept was substituted with the idea of the manifestation of Christ to the Gentiles. Scriptural references associated with this event include passages from Matthew (iii. 1-11, xi. 2-15, xxi. 1-9), Mark (i. 1-8), and Luke (iii. 1-18). Additionally, Pauline lections refer to the Epiphany of the Second Advent and the Messianic or prophetic kingdom.
