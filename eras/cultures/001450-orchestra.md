# A. With Orchestra

The term "orchestra" encompasses both the musical ensembles that accompany compositions and the physical architectural spaces used in ancient theatrical performances.

## The Musical Orchestra and Instrumentation

The evolution of the orchestra is marked by the gradual introduction and integration of various wind and brass instruments. The natural horn, for example, was first introduced into the orchestra by the Italians, though initially only to suggest calls to arms or hunting scenes. In Germany, Reinhard Keiser, the founder of German opera, utilized two horns in C in the opening chorus of his 1705 opera *Octavia*, adding them to oboes and a string quartette. By 1711, the court orchestra in Dresden added two Bohemian horn-players, Franz Adam Samm and Johann Adalbert Fischer, and Vienna added the instrument to the opera in 1712. Earlier solitary instances of the horn in Vienna include Cesti's *Il Pomo d'Oro*, performed for the wedding of Margareta, infanta of Spain, and Kaiser Leopold in 1667 and 1668.

In France, the horn's use by Lulli in one ballet appears to be an isolated case; it did not enter the French opera orchestra until 1735 in Andre Campra's *Achille et Deidamie*, where it was used only for a fanfare. Meanwhile, Handel introduced the horn to the London orchestra in his *Water-music*, composed for George I. During the period between 1717 and 1723, while Bach served as conductor for the prince of Anhalt-Cothen's orchestra, it is probable that horns in several keys were employed.

Other instruments have also seen specific orchestral developments. The bassoon has been used by many masters; Bach included parts for two bassoons in his mass in B min., while Beethoven utilized it extensively in his symphonies. Mozart wrote a concerto for bassoon with orchestra (Kochel, No. 191), and Weber wrote a concerto for bassoon in F (op. 75) scored for full orchestra. At the request of Wagner, W. Heckel of Biebrich-am-Rhein created bassoons with an extra key to extend the compass downward to A.

The bass clarinet was introduced to the opera orchestra through the improvements of Adolphe Sax. It first appeared in 1836 in Act V of Meyerbeer's *Huguenots*, and later in *Dinorah*. In 1838, a different model of bass clarinet by P. Maino was introduced at the theatre of Modena. Wagner later used bass clarinets in Bb and C in *Tristan und Isolde* to characterize the utterance of King Mark.

The bass of medieval orchestras was formed by the contrabass pommer (bombard), known in Italian as *bombardone*. This evolved into the bass tuba (or bombardon), the first bass wind instrument to feature valves. Originally known as the *clavicor*, *corno basso*, or *bass horn*, the original *corno basso* was a narrow-bore brass instrument with horizontal pistons. In Prussia, the keyed bass horn was introduced to Jager military bands in 1829, followed by the valve-ophicleide in 1831. By 1835, these were superseded in the Guards by the bass tuba invented by J.G. Moritz and Wieprecht.

## Orchestral Works and Conductors

Orchestral arrangements often transform solo works. An example is the prelude of the E major partita for unaccompanied violin, which was transformed into a sinfonia for organ obligato accompanied by a full orchestra, including a pair of drums and three trumpets, for the church cantata *Wir danken dir, Gott*.

Notable figures in orchestral history include John Tiplady Carrodus (1836-1895), an English violinist who studied in Stuttgart with Wilhelm Bernhard Molique. Carrodus was a member of the Covent Garden opera orchestra from 1855, became its leader in 1869, and led the Philharmonic orchestra and various provincial festivals. Similarly, Sir Alexander Campbell Mackenzie (1847- ) entered the ducal orchestra as a violinist after studying at the conservatorium in Sondershausen from 1857 to 1861. Mackenzie later played in the orchestra of the Birmingham Festivals from 1864 to 1873 and served as the conductor of Novello's oratorio.

## The Ancient Greek Orchestra

In the context of ancient architecture, the orchestra refers to the circular space of a theatre. The Dionysiac theatre, located beneath the south side of the Acropolis, may have received play representations from an earlier orchestra in the Agora at the start of the 5th century B.C. Traces of the original circular Greek orchestra there were identified by Dorpfeld in 1886. Over time, the orchestra space was encroached upon by subsequent proscenia, including a stage from the time of Nero and the "bema" of Phaedrus in the 3rd century A.D.

The theatre at Epidaurus is considered the most typical of Greek theatres. Its orchestra is a complete circle marked by a sill of white limestone. The site is noted for its extraordinary acoustic properties, allowing a speaker in the orchestra to be heard throughout the auditorium without raising their voice. The proscenium of this theatre, which is nearly tangent to the circle of the orchestra, is approximately 12 ft. broad and 14 ft. high.
