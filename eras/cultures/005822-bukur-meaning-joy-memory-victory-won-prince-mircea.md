# Bukur and the Origins of Bucharest

## Etymology and Legend
The origins of the name of the city of Bucharest are a subject of significant dispute. One specific account derives the name from the Albanian word *Bukur*, which translates to "joy." According to this tradition, the name was chosen in memory of a victory achieved by Prince Mircea of Walachia (c. 1383-1419) over the Turks. Due to this linguistic association with joy, Bucharest is frequently referred to as "The City of Joy." However, the archive notes that the legend of Bucur is considered plainly unhistorical.

## Early Foundations and Development
The foundation of the city has been attributed to various sources. Some accounts ascribe its establishment to Radu Negru (c. 1290-1314), who is described as the first Walachian prince and a half-mythical figure. Conversely, more modern historians suggest that the city began as a fortress. This fortress was reportedly erected on the site of the Daco-Roman Thyanus for the purpose of commanding the approaches to Tîrgovishtea, which served as the former capital of Walachia. In its early development, the city functioned as the summer residence for the court.

## Conflict and Growth
The city's history is marked by periods of destruction and recovery. In 1595, Bucharest was burned by the Turks. Following its restoration, the city experienced a period of growth in both prosperity and size. This expansion continued until 1698, when Prince Constantine Brancovan designated the city as his capital.

During the 18th century, Bucharest was a site of frequent dispute, with the Turks, Russians, and Austrians competing for possession of the city. Its political importance is highlighted by the treaty of 1812, which took its name from the city; this treaty resulted in the cession of a third of Moldavia and Bessarabia to Russia.

## 19th Century Transitions
The mid-19th century saw repeated foreign military interventions. During the war of 1828, the city was occupied by Russian forces, though it was returned to the prince of Walachia the following year. In 1848, a rebellion against Prince Bibescu led to further interference from Russia and Turkey. Russian troops held the city again from 1853 to 1854, after which an Austrian garrison took possession, remaining until March 1857.

In 1858, Bucharest hosted the international congress organized for the organization of the Danubian principalities. Following the proclamation of the union of Moldavia and Walachia in 1861, Bucharest became the capital of Rumania. The city remained a center of political volatility, as evidenced in 1866 when an insurrection in Bucharest drove Prince Cuza, the first ruler of the united provinces, from his throne.
