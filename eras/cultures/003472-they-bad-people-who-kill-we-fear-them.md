# Fear and Conflict in Human History

The archive's records indicate that fear is a recurring theme in human interaction, often stemming from the threat of violence, the actions of oppressive leaders, or the anticipation of divine retribution. These experiences are documented across linguistic studies, sacred texts, and historical chronicles.

## Fear of Violence and Oppression
In various historical and religious contexts, fear is closely linked to the threat of being slain or oppressed. In the accounts of Musa (Moses), fear appears as a response to the threat of death. After killing a man, Musa expressed fear to his Lord that the people among whom he lived would slay him. Later, upon receiving advice from a man running from the furthest end of the city, Musa was warned that the chiefs were consulting together to slay him. This led him to escape in a state of fear and vigilance, praying for deliverance from "unjust people" and "people given to wrong-doing."

Similarly, in the history of Norway, fear is associated with oppressive governance. It is noted that kings in Norway were oppressive to the people, leading to a state where every man who had service or tax to pay was against them. In the Viken region, records suggest that no safety was found until the people, being afraid, paid their scat and pledge.

## Divine Retribution and Spiritual Fear
Sacred texts present fear not only as a response to human threats but as a spiritual caution. Noah (Nuh) expressed fear for his people regarding the "retribution of an Awful Day" and the "chastisement of a grievous day," urging them to worship Allah, as there is no other god but Him. 

Additionally, believers are commanded to "fear Allah," who is described as Most Merciful and Oft-Returning. This spiritual fear is linked to moral conduct, such as avoiding suspicion—which can be a sin—and refraining from spying on others.

## Conflict and Hostility
The archive contains numerous accounts of individuals and groups characterized by hostility or "bad" behavior. In the history of Norway, figures such as Bjarne the Bad are mentioned as having plundered lands and killed the friends of others. Conflict often escalated into mass violence; for instance, Erling once surrounded the house of Assur and burnt him in it, killing one hundred men in total and burning three houses.

In other contexts, fear is used as a tool of persuasion. In the history of the Peloponnesian War, Brasidas was able to persuade the multitude in Acanthus to admit him because they feared for their fruit, which had not yet been harvested.

## Linguistic Representations of Fear
The study of the Bantu mother-tongue provides a linguistic example of how fear and hostility are structured in language. In a reconstructed sentence of Old Bantu, the phrase "_Babo mbaba-ntu ba bi ba-bo-ta tu-ba-oga_" is used to illustrate the concord of pronominal particles. This sentence is translated as: "They these-they person they bad they who kill we fear them." A modern Luganda dialect renders this as "_Bo aba-ntu ba-bi ba-bota tu-ba-tia_," maintaining the meaning: "They are bad people who kill; we fear them."
