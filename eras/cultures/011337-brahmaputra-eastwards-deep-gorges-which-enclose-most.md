# The Brahmaputra River System

The Brahmaputra, whose name literally translates to the "Son of Brahma," is a major river system that originates in the Tibetan plateau, far to the north of the Himalayas. It follows a course of 1,800 meters before ending in the Bay of Bengal.

## Hydrography and Course
The river skirts the eastern passes of the Himalayas, positioned not far from the great river of Cambodia and the Yang-tsze-kiang. It enters Assam through a series of rapids and waterfalls characterized by accumulations of rocks and vast boulders. In the Lakhimpur district, the southernmost branch of the river passes through a gorge known as the Parasuramkunda or Brahmakunda. This site has been held in reverence by Hindus from time immemorial and is visited annually by thousands of devotees, despite the dangerous and difficult journey required to reach it.

Within the Assam valley, the river flows rapidly westwards. During the rainy season, the Brahmaputra expands into a sheet of water several miles wide, forming various islands within its bed. After traversing the length of the Assam valley, the river turns sharply south, spreading across the alluvial districts of the Bengal delta.

## Tributaries and Connections
The Brahmaputra is fed by an infinite number of watercourses and tributaries descending from the surrounding mountains. Its first tributaries after crossing the frontier are the Digaru and Kundil, which flow from the north (Mishmi hills), and the Dihing and Tengapani, which originate in the south-east (Singpho hills). It also receives the Dihang from the north-east.

A principal confluent is the Dihong. Originating near the source of the Sutlej under the name Tsangpo, the Dihong traverses the Tibetan table-land behind the great Himalaya range, flowing in a direction opposite to the Sutlej. It joins the Brahmaputra at 95 deg. 26' E. long. and 27 deg. 48' N. lat. after a course of nearly 1,000 meters. While there were long-standing doubts regarding whether the Dihong was the continuation of the Tsangpo, these were settled by the 1886 voyage of F.J. Needham. Research has since confirmed that the great Tsanpo of Tibet and the Brahmaputra are the same river.

## Regional Geography and Water Divides
The hydrography of the wilderness dividing China from Burma and Tibet has been extensively investigated by native surveyors, travellers, and commercial exploration parties. North of the point where the river effects its great countermarch from east to west, the sources of the Hwang-ho (Yellow river), Yang-tsze-kiang, Mekong, and Salween are located, ordered from west to east. South of this point, the dual sources of the Irrawaddy rise between the Salween and the extreme eastern edge of the Brahmaputra basin.

A short distance of 100 meters separates the water-divide of the Brahmaputra's most eastern affluent from the deep gorges enclosing the most westerly branch of the upper Yang-tsze-kiang (which runs north to south). Within this 100-meter space, the Mekong and Salween rivers send their torrents toward Siam and Burma. For approximately 300 meters, these three rivers flow parallel to one another, hidden in precipitous, narrow troughs. They begin to spread apart north of the 25 deg. parallel where the Yang-tsze turns eastwards.

## Infrastructure and Transport
The region is served by several railway systems. The Assam-Bengal railway is a state line financed by the government and worked by a company; as of the end of 1904, its open mileage was 576 meters. This line runs from the seaport of Chittagong to the Surma valley, crossing hills to reach Dibrugarh at the head of the Brahmaputra valley, with a branch extending to Gauhati. Construction of the hill section was exceedingly difficult and suffered extensive damage during an earthquake in 1897.

Additionally, the Dibru-Sadiya railway operates at the head of the Brahmaputra valley and includes a branch serving coal-fields. In Bengal, the Eastern Bengal railway runs north to Darjeeling and maintains a service of river steamers on the Brahmaputra, with its terminus located at Sealdah, an eastern suburb of Calcutta.
