# BALTIC SEA

The Baltic Sea (known in German as *Ostsee* and in Russian as *Baltiyskoe More*) is a sea located between 54° and 66° N. latitude and 9° and 30° E. longitude. It is surrounded by the territories of Denmark, Germany, Russia, and Sweden. The sea's central axis runs approximately from the south-west to the north-east, reaching a greatest length of about 960 m. and a greatest breadth of about 400 m. Its coastline extends for 5,000 m.

## Geography and Physical Boundaries
The Baltic is connected to the North Sea via a winding channel situated between the Cimbrian peninsula and the south of Scandinavia. This channel is generally considered part of the Baltic. It is divided by a line joining Christiania fjord with the Skaw; the area to the west is the Skagerrak, and the area to the east is the Kattegat. At its southern end, the Kattegat is blocked by Danish islands, communicating with the Baltic proper through narrow channels known as the Little Belt, the Great Belt, and the Sound.

The physical boundary between the North Sea and the Baltic is formed by a plateau encompassing the islands of Laaland, Funen, and Zealand, extending from the coasts of Ruegen and Mecklenburg to the islands of Moen and Falster.

The Baltic is divided into several basins and arms:
* **Aland Haf:** A depression between Sweden and the Aland Isles, separated from the Baltic basin proper by an unbroken ridge extending from Hango in Finland to Stockholm.
* **Bothnian Sea:** A depression north of the Aland Haf.
* **Gulf of Bothnia:** The most northerly division, separated from the Bothnian Sea by a ridge across the Kvarken (or Quarken) Strait.
* **Gulf of Finland:** An arm running eastwards for about 250 m., separating Esthonia from Finland.
* **Gulf of Riga:** A shallow, roughly circular inlet between Courland and Esthonia, measuring about 100 m. in diameter with a maximum depth of 27 fathoms.

The total area of the Baltic, including the Kattegat and Skagerrak, is 166,397 sq. m. with a volume of 6,907 cub. m. This results in a mean depth of 36 fathoms. The seabed consists of hard clay or soft grey and brown mud in the deeper southern hollows, while shallow banks and low coasts typically feature small pebbles and fine sand of brown, yellow, or white color.

## Hydrology and Drainage
The Baltic possesses a relatively large drainage area, measured by Sir J. Murray at 461,450 sq. sea m. (611,700 sq. English m.). The largest river-basin is the Neva in the east, followed by the Oder and Vistula in the south. A notable feature of the drainage system in western Finland and Sweden is the presence of narrow parallel troughs at right angles to the coast.

The mean surface level of the Baltic varies by location. At Swinemuende, it is 0.093 metres (.305 ft.) below the North Sea surface at Amsterdam and 0.066 metres (.216 ft.) below the level at Ostend. It is 0.499 metres (1.6 ft.) above the Adriatic Sea. Due to prevailing westerly winds, the mean level rises approximately 0.5 metres (1.6 ft.) from Memel to the coast of Holstein.

The sea's circulation is complex, characterized by a distinction between inflowing waters (more than 32 *pro mille* salt) and outflowing waters (less than 30 *pro mille* salt). Because rainfall and river discharge exceed evaporation, a surface current called the "Baltic Stream" carries surplus fresh water out of the sea. This stream keeps close to the Scandinavian coast due to the earth's rotation and is periodic; it is abundant in summer but largely dries up in winter when land water freezes.

## Geological History
The Baltic's form has been shaped by cycles of subsidence and upheaval. During glacial times, a sea arm submerged much of the littoral up to the Gulf of Bothnia, including the present Malar, Hjelmar, and Vener lakes, and was salty enough for oysters. Subsequent upheaval restricted communication to the Danish channels, making the water fresher and causing oysters to disappear, though seals and various crustaceans became acclimatized.

The sea later became a vast freshwater lake known as the *Ancylus* lake (named for the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*), covering approximately 220,000 sq. m., including Lake Ladoga and much of Finland. A later subsidence re-established communication with the open sea, allowing salt-water forms like *Littorina littorea* and *Cardium edule* to return. Deposits from this epoch are 100 metres (328 ft.) thick near Hernosand but only 25 metres (82 ft.) near Gotland. The current process of elevation continues, with the highest amplitude in the Gulf of Bothnia and the southern Swedish *massif*, while movement is almost nil in the Gulf of Finland and along the Baltic's axial lines.

## Maritime and Political History
The Baltic has been a subject of controversy regarding territorial waters. Bynkershoek's 1702 formula in *De dominio maris* restricted sea dominion to the range of cannon protection, which evolved into the three-mile belt. 

Following a period of conflict, the Allies required Germany to demolish all fortifications in the Baltic that defended the passages through the belts. Additionally, Germany was required to sweep up mines in the Baltic Sea and the North Sea.
