# Book-Lovers and the Development of Civilization

The relationship between the advancement of civilization and the appreciation of books is characterized by a shift from the pursuit of literary enjoyment to the systematic collection of volumes. As societies reach high degrees of civilization, the number of book-lovers tends to multiply, leading to the emergence of distinct types of owners.

## The Evolution of Book-Collecting
While a student may possess a modest desire to read a favorite author in a well-printed or well-written copy, higher levels of civilization introduce a class of owners who are suspected of valuing the external aspects of books more than the enjoyment derived from reading them. Although early indications of this trend existed during the Roman Empire and toward the end of the Middle Ages, book-collecting as it is currently understood is essentially a modern growth.

Historical texts illustrate this transition. The *Philobiblon*, attributed to Richard de Bury and written in 1345, is regarded as a medieval text-book on the love of books; however, it focuses almost exclusively on the delights of literature. Similarly, a work by Sebastian Brant written a century and a half later argued that possessing books is a poor substitute for actual learning.

The emergence of modern book-collecting required a sufficient abundance of books to be available. Even after the invention of printing reduced book costs by approximately 80%, collectors did not appear immediately. Many early owners of prestigious libraries were not collectors in the antiquarian sense. For example, Jean Grolier (1479-1565) commissioned tasteful bindings for himself and his friends to encourage the best printers of his era and to share the most recent scholarship of the Renaissance. Other figures who assembled the best books of their day in various departments of learned literature—including the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou (1553-1617) in France, and in England, Henry, prince of Wales (1594-1612), Lords Lumley and Arundel, and Archbishop Cranmer—focused on housing these works in handsome leather jackets adorned with heraldic badges and coats of arms.

## Literacy and Scholarship in Japan
In early historic Japan, the preservation of history was the responsibility of a hereditary corporation of raconteurs known as *Katari-be*. These individuals recited the deeds of heroes and exploits of sovereigns, accompanying themselves with musical instruments. Over time, they embellished these accounts with supernatural elements and romance. Until the sixth century of the Christian era, the *Katari-be* served as the sole repository of the country's annals, often exalting the pedigrees of the high personages who provided them with patronage.

The introduction of the art of writing, occurring around the close of the fourth century or the beginning of the fifth, shifted this dynamic. Observing that China—the center of civilization and learning—used writing for ethical volumes and national history, the Japanese sought to utilize this attainment. However, applying the ideographic script to the Japanese language was a task of immense difficulty that required many years of proficiency.

Even as scholarship grew, it remained limited to a narrow circle. For instance, a rescript penned by Tachibana no Hiromi was not clearly understood outside this small group of scholars, and official enactments were intelligible to very few women or men of the trading classes. Despite this, high literature flourished. The Nara epoch produced the *Manyo-shu* (Myriad Leaves), and the Engi era produced the *Kokin-shu*, an anthology of over eleven hundred poems. By the Heian epoch, the Japanese language had reached full development, utilizing a copious vocabulary drawn almost exclusively from native sources, making it a pliant instrument for writers.

## Learning and Sacred Texts
The pursuit of learning and the collection of books have also been central to royal and sacred traditions. The second of the Ptolemies was noted for being extraordinarily diligent in the collection of books and learning. He sought a Greek translation of the law and government constitution of the Israelites. This request was granted by Eleazar the high priest, following the national custom of not hindering the communication of their esteemed knowledge to others. The sacred books of the Israelites contain the history of five thousand years, documenting mutations of government, wars, and the actions of commanders. These texts teach that events succeed well for those who follow the will of God and do not break His laws, while those who apostatize from these observations face incurable calamity.

In the Qur'an, the "Book" and "Scripture" are mentioned in relation to prophets. The text mentions Idris, described as a prophet and a man of truth and sincerity whom Allah raised to a high station in Heaven. It also mentions Ismail, a prophet and messenger who was truthful in his promise and enjoined prayer and almsgiving upon his family and people.
