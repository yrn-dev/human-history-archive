# Volcanic Activity and Formations

Volcanic activity is distributed across various global regions, manifesting as isolated peaks, expansive plateaus, and island chains. These formations range from active volcanoes that continue to shape their landscapes to quiescent or extinct structures.

## Asia and the Pacific
In Japan, Fuji (Fuji-san) is a celebrated isolated cone rising to 12,395 ft. It is a quiescent volcano with a summit crater approximately 2000 ft. in diameter; small outbursts of steam occur at some points, and an eruption was recorded in the first decade of the 18th century. In the Luchu archipelago, active volcanoes are found at the extreme north on the islands of Suwanose-shima (2697 ft.) and Nakano-shima (3485 ft.).

In Korea, recent volcanic and eruptive rocks are found in the interior and on the island of Quelpart. Chinese stories suggest that Hal-la-san (Mount Auckland), the principal mountain of Quelpart, erupted in 1007. Aside from this possibility, Korea has no active volcanoes and has been remarkably free of earthquakes throughout historic times. On a broader scale, the continent's volcanic history since the Cretaceous period includes the "harras" of Arabia and the Deccan Trap in India. While the central folded belt contains mostly extinct volcanoes in Persia, Armenia, Baluchistan, and Asia Minor, and Burma has at least one extinct volcano, present-day active volcanoes are primarily located on islands off the southeastern and eastern coasts.

## Southeast Asia and the East Indies
The Sunda Strait, between Sumatra and Java, is home to Krakatoa. Originally a large volcano, it was blown away in a prehistoric explosion, leaving a ring of islands. Subsequent eruptions created new cones, the most significant reaching 2623 ft. After frequent earthquakes beginning around 1877, Krakatoa erupted in May 1883. Between August 26 and 28, 1883, paroxysmal explosions destroyed the northern and lower portions of the island and the northern part of the Rakata cone. A previous eruption in 1680 was accompanied by earthquakes and the ejection of pumice.

In the Banda Sea, south of Ceram, the Banda Islands exhibit a volcanic formation where Lontor, Neira, and Gunong Api form a crater circle similar to Santorin in the Aegean Sea. Gunong Api (Fire Mountain) is an active volcano 2200 ft. high. Its earthquakes and eruptions have frequently caused destruction, most notably in 1852, when a huge sea wave caused the primary damage.

## Africa
In British East Africa, a volcanic belt exceeding 150,000 sq. m. extends across territorial limits. This activity is divided into two sets. An older group, post-Jurassic and likely extending through the Cretaceous into early Tertiary times, involved fissure eruptions that created the Laikipia and Kapte plateaus, as well as eruptions at Chibcharagnani, Elgon, Kimawenzi, and Kenya. A later period saw the eruption of Kibo, which produced leucite and nepheline basanite lavas rich in soda felspars. More recent volcanic cones include those of the Kyulu mountains, Donyo Longonot, and various rift-valley craters. While some in the rift-valley are extinct or dormant, Donyo Buru still emits steam, and Mount Teleki erupted at the close of the 19th century.

Further west, along the mountains marking the edge of the high plateau, a system of lakes (including Nyasa, Tanganyika, Kivu, and Albert) was formed by a prehistoric convulsion. Great volcanoes exist in this region; while most are extinct, some north of Kivu remain active.

## Atlantic Archipelagoes
The archipelago containing Pico (7612 ft.) and Corvo (350 ft.) is entirely volcanic, characterized by basalt and scoria. Activity has been frequent since its discovery, though Flores, Corvo, and Graciosa have been exempt. Fayal experienced one eruption in 1672. St Michael's has been the primary center of activity, with eruptions and earthquakes in 1630, 1652, 1656, 1755, and 1852. A violent convulsion in 1522 buried the capital, Villa Franca, and its 6000 inhabitants. Other recorded eruptions occurred in Pico (1572) and St George (1580), and a town in Terceira was destroyed in 1614. Subterranean eruptions in 1638, 1720, 1811, and 1867 sometimes created temporary islands, such as Sabrina, which appeared in June 1811 near St Michael's.
