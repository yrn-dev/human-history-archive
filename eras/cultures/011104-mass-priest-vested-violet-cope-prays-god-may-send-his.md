# Mass and Sacerdotal Rites

In the Roman Catholic Church, the High Mass is a central liturgical service that may include specific ceremonial observances depending on the religious calendar. One such observance occurs on Ash Wednesday, which marks the first day of Lent in the Western Church. This day, referred to as *dies cinerum* (day of ashes) or *dies cineris et cilicii* (day of ash and sackcloth), utilizes ashes as a symbol of penitence, a custom based on the "sackcloth and ashes" mentioned by Old Testament prophets.

## Ash Wednesday Ceremonies
During the service for Ash Wednesday, ashes—which are produced by burning the palms or substitutes used on the previous Palm Sunday—are placed in a vessel on the altar before High Mass. The priest, wearing a violet cope, offers a prayer requesting that God send His angel to hallow the ash so that it may serve as a *remedium salubre* (healthy remedy) for all penitents. Following this, the ashes are censed three times and sprinkled three times with holy water.

The priest then invites those present to kneel. He marks their foreheads with the sign of the cross using his thumb dipped in the ashes. For clerics, the mark is placed on the site of the tonsure. This action is accompanied by the words: *Memento, homo, quid pulvis es et in pulverem reverteris* (Remember, man, that thou art dust and unto dust thou shall return). The celebrant may either receive the ash from the highest-ranking priest present or sprinkle it on his own head in silence.

## Sacerdotal Benedictions
Within the sacramental system of the Catholic Church (both East and West), the priesthood is vested with special powers of blessing. These sacerdotal benedictions are classified as sacramentals (*sacramenta minora*) rather than sacraments, as they were ordained by the authority of the Church rather than by Christ himself. Priests act as plenipotentiaries of God, operating under the principle that whatever they consecrate may be consecrated and whatever they bless may be blessed.

While some associate benediction with the sacrifice of the Mass, the blessing itself does not act *ex opere operato* and does not confer grace. In the Catholic tradition, this power implies a specific divine authority over invisible forces of evil, making the benediction a complement to the rite of exorcism.

## Authority and Vestments in Blessing
The power to bless is generally reserved for priests, with the exception of the deacon blessing the paschal candle on Holy Saturday (though the deacon uses incense previously blessed by a priest). Certain benedictions are reserved for bishops, including the blessing of:
* Churches, cemeteries, and oratories.
* Articles used with the altar, such as patens, chalices, and vestments.
* The benediction of priests at ordination, abbots, and virgins taking the veil.
* Military arms, soldiers, and military colours.

In the Roman Catholic Church, bishops also bless the holy oil, whereas in the Greek Church, the priest blesses the oil for the chrism at baptism. The pope holds exclusive authority to bless royal swords, the "Agnus-Dei," the golden rose, and the pallium, and is the only authority who can issue blessings involving some days' indulgence.

For benedictions performed outside of the Mass, the *Rituale Romanum* prescribes that the priest be bare-headed and vested in a stole and surplice. He is assisted by a minister carrying a copy of the missal or *Rituale*, an *aspergillum*, and a vase of holy water. The process involves specific prayers, the sign of the cross, and the sprinkling or censing of the object or person.

## The Role of Holy Water and Salt
Holy water is considered the essential instrument of all benedictions, intended to establish potency against evil spirits. The process begins with the exorcism of the "creature of salt" to ensure it provides health of body and soul and causes "every unclean spirit" and "diabolic deceit" to flee. This is followed by a prayer for the blessing of the salt. Subsequently, the "creature of water" is exorcized to enable it to uproot and expel the enemy and his apostate angels.

## Liturgical Vestments and Field Service
The *dalmatica* is a loose tunic with wide sleeves that originated in Dalmatia and became fashionable in the Roman world during the 2nd century A.D. It was introduced into public worship by Pope Silvester I (314-335) for deacons, though some evidence suggests popes wore it as early as the 3rd century. For centuries, it remained a distinctive vestment for the pope and his deacons, granted to other clergy only by papal concession. For example, Pope Stephen II permitted six deacons to wear the "robe of honour of the dalmatic" while assisting at mass in 757.

In military contexts, such as with the German armies in the West, chaplains may celebrate High Mass for soldiers in trenches, sometimes operating in cold and rain. These chaplains may be identified by a white and violet band around the left arm of their field tunic and a cross around their neck.
