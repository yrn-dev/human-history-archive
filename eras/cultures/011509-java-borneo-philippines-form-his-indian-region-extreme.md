# Java, Borneo, and the Philippines

The islands of Java, Borneo, and the Philippines, along with Sumatra, constitute a significant portion of the south-eastern region of Asia. This area is characterized by a complex intersection of zoological, botanical, and ethnic influences, often defined by its position relative to the Australian continent and the dividing line proposed by Dr. Sclater and Alfred Russel Wallace.

## Zoological and Botanical Regions
According to the primary divisions of Dr. Sclater, Java, Borneo, and the Philippines, together with Sumatra, form the Indian region. This stands in contrast to the Palaearctic region to the north and the Ethiopian region to the south-west. The boundary of the Indian region is marked by "Wallace's line," which runs from the south of the Philippines between Borneo and Celebes, and further south between Bali and Lombok.

The distinction provided by this line is evident in the distribution of mammals and birds. Marsupials are found in Celebes and the Molucca Islands but are entirely absent from Borneo and Java. Similarly, mammals such as Ruminants, Carnivora, Insectivora, and Quadrumana abound in the western part of the Archipelago but are absent from Celebes and the Moluccas. In the Indian region, the wild ox of Java and Borneo, known as the bantin (or *Bos sondaicus*), is found; adult bulls are black with a white rump and legs, while cows and young are rufous.

Botanically, the vegetation of the Malayan Islands largely mirrors the hotter and wetter regions of India. However, the stability of temperature and humidity allows for the predominance of certain tropical forms, such as nutmeg and liquidambar—the latter being one of the most conspicuous trees in Java. The proximity of Australia has introduced Australian types into the region; for example, the casuarina is abundant on the eastern mountains of Java, and temperate Australian forms appear on the higher mountains of Borneo. While rhododendrons occur in Sumatra and Borneo down to sea level, Java's mountains lack truly alpine flora, and *Saxifraga* is not present.

## Geography and Natural Resources
The Philippine Islands are situated between Borneo and southern China, lying between 5 and 20 degrees North. Their highest point reaches 10,250 feet. The islands are generally fertile and well-suited for agriculture, though they do not reach the population or wealth levels of Java.

The region is a source of valuable resins. East Indian dammar, also known as cat's eye resin, is produced by the *Dammara orientalis* tree, which grows in Borneo, Java, Sumatra, and other eastern islands. These trees can reach heights of 80 to 100 feet and produce a soft, aromatic viscous resin used for incense-burning in oriental communities. This resin is imported into England via Singapore, where it is used to create transparent varnishes when mixed with oil of turpentine.

Geologically, strata from the Eocene period occur in Java, Borneo, and the Philippines, as well as in other regions such as Japan, New Guinea, and the Moluccas.

## Ethnography and History
The islands are inhabited by the Malays, a seafaring race with exceptional powers of dispersal. While they have no political union, they occupy Java, Borneo, the Philippines, Sumatra, the extremity of the Malay Peninsula, and Madagascar. Some theories suggest they spread from an island center, possibly Java, while other theories propose a larger linguistic group including the Munda, Monkhmer, Polynesian, and Micronesian languages originating from the extreme west.

The history of the Asiatic Malays is divided into three periods:
1. A semi-barbarous first period, in which tribes such as the Dyaks remained.
2. A second period marked by the arrival of Hindu civilization in Java, Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and other islands, evidenced by Indian customs, words, and ruins.
3. A third period in which Islam superseded Hindu influence, spreading to the Peninsula and Archipelago before the 16th century.

Currently, while the Arabic alphabet is used on the mainland, Indian alphabets continue to be used in Sumatra and Java.

## Ichthyology and Avifauna
The region's freshwater fishes are divided into Cyprinoid and Acyprinoid divisions. Borneo is noted for abounding in Cyprinids, whereas only a few are known from the Philippines and two species have been found in Bali. None are known from Lombok or Celebes.

In terms of avifauna, the Malayan province—which includes the Philippines and the Malay islands—is noted for an extraordinary number of interesting and peculiar genera. The influence of the Australian realm is seen in the presence of Megapodes in Borneo and Labuan. However, cockatoos are strictly absent from this region, remaining on the other side of Wallace's line.
