# New Zealand: Maoris, Chinese, and Half Castes

## Population and Racial Classifications
In the region of Australasia, the population of New Zealand consisting of Maoris (described as brown), Chinese, and half castes is estimated at 53,000. Within the broader classification of the native races of Australia and the Polynesian island groups, these populations are divided into dark and light Polynesian types. The Maoris of New Zealand are categorized as part of the white Polynesian races, which are described as a very superior type exhibiting characteristics of a high order.

## Cultural Practices
The Maoris have engaged in several distinct traditional arts and pastimes from time immemorial.

### Kite-Flying
Kite-flying is a practice found among the Maoris, and unlike its status in Europe, it retains a distinctly religious character. The act of ascending a kite is accompanied by a specific chant known as the kite-song.

### Dyeing
The Maoris are cited as an example of how dyeing may be practiced among uncivilized tribes. This process involves using the juices of fruits and decoctions from roots, barks, leaves, and flowers to create stains.

## Political History and Native Affairs
The governance of New Zealand involved various British colonial statesmen and local leaders. Sir George Grey served as a British colonial governor, and John Ballance (1839-1893) was a New Zealand statesman who served as premier from 1891 to 1893. Ballance had previously served in the ministry of Sir George Grey between 1877 and 1879, though the two later experienced a painful and notorious quarrel.

During his tenure as native minister, Ballance pursued a policy described as humane and pacific. He worked to equitably adjust long-standing difficulties regarding reserved lands located on the west coast of the North Island. Additionally, Ballance was involved in military activity in 1867 during fighting with the Maori chief Titokowaru, for which he was awarded the New Zealand war medal.

## Natural History and Local Interaction
New Zealand is the exclusive home of the kiwis, a group of birds in the subclass *Ratitae*. These nocturnal birds, which feed primarily on earth-worms, include species such as *A. mantelli* in the North Island, *A. australis* in the south, and *A. oweni*, which occurs in both islands.

Other local fauna, such as the tui, are noted for their power of mimicry and are popular cage-birds among both colonists and natives. An account by Sir Walter Buller describes an instance where a tui in a cage responded to a political speech delivered to a meeting of Maoris; the bird uttered "Tito!" (meaning "false"), which caused merriment among the audience and the chief Nepia Taratoa.
