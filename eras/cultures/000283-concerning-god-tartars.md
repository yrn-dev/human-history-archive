# CONCERNING THE GOD OF THE TARTARS

The religious system of the Tartars is characterized by the worship of both a celestial deity and a terrestrial god, with specific rituals governing their daily interactions with these figures.

## The Most High God and Natigay
The Tartars recognize a Most High God of Heaven. This deity is worshipped on a daily basis through the use of incense and a thurible. The prayers directed toward the Most High God are specifically focused on requests for the health of the body and mind.

In addition to the celestial deity, the Tartars worship a god of the Earth named Natigay. Natigay is believed to be the protector of crops, cattle, and children. Because of this role, he is shown great honor and worship.

## Domestic Worship and Iconography
Worship of Natigay is integrated into the home. Every man keeps a figure of Natigay in his house, which is constructed from cloth and felt. These figures are not solitary; images of Natigay's children and wife are also created in the same materials. These figures are arranged in a specific order: the wife is placed to the left, and the children are positioned in front.

## Rituals of Offering
The Tartars perform specific offerings to Natigay and his family during their meals. When eating, they take the grease and fat from the meat and use it to grease the mouths of the figures of the god, his wife, and his children. Furthermore, they sprinkle broth before the door of the house. Once these actions are completed, the Tartars consider that the god and his family have received their share of the dinner.

## Cultural Associations
The religious and social life of the Tartars includes the consumption of a drink called *Kemiz* (also referred to as *Kumiz*), which is made from mare's milk. This drink is prepared in a manner that makes it resemble white wine.

Regarding broader historical perspectives on their beliefs, some sources mention that the Scythians—a term used by Byzantine writers to refer to the Tartars—originally practiced "ancestral atheism." It is noted that they later adopted the religions and manners of the Persians, Chaldæans, and Assyrians through contact with those peoples. Additionally, some traditions among the Yakuts suggest a belief that the gods favor the rich while rejecting and casting out the poor.
