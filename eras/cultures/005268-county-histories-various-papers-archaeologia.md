# County Histories and Archaeologia

The study of regional history and antiquity in Britain is supported by a diverse array of sources, ranging from specialized county histories and topographical surveys to the publications of archaeological societies, most notably *Archaeologia*. These works serve as repositories for genealogical, architectural, and cultural data, providing detailed records of specific locales and the material remains of past civilizations.

## County Histories and Topographical Surveys

County histories are comprehensive records that often combine historical narrative with directory and gazetteer information. Edward Baines (1774–1848), a politician and newspaper proprietor, authored several such works, including the *History, Directory and Gazetteer of the County of York*, the *History, Directory and Gazetteer of the County of Lancaster*, and the *History of the County Palatine and Duchy of Lancaster*.

In the case of Kent, a vast bibliography of earlier works exists, as detailed in J. R. Smith's *Bibliotheca Cantiana* (1837). Notable historical surveys of Kent include:
* **Early Works:** W. Lambarde's *Perambulation of Kent* (1576, 1826) and R. Kilburne's *Topographie or Survey of the County of Kent* (1659).
* **18th and 19th Century Surveys:** J. Harris's *History of Kent* (1719) and E. Hasted's *History and Topographical Survey of Kent*, which was published in four folio volumes (1778–1799) and later in twelve 8vo volumes (1797–1801). Other contributions include W. H. Ireland's *History of the County of Kent* (1828–1830) and R. Furley's *History of the Weald of Kent* (1871–1874).
* **Specialized Regional Studies:** L. B. Larking's *The Domesday Book of Kent* (1869) and the *Victoria County History* for Kent.

Other regional histories include John Hodgson's *History of Northumberland* (1840), which provides information on Hadrian's Wall.

## The Role of Archaeologia and Archaeological Societies

The publication *Archaeologia* and the journals of various archaeological societies are essential for the study of material culture. These sources provide examples and illustrations of early costume that are considered of great value. They also document specific archaeological findings and theories.

For instance, *Archaeologia* (vol. xxx, p. 113) contains communications from George Godwin, F.R.S., F.S.A., who first drew attention to "masons' marks" in England. These signs, found on stone blocks in buildings, are used to connect the work to the stone worker and potentially to an overseer. 

Furthermore, *Archaeologia* has been used to document specific sites, such as Silchester (1890–1908) and Caerwent (1901–1908). Other related publications include *Archaeologia Cantiana*, which contains translations from the Kent Archaeological Society (from 1858), and *Archaeologia Cambrensis*, which includes the index of Llyfr Coch Asaph.

## Contributions of Antiquaries

Individual antiquaries have contributed significantly to these archives through the publication of papers and the curation of collections. Francis Douce (1757–1834), a prominent member of the Society of Antiquaries and former keeper of manuscripts in the British Museum, contributed numerous papers to *Archaeologia* and *The Gentleman's Magazine*. His work included *Illustrations of Shakespeare and Ancient Manners* (1807) and a *Dissertation on the various Designs of the Dance of Death* (1833).

## Integration of Sources in Historical Research

Archaeological and county records are often used in conjunction with primary authorities to reconstruct the history of Britain. For example, research into Roman Britain utilizes the *Victoria County History* alongside the works of Prof. F. Haverfield. Studies of the Anglo-Saxon period rely on primary texts such as the *Saxon Chronicle*, the *Anglo-Saxon Laws*, and the *Cartularium Saxonicum*, as well as modern analyses like F. Seebohm's *The English Village Community* (1883) and P. Vinogradoff's *The Growth of the Manor* (1905).
