# West Indies

The West Indies is a region comprising seventeen islands or groups of islands, as well as continental colonies including Honduras and British Guiana. Historically, the region has been a site of European colonial competition, naval warfare, and the presence of diverse populations.

## Population and Demographics
The total coloured population of the West Indies is approximately 1,912,655. Within this group, the colonies with the largest coloured populations are:
*   **Jamaica**: 790,000 (chiefly black, with some yellow and brown).
*   **British Guiana**: 286,000 (black and brown).
*   **Trinidad and Tobago**: 250,000 (black and brown).

Together, these three colonies account for 1,326,000 of the region's coloured population.

## Colonial History and European Presence
By 1815, the British Empire's holdings in the West Indies included Jamaica and several minor possessions characterized as slave-labour possessions, alongside British Guiana in South America. Other European powers also maintained a presence in the region during this era; France held one or two West Indian islands and French Guiana, while Denmark possessed an island or so in the West Indies. Spain retained Cuba.

The region was also a theater for maritime activity and conflict. In 1688, Davis arrived in the West Indies after clearing Cape Horn, while French buccaneers also reached the area by crossing New Spain. The history of the buccaneers in the West Indies is documented in the *Calendar of State Papers, Colonial Series* and various narratives, such as those by Exquemelin and Captain James Burney.

## Military Conflicts
The West Indies were a primary setting for the war between Great Britain and Spain following the declaration of war on October 23, 1739. British strategy involved a combined operation to assault Spanish colonies from the east and west. Admiral Edward Vernon led the force in the West Indies, successfully capturing the Spanish harbour of Porto Bello on November 21, 1739. 

However, subsequent operations faced significant difficulties. Between March 9 and April 24, 1741, an attack was launched on Cartagena (located in present-day Colombia). This siege failed with a heavy loss of life, attributed to the preparations of the Spanish admiral Don Bias de Leso, as well as the incompetence and "brutal insolence" of the British admiral and military officers. Following two additional unsuccessful attacks on Spanish territory, the war in the West Indies subsided until 1748. During this period, Spanish privateers also operated with destructive effect against British trade within the West Indies.

## Health and Medicine
The West Indies is noted as a region where dengue—an infectious fever occurring in warm climates—is most nearly endemic. The disease is characterized by a sudden attack of fever, severe headache, erythema, and rheumatic pains in the joints and muscles. A distinguishing feature is an eruption resembling measles that follows a crisis and a brief interval of recovery.

The term "dengue" is identified with a Spanish word meaning "stiff or prim behaviour," which was adopted in the West Indies to describe the cramped movements of those suffering from the illness. The population of negroes in the region referred to the condition as "dandy-fever." Some linguistic sources suggest both "dengue" and "dandy" are corruptions of the Swahili word *dinga* or *denga*, meaning a sudden attack of cramp. The region experienced a chief epidemic of this fever between 1824 and 1826.
