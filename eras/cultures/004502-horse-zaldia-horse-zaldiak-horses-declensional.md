## Linguistic Examples in Basque
In the Basque language, which utilizes the Roman character system (Spanish or French) and lacks its own graphic system, declension is replaced by a highly developed postpositional system. The definite article, *a* (plural *ak*), functions as a postposition. This is exemplified by the word for "horse," *zaldi*. When the definite article is added, it becomes *zaldia* ("the horse"), and in the plural form, it is *zaldiak* ("the horses"). 

The language employs various declensional suffixes or postpositions that can be added to one another and are placed after the article in definite nouns. ## Prehistoric and Wild Horses
During the prehistoric Neolithic or polished-stone period in Europe, wild horses were abundant. Evidence from bone remains and carvings on reindeers' antlers or bones suggests these animals were heavy in build and small in size, featuring large heads and rough, shaggy tails and manes. They are compared to the Mongolian wild pony (Przewalski's horse) and the extinct tarpans of the south Russian steppes. During this era, the chase of these animals was a primary occupation for humans, providing a critical food supply.

Following their domestication by early Europeans, horses spread globally via human agency. Selective breeding, crossing, and domestication have resulted in extreme variations, such as the London dray-horse, the thoroughbred racer, and the Shetland pony, the latter of which was dwarfed by an uncongenial climate. In America and Australia, horses introduced by European settlers escaped into unreclaimed lands, where they multiplied into vast herds on uncultivated plains.

## Breeds and Breeding
British horse breeds are categorized into light and heavy varieties. Light horses include the Pony, Hackney, Cleveland Bay, Yorkshire Coach-horse, and Thoroughbred. Heavy horses include the Suffolk, Clydesdale, and Shire.

Historically, the British horse was significantly influenced by the Norman Conquest. William the Conqueror used a Spanish breed, and other barons introduced similar Spanish horses to their estates. Roger de Bellesme, an earl of Shrewsbury and follower of William I, also imported Spanish stallions. 

Later, Edward III showed a strong interest in horses, procuring fifty Spanish horses (likely jennets). During his reign, there was a trend toward breeding speedier, lighter horses. However, the traditional ponderous cavalry horse was maintained for a time because the lighter variety could not support the weight of the armor used to protect both rider and horse. Edward III forbade the exportation of improved horses to preserve English breeding advantages, leading to a rise in value that eventually forced Richard II to set a maximum price for them. In the ninth year of his reign, Edward also received two valuable running horses from the king of Navarre. Later, during the reign of Richard III, post-horses and stages were introduced.

## Historical Uses and Racing
### Ancient Racing
The earliest literary record of horse racing appears in *Il.* xxiii. 212-650, describing chariot races at the funeral games for Patroclus. In the Olympic games, four-horse chariot races were introduced by the 23rd Olympiad, followed by mounted horse races in the 33rd. Other variations included loose-horse races, mule races, two-horse chariot races, and races for under-aged horses. Candidates had to send their horses to Elis thirty days before the games, and riders underwent a month of prescribed exercise. Similar contests occurred at the Nemean, Isthmian, and Pythian games, as well as the Panathenaea and Athenian Olympia. In Athens, the passion for racing was evident in the opening of *The Clouds*; additionally, the Boeotians named a month Hippodromius ("Horse-race month").

Single-rider races were also held, and victors were granted high glory. Pindar's first ode celebrates Hiero, king of Syracuse, calling him *Kéλης* ("Victor in the horse-race"). Horses used for single riders were called *Kέλητες*. A specific type of race involved *Desultores* (riders) and *Desultorii* (horses), where the rider led a second horse by the bridle and vaulted from one to the other after several turns in the Stadium. This required great skill as they lacked saddles and stirrups. Numidian cavalry in Africa also utilized this vaulting technique.

### Military and State Use
In the Persian army under Xerxes, a thousand chosen horsemen led the way, followed by ten "Nesaian" horses with fine trappings. These horses originated from the Nesaian plain in Media. Following them was the sacred chariot of Zeus, drawn by eight white horses and led by a charioteer on foot. Xerxes himself followed in a chariot drawn by Nesaian horses, accompanied by a charioteer named Patiramphes.

### Agriculture and Infrastructure
The use of horses in agriculture was a late development. The Bayeux tapestry shows a horse drawing a harrow, though this was exceptional, as oxen were preferred until a later period. In Wales, a law specifically forbade using horses for ploughing.

In modern cavalry barracks, stables are designed for sanitary reasons, separated from barrack-rooms. Each horse is provided 1,500 cubic feet of air space, with heads facing outer walls and fresh air supplied via traversed air-ducts below the mangers. Windows open inwards with hopper checks to prevent direct draughts.
