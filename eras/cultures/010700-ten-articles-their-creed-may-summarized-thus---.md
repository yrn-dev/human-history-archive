# Creeds and Articles of Faith

In historical and religious contexts, the term "creed" refers to a formalized set of beliefs or dogmas. These are often organized into "articles," which serve as specific heads of belief or doctrinal statements. The structure and number of these articles vary significantly across different traditions, ranging from administrative ethics to theological confessions.

## The Japanese Imperial Creed
Based on ancient annals, the Japanese creed centers on the divine origin of the Imperial Throne, which is held to be coeval with heaven and earth. This belief stems from the Sun goddess, who handed three sacred objects to Ninigi (also known as Tenson, the "heavenly grandchild"). These objects provide a code of administrative ethics:

*   **The Mirror:** An emblem of purity and honesty, reflecting both good and evil qualities without perversion.
*   **The Jewel:** A piece of white or green jade symbolizing virtue and benevolence, illustrating obedience, amiability, softness, and gentleness.
*   **The Sword:** Associated with knowledge and intelligence, indicating practical decision, sharpness, and strength.

The creed maintains that the absence of misrule is guaranteed as long as these qualities are exercised during the discharge of administrative functions.

## Lutheran Dogmatic Symbols
Lutherans—those Christians following the principles of Martin Luther in opposition to the Roman Church, as well as followers of Calvin and other Reformation sectaries—utilize a collection of dogmatic symbols. These are generally grouped into nine separate creeds that comprise the *Book of Concord* (*Liber Concordiae*).

These symbols are divided between early Christian origins and the 16th century. The three early creeds are the Apostles' Creed, the Athanasian Creed, and the Nicene Creed (in its Western form containing the *filioque*). The six 16th-century documents include the *Form of Concord*, Luther's two *Catechisms*, the *Apology for the Augsburg Confession*, the *Schmalkald Articles*, and the *Augsburg Confession*.

While the three early creeds and the Augsburg Confession are recognized by all Lutherans, other documents are not universally accepted; for instance, many Lutheran churches expressly reject the *Form of Concord*. The Augsburg Confession exists in two versions, the *invariata* and the *variata* (the latter signed by Calvin), which differ slightly regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Supper.

## The Arya Samaj
The Arya Samaj is a movement characterized by an intensity of conviction and a narrower scope than the Brahma Samaj. It appealed to educated natives who were predisposed against foreign religious influence and felt their faith in current Hinduism was undermined. The movement presented a view of India's original faith that harmonized with European civilization's ethics and modern science.

The Samaj maintains a creed consisting of ten articles. A central tenet of this creed forbids the consumption of animal food and the destruction of animal life. This specific article led to a cleavage within the movement: the *Ghasi* (vegetarians) and the *Mansi* (flesh-eating). The *Mansi* section includes Rajputs who supported the movement but declined to adhere to the prohibition on animal products. Admission to the Samaj is permitted at age eighteen, and members are expected to contribute at least 1% of their income to its funds. By 1900, the membership had grown to over 92,000.

## Evolution of Dogma and Articles in Christianity
The conceptualization of "dogma" and "articles" evolved through the Greek and Latin Fathers. Greek Fathers generally used "dogma" to mean doctrine, and Cyril of Jerusalem's fourth *Catechetical Discourse* referred to "Ten Dogmas," a figure possibly derived from the commandments. This influenced the later division of creeds into fourteen or twelve articles. In the case of the twelve-article division, legend suggests that the twelve apostles each contributed a single sentence to the formula.

In the Latin tradition, Jerome used the phrase *ecclesiastica dogmata* to contrast church teachings with heretical ones. By the medieval period, Thomas Aquinas replaced the term with *sacra doctrina*. During the Reformation and the subsequent period, the term "dogma" was often used by both Protestant and Roman Catholic writers to describe heretical teachings. 

The Council of Trent (1545-1562) used "dogma" to mean doctrine, though the term eventually shifted toward a narrower meaning: truth defined by church authority. In modern Roman Catholic theology, a distinction is made between "pure" dogmas (of revelation) and "mixed" dogmas (taught by both revelation and natural theology).
