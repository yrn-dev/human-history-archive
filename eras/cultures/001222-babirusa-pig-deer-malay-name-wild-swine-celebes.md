# BABIRUSA

The babirusa, known by the Malay name meaning "pig-deer," is a remarkable species of wild swine found on the islands of Buru and Celebes. In the field of zoology, the animal is the sole representative of its genus and is scientifically designated as *Babirusa alfurus*.

## Physical Characteristics
The babirusa is characterized by a skin that is very rough, rugged, and nearly naked. Its dental structure consists of 34 teeth in total, following the formula *i.2/3. c.1/1. p.2/3. m.3/3*. While its molars—particularly the final ones—are simpler and smaller than those found in pigs of the genus *Sus*, the most distinctive feature of the babirusa is the extraordinary development of the canines, or tusks, in the male.

These tusks are slender, long, and curved, lacking enamel and growing continuously. The upper jaw tusks grow upward from their bases, piercing the skin of the face rather than entering the mouth. This growth pattern makes them resemble horns; they curve backwards and downwards, and frequently curve forwards again until they almost or entirely touch the forehead. In older animals, these tusks can reach an enormous size, though they are generally broken off, as if through fighting.

## Theories on Tusk Function
The purpose of these horn-like teeth has been a subject of debate among observers. Some early writers suggested the tusks served as hooks to allow the animal to rest its head on a branch. Dr. A. R. Wallace noted that because the tusks diverge in front of and over the eye, they might serve to protect the eyes from spines and thorns while the animal hunts for fallen fruits in thickets of other spiny plants and rattans. 

However, Wallace found this theory unsatisfactory because the female babirusa, who seeks food in the same manner, does not possess these tusks. He proposed instead that the tusks were once useful and worn down as they grew, but that changes in life conditions rendered them unnecessary. Consequently, they now develop into a "monstrous form," similar to how the incisors of a rabbit or beaver continue to grow if the opposite teeth do not wear them away. Under this view, the male's tusks are examples of redundant development, comparable to the single pair of lower teeth found in certain beaked whales.

## Regional Context
The babirusa is native to Celebes and Buru. Celebes is an island in the Dutch East Indies where the capital of a district, Macassar (also known as Makassar or Mangkasar), serves as the chief town of the Dutch government. Macassar is located on the west coast of the island's southern peninsula. 

The fauna of Celebes and the Molucca Islands exhibits specific biological boundaries. The presence of mammals of the Marsupial order in these areas, contrasted with their absence in Borneo and Java (which lie west of Wallace's line), indicates the margin of the Australian region. Other singular animals found in Celebes include the *Anoa* (a ruminant) and *Cynopithecus* (a form of baboon), both of which appear to be allied to African animals rather than Asiatic ones.

## Cultural Beliefs in Celebes
Among the Toradjas of central Celebes, there are beliefs regarding a man's "inside" taking the form of various animals, including the wild pig, deer, ape, or cat, before resuming human form; this phenomenon is called *lamboyo*. Some believe this power of transformation is a gift from the gods, while others view this "werwolfism" as contagious. According to some, it can be acquired by leaning one's head against a pillar or eating food left by a werwolf. The Todjoers specifically believe that anyone who touches blood becomes a werwolf. This condition is believed to be curable by rubbing and pinching the stomach and breast of the "werman" to extract the witch object, accompanied by medicine that causes the contagion to leave the body as worms and snakes.
