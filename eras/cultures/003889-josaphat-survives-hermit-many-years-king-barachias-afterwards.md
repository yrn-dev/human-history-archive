# Josaphat and King Barachias

The narrative of Prince Josaphat describes his transition from a secluded royal upbringing to a life of asceticism and his eventual role as a saint. His story is characterized by a journey from ignorance of human suffering to the adoption of the Christian faith and the eremitic life.

## Early Life and Awakening
Josaphat was the son of King Abenner, a powerful pagan ruler who persecuted Christians and ascetics. Due to a prophecy from a learned sage—who predicted that Josaphat would adopt the faith his father persecuted and find glory in a kingdom more exalted than his own—Abenner attempted to shield the boy from all knowledge of sorrow. Josaphat was raised in a splendid palace within a secluded city, attended only by healthy youth. He was strictly forbidden from encountering poverty, disease, old age, or death, and was kept ignorant of the religion of Christ.

Despite these precautions, Josaphat eventually encountered a leper and a blind man, leading him to realize that such ills are liabilities to which all men are subject. A subsequent encounter with a decrepit old man introduced him to the inevitability of death. These experiences left the prince in a state of deep depression and pensive reflection regarding the certainty of death and the nature of existence thereafter.

## Conversion and Governance
Josaphat's convictions were further shaped by Barlaam, an eremite of great knowledge and sanctity from the wilderness of Sennaritis. Barlaam traveled to India disguised as a merchant to gain access to the prince, to whom he taught Christian doctrine and commended the monastic life. 

The prince's new beliefs met with suspicion, and although the king attempted to distract him by employing a magician named Theudas—who replaced Josaphat's attendants with seductive girls—the prince resisted these blandishments through prayer. Eventually, King Abenner abandoned these efforts and allowed Josaphat to associate in the government. Josaphat used his political power to promote religion, leading to prosperity in his administration. Ultimately, Abenner himself converted to the faith and died after several years of penitence.

## Life as a Hermit
Following the death of his father, Josaphat surrendered the kingdom to a friend named Barachias and departed for the wilderness. He spent two years in a painful search, during which he was much buffeted by demons, before he finally located Barlaam. After Barlaam died, Josaphat continued to live as a hermit for many years.

## Legacy and King Barachias
King Barachias later arrived at the site of the hermit's dwelling. He transferred the bodies of the two saints, Josaphat and Barlaam, back to India. In India, these remains became the source of many miracles.

## Historical and Literary Analysis
Historians have noted a strong parallel between the story of St. Josaphat and that of the Buddha. Similarities include the education in a secluded palace, the successive encounters with sickness, old age, and death, and the eventual abandonment of royalty for an ascetic life. The phraseology in some versions of Josaphat's history almost literally renders the Sanskrit of the *Lalita Vistara*. Furthermore, the name Josaphat (or Joasaph) is considered a corruption of "Bodisat," derived from "Bodisatva," a common title for the Buddha.

The identity of these stories was recognized by Diogo do Couto (1542-1616) and later by Laboulaye (1859) and Felix Liebrecht (1860). Other researchers who have studied the material include Zotenberg, Max Mueller, Rhys Davids, Braunholtz, and Joseph Jacobs. Zotenberg suggested that the Christian version reflects religious disputes in 7th-century Syria and was edited by a monk named John of Saint Saba. The story was later incorporated into Simeon Metaphrastes' *Lives of the Saints* around 950 and translated into numerous languages, including a 13th-century Icelandic version for Prince Hakon and a 1712 Tagala translation in Manila. The narrative has also influenced writers such as Boccaccio, John Gower, and Shakespeare, and was expanded into a poem of 16,000 lines by Rudolph of Ems around 1220.
