# Climate, Geography, and Flora of Afghanistan, Persia, Asia Minor, and Syria

## Seasonal Precipitation and Climate
In the regions of Afghanistan, Persia, Asia Minor, and Syria, winter and spring serve as the primary seasons of condensation. This pattern differs from other parts of Asia, where the majority of rainfall occurs between May and September during the hottest half of the year, or in equatorial islands, where the heaviest falls take place between October and February.

A temperate climate zone extends from the Mediterranean across the highlands of Asia Minor, Persia, and Afghanistan, reaching as far as the southern slopes of the Himalaya and northern China. In this belt, rainfall is sufficient to support vigorous vegetation. While the winters are severe and the summers are hot, neither is considered extreme.

## Flora and Fauna
The plants and animals found in the zone spanning from the Mediterranean to the Himalaya and northern China exhibit a marked similarity to those of southern Europe, with which the region is virtually continuous. Specifically, the flora of northern Persia and Asia Minor differs very little from that of southern Europe. In areas of Asia Minor where rainfall is abundant, the mountains are covered in forests of *Ulmus*, *Acer*, *Carpinus*, *Corylus*, *Fagus*, and *Quercus*, as well as various Coniferae. Of these, *Fagus* is the only genus not found on the Himalaya. Additionally, fruit trees of the plum tribe are abundant in these areas, and the cultivated plants are those of southern Europe.

In Afghanistan, the flora of the northern region approximates that of the contiguous western Himalaya. The evergreen oak of southern Europe, *Quercus Ilex*, is found in forests extending east to the Sutlej, accompanied by other European forms. In the higher elevations of Persia and Afghanistan, thistles and Boraginaceae are common. These districts are also characterized by gigantic Umbelliferae, including *Dorema*, *Bubon*, *Galbanum*, *Ferula*, *Prangos*, and *Peucedanum*, some of which extend into Tibet.

## Physical Geography and Hydrography
Asia Minor is noted for its numerous mineral and thermal springs, many of which were famous in antiquity and listed by Strabo. Notable springs include those in the Ismid sanjak (Yalova), the Brusa vilayet (Brusa, Chitli, Terje, and Eskishehr), the Karasi (Tuzla), the Aidin (Alashehr, Hierapolis—which has enormous alum deposits, Cheshme, and Ilija), the Angora (Iskelib and Terzili Hammam), the Kastamuni (Boli), and the Sivas (Khavsa). The Maeander valley is particularly recognized for its hot springs.

The region also contains various lakes. Freshwater lakes include Beishehr Geul (ancient *Karalis*), which is 30 miles long and discharges south-east to the Soghla Geul, and Egirdir Geul, which is also 30 miles long and known for its variety and abundance of fish. In the north-west of Asia Minor, there are also Maniyas Geul (L. Miletopolis), Abulliont Geul (L. Apollonia), and Isnik Geul (L. Ascania). Salt lakes include Buldur Geul and Aji-tuz Geul. Tuz Geul (ancient *Tatta*) is located in the great central plain; it measures approximately 60 miles long and 10 to 30 miles broad in winter, though it becomes little more than a saline marsh during the dry season.

## Human Geography and Demographics
The temperate zone extending from the Mediterranean through the highlands of Afghanistan, Persia, and Asia Minor to India is occupied by Aryans, defined as races speaking languages derived from or akin to an ancient form of Sanskrit. To the south-west of Persia, in countries including Syria and Arabia and extending into North Africa, are the Semitic races, who speak languages akin to ancient Assyrian, primarily represented today by Arabic. Both groups are considered branches of the Melanochroi, modified by admixture with Xanthochroi, Australoids, and Mongols.

## Cartography and Surveying
General government-directed surveys are absent in Asiatic Turkey, leaving researchers dependent on compilations. These include the Russian general map (1880-1885), the Royal Geographical Society's 1910 map of western Persia, Syria, and eastern Turkey in Asia, and R. Kiepert's *Asia Minor* (1904-1908). For Afghanistan and Persia, sources include a Russian staff map (1886) and Colonel Sir T. H. Holdich's map of Persia (1897-1899). Some specific surveys of interest were necessitated by the settlement of boundaries in Seistan (1870) and northern Afghanistan (1883).

Furthermore, minor extensions of the Indian triangulation have reached the eastern districts of Persia through Baluchistan and Afghanistan. These extensions have helped raise the accuracy of Asiatic geographical surveys. By the end of the 19th century, western Asia (excluding Arabia) was considered free from geographical perplexity.
