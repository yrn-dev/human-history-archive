# Slavic Race

## Early History and Origins
The presence of Slavic inhabitants in Bohemia has been proven by recent archaeological research to extend as far back as the beginning of the Christian era. While the region was previously inhabited by the Boii—a Celtic tribe whose capital was Boiohemum—and later subdued by the Teutonic Marcomanni around 12 B.C., the land was eventually conquered by Slavic tribes. Among these, the Cechs were the most important. Although the exact date of the Cechs' arrival is uncertain and classical or Byzantine references are often misleading, they appear to have become the masters of Bohemia in the 5th century.

One of the first historical rulers of the Cechs was Samo, who defeated the Franks at a great battle at Wogatisburg in 630 (likely near the present town of Eger) and defeated the Avars, a Turanian tribe that had briefly held overlordship over Bohemia. Following a period of obscurity lasting approximately 130 years, chroniclers such as Cosmas record the foundation of a Bohemian principality by Krok (or Crocus) and his daughter Libussa. Libussa is said to have married a peasant named Premysl who was found ploughing his field; this legend is noted as being common across most Slavic countries. From this semi-mythic figure, ancient chroniclers developed a continuous list of Premyslide princes, though the dates of their reigns and their deeds are not considered historical.

## Ethnological Theories and Early Tribes
In the study of early peoples, Paul Joseph Safarik provided plausible grounds for believing that the Budini and Neuri were of the Slavic family, challenging the view of Mannert, who conceived the Budini as a Teutonic tribe. Safarik noted that the names Budini and Neuri can be traced to Slavic roots. Furthermore, the wooden town described by Herodotus among the Budini serves as an exact parallel to primitive Slavic towns existing as late as the twelfth century. The environmental descriptions of the region—characterized by marshes and woods containing otters and beavers—align more closely with Russia and southern Poland than with the Ural mountains. Regarding the physical appearance of the Budini, while some construed descriptions in favor of a Teutonic family, Safarik argued in favor of the Slavic family.

## The Bohemian Revival
The revival of Bohemian literature and language was primarily driven by four men: Joseph Jungmann, John Kolar, Paul Joseph Safarik, and Francis Palacky. 

Joseph Jungmann (1773-1847) contributed to the development of the Bohemian language through his monumental German and Bohemian dictionary and his work *Dejepes literatury ceska* (history of Bohemian literature), as well as numerous translations of English and German writers. Paul Joseph Safarik (1795-1861) wrote *Starozitnosti Slovanske* (Slavic antiquities), which attempted to record the then almost unknown literature and history of the early Slavs. Francis Palacky (1798-1876), regarded as the greatest of Bohemian historians, produced a significant history of Bohemia covering the period from the earliest times to 1526.

John Kolar (1793-1852) is recognized as the greatest poet of the Bohemian revival. His principal poem, *Slavy dcera* (daughter of Slavia), serves as a personification of the Slavic race. The principal importance of this work at the present time is found in the role it played in the revival of Bohemian literature rather than in its artistic value.

## Modern Ethnological Perspectives
In more recent historical contexts, some "perverted ethnology" and "distorted history" sought to persuade mixed Teutonic, Keltic, and Slavic Germans that they constituted a "wonderful race apart." This ideology of racial superiority was mirrored by English writers who invented the concept of the "Anglo-Saxon" as the culmination of humanity. These legends of superiority contributed to increased irritations among the French in Lorraine and the Poles in Posen.
