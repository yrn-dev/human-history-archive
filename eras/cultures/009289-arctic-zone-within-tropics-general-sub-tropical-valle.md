# Arctic Zones within the Tropics and the Sub-Tropical Valle

In certain geographical regions, specifically within the torrid zone, the interaction of latitude and extreme elevation creates a vertical arrangement of climatic zones. This phenomenon allows for the existence of an "Arctic zone within the tropics," where conditions of extreme cold and permanent snow exist despite the tropical location of the territory.

## The Bolivian Climatic Gradient
Bolivia serves as a primary example of this vertical climatic diversity. Because the country lies entirely within the torrid zone, temperature variations are driven by prevailing winds, mountain barriers, and elevation. This results in every gradation of temperature, ranging from the heat of the tropical lowlands to the Arctic cold found on snow-capped peaks.

The native classification of these zones is as follows:
*   **Yungas:** Comprising lowlands and mountain valleys up to 5,000 ft., this zone is tropical, exceedingly humid, and lacks a winter. The official mean temperature is 70° F.
*   **Valle:** This sub-tropical zone includes deep valleys from 5,000 to 9,500 ft. It is characterized by a warm climate, moderate temperature variations, and an absence of cold weather, leading some to describe it as a region of perpetual summer.
*   **Cabezera de Valle:** Located at the heads of deep valleys between 9,500 and 11,000 ft., this temperate region has regular seasons favorable for vegetables and cereals.
*   **Puna:** This zone, covering the great central plateau between 11,000 and 12,500 ft., has a mean temperature of 54° F. The air is cold and dry, with only two seasons (winter and a cold summer or autumn), limiting production to barley and potatoes.
*   **Puna Brava:** Extending from 12,500 ft. to the snow limit at approximately 17,500 ft., this is a bleak and inhospitable territory inhabited by miners and shepherds.
*   **The Arctic Zone:** Above the snow limit lies the region of eternal snow, which constitutes an Arctic zone within the tropics.

## Environmental Health and Flora
The sub-tropical (*valle*) and temperate (*cabezera de valle*) regions are considered agreeable and healthy, featuring moderate temperatures in the shade and plentiful rainfall. In contrast, the tropical *yungas* are prone to fevers and malaria due to the presence of decaying vegetation on the ground. While there is a high mortality rate among natives, this is attributed to diet and unsanitary habits rather than the climate.

The flora of Bolivia reflects these diversities, ranging from luxuriant tropical forests in the Amazon basin to the scanty Arctic vegetation of the lofty Cordilleras. Indigenous plants of the sub-tropical and tropical zones include cassava, pineapple, peanuts, vanilla, rubber-producing trees, and hardwoods such as mahogany and cedar. In the *puna* region, the vegetation is characterized by quinoa, oca, the potato, and coarse grasses.

## Comparative Tropical and Arctic Distributions
The concept of Arctic and Tropical zones extends beyond climate to the distribution of flora and fauna.

### Botanical Distribution
The *Bambuseae* (bamboos) are found throughout the tropical zone, extending into temperate and sub-tropical areas. In the Andes of South America, these plants reach as far as the snow-line.

### Ichthyology (Fish Distribution)
In the study of shore-fishes, the world is divided into several regions, including the Arctic and Antarctic regions. The tropical zone, regardless of the ocean, is where fishes are most abundant and exhibit the most gorgeous coloration and bizarre forms, particularly around coral reefs. While the Arctic and Antarctic regions show a circumpolar distribution of shore fishes, there is no separate arctic zone for freshwater fishes. In high latitudes, freshwater ichthyic life becomes extinct wherever water remains frozen for most of the year; the highest latitude where fishes have been recorded is 82° N. lat.

## Cultural Adaptations to Climate
The distinction between tropical and arctic environments is also reflected in human clothing. Some classifications divide dress into "tropical" (based on the girdle or cloak) and "arctic" (based on the trouser). Historically, the use of trousers was a striking characteristic of northern barbarians to the Romans and Greeks. While the arctic dress was adopted by both sexes in the most northerly races, women in more southern regions often retained tropical forms of dress, partly because their lives were less active or confined to the home.
