# Throwing Weapons and Metalwork in Africa

## Throwing Weapons of Africa
In North-East Africa, a weapon that bears a close resemblance to the boomerang survives to the present day. From this region, the weapon spread in allied forms constructed from metal, specifically in the form of throwing knives. 

In South Africa, particularly among the Zulu and other Kaffir tribes, a weapon for hurling or throwing known as the assegai (or assagai) is utilized. Derived from the Berber-Arab term *as-zahayah* via the Portuguese *azagaia*, the assegai is a light spear or javelin. These weapons are constructed from wood and pointed with iron. They exist in two primary forms: a long-handled version and a shorter weapon designed for use at close quarters.

## Development of Metal Use in Africa
The progress of the negro in central and southern Africa is described as having been slower than in other old-world centres. This development occurred under the stimulus of invasions by whiter tribes from Mediterranean regions. These migrating groups brought with them the use of metals and cultivation. 

These Mediterranean peoples entered Africa via two primary routes:
*   **The West:** Berbers, Tuaregs, and similar groups crossed the Sahara, mixing with the negro population to create quasi-white races, such as the Fulas.
*   **The Nile:** This route provided another point of entry, and it is possible that the Baganda (Gandafolk) of Uganda are of remote white origin.

## Related Geographic and Cultural Contexts
Historically, the African forests were denser than they are today, spreading northward and eastward from the Upper Nile. In the south, the Zambezi river serves as a definite boundary line from an ethnological standpoint, as well as for flora and fauna; Barotseland is considered to belong to Central Africa rather than South Africa.

Regarding prehistoric influences, some theories suggest that the Basques may be descended from fairer Berber tribes ("Eurafrican") through the ancient Libyans, a people depicted on Egyptian monuments. Additionally, megalithic remains—including stone circles, cromlechs, menhirs, and dolmens—are said to resemble those of northern Africa more closely than the larger remains found in the British Isles or Brittany.
