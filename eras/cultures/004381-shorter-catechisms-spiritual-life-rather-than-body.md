# Basilian Monasticism

## Origins and Foundation
Basilian monks are those who follow the rule established by Basil the Great. Around 360, Basil founded his monastery in the region of Pontus, specifically in the neighborhood of Neocaesarea. This foundation was a deliberate reaction against the monastic trends Basil observed while visiting the monasteries of Palestine and Egypt. In Syria and Palestine, he noted that monastic life was becoming increasingly eremitical and was characterized by "great extravagances" regarding bodily austerities.

## The Basilian System
Basil introduced a new departure in monachism by declaring the cenobitical life superior to the eremitical. His system emphasized that work should be an integral part of monastic life, serving both as an occupation and as a means to do good for others. Consequently, he advocated that monasteries be located near towns. 

Under Basil's establishment, the life was strictly cenobitical, featuring:
* Common meals.
* Common work, which included heavy field-work.
* Common prayer conducted seven times a day.

Despite these innovations, the life remained austere and contemplative, dedicated to the reading of the Scriptures and prayer. Basil maintained that austerities and fasting should not interfere with work or prayer.

## The Rules and Virtues
The monastic system of St. Basil is documented in the "Longer" and "Shorter" Rules. Rather than being a body of regulations for the corporate working of a community, these Rules are described as catechisms of the spiritual life. While the authorship of these and other ascetical writings has been questioned, the current tendency is to recognize the two sets of Rules as his. Further insight into his system can be derived from correspondence between Basil and St. Gregory Nazianzen at the start of Gregory's monastic life.

Regarding the conduct of the monks, there is no evidence that vows were taken. However, the system strongly emphasized several monastic virtues, including:
* Personal poverty.
* Chastity.
* Obedience.
* Self-denial.

## Historical Influence
The reconstruction of monastic life by St. Basil serves as the basis for monasticism within the Slavonic and Greek Churches to this day, although the monks in these traditions do not refer to themselves as Basilians.
