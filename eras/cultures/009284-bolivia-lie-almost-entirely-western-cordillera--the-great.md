# Geography and Geology of Bolivia

Bolivia is a country characterized by diverse physical features, ranging from high mountain ranges to low alluvial plains. While often conceived as an extremely rugged mountainous country, three-fifths of Bolivia consists of gently undulating forest regions, great swamps, flooded bottomlands, and low alluvial plains, including the Chiquitos region.

## The Andean Cordilleras
Roughly two-fifths of Bolivia's total area is comprised of the Andean cordilleras. These mountains cross the south-west corner of the country and project east toward the Brazilian highlands in a great obtuse angle. The system is divided into two great parallel chains—the Cordillera Occidental (Western Cordillera) and the Cordillera Oriental (Eastern Cordillera)—with accompanying spurs and flanking ranges to the east.

The Cordillera Occidental serves as part of the boundary between Bolivia and the northern provinces of Chile. It acts as the western rampart for the *alta-planicie*, or the great Bolivian tableland. To the north-east of Lake Titicaca, the ranges form a "knot" of intersecting lofty ridges known as the Nudo de Apolobamba, which contains some of the highest peaks in South America.

## The Bolivian Tableland (Alta-Planicie)
The *alta-planicie* is a great lacustrine basin that once held an inland sea with an eastern outlet through the La Paz gorge. It extends 500 miles in length and approximately 80 miles in breadth, covering about 40,000 square miles. The plateau reaches an average elevation of 12,650 feet near Lake Titicaca, descending about 1,000 feet toward its southern extremity.

The plateau is divided into two primary regions: the northern part, known as the *puna*, and the southern part, called the "desert of Lipez," which is part of the great Puna de Atacama. The landscape is bleak and inhospitable in the north and barren and arid in the south, featuring saline depressions that fill with water during the rainy season. The highest peak on the plateau is the Cerro de Tahua at 17,454 feet. Overlooking the plateau from the west are several snow-clad peaks, including Sajama (21,047 ft), Huallatiri (21,654 ft), Parinacota (20,918 ft), and Pomarape (20,505 ft).

## Geology and Volcanism
The geological composition of the cordilleras varies significantly between the two main chains. The eastern ranges are formed of Palaeozoic rocks with other intrusions and granitic elements. In contrast, the Western Cordillera consists chiefly of Jurassic and Cretaceous beds, along with ashes and lavas from volcanoes. Consequently, the volcanoes of Bolivia lie almost entirely in the Western Cordillera. The great summits of the eastern range, such as Sorata and Illimani, are composed of Palaeozoic rocks.

The intervening plateau is covered by terrestrial and freshwater deposits. Ridges of Palaeozoic rock and a series of gypsiferous marls and red sandstones rise through these deposits. In the north-eastern ranges, Carboniferous and Devonian beds are extensively developed, while Cambrian and Ordovician beds form the greater part of the eastern Andes in the south. The hills of the Chiquitos, rising from the eastern plains, are composed of ancient sedimentary rocks of an unknown age.

## Hydrography and Drainage
Bolivian river systems are categorized into three distinct regions: the Central Plateau, La Plata, and the Amazon. The Amazon system covers more than half of the republic and includes rivers flowing into the Madeira. The two principal rivers of this system are the Mamore and the Beni. The Beni rises near La Paz as the Rio de La Paz and flows through mountainous districts as a raging torrent before becoming navigable for 217 miles from Reyes to the Esperanza rapids. Its principal affluent, the Madre de Dios, rises in the eastern Cordilleras.

The south-east drainage basin discharges into the Paraguay. Its only large Bolivian river is the Pilcomayo, which rises on the east slopes of the Cordillera Oriental. Other streams in this region include the Tarija (or Rio Grande), the Otuquis, and the Parapiti.

## Climate and Flora
Bolivia is located entirely within the torrid zone, meaning temperature variations are driven by prevailing winds, mountain barriers, and elevation. The country exhibits every gradation of temperature, from Arctic cold on snow-capped peaks to tropical heat in the lowlands. The Cordillera extracts moisture from easterly winds, leaving the elevated plateaus with limited rainfall in the north and absolute barrenness in the south.

The flora mirrors these climatic zones. The *puna* region is characterized by quinoa, oca, the potato, and coarse grasses. The sub-tropical and tropical zones feature rubber-producing trees, hardwoods (such as mahogany, cedar, and rosewood), and indigenous plants like cassava, pineapple, and peanuts. Coca is cultivated on the eastern slopes of the Andes at altitudes between 5,000 and 6,000 feet. Additionally, the eastern Andes produce quina (calisaya) at altitudes from 3,000 to 9,000 feet, the bark of which is a significant export.
