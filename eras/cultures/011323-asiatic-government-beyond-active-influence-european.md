# Asiatic Government and European Influence

The relationship between Asia and Europe has been characterized by alternating periods of conquest, cultural exchange, and varying degrees of influence. While European power has asserted itself in middle and eastern Asia through improved communication, there remain regions within western Asiatic states that operate under Asiatic government and exist beyond the active influence of European interests.

## Historical Epochs of European Influence
European influence on the Asian continent has been particularly strong during two distinct periods. The first followed the conquests of Alexander the Great, which led to the establishment of Perso-Greek kingdoms. These entities hellenized their immediate areas and extended their influence into the religion and art of India and, to a lesser degree, China. Following this, a long interval occurred where eastern Europe was primarily occupied with combating invasions from Asia, leaving little opportunity to Europeanize the East. 

The Crusades later maintained communication with the Levant and established the power of the Roman Church, though this occurred to the detriment of oriental Christianity. During this time, intercourse with farther Asia was limited to the voyages of a few travelers. In the second major epoch, beginning in the 16th century, western Europe asserted power in middle and eastern Asia.

## Cultural and Religious Distinctions
There is a deep-seated difference in religious feelings between the two continents. Asia is the birthplace of the world's great religions, including Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam, as well as secondary religions such as Taoism, Parseeism, and Judaism. In contrast, European races, left to themselves, developed only unsystematic paganism; while Greek philosophy advanced significantly, it did not produce a popular religion.

Christianity, despite its Asiatic origin and essential ideas, took its current form on European soil. European reconstructions, such as the Roman Church, contain little of the original Asiatic element. Christianity has seen little success east of Asia Minor; while Nestorians and Jacobites received respectful hearings in India, China, and central Asia, they never achieved the success of Islam or Buddhism. Conversely, Buddhism has made little impression west of India, and Islam is viewed as repugnant by Europeans, who have refused to accept it even under Moslem rule in Turkey in higher proportions than Hindus did in similar circumstances.

## Governance and Material Progress
In regions under Asiatic government and beyond active European influence, material progress has appeared to remain stationary. However, geographical knowledge has increased. In Persia, scientific inquiry and geographical explorers have mapped the country, providing detailed information on the interior routes, passes, and the harbors of the Persian Gulf. This has highlighted possibilities for commercial development via trade roads linking India, the Persian Gulf, the Karun, and the Caspian via Seistan, positioning Persia as a factor in future Eastern politics.

While European influence has brought enormous changes—such as the establishment of peace in formerly disturbed areas like India and the construction of railways across nearly all parts of the continent—the mental constitution of Asiatics is considered less easily modified than their institutions. For example, Japan has assimilated European methods rather than European ideas.

## Intellectual and Physical Observations
Historical observations have noted differences between the two regions. Hippokrates contrasted the physical forms of Asiatics—described as rounded, full-grown, and voluptuous but inactive—with Europeans, whom he described as more compact, muscular, and vigorous. Similarly, Dio Chrysostom noted a Persian preference for eunuchs as slaves, suggesting a taste for feminine beauty in males, whereas Greeks associated male beauty with active power and graceful motion.

Intellectually, the period of Byzantine culture saw a shift in productive force. Nine-tenths of the greater Greek authors of the first eight centuries of the Christian era originated from Asiatic and African districts opened by Alexander the Great, outstripping the original European Greece. This literature and the subsequent Byzantine art were heavily influenced by the Oriental soil, though this was tempered by the spirit of Christianity and Greek tradition.

## Geographic and Scientific Constraints
The physical environment has often hindered the application of European scientific methods in Asia. In Asiatic Russia, the arid nature of trans-Caspian deserts prevented the use of first-class triangulation used in Europe. Consequently, Russian geography in central Asia relies on the Tashkent astronomical observatory as a starting point for second-class triangulation extending to the Pamir frontier beyond Osh.

## Internal Asiatic Dynamics
Apart from European conquests, the internal history of Asia over the last 2000 years has been shaped by four main influences: Indian, Chinese, Mahommedan, and Central Asian. The first three represent distinct types of civilization, while the Central Asian influence, though lacking originality, significantly affected political power and the distribution of races. Regarding antiquity, except for Assyria and Babylonia, little is known of the continent before 1500 B.C., a period when the Aryans were entering India and the Chinese were advancing along the Hwang-ho.
