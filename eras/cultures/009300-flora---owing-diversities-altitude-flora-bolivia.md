# Flora

The distribution and variety of flora across different global regions are heavily dependent on climatic conditions, altitude, and soil composition. This relationship is evident in diverse environments ranging from the high altitudes of the Andes and Carpathians to the plains of Asia and Europe.

## Flora of Bolivia
Due to diversities in altitude, the flora of Bolivia represents every climatic zone. This range extends from the luxuriant tropical forests of the Amazon basin to the scanty Arctic vegetation found in the lofty Cordilleras. Because these inland regions are inaccessible, the flora of Bolivia has been studied less than that of neighboring republics.

The vegetable life varies by region:
*   **Puna Region:** Characterized by coarse grasses, quinoa (*Chenopodium quinoa*), oca (*Oxalis tuberosa*), and the potato. Barley is also widely grown here for fodder, though it is an exotic species.
*   **Temperate and Warm Regions:** Indian corn was cultivated here before Europeans arrived. European introductions include rye, wheat, oats, pease, beans, and various Old World fruits and vegetables.
*   **Sub-tropical and Tropical Zones:** Indigenous plants include peanuts, pineapple, cassava (*Manihot utilissima* and *M. aipi*), sweet potato, pawpaw (*Carica papaya*), guava, chirimoya (*Anona cherimolia*), vanilla, sarsaparilla, *ipecacuanha* (*Cephaelis*), copaiba, tolu (*Myroxylon toluiferum*), cotton, false jalap (*Mirabilis jalapa*), and rubber-producing trees. This zone also features dyewoods and hardwoods such as cedar, mahogany, rosewood, walnut, jacaranda, colo, and quebracho. Common exotic fruits include the date, fig, grape, lime, lemon, and orange, while the avocado (alligator pear) and caju or cashew (*Anacardium occidentale*) have a disputed origin.

Specific high-value plants include Coca, cultivated on the eastern slopes of the Andes at 5,000 to 6,000 ft. where frosts are unknown and temperatures are uniform. Quina or calisaya is found in the eastern Andes between 3,000 and 9,000 ft. above sea level; the bark of these trees is a significant item in foreign trade, though natural sources are diminishing due to destructive collection methods.

## European and Mediterranean Flora
In the Carpathian mountains, vegetation is divided into four zones based on altitude: beech extends to 4,000 ft., followed by Scottish fir up to 5,000 ft., and then a species of pine that disappears around 6,000 ft. Beyond this is a zone of almost bare rock or moss and lichen. The Transylvanian Mountains and High Tatra possess flora similar to the middle region of the Alps. Transylvania also features remarkable sea-shore flora in its marshes, ponds, and salt-impregnated lakes.

In Bulgaria, the flora is divided into five distinct regions:
1.  **Northern Plain:** Vegetation resembles the Rumanian and Russian steppes, featuring tall grasses, umbelliferous growths, *astragali*, *dianthi*, and spring bulbous plants like the tulip, iris, orchis, and crocus.
2.  **Southern Plain:** Richer vegetation reminiscent of Asia Minor and the neighborhood of Constantinople.
3.  **Black Sea Districts:** Displays types of Mediterranean, Transcaucasian, and Crimean flora.
4.  **Elevated Basins:** Sofia and Samakov contain sub-alpine plants (where the vine disappears), while Kiustendil features vegetation of the Macedonian valleys.
5.  **Alpine and Sub-Alpine Regions:** The Balkans' flora corresponds with that of the Carpathians. The Rhodope and Rila group combine indigenous types with those of Asia Minor and the central European Alps. Some Alpine types appear as variants, such as *Gentiana bulgarica* (for *G. germanica*), *Primula frondosa* and *P. exigua* (for *P. farinosa*), and *Campanula orbelica* (for *C. alpina*). Some isolated types in the southern mountain group and Macedonia correspond with those of the Caucasus.

The Balearic Isles possess flora and fauna resembling the Mediterranean coasts of France or Spain, supported by a generally healthy and mild climate.

## Asian Flora
The flora of northern Asia is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, differing primarily in species rather than genera. Notable absences east of the Ural include all heaths and the oak. On mountains, the principal trees are birch, larch, and pines; on lower ground, poplars, alders, and willow prevail. In Siberia, the pine reaches a northern limit of approximately 70 deg. N.

A flora of essentially European character extends along the warm temperate zone from the Himalaya to the Mediterranean. While many European species reach the central Himalaya, they are few in the eastern parts. Conversely, Japanese and Chinese forms are most numerous in the eastern Himalaya and less frequent in the west.

## Historical and Prehistoric Flora
During the Eocene period in Northern Europe, the presence of laurels, chestnuts (*Castanea*), willows, and oaks indicates a temperate climate. In the Middle Eocene, the Isle of Wight and adjacent continental areas saw the flourish of cinnamon, figs, palms, ferns, cypress, sequoia, magnolia, and cactus. The late Eocene flora of Europe was very similar to modern Australasia. In the Cretaceous period, a mixed assemblage of mild-temperate trees and tropical palms developed.

The Mesozoic era was characterized by a "hothouse flora" that could not endure frosts, supporting a corresponding hothouse fauna.
