# The Creation and Structure of Bantu Languages

The creation of the Bantu type of language, characterized by its pronominal-prefix system, is an event that occurred significantly earlier than the exodus from the Bantu mother-land. The exact birthplace of the language and the specific group of dialects or languages from which it sprang remain unknown, presenting a puzzle similar to that of Aryan speech.

## Origins and Linguistic Affinities
There is speculation regarding the roots of the Bantu language. It is possible that ancient Nilotic, Hamite, or Libyan conquerors adopted a form of speech—such as Kposo, Kiama (Tern), Fula of northern Togoland, or languages from the Benue or lower Niger—and cast it into the Bantu type. Despite these possibilities, Bantu is a division of sexless Negro speech that shows no obvious traces of Caucasian (Hamitic) influence.

Certain affinities exist between Bantu and other languages, though they vary in nature:
*   **North Togoland, Gold Coast, Benue river, lower Niger, and Calabar:** These languages show a distinct affinity with Bantu in a few verbal roots and in the numerals 2, 3, 4, and sometimes 5.
*   **Fula:** This language shows some resemblance in grammatical construction, where suffixes replace prefixes. It possesses a form of concord similar to Bantu and shares affinities in the numerals 3 and 4, as well as a few verbal, pronominal, and nominal roots.
*   **Sierra Leone and north Guinea coast (Timne and cognate languages):** These use a system of concord and pronominal prefixes in a manner precisely similar to Bantu; however, there is absolutely no resemblance in word-roots, including verbs, pronouns, numerals, and substantives.
*   **Central Africa (Madi and possibly Nyam-Nyam/Makarka):** These groups show some affinity, though they lack any trace of the concord.

Additionally, some scholars have speculated on broader connections. Sir Richard Burton suggested a connection between Sumerian and Bantu in 1885, and Sir H. H. Johnston linked "class and prefix" languages, including the African Bantu, to a common late Neolithic and early bronze culture.

## The Pronominal-Prefix System and Concord
The defining feature of Bantu languages is the division of substantives into genders or classes, indicated by a pronominal particle prefixed to the root. These prefixes function in either a singular or plural sense. With the exception of the "abstract" prefix *Bu* (No. 14), singular prefixes cannot be used as plurals, and vice versa.

This system creates a "concord," where the pronominal prefix of the noun is attached to the roots of verbs, pronouns, prepositions, and adjectives within the sentence that connect to the governing noun. While these particles may evolve to differ in form from the substantive's prefix, they share a common origin. These particles must always precede the verbal, adjectival, nominal, and pronominal roots, though they may follow the roots of some prepositions and auxiliary prefix-participles used in verb conjugation.

## Prefix Classification and Evolution
There are approximately sixteen prefixes common to the entire Bantu family. Some may have resulted from the combination of a prefix indicating number or quality with a demonstrative pronoun.

*   **Archaic Forms:** The most archaic dialects are found in the Bemba country, the north coast of Tanganyika, Uganda, Unyoro, Ruwenzori, Mount Elgon, the vicinity of Lake Bangweulu, the western Zambezi (Subiya speech), and the north and north-east coasts of Lake Nyasa (Kese and Nkonde dialects). The Kavirondo-Masaba section is noted as being the most archaic in its prefixes.
*   **Specific Prefix Examples:**
    *   **Prefix No. 2 (*Ba-* or *Aba-*):** In the most archaic speech of Mt. Elgon, this is *Baba-*. It can degenerate into *Ma-*, *Va-*, *A-*, or *Wa-*.
    *   **Prefix No. 7 (*Ki-*) and No. 8:** No. 8 is invariably the plural of No. 7. The oldest form of No. 8 is *Ibi-* or *Mbibi-*, which varies across dialects into forms such as *Vi-*, *Zi-*, *Ri-*, or *By-*.
    *   **Prefix No. 10:** Originally *Ti-*, *Tin-*, *Zi-*, or *Zin-*. Even in languages like the Swahili of Zanzibar, where it has ceased to be a plural prefix for nouns (e.g., *Nombe* instead of *zinombe*), it persists in the concord (e.g., *Nombe hizi zangu*).
    *   **Prefix No. 11 (*-Lu-*):** Likely descended from *Ndu-*.
*   **Additional Prefixes:** Some western and north-western tongues (Cameroon and the northern Congo basin) use prefix No. 17 (*Fi-* or *Vi-*) for a diminutive singular sense. The Luganda and Masaba dialects, and possibly branches of Unyoro, use No. 18 (*Ogu-*) and No. 19 (*Aga-*) for augmentative senses. Prefix No. 20 (*Mu-*) acts as a preposition meaning "into" or "in" and lacks a complete concord.

## Linguistic Characteristics
Bantu languages do not recognize sexual gender in their concord or pronouns. Gender is instead indicated by specific prefixes or suffixes: a male prefix (often identical to the word for "father") and a feminine prefix (*na* or *nya*, connected to "mother") or suffix (*ka* or *kazi*, indicating "female" or "wife"). The first and second prefixes are usually restricted to humanity and living beings.

In verb construction, the root is the second person singular of the imperative. The negative verbal particle is traced back to the Bantu mother-tongue as *ka*, *ta*, *sa*, *ki*, *ti*, or *si*. In modern dialects, these have evolved into forms such as *ga*, *nga*, *ha*, *a*, or *hi*, and often coalesce with the pronominal particle.
