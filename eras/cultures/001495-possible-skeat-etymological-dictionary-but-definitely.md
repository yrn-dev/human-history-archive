# Walter William Skeat and the Etymological Dictionary

Walter William Skeat was a philologist and author of several works regarding the English language, including the *Principles of English Etymology* (published between 1887 and 1891) and the *Etymological Dictionary of the English Language* (with editions noted in 1898 and 1910). His work focuses on the derivation of English words, often providing theories that are compared with other authorities, such as the *New English Dictionary*.

## Etymological Theories and Disagreements

Skeat's findings frequently intersect with and sometimes diverge from those of other lexicographers. In the case of the word "bachelor," Skeat mentioned the possibility that the term derived from the Welsh word *bach*, meaning "little." However, this derivation was "definitely discarded" by the *New English Dictionary*.

Similarly, regarding the word "lumber," Skeat preferred the theory that the term's meaning as "useless discarded furniture or other rubbish" originated from "Lombard" (a term for a banker, money-changer, or pawnbroker), referring to the accumulation of unsaleable articles in pawnbrokers' shops. While Skeat believed the word might have been influenced by both this source and the sense of "lumber" as a loud rumbling noise or clumsy movement, the *New English Dictionary* preferred the latter derivation, despite admitting the probability of the association with "Lombard."

## Analysis of Technical and Common Terms

Skeat provided specific derivations for a variety of English terms across different categories:

*   **Maritime and Military Terms:** Regarding the words "fuze" (or "fuse") and "fusee" (in the sense of a match), Skeat argued in his 1898 *Etym. Dict.* that these forms derived through the French *fusil*, from the Late Latin *focile* (steel for striking fire from a flint), which in turn came from the Latin *focus* (hearth). This contrasts with the *New English Dictionary's* view that the word is an adaptation of the Latin *fusus* (spindle).
*   **Household Objects:** For the word "casket," Skeat suggested the term is a corruption of the French *cassette* (a diminutive of *casse*, meaning box), which comes from the Latin *capsa* (from *capere*, meaning to hold or contain).
*   **Natural History:** Skeat derived the word "kipper"—referring to the male salmon during the breeding season—from the Old English *kippian*, meaning "to spawn."
*   **Food and Drink:** In his 1910 *Etymological Dict. of English Language*, Skeat provided entries for "nunchion," a word appearing as early as the 14th century. The term literally means "noon-drink," combining *none* (the ninth hour or midday) and *schenchen* (to pour out).
