# THE HISTORIOGRAPHER'S ART IN OLD JAPAN

The historiographer's art in old Japan was a highly esteemed pursuit, particularly during the eighth and ninth centuries. The practice was characterized by the involvement of men of the highest social rank, including prime ministers, who served as the recorders of the nation's past.

## The Six National Histories
Central to the development of Japanese historiography is the *Nihon Shoki*. From its inception, this work held a position of high national esteem. Following its compilation, Empress Gensho established a tradition of summoning eminent scholars to the Court to deliver lectures on its contents. This practice was maintained by subsequent sovereigns and continues to be part of New Year ceremonials.

The *Nihon Shoki* served as the precursor to five other works, which together are honored as the Six National Histories. These associated compilations include:
*   **Zoku Nihongi (Supplementary Chronicles of Japan):** A forty-volume work finished in 798, covering the period from 697 to 791.
*   **Nihon Koki (Later Chronicles of Japan):** A forty-volume work (of which only ten survive) covering the period from 792 to 833.
*   **Zoku Nihon Koki (Supplementary Later Chronicles):** A twenty-volume work compiled in 869, covering the reign of Emperor Nimmyo (834–850).
*   **Montoku Jitsu-roku (True Annals of Montoku):** A ten-volume work compiled in 879, covering the reign of Montoku (851–858).
*   **Sandai Jitsu-roku (True Annals of Three Reigns):** A fifty-volume work compiled in 901, covering the period from 859 to 887.

Despite their value, these works are viewed with certain limitations by historians. The authors often unconsciously blended the past with the present, coloring their descriptions of ancient manners and customs with the influences of their own times, specifically the deep modifications brought about by Chinese influences on national institutions and thought.

## Chronological Challenges and External Verification
Authentic Japanese history, specifically regarding dates, is generally considered to begin in the fifth century. Prior to this, Japanese records often demand a level of credulity not found in neighboring histories. For instance, the first seventeen Emperors of Japan, said to have reigned from 660 B.C. to A.D. 399, had an average age of 109 years. This trend of extreme longevity ceased abruptly in the fifth century, with the next seventeen sovereigns averaging sixty-one and a half years. The year A.D. 461 marks the first date in accepted Japanese chronology confirmed by Korean authorities.

Comparison with Chinese and Korean annals reveals further discrepancies:
*   **The Reign of Empress Jingo:** Chinese annals confirm Japan was ruled by a renowned queen in the first half of the third century. However, Chinese historiographers date Empress Jingo's death to A.D. 247, while Japanese annalists date it to 269.
*   **The 120-Year Gap:** Korean history indicates that Japanese dates for certain cardinal incidents are erroneous by exactly 120 years. This is attributed to the dropping of two cycles in the sexagenary system of reckoning by recorders who relied solely on tradition.

Chinese annals are regarded as highly accurate due to a centuries-old habit of continuous written compilation. Similarly, Korean annals are seen as reliable, partly because the Korean peninsula was divided into three principalities whose records served as mutual checks.

## Linguistic and Conceptual Analysis
Japanese historiographers have also applied a detailed art of analysis to the language used in prehistoric records. An example is found in the records of the chieftains sent by Amaterasu. Historiographers distinguish between two specific terms regarding the land: *ushi-haku* and *shirasu*. 

*   **Ushi-haku** signifies "to hold in intimate lordship," likened to the wearing of a garment.
*   **Shirasu** means "to exercise public rights as head of a State."

While the Emperor occupied both positions toward confiscated estates and naturalized or conquered folks, his general functions regarding the land and people were limited to governing (*shirasu*).
