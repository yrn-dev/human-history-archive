# Bali

Bali is an island located in the East Indies, situated to the east of Java. The two islands are separated by the Bali Strait, which is shallow and reaches a minimum width of just over one mile at its narrowest point. Physically, Bali is associated with Java, sharing the same soil and climate, as well as mountains of proportionate height. The island measures 93 miles in length with a maximum breadth of 50 miles, covering a total area of 2,095 square miles.

## Geography and Geology
The geological formation of Bali consists of three distinct regions: alluvial plains, a southern peninsula of Tertiary limestone, and a central volcanic region. The central chain of mountains has an average altitude of 3,282 feet. The highest volcanoes on the island are Gunung Agung (Bali Beak) at 10,497 feet, Tabanan at 7,545 feet, and Batur at 7,383 feet. The island features several deep lakes and streams that the native population utilizes for irrigation.

In terms of fauna, Bali is associated with Java. However, the deep strait separating Bali from Lombok to the east was used by A. R. Wallace to establish "Wallace's Line," a demarcation between Asiatic and Australian fauna. This line is also noted in the distribution of freshwater fishes; while two species of Cyprinid fishes have been found in Bali, none are known from Lombok or islands further east.

## Political Administration
In 1882, Bali was separated from Java for administrative purposes and combined with Lombok to form the Dutch residency of Lombok and Bali. Politically, the island is divided into two categories:
1. Dutch territory, which includes the districts of Jembrana and Buleleng (the latter being the chief town).
2. Autonomous states, which include Bangli, Mengui, Tabanan, Badung, and Klung Lung.

As of 1905, the population of the entire residency on Dutch territory was 523,535.

## The Balinese People
The natives of Bali are of the same stock as the Javanese and resemble them in general appearance, though they are noted for exceeding the Javanese in muscular power, stature, activity, and enterprise. The Balinese language is part of the same Malayan class as Sundanese, Madurese, and Javanese, though it is as distinct from these as French is from Italian. It is most closely related to the Sasak language spoken on the east coast of Bali and in Lombok. While the vulgar dialect has remained free, the literary language incorporated many elements from Old Javanese as it was spoken during the 15th-century fall of Majapahit.

## Economy and Industry
Agriculture serves as the primary means of subsistence, with rice being a crop of particular importance. Other crops grown for export include indigo, cocoa, tobacco, and coffee. The natives are described as skilful artisans and agriculturists, particularly in the manufacture of arms and textile fabrics. Other significant industries include gold-working, wood-carving, the production of musical instruments, and weaving using gold thread, silk, and cotton. Tame bantin (wild ox) are also bred on the island and exported to Singapore.

Trade is supported by numerous Chinese and Arab traders. While the rivers are not navigable, the north coast provides a safe year-round roadstead at Temukus.

## Society and Religion
The Balinese society adheres to the Hindu system of four castes, with a fifth caste known as the *Chandala* (or Pariah). Slavery has been abolished. Although native rule is characterized as arbitrary and tyrannical—specifically in the principalities of Tabanan and Badung—trade and industry flourish due to a general security of property and persons. To counter the aggression of their rulers, the natives employ a remedy called *Metilas*, which involves a renunciation of allegiance and a general rising; this method is mostly successful. Justice is administered via a written criminal and civil code.

Hinduism remains in vogue on Bali and Lombok, though it was extinct in Java four centuries ago. This form of Hinduism is partly blended with Buddhism and a belief in *Kalas* (evil spirits). To appease these spirits, offerings are made directly or through *Devas* (agrarian or domestic deities). If these fail, the *Menyepi* (Great Sacrifice) is performed. During this ceremony, men carry torches in all directions, and women use fire-brands or knock on houses with heavy implements and rice-crushers to drive away evil spirits. The custom of widow-burning (*suttee*) is still practiced. Additionally, the Mahommedan religion is found among the population living on the coast.
