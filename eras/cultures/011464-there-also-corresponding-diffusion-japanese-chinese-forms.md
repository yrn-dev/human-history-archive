# Diffusion of Japanese and Chinese Forms

The diffusion of Japanese and Chinese forms manifests across various domains, including botany, linguistics, mythology, and historical record-keeping. These influences are characterized by a pattern of Japanese adaptation and borrowing from Chinese systems, often modified to suit local needs or conditions.

## Botanical Distribution
In the context of Asian flora, there is a specific distribution of Japanese and Chinese forms along the warm temperate zone, which extends from the Mediterranean to the Himalaya. These forms are most numerous in the eastern Himalaya and become less frequent as one moves toward the west.

## Linguistics and Writing Systems
The Japanese writing system is derived from the Chinese. It utilizes a system of forms that are written more rapidly than the Chinese original. This system incorporates a large number of Chinese ideograms used exactly as they are in China, alongside a Japanese syllabary used to express syllables, similar to the Sumerian syllabary.

The application of an ideographic script to phonetic purposes presented significant challenges because Chinese characters possess distinct sounds and meanings that do not align with Japanese words. For example, while the Chinese character *shan* means "mountain," the Japanese word is *yama*. To resolve this, an early Japanese scholar employed two methods: using ideographs solely to represent sounds to build Japanese words, or altering the sounds of Japanese words to match their Chinese equivalents.

While the Japanese language has come to embody a vast number of Chinese words, they are not pronounced as they are in Chinese. This borrowing was facilitated by the nature of the Chinese language, which consists of numerous monosyllables capable of combination and permutation to represent various shades of meaning—a capacity for expansion that the original form of the Japanese language lacked.

In the modern era, Japan has used this linguistic elasticity to create new vocabulary for Western arts and sciences using self-explaining forms. Examples include *tetsu-do* (railway), combining "iron" (*tetsu*) and "way" (*do*), and *kagaku* (chemistry), combining "changes" (*ka*) and "learning" (*gaku*).

## Mythology and Folklore
Japanese conceptions of the afterlife and legendary figures show clear parallels with Chinese lore. One Japanese conception of hades describes a place where people live and act as they do on earth, which corresponds exactly to a Chinese conception. 

Specific mythological elements also appear to be suggested by Chinese legend:
* **The Peach:** The use of peaches by Izanagi to repel the thunder Kami mirrors the Chinese legend of the female Si Wang-mu, who possessed a peach tree whose fruit repelled demons of disease and conferred immortality.
* **The Ocean Palace:** The tale of the ocean Kami's palace, featuring a castle gate and a cassia tree overhanging a mirror-like well, is found in Chinese legendary lore.
* **The Dragon King:** A sea monster in Japanese myth is written with a Chinese ideograph for "crocodile." However, as the Japanese had no knowledge of crocodiles and the creature is pictured as a dragon, it is identified as the Dragon King of Chinese and Korean folk-lore. The Japanese refer to this edifice as *Ryu no jo* (the Dragon's castle).

## History and Chronology
The habit of compiling written annals was practiced in China for many centuries before Japanese events were recorded. Consequently, Chinese annals are often used to verify Japanese and Korean history. 

Chinese records provide one of the few early confirmations of Japanese history, noting that a renowned queen ruled Japan during the first half of the third century of the Christian era, which aligns with Japanese accounts of Empress Jingo. However, discrepancies exist; Chinese historiographers date Jingo's death to A.D. 247, while Japanese annalists date it to 269. Furthermore, Korean history suggests that some Japanese dates for cardinal incidents are erroneous by exactly 120 years, which corresponds to two cycles in the sexagenary system of reckoning.

## Currency and Material Culture
By the 14th century, Japan utilized a paper currency that differed in form from the Chinese system. These notes consisted of strips of strong doubled paper featuring inscriptions in Chinese characters, seals in black and red, and representations of the tutelary god of riches, while the indication of value was written in ancient Japanese characters.
