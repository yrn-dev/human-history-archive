# The Rig Veda and the Dawn

## Linguistic and Geographic Context
The *Rig Veda* is an early piece of literature, roughly assigned to approximately 1000 B.C. At the time of its composition, the people responsible for it were settled in the valley of the Indus. These people were part of the Eastern branch of the Indo-Europeans, a group that referred to themselves by the term *arya-*, a word associated with dignity. Scholars use the compound term "Indo-Aryan" to describe this specific part of the Eastern branch that settled in India, distinguishing them from the Iranians (a term of the same origin) who remained in Persia and Bactria. 

The Indo-Aryans are not an indigenous people of India; it is concluded that they must have entered the region from the other side of the Hindu Kush, pushing through the Kabul valley into the open country of the Indus.

## The Composition of the Rig Veda
Professor E.W. Hopkins, in his 1901 work *India Old and New*, argues that the *Rig Veda* was composed in the district surrounding Umballa. To support this geographic placement, Hopkins points to the specific imagery used in the text. He argues that the authors must have been located to the west of the great rivers based on the way the dawn is addressed. In the text, the dawn is described as one who "in shining light, before the wind arises, comes gleaming over the waters, making good paths."

## Related Linguistic Connections
The vocabulary of the *Rig Veda* remains largely similar to that of related Eastern Indo-European languages. The connection is so close that entire sentences can be transliterated from one language to another through regular phonetic changes without needing to vary a single word.
