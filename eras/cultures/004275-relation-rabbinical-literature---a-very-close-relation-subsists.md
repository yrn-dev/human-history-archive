# Relation to Rabbinical Literature

## Parallels in Text and Content
A very close relation exists between the Book of the Secrets of Enoch and rabbinical literature. In certain instances, the parallels are so precise that they are nearly word for word. For example, the description of angels destroying Jerusalem found in sections vi.-viii. is also present in the *Pesikta Rabbati* 26. This specific passage has been used, as demonstrated by Ginzberg, to correct a corrupt reading in section vi. 7, changing "the holy Ephod" ([Hebrew: 'PWD HQWDSH]) to "the holy Ark" ([Hebrew: 'RWN HQWDSH]).

Furthermore, elements that may appear to be poetic fancies within the text are presented as historical facts in rabbinical literature. An example is found in section x. 18, where priests are told to take the keys of the sanctuary, cast them into the height of heaven, and tell the Lord to guard His own house because they were found to be unfaithful stewards. This account is mirrored in various descriptions of the fall of Jerusalem, including the *Aboth* of Rabbi Nathan vii., *Yalqu[t.] Shim'oni* on Is. xxi, *Ta'anith* 29a, and the *Pesikta Rabbati*. Additionally, the claim that the garments and armour of Sennacherib's soldiers remained unconsumed while their bodies were burned has a parallel in *Sanh.* 94a.

## Language and Provenance
Evidence suggests that a large portion of the book was originally written in Greek. This is inferred from the chronology of the Septuagint and the author's use of the Septuagint text of Deuteronomy xxxii. 35 in 1, 4, rather than the Hebrew text. A further indication is found in xxx. 13, where the name Adam is derived from the initial letters of the four substances—the East, the West, the North, and the South ([Greek: anatole, dusis, arktos, mesembria]). Such a derivation is impossible in Semitic languages and is found in other Greek writings and the Sibyllines iii. 24 sqq.

Despite this, some sections may be based on Hebrew originals. A Hebrew Book of Enoch is attributed to R. Ishmael ben Elisha, who lived between the close of the 1st century and the beginning of the 2nd century B.C. This work is heavily dependent upon, or very closely related to, the Book of the Secrets of Enoch. It remains undetermined whether Ishmael ben Elisha utilized the Greek form of the Book of the Secrets of Enoch or found portions of it in Hebrew. Additionally, rabbinical literature from the middle ages contains extensive quotations from a Book of Enoch, though the provenance of these quotations has not yet been determined.

## Scholars of Rabbinic Literature
Johannes Buxtorf (1564-1629), a German Hebrew and Rabbinic scholar, dedicated himself to the study of rabbinic literature and Hebrew from 1591 until his death. Based in Basel, Buxtorf was known for his zeal in these studies and frequently hosted learned Jews in his home to discuss difficulties in the texts. He was often consulted by Jews regarding their ceremonial law and earned the title "Master of the Rabbins." His preference for Jewish society occasionally caused conflict with city authorities due to strict laws against Jews, although his general relationship with the city of Basel remained friendly.
