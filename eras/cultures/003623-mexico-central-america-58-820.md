# Mexico and Central America

## Geography and Natural History
Mexico and Central America are situated within the Neotropical region, also known as Neogaea. This region encompasses all of Central America and South America, as well as the Galapagos, the Falkland islands, and the Antilles (West India islands) up to the Florida channel. In the north, the Neotropical region meets and inosculates with the Nearctic subregion in Mexico.

The region's flora includes various species of fuchsia. These include *F. fulgens* and *F. splendens* (both from Mexico), as well as *F. microphylla* from Central America. The fauna of the area is diverse, featuring the grison (*Galictis vittata*), a bluish-grey carnivorous mammal of the family *Mustelidae* that feeds on birds and small mammals. Other related species in the region include Allamand's grison (*G. allamandi*) and the tayra (*G. barbara*), the latter of which ranges from Mexico to Argentina and hunts in companies. Additionally, the coati (*Nasua narica*) is found in Mexico and Central America; these arboreal animals live in small troops of eight to twenty and feed on insects, eggs, young birds, and fruits. In Costa Rica, the species *Bassaricyon gabbi* is present.

## Early History and Colonization
The origins of pre-European civilizations in Mexico and Central America are a subject of ongoing inquiry. One theory suggests that semi-civilized inhabitants crossed from Asia via the Bering Straits and moved down the west coast.

European contact began in 1502 when Columbus landed at Cape Honduras, taking possession of the land for Spain. In 1524, Hernando Cortes ordered the establishment of the first settlement after hearing reports of populous and rich empires in the region. Cortes sent his lieutenant, Christobal de Olid, to found a Spanish colony. However, when Olid attempted to create an independent principality, Cortes marched across Guatemala and southern Mexico to regain control. In the spring of 1525, Cortes founded the city of Puerto Cortes and appointed a new governor.

By 1539, Honduras was incorporated into the captaincy-general of Guatemala. During this period, the province's mines were discovered to be the richest in the New World, leading to the growth of several large cities. This administrative system continued until 1821, when Honduras repudiated Spanish crown authority.

## Political History and Sovereignty
Following the independence of Central America between 1819 and 1822, various treaties defined the region's boundaries and sovereignty. Great Britain secured its position through a 1826 treaty with Mexico, as well as drafts with the United States of Central America (1831) and New Granada (1825). British sovereignty over the colony of Belize was further confirmed by the settlement of boundary disputes with Guatemala in 1859, though the Bay Islands were recognized as part of the republic of Honduras in that same year.

The region also experienced internal and external conflicts, including the invasion of William Walker and his fellow-adventurers from 1856 to 1860. In the northern and north-western marches of the British colony, rebel bands of Indians who had risen against Mexican rulers in 1849 conducted occasional attacks until they were finally subjugated in 1901.

## State Honors and Cartography
In terms of state honors, Nicaragua founded the American Order of San Juan (or Grey Town) in 1857, which consists of three classes. Mexico previously had orders such as the *Mexican Eagle* (1865) and *Our Lady of Guadalupe* (1853), but these have since been abolished.

For cartographical purposes, the British general staff published a map of Central America at a scale of 1:170,300. Additionally, American engineers conducted a survey between 1897 and 1898 for a continental railway intended to run along the west coast from Mexico to Chile.
