# Delagoa Bay

Delagoa Bay is a coastal region in south-eastern Africa, serving as a significant geographic and economic point of connection between the Indian Ocean and the interior of the continent, specifically the Transvaal.

## Geography and Hydrography
The bay is the terminus for several watercourses, most notably the Komati River. The Komati rises in the Ermelo district of the Transvaal at an elevation of approximately 5,000 feet. After a course of some 500 miles, it reaches the Indian Ocean at Delagoa Bay, entering the sea 15 miles north of Lourenço Marques. The river is navigable from its mouth—where depths range from 12 to 18 feet—up to the foot of the Lebombo.

The Komati River's path includes a junction with the Crocodile River (which rises as the Elands river in the Bergendal). The combined stream, also known as the Manhissa, passes through Komati Poort, a 626-foot-high cleft in the Lebombo. This point marks the frontier between Portuguese and British territory.

## Lourenço Marques
The town of Lourenço Marques is named after a Portuguese navigator who, along with Antonio Calderia, explored the lower courses of rivers emptying into Delagoa Bay, such as the Espirito Santo, in 1544. While the Portuguese established various forts and trading stations on the river's north bank over time, the existing town dates from approximately 1850, as previous settlements had been destroyed by natives.

By 1871, the town was described as a poor settlement with narrow streets, grass huts, decayed forts, and a 6-foot-high wall. Due to the growing importance of the Transvaal, the Portuguese government sent a commission in 1876 to plant blue gum trees, drain marshy land, and construct a church and hospital. Significant development did not occur until the end of the 19th century; until 1903, cargo operations required the use of lighters and tugs.

## Railway Development and Arbitration
The connection between the Transvaal and Delagoa Bay was a subject of prolonged effort and diplomatic conflict. Between 1873 and 1877, Thomas François Burgers, president of the Transvaal, unsuccessfully attempted to build a railway from Pretoria to the bay. In 1875, Burgers traveled to Europe to promote a scheme to link the Transvaal to the coast, eventually concluding a treaty with the Portuguese in December 1875.

In 1883, the Lisbon cabinet granted a concession to Colonel Edward McMurdo, an American citizen, to build a railway from Lourenço Marques to the Transvaal frontier. McMurdo formed the Delagoa Bay and East African Railway Company in 1887. The line opened on December 14, 1888, reaching the frontier at Komati Poort. However, a dispute arose regarding the extension of the line to Komati Poort; the Portuguese government demanded completion within eight months, a requirement deemed impossible. Consequently, Portugal seized the line on June 25, 1889, and cancelled the concession.

This seizure led to protests from the United States and Great Britain. In 1890, the matter was referred to three Swiss jurists for compensation. On March 29, 1900, the arbitration award ordered Portugal to pay a total sum including interest of £950,000, in addition to an initial £28,000 payment made in 1890.

## Regional Connectivity and Economy
Delagoa Bay serves as a rail hub for the interior. The town of Barberton in the Transvaal, founded in 1886 following the discovery of gold in the Kaap valley, is located 136 miles west-north-west of Delagoa Bay. Barberton is connected to the Lourenço Marques-Pretoria trunk railway via a 35-mile branch line that joins the main line at Kaapmuiden.

Further north, the seaport of Beira is located 488 miles north of Delagoa Bay at the mouth of the Pungwe river. Beira is connected by railway to Cape Town via Bulawayo, Salisbury, and Umtali.

## Linguistics of the Region
The languages spoken in the vicinity of Delagoa Bay and the surrounding Portuguese South-East Africa are categorized within the Bantu field. The Ronga (or Tonga) languages, found in Delagoa Bay, Gazaland, the Lower Limpopo valley, and patches of the North Transvaal (Shi-gwamba), are related to both the Zulu and Nyanja groups. This linguistic profile likely represents a mingling of the two influences, though the Zulu influence predominates.

The Nyanja group is more extensive, associated with the shores of Lake Nyasa, the Shire valley, and the banks of the Zambezi. Dialects such as Ci-shangane, Chopi, or Shi-lenge, found in the Inhambane and Beira coast-lands, have been significantly influenced by Ronga and Tebele (Zulu) dialects.
