# Physiography and Geography of Bolivia

## Topography and the Andean System
Approximately two-fifths of Bolivia's total area is located within the Andean cordilleras. These mountains cross the south-west corner of the country and project eastward toward the Brazilian highlands, forming a great obtuse angle. The system is divided into two primary parallel chains—the Cordillera Occidental and the Cordillera Oriental—with accompanying spurs and flanking ranges to the east.

The Cordillera Occidental serves as part of the boundary between Bolivia and the northern provinces of Chile, following the coast outline. This range forms the western rampart of the *alta-planicie*, or the great Bolivian tableland. This plateau extends from the Vilcanota knot in Peru in the north to the Serrania de Lipez on the Argentine frontier in the south. The tableland is approximately 500 meters long and 80 meters broad, covering about 40,000 square miles. 

The plateau is a lacustrine basin that once held an inland sea with an eastern outlet through the La Paz gorge. Its average elevation near Lake Titicaca is about 12,650 feet, descending roughly 1,000 feet toward the south. The northern region of the plateau is known as the *puna*, while the southern portion is the "desert of Lipez," which is part of the Puna de Atacama. The landscape varies from bleak and inhospitable in the north to barren and arid in the south, featuring saline depressions and peaks, the highest of which is Cerro de Tahua at 17,454 feet. To the west, the plateau is overlooked by snow-clad peaks, including Sajama (21,047 ft.), Huallatiri (21,654 ft.), Parinacota (20,918 ft.), and Pomarape (20,505 ft.).

North-east of Lake Titicaca lies the Nudo de Apolobamba, a knot of intersecting ridges containing some of South America's highest peaks.

## Geology
The geological composition of the Andes in Bolivia varies by range. The eastern ranges consist of Palaeozoic rocks with other intrusions, including granitic ones. These beds have yielded fossils from the Carboniferous, Devonian, Ordovician, and Cambrian ages. In the north-eastern ranges, the Carboniferous and Devonian are extensively developed, while Cambrian and Ordovician beds dominate the eastern Andes of southern Bolivia.

The Western Cordillera is composed primarily of Cretaceous and Jurassic beds, along with ashes and lavas from great volcanoes. The intervening plateau is covered by terrestrial and freshwater deposits, featuring ridges of Palaeozoic rock and gypsiferous marls and red sandstones. In eastern Bolivia, the Chiquitos hills are made of ancient sedimentary rocks of unknown age.

## Climate and Environmental Zones
Bolivia is located entirely within the torrid zone, meaning temperature variations are driven by prevailing winds, mountain barriers, and elevation. This creates a vertical arrangement of climatic zones:

*   **Yungas:** Lowlands and mountain valleys up to 5,000 feet. This zone is exceedingly humid with a tropical temperature (mean official estimate of 70° F) and no winter.
*   **Valle (or medio yungas):** Deep valleys between 5,000 and 9,500 feet. This sub-tropical region is described as a region of perpetual summer.
*   **Cabezera de valle:** The heads of deep valleys from 9,500 to 11,000 feet. This temperate zone has regular seasons.
*   **Puna:** The central plateau between 11,000 and 12,500 feet. It has a cold, dry air and only two seasons: winter and a cold autumn or summer. The mean temperature is officially estimated at 54° F.
*   **Puna brava:** From 12,500 feet up to the snow limit (approximately 17,500 feet). This is a bleak territory inhabited by miners and shepherds.
*   **Eternal Snow:** An Arctic zone located above the snow limit.

Rainfall is governed by the prevailing winds and mountain ranges. The Cordillera extracts moisture from easterly winds, leaving the plateaus with limited rainfall in the north and absolute barrenness in the south. East of the Cordillera Oriental, rain falls throughout the year, with the rainy season typically occurring from November to March.

## Hydrography and Drainage
Bolivia features two primary drainage basins. The larger is the Madeira basin; the Beni River, a tributary of the Madeira, rises near La Paz in the Cordilleras. For half its length, the Beni is a raging torrent fed by snow-clad peaks. Its principal affluent is the Madre de Dios, which rises in the eastern Cordilleras.

The south-east drainage basin is smaller and discharges into the Paraguay. Its only large Bolivian river is the Pilcomayo, which rises on the east slopes of the Cordillera Oriental and flows through the Bolivian Chaco. Other streams in this region include the Tarija (or Rio Grande), the Otuquis, and the Parapiti.

## Flora, Fauna, and Agriculture
The indigenous fauna includes monkeys, pumas, jaguars, tapirs, and the spectacled bear (*Ursus ornatus*) in the wooded Andean foothills. The colder plateau regions are inhabited by the chinchilla and the viscacha. In the tropical districts, alligators, boa-constrictors, and various snakes are common.

Agricultural production is varied. In the eastern tropical districts, rice and tobacco are grown. Sugar-cane is cultivated in eastern Bolivia, primarily for the manufacture of rum. Cacao is grown in the department of Beni and the Andean valleys of Cochabamba and La Paz. Coffee is produced in the warmer districts of the eastern Andes. Two significant indigenous products are quinoa, a staple food for natives, and coca, which is masticated by natives and exported for medicinal use.
