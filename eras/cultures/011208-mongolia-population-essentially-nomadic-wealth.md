# Mongolia: Geography, Population, and History

## Geography and Climate
Mongolia is characterized by a very severe climate marked by great extremes of heat and cold. The region suffers from significant drought, with rain falling rarely and in small quantities. Much of the surface consists of a hard stony desert, though areas of blown sand occur exceptionally. 

Due to these conditions, trees are hardly seen except in specific locations. Settled villages and towns are few and are primarily found along the slopes of higher mountains where rain falls more abundantly or where melting snow provides streams for irrigation. These specific environments are the only areas where cultivated lands are found.

## Population and Economy
The population of Mongolia is essentially nomadic. Their wealth is comprised of herds of horses, camels, sheep, and horned cattle. In the north-western corner of Mongolia, the district of Kobdo is peopled chiefly by Mongols, as well as Khotons, Uryankhes, Soyotes, and Kirghiz. In western Mongolia, the region is occupied by Turki tribes, who are described as being among the least civilized of human beings, characterized by extreme cruelty and barbarity.

Trade is a significant activity for the cattle-breeding nomads. The town of Kobdo, founded by the Chinese in 1731, serves as an important trade center between these nomads and Peking. The nomads export considerable numbers of sheep (approximately 1,000,000), as well as sheepskins and wool from camels and sheep, to China. In exchange, they import various small goods, brick tea, and Chinese cottons. Trade also exists with Russia, where cattle, wool, and furs are exchanged for iron, copper goods, leather, cotton, velveteen, and boxes. The development of this trade is currently hindered by the absence of a cart road to Biysk.

## Historical Interactions and Expansion
Mongolia has been a site of continuous pressure and expansion from the Han empire of north China. During this period, Chinese populations moved beyond the Great Wall, bringing farmers with ploughs and horses. These settlers enclosed winter pastures and ploughed up the grasslands. While Hunnish peoples responded by raiding and murdering these settlers, Chinese punitive expeditions eventually overwhelmed them. This forced the nomads into a choice: they could either shift in search of fresh summer pastures or settle down to the plough and become Chinese tax-payers. Those who chose the latter were absorbed into the Chinese population, while others drifted eastward or north-eastward through mountain passes into western Turkestan.

Later, the Manchu conquerors, who overthrew the Ming Dynasty in 1644, brought new energy to Chinese affairs. Their northern interests led to a further expansion of Chinese influence and civilization into Mongolia and Manchuria. By the middle of the eighteenth century, this expansion resulted in the Russians and Chinese coming into contact within Mongolia.

## Regional Context
Mongolia is situated in a broader Asian context between the Arctic Ocean to the north and the mountainous belts of Turkestan and Manchuria. To the north lies Siberia, where indigenous nomadic Mongols are described as being of a peaceful character, though in a backward state of civilization. To the south, the central plains of the Gobi are connected to Tibet via the northern slope of the Kuen-lun mountains.
