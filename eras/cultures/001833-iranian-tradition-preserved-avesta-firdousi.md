# The Iranian Tradition

The Iranian tradition is a complex body of myths, religious teachings, and linguistic heritage preserved primarily in the *Avesta* (and its *Zend* version) and in Firdousi's *Shahnama*. This tradition reflects the cultural evolution of the Iranian people, their religious shifts, and their ancestral connections to the broader Aryan group.

## Myth and History
The *Shahnama* and the *Avesta* localize many of their myths and heroes in eastern Iran. Over time, the tradition transformed ancient narratives of gods fighting a great snake into stories of Iranian kings battling the Turanians. While some modern authors have interpreted these accounts as evidence of a vast ancient Bactrian empire that defeated the Turanians, scholars view this historical interpretation as a late development. It is considered a reflex of the later Iranian empires founded by the Achaemenids and restored by the Sassanids.

The primary historical reality preserved in these traditions is the eternal feud between the Iranian peasants and the Turanian nomads, a conflict rooted in natural geographical conditions. Contrary to some theories, there was no great Bactrian empire in antiquity; instead, the region was ruled by petty local kings, including Vishtaspa, who served as the protector of Zoroaster.

## The Zoroastrian Faith
Zoroaster (Zarathustra), an Aryan, founded a religion whose teachings are preserved in the *Zend Avesta*. The central tenet of this faith is the opposition between two deities: Ormuzd, the god of light, truth, frankness, and the sun, and Ahriman, the god of darkness, night, secrecy, cunning, and diplomacy.

The religion is characterized by a sacerdotal and ceremonial system. It eschews the use of images but utilizes priests, temples, and altars where sacred fires are kept burning. Rituals include the burning of incense—specifically *vohu gaono* (likely frankincense)—five times a day. A distinctive funerary practice is the prohibition of burying or burning the dead; the Parsees, the last surviving Zoroastrians in India, continue the tradition of placing the deceased in open "Towers of Silence" for vultures.

## Cosmogony and Creation
The Iranian account of creation, as detailed in the *Bundahish* (the Parsee Genesis), describes a universe divided by spiritual opposition. In the beginning, a good spirit existed in omniscient, endless light, while an evil spirit existed in endless darkness with limited knowledge. Both created spiritual beings that remained separate for 3,000 years.

Following this, the evil spirit opposed the good creation under an agreement that his power would last only 9,000 years, with success limited to the middle 3,000 years. The good spirit used a sacred formula to confuse the evil spirit for another 3,000 years, during which time he created the archangels and the material world, including the stars, moon, and sun. The evil spirit and his demons then attacked six classes of creation: the sky, water, earth, plants, animals (represented by the primeval ox), and mankind (represented by the first man, Kayumarth or Gayomard).

## Social Structure and Caste
The *Zend Avesta* describes an ancient fourfold division of the Iranian inhabitants: priests, warriors, agriculturists, and artificers. There is also a sevenfold division corresponding to the seven amschespands (servants of Ormuzd), a tradition attributed to the golden age of Diemschid (or Jemshid).

The priestly class, known as the Magi, was further stratified into:
* **Herbeds**: Disciples.
* **Mobeds**: Masters.
* **Destur Mobeds**: Complete masters.

Only the Destur Mobeds were permitted to read the liturgies of Ormuzd, predict the future, and carry sacred objects such as the *costi* (girdle), *havan* (cup), and *barsom* (a bunch of twigs). Some scholars suggest that the division of followers into Atharvas, Rathaesvas, and Vastrya mirrors the superior Indian castes, and among the Parsis, the rule that only sons of priests can become priests remains in effect.

## Linguistic and Ancestral Origins
The Iranians belong to the Aryan group, a branch of the Indo-Europeans. Linguistically, the Iranian languages are distinguishable by specific phonetic changes, such as the confusion of original *e* and *o* with *a*, and the representation of the short schwa-sound by *i* (e.g., *pita* for "father"). While remarkably similar to Sanskrit, Iranian differs in its treatment of voiced aspirates and the use of *h* initially before vowels (e.g., Zend *hapta* compared to Sanskrit *sapta*).

The Aryans likely originated in southern Russia or Asia Minor, migrating east and dividing into the Hindus (who settled in India) and the Iranians (who remained in Persia and Bactria). The eastern part of Iran is believed to be the region where the Aryans lived as a single people before this separation.
