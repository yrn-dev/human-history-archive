# Islands of East and South-East Asia

The eastern and south-eastern regions of Asia are characterized by several significant groups of islands. Some of these island groups serve as geographical links between the Asian continent, Australia, and the islands of the Pacific.

## Outliers of Asia
Certain island groups are regarded as unquestionable outliers of the Asian continent. These include:
* The Japanese group
* The Kurile islands
* Formosa
* Luchu
* The Philippines

## The Malay Archipelago and Wallace's Line
The region extending from Sumatra to New Guinea comprises the islands of the Malay archipelago. Historically, old geographers did not establish a distinctly marked boundary for Asia in this quarter, and no definite relations appear to have ever existed between the neighboring Asiatic continent and these islands.

However, modern science has identified a physical line of separation known as Wallace's line, named after the naturalist A.R. Wallace. This boundary follows the Straits of Macassar—the channel between Celebes and Borneo—approximately along 120 deg. E. This line serves as a biological marker: to the west of the line, the fauna and flora are essentially Asiatic in type, whereas the Australian element becomes distinctly marked and eventually predominant to the south and east.

## Islands of the Bay of Bengal and Indian Ocean
In the Bay of Bengal, the Andaman and Nicobar groups are noted. These islands are positioned on a line that joins Cape Negrais (the south-western extremity of Burma) and the north end of Sumatra. It is possible that these groups owe their existence to volcanic agencies that extend from Sumatra across this portion of the Indian Ocean.

Additionally, the Maldives and Laccadives are groups of small coral islands. They are situated along the 73rd meridian and are located a short distance from the Indian peninsula, upon which they maintain a political dependency.

## Regional Biology
The biological characteristics of southern Asia, including China, Siam, the Malayan Islands, and India south of the Himalaya, are reflected in its insect populations. The insects of these regions belong to a single group, with species and genera often remaining the same on opposite sides of the Bay of Bengal. 

There is a marked connection between India and Africa, evidenced by many genera that are common to both and confined to those two regions. In terms of broader distribution, Indo-Malayan types are generally met south of 30 deg. N., while Japan belongs to the Europeo-Asiatic group.
