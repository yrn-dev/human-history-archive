# Elizabethan Eulogies and Related Records

The study of Elizabethan literature and history often involves the analysis of eulogies and the evaluation of available records to determine the veracity of historical claims. In the Elizabethan era, eulogies appeared in various forms, ranging from poetic tributes in literary works to retrospective accounts of historical figures.

## Literary Eulogies and Poetic Tributes
In the realm of Elizabethan poetry, eulogies were frequently integrated into larger collections of verse. For example, the poet Barnabe Barnes (1569?–1609), the fourth son of Dr. Richard Barnes, Bishop of Durham, authored the work *Offices*. This prose treatise, dedicated to King James in 1606, featured prefixed eulogies, one of which included a sonnet contributed by William Percy, a "dearest friend" of Barnes.

Other Elizabethan writers received posthumous eulogies from later critics. The poet Daniel, whose works include *The Complaint of Rosamond* and *The Epistles to Distinguished Persons*, was long neglected despite the eulogies of the best critics. In later periods, figures such as Charles Lamb and Coleridge provided genial criticisms of his work, particularly praising his dramatic writing, such as the masque *Hymen's Triumph*, and his elegiac verse, specifically his address *To the Angel Spirit of the Most Excellent Sir Philip Sidney*.

## Historical Records and the Case of Anne Boleyn
The availability and nature of records from the Elizabethan and preceding Tudor periods significantly impact the ability of historians to prove or disprove guilt in legal matters. A prominent example is the case of Anne Boleyn. The archive notes that her guilt remains unproved due to several factors, including the absence of the records upon which her sentence was pronounced and the existence of later Elizabethan eulogies and vituperations from adherents of the papacy.

The evidence against Anne Boleyn was characterized by inconsistencies:
* **Confessions:** While Smeaton made a full confession—possibly under threats of torture or hopes of pardon—and Norris initially confessed before claiming he was betrayed, other accused lovers "confessed in a manner" on the scaffold. These general confessions are viewed as declarations of submission to the king's will and general repentance rather than admissions of specific crimes.
* **Witnesses:** A principal witness for the charge of incest was the wife of Rochford, a woman of "infamous character" who was later executed for her role in the intrigues of Catherine Howard.
* **Defense:** Anne Boleyn protested her entire innocence to Sir William Kingston and made no confession on the scaffold, though she did express submission and praised King Henry as a "gentle and sovereign lord."

Despite these gaps in the record, some argue her guilt is probable because two grand juries, a petty jury, and a tribunal of nearly all the lay peers of England unanimously passed a sentence of guilt—a sentence supported by her own father and uncle.

## Comparison with Other Historical Eulogies
The use of eulogy as a tool for characterization is also seen in non-Elizabethan contexts within the archive. In ancient Athens, the leader Thrasybulus received a "marked and unaffected eulogy" from the philo-Laconian and oligarchical Xenophon, highlighting Thrasybulus's patriotism and lack of vindictive antipathies. Conversely, the Spartan leader Lysander received eulogies that reached the level of worship; altars were erected to him, hymns were composed, and the Samians renamed their Heræa festival to *Lysandria*.
