# Book-Collecting

Book-collecting is the process of bringing together books that possess an element of permanent interest due to their form, their contents, or the history of the individual copy. For a book to be collected, it must be rare—meaning it is difficult to procure—either actually or prospectively. While rarity is a necessary condition, it is subordinate to interest; a book that is rare but devoid of interest is of no concern to the collector. Conversely, if a book is perceived as being easily procurable at any moment, there is no reason to collect it. This anticipation of availability often prevents the collection of certain items, resulting in some objects, such as Horn-books, becoming much rarer than First Folio Shakespeares. It has been suggested that the ultimate rarity of books varies in an inverse ratio to the number of copies originally printed.

## Nature and Evolution of the Practice
In its essence, the pastime of the book-collector is identical to the official duties of a librarian of a respectable age or the work of a museum curator. Every library begins as a literary workshop. As a library expands, the editions and books used by one generation eventually pass out of use, turning the library into a storehouse of "dead" stock. Critics and antiquaries then salvage books from this stock that remain "alive," creating a literary museum of items whose interest cannot be fully reproduced by a mere reprint.

Historically, book-collecting often emerged as an added grace to the formation of a fine library rather than a separate pursuit. During the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries, large book-buyers typically collected with a public object in mind. Many bequeathed their treasures to public institutions:
* **Sir Thomas Smith** left his books to Queens' College, Cambridge.
* **Archbishop Parker** left his to Corpus Christi College, Cambridge.
* **Sir Thomas Bodley** refounded the Oxford university library, which also received contributions from Laud and Selden.
* **Archbishop Williams** left his library to St John's College, Cambridge.
* **Archbishop Usher's** library was acquired for Trinity College, Dublin.
* **Pepys** left his library to Magdalene College, Cambridge.
* **Thomas Howard, earl of Norfolk**, had his mathematical and scientific books given to the Royal Society.
* **Ralph Sheldon** left heraldic collections to Heralds' College.

Other significant contributions include those of Bishop Moore, whose Caxton quartos were presented to the university library at Cambridge by George I, and Archbishop Marsh, who founded a library at Dublin. The trend toward public ownership culminated in the foundation of the British Museum in 1753, following the offer of the collections of Sir Hans Sloane (which included approximately 50,000 printed books) and the need to preserve the Cotton manuscripts.

## Methods and Theories of Collecting
Book-collecting offers various grades of difficulty, making it a popular hobby. During the reign of George III, some collectors focused on specific early English presses; for example, James West possessed 34 Caxtons, while John Ratcliffe possessed 48. 

In more recent times, the "cabinet theory" of collecting became popular in the United States, England, and France. This approach was exemplified by Henry Perkins (d. 1855), whose collection of 865 specimens of early printing and fine manuscripts sold for nearly £26,000 in 1870. Unlike the miscellaneous libraries of the past, which required large galleries to house every class of book, the cabinet theory emphasizes unity and compactness. These collectors aim for small collections that epitomize the owner's tastes and can be housed in a neat bookcase or cabinet. In France, this movement was characterized by a group of amateurs who prided themselves on their compact treasures and distinguished themselves from their predecessors, whom they called the *bibliophile de la vieille roche*.

## Bibliographical Literature and Tools
The tools used by collectors have shifted as ideals have changed. Old-fashioned collectors relied on general manuals such as Brunet's *Manuel du libraire* (first published 1810) and W.T. Lowndes' *Bibliographer's Manual of English Literature* (first published 1834). J.G.T. Graesse's *Tresor de livres rares et precieux* (1859-1867) provided additional information, though it added little to Brunet's work. 

The era of the "omnivorous collector" has passed, and these general manuals have been replaced by more detailed handbooks and bibliographies focusing on special books. Despite their potential utility to booksellers and librarians, no publisher has produced new editions of the works by Lowndes or Brunet.

## Related Arts: Book-Plates and Ornamentation
The art of the book-plate has evolved from the routine skill of heraldic stationers into a recognized minor branch of higher art. Modern designs have shifted from purely heraldic elements toward symbolic or allegorical themes. The popularity of book-plates has increased due to process work, which has cheapened the reproduction of elaborate designs. Notable English artists in this field include C.W. Sherborn, G.W. Eve, and Robert Anning Bell, while American artists include J.W. Spenceley, K.W.F. Hopson, and E.D. French.

Additionally, the history of book production includes specialized ornamentation, such as the Celtic style found in Irish manuscripts as early as the 7th century. This school utilized interlaced ribbons, zoomorphic creatures, spirals, and intricate knots painted in thick pigments to create an enamel-like appearance, notably avoiding the use of gold. The *Book of Kells*, preserved in Trinity College, Dublin, is cited as the finest example of this minute and accurate workmanship.
