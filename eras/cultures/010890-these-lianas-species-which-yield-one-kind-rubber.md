# Rubber-Producing Plants and Lianas

Rubber is harvested from various forest plants across different global regions, ranging from immense lianas and vines to specific forest trees. These plants are significant both for their contribution to the physical appearance of their environments and their commercial value.

## Lianas and Vines in Africa
In Ashanti, a great part of the region is covered by primeval forest that is almost impenetrable. This forest is characterized by lianas, also known as monkey-ropes, which hang from tree to tree in immense festoons. These lianas are largely responsible for the "weird aspect" of the forest, and certain species among them yield one kind of the rubber used in commerce.

Other African regions also possess rubber-producing vines. In the districts bordering Victoria Nyanza, two varieties of valuable rubber-vine are found in the forests and near the coast: *Landolphia florida* and *Landolphia Kirkii*. Additionally, various kinds of rubber vine are found in Bagirmi, a country in north-central Africa located southeast of Lake Chad. In the dense jungles of the south in the eastern Sudan, immense creepers are present, some of which are rubber-vines.

## Rubber Production in South America
In Bolivia, the exploitation of forest products provides the greatest immediate profit for the country, with rubber-collecting being the most prominent and profitable industry. This industry began between 1880 and 1890. Just before Bolivia's best rubber forests were transferred to Brazil in 1903, the registered annual output reached nearly 3,500 metric tons. 

The rubber-producing species *Hevea* can still be found in extensive forest areas on the Madre de Dios and the Beni.
