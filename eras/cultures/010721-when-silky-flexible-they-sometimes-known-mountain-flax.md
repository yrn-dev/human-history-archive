# Silky and Flexible Materials in Nature

The term "silky" is used across various scientific and commercial classifications to describe the texture, lustre, and flexibility of minerals, animal furs, and biological coats. These characteristics often determine the material's value, its application in the arts, or its classification within a species.

## Mineral Fibres and Lustre
In the study of minerals, a silky appearance is frequently associated with fibrous structures. Asbestos, for instance, varies greatly in character depending on the variety. When asbestos fibres are flexible and silky, they are sometimes referred to as "mountain flax." Finer kinds of this mineral are termed amianthus. While amphibole-asbestos (such as tremolite or anthophyllite) is typically grey or white and can reach lengths of 5 to 6 feet, it is often brittle and harsh. In contrast, serpentine-asbestos (chrysotile) occurs in narrow veins and produces shorter fibres of 2 to 3 inches. These serpentine fibres are characterized by a delicate silky lustre, elasticity, and flexibility, typically appearing in greenish or yellowish hues.

Similarly, certain varieties of calcite are distinguished by their state of aggregation. A finely fibrous form of calcite is known as satin-spar. A typical example of this is a snow-white material, sometimes possessing a rosy tinge and a pronounced silky lustre, found in the Carboniferous shales of Alston Moor in Cumberland.

## Animal Furs and Pelts
In the fur trade, the presence of silky top hair is a primary indicator of quality and value.

### Martens and Sables
The Russian sable, a species of marten, is highly esteemed by connoisseurs specifically because its fur is much more silky than that of European or American martens. Its top hair is described as fine, silky, and flowing, ranging from 1-1/2 to 2-1/2 inches in depth. The most valuable sables are the darkest varieties from Yakutsk in Siberia, particularly those with evenly distributed silvery hairs.

Other members of the marten species vary in texture. The Baum marten possesses a thick underwool with strong top hair. The Japanese marten, by contrast, is woolly with coarse top hair and lacks a silky, bright, and fresh appearance, making it less desirable except for economical purposes. The fitch (or pole cat) has a more open growth of hair than the marten; however, Russian fitch is more silky than other varieties and is used as a cheap substitute for sable.

### Other Furs
Among foxes, the Blue fox from Archangel is noted for being more silky and having a smoky bluish colour, which increases its value. In the case of rodents, the Russian musquash is characterized by a silvery-blue shade of even wool with very little silky top hair. The nutria, a rodent from northern South America, is described as having a woolly nature, which makes it less effective than the more silky musquash when dyed as sealskin.

## Biological Coats and Plant Fibres
The term silky is also applied to the coats of specific dog breeds. The Cocker spaniel possesses a silky and wavy coat. Similarly, Irish water spaniels are noted for having silky, oily coats.

Regarding plant fibres, flax (*Linum*) is a plant with bark full of strings and fibres used to produce fine linen. In ancient Egypt, the manufacture of this linen reached such perfection that the resulting threads were almost too small for the sharpest eye to observe. This industry was a significant branch of Egyptian trade, employing many workers, particularly women. Historical records indicate that a plague of hail in March, called down by Moses, destroyed all the flax that was bolled at the time.
