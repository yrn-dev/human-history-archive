# Introduction of the Domestic Fowl to Africa

## Arrival via Egypt
The domestic fowl first entered the African continent through Egypt. This introduction occurred during the period of Persian occupation, specifically between 500 and 400 B.C. Following its arrival in Egypt, the bird did not immediately reach the interior of the continent; it is estimated that it took at least two hundred years for the domestic fowl to spread from people to people and tribe to tribe up the Nile valley before reaching the equatorial regions of Africa.

## Linguistic Evidence and the Bantu Migration
The timing of the fowl's arrival serves as a critical marker for reconstructing the history of the Bantu people, particularly in the absence of historical records. Researchers have utilized linguistic evidence—specifically the study of existing dialects and common roots—to determine the date of the great southern exodus of the Bantu from their primal home in the heart of Africa.

A primary piece of evidence is the root word for "domestic fowl," which is *kuku* (appearing in variations such as *nkuku*, *ngoko*, *nsusu*, *nguku*, and *nku*). This root is found throughout Bantu Africa, with rare exceptions, and is notably absent among non-Bantu Negro races living in close proximity to Bantu speakers. Because the root *kuku* is so widely established among the purest Bantu tribes, it is concluded that the domestic fowl was already well established among the Bantu people before they left their original home. Based on this linguistic evidence and the known date of the fowl's arrival in Africa, the approximate date for the Bantu exodus is fixed at not much more than 2,000 years ago.

## Etymology and Comparisons
The root *kuku* bears a marked resemblance to several Persian words for "fowl," and it is believed to be remotely derived from the actual cry of the bird. 

The rate of spread of the domestic fowl provides a historical parallel to more recent biological introductions in Africa. For example, the Muscovy duck was introduced to Negro Africa by the Portuguese from Brazil at the beginning of the 17th century and has spread across the continent at a similar rate to that of the domestic fowl.
