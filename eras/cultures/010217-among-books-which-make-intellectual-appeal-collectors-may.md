# Intellectual and Imaginative Appeals in Book Collecting

Book collecting is defined as the gathering of books that possess an element of permanent interest in their contents, form, or the history of the individual copy, and which are rare—meaning they are difficult to procure. While rarity is a significant factor in the popular view of the hobby, it is subordinate to interest; a book that is rare but devoid of interest is of no concern to the collector.

## Intellectual Appeals
Books that make an intellectual appeal to collectors generally include works of historical value. This category encompasses works that have not been reprinted, or those where the original editions are more convincing or authentic than modern reprints. Because these works cover a vast field, the collector often acts as both a rival and a servant to historical students.

Examples of collecting for the promotion of historical research include:
*   Lord Crawford's extensive collections of papal bulls and proclamations from Ireland, Scotland, and England.
*   The philological library assembled by Prince Lucien Bonaparte.
*   The Foxwell collection of early books on political economy, which was presented to the University of London by the Goldsmiths' Company.

While libraries of societies and institutes connected to special studies and professions now conduct much of this collecting, there remains ample room for private collectors.

## Imaginative Appeals
Books that appeal to a collector's imagination are often those associated with famous events or persons. This includes books that belonged to great poets, soldiers, statesmen, or royalty—such as Mary, Queen of Scots—particularly if the book bears a mark of being valued by the owner or is connected to a striking incident in their life. 

Collectors also value the "pedigree" of a book, favoring copies that can be traced through a long series of book-loving owners. This interest has led to the publication of works such as *English Book-Collectors* by W.Y. Fletcher (1902), *Great Book-Collectors* by Charles and Mary Elton (1893), and Guigard's *Nouvel armorial du bibliophile* (1890). Other imaginative interests include books that caused the persecution of their authors or were condemned to be burnt, though this specific interest has appealed more to writers than to collectors.

## The Value of First Editions
The desire for first editions is driven by a combination of factors: their positive superiority for study purposes and the associations—either proven or imagined—that they possess. However, the value placed on some first editions can be fanciful. For example, the high prices paid during the reign of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics are difficult to justify, as they are of little value as texts and lack associations with the authors' personalities. Today, such books are primarily collected as specimens of printing. Conversely, the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces has increased significantly, as evidenced by the sale of a first folio Shakespeare (1623) in 1906.

## Aesthetic Appeals
A book may attract a collector through its visual beauty, which can be derived from its illustrations, its binding, or the harmony of its paper and print. In France, high prices for French first editions and Elzevirs are often attributed more to their 18th and 17th century jackets than the books themselves. 

In England, the appreciation for old bindings grew following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club. Collectors have since more actively sought out embroidered bindings, English blind stamped bindings, and those attributable to Samuel Mearne during the time of Charles II.

## Evolution of Collecting Methods
Modern book collecting is a relatively recent growth. Earlier works, such as the *Philobiblon* (1345) attributed to Richard de Bury, focused on the delights of literature rather than collecting. Early owners, such as Jean Grolier (1479-1565), bought books to encourage printers or provide friends with Renaissance scholarship rather than out of antiquarian instinct. Similarly, figures like Archbishop Cranmer and the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou collected the best books of their day and housed them in handsome leather jackets featuring heraldic badges and coats of arms.

The "omnivorous collector" of the past, who relied on general manuals like Brunet's *Manuel du libraire* (1810) or W.T. Lowndes' *Bibliographer's Manual of English Literature* (1834), has been replaced by collectors using detailed handbooks on special books. A "cabinet theory" of collecting, popularized by Henry Perkins (d. 1855), emphasizes small, compact collections that epitomize the owner's tastes and fit within a neat cabinet or bookcase, rather than miscellaneous libraries requiring large galleries.
