# East Africa

East Africa is a region characterized by a diverse population and a complex colonial geography. According to archival records, the total population of East Africa is estimated at 7,425,000. This total is comprised of 3,200,000 people in Uganda, 200,000 in Zanzibar, and 4,000,000 in the Protectorate, the latter of which includes an estimated 25,000 Asiatics.

## Topography and Boundaries
The Protectorate in East Africa covers an area of approximately 240,000 square miles. Its eastern boundary is the Indian Ocean, featuring a coastline of about 400 miles. To the north, it is bounded by Italian Somaliland and Abyssinia, and to the west by Uganda. The southern frontier is coterminous with the northern frontier of German East Africa; this boundary runs north-west from the mouth of the Umba river (4° 40' S.) to Victoria Nyanza (1° S.), though it deviates to ensure that Mount Kilimanjaro remains entirely within German territory.

## Demographics and Racial Composition
The population of the East African Protectorate is estimated between 2,000,000 and 4,000,000. This includes approximately 25,000 Indians and 3,000 Europeans, many of whom are Boer families and emigrants from South Africa.

Broadly, the aboriginal nations across Africa are mostly of the black negroid type. On the east coast, however, higher types are found that approach the Christian races of Abyssinia. There has been a wide admixture of Arab blood from east to west, resulting in a light-brown type. In Uganda specifically, a large proportion of the population is Arab and relatively light-skinned.

## Linguistic and Historical Theories
Historical linguistic analysis suggests that a Hottentot-like language with Bushman affinities is still spoken in equatorial east Africa. This has led to the theory that the entirety of east Africa may have once been Hamitic-speaking. It is noted that Bantu-speaking peoples and languages spread from a center of origin in west central Africa in comparatively recent times, which served to cut off the Hottentots from other Hamitic peoples.

## Major Settlements and Ports
The region contains several significant seaports, particularly within German East Africa:

**Dar-es-Salaam**
Known as "The harbour of peace," Dar-es-Salaam is the capital of German East Africa, located at 6 deg. 50' S. 39 deg. 20' E. As of 1909, its estimated population was 24,000, including 500 Europeans. The town is European in character with wide, regularly laid out streets. Its infrastructure includes a floating dock completed in 1902, government and customs offices, a state hospital, a post office, the Boma (barracks), and the governor's house. The city also houses botanical gardens for testing the acclimatization of European plants, various churches, mission schools, and the head offices of the Deutsch-Ostafrikanische Gesellschaft, the largest trading company in German East Africa.

**Bagamoyo**
Located at 6 deg. 22' S. 38 deg. 55' E., Bagamoyo is a seaport with a population of about 18,000, which includes a considerable number of British Indians. It is situated 26 miles from the town of Zanzibar and serves as an entrepot of trade with the interior and a starting point for caravans traveling to the great lakes. Unlike Dar-es-Salaam, Bagamoyo has no natural harbour, and ships must anchor approximately 2 miles off the coast. The town is oriental in character and contains a mosque, a Hindu temple, a government school for natives, and the establishment of the Mission du Sacre Coeur, which maintains a large coco-nut palm plantation. Bagamoyo was the starting point for journeys into the interior by explorers such as J. A. Grant, J. H. Speke, Sir H. M. Stanley, and Sir Richard Burton.
