# Ash Wednesday

Ash Wednesday is the first day of Lent in the Western Church. The day is characterized by the ceremonial use of ashes as a symbol of penitence. In the Roman Catholic Church, the day is referred to as *dies cinerum* (day of ashes) or *dies cineris et cilicii* (day of ash and sackcloth).

## Origins and History
The custom of using ashes is ultimately based on the penance of "sackcloth and ashes" mentioned by the prophets of the Old Testament. Historically, the ceremony is derived from the early Church's practice of public penance, where a sinner seeking reconciliation was required to appear before the congregation covered in ashes and clad in sackcloth.

The exact date when this practice was extended to the entire congregation is unknown. However, the term *dies cinerum* is found in the earliest extant copies of the Gregorian Sacramentary, suggesting the custom was established by the 8th century. Aelfric, an Anglo-Saxon homilist, noted its common use in his *Lives of the Saints* (written in 996 or 997). The first evidence of an authoritative prescription for the day is a decree from the synod of Beneventum in 1091.

## Roman Catholic Ceremonial
In the Roman Catholic Church, the ashes used in the service are obtained by burning the palms (or their substitutes) that were used during the previous Palm Sunday. These ashes are placed in a vessel on the altar before High Mass. 

The priest, wearing a violet cope, performs the following rites:
* He prays for God to send His angel to hallow the ash, intending for it to become a *remedium salubre* for all penitents.
* The ashes are censed three times and sprinkled three times with holy water.
* The priest invites those present to kneel. He dips his thumb into the ashes and marks the forehead of the faithful (or the place of tonsure for clerics) with the sign of the cross.

During this marking, the priest says: *Memento, homo, quid pulvis es et in pulverem reverteris* ("Remember, man, that thou art dust and unto dust thou shall return"). The celebrant either receives the ash from the highest-ranking priest present or sprinkles it on his own head in silence.

## Observances in Reformed Churches
The use of ashes was dropped by reformed Churches that continue to observe the fast. In England, the ceremonial of ashes was not proscribed during the Reformation; it was specifically enjoined by proclamations from Henry VIII in 1538 and Edward VI in 1550. Despite this, the practice fell into complete disuse by the start of the 17th century.

Among reformed Churches, only the Anglican Church marks the day with a special service, known as the Commination service. This service is characterized by the solemn reading of "the general sentences of God's cursing against sinners," which are gathered from other places of Scripture and the twenty-seventh chapter of Deuteronomy. 

In the American Prayer Book, the office of Commination is omitted, though it retains three concluding prayers. These prayers are derived from the anthems and prayers used during the distribution and blessing of ashes according to the Sarum Missal. The lections for Ash Wednesday in the Anglican Church are the same as those in the Roman Church: Matthew vi. 16, &c., and Joel ii. 12, &c.

## Liturgical Calendar
Ash Wednesday occupies a specific place in the church calendar relative to Easter. It occurs 46 days before Easter. In the broader sequence of church feasts, it follows Septuagesima Sunday (which is 9 weeks before Easter) and precedes Rogation Sunday (which is 5 weeks before Easter).
