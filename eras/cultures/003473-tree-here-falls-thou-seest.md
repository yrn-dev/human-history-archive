# The Concept of the Tree and Visual Observation

The archive contains diverse references to trees and the act of seeing, ranging from linguistic reconstructions of Bantu languages to sacred parables and historical accounts. These entries illustrate the use of the tree as a symbol of stability, a metaphor for human ruin, and a subject of grammatical study.

## Linguistic Analysis of the Bantu Mother-Tongue
In the study of the original Bantu mother-tongue, the "concord" is used to explain the repetition of pronominal particles. This is demonstrated through the reconstruction of sentences to show how these particles function across different dialects. 

An example of this is found in a sentence describing a falling tree. In Old Bantu, the phrase is rendered as: "*Ngu-mu-ti ng_uno ngu-gwa ku-ngu-mbona*," which translates to "This tree this here this falls; thou this seest?" When this is rendered into the Kiguha dialect of North-West Tanganyika, it becomes: "*U_m_u_ti gu_no gu_gwa u_gu_mona?*" (The tree falls; dost thou see it?).

## The Tree as a Divine Parable
In the sacred texts of the Qur'an, the tree is utilized as a similitude to convey spiritual truths. One parable describes a "goodly word" as being like a "goodly tree." This tree is characterized by having a root that is firmly fixed and branches that reach into the heavens, belonging to its Lord. This imagery is presented so that men may receive admonition.

Furthermore, the texts describe a universal state of worship, noting that all things in the heavens and on earth bow down to Allah. This includes the sun, the moon, the stars, the hills, the animals, and the trees, along with a great number of mankind.

## Imagery of Ruin and Destruction
Conversely, the archive contains imagery where trees represent the aftermath of divine punishment. In one account, a people were subjected to a rage that lasted seven nights and eight days. The result was that the people were left lying prostrate or overthrown, compared to the "trunks of hollow palms" or "roots of hollow palm-trees tumbled down."

## Visual Observation and Divine Sight
The act of seeing is a recurring theme across several archival entries, often contrasting human perception with divine knowledge.

### Human Observation
The texts frequently question the observer's perception. Examples include:
*   **The Afterlife:** Descriptions of the Gardens where those who believe and work righteousness dwell. Observers are told that if they see the immortal youths serving there, they would think them to be scattered pearls. Looking upon this realm reveals "bliss and high estate" (or a "Realm Magnificent").
*   **Human Nature:** Observations are made regarding those "in whose hearts is a disease," who are seen racing toward others out of fear that a change of fortune or calamity might befall them.
*   **Moral Conduct:** Questions are posed regarding those who forbid a servant or votary from praying, asking if such a person is on the road of guidance or if they deny the truth.

### Divine Sight
The texts emphasize that while humans observe the physical world, Allah possesses absolute sight. This is highlighted in the warning to those who are inordinate or rebellious: "Knoweth he not that Allah doth see?" or "Is he then unaware that Allah seeth?"

## Other Historical and Sacred Narratives
The archive also records the interactions of various groups and figures. The Qur'an mentions that Allah will judge between those who believe, the Christians, the Sabians, the Magians, the Polytheists, and the followers of the Jewish scriptures on the Day of Judgment. It also recounts the dialogue between Allah and a creature (Satan) who questioned why a human was honored above him, leading to a respite until the Day of Resurrection.

In the historical accounts of Herodotus, the figure Heracles encounters a twofold creature—part maiden and part serpent—in the region of Hylaia. After a condition is met, the creature returns mares to Heracles and asks for guidance on her sons. Heracles instructs her that when she sees the boys have grown to men, she should allow the one who is able to stretch his bow and wear his girdle to settle the land, while the others should be sent forth.
