# Manatee Hills and Cockscomb Mountains

In the region of British Honduras, the landscape is characterized by a variety of terrains that transition from the coast toward the interior. The approach to the coastline is marked by coral reefs and islets known as cays, a route described as both dangerous and difficult. Moving inland, the terrain begins as low, swampy ground densely covered by tropical jungle and mangroves. This is followed by a narrow belt of rich alluvial land, which does not exceed one mile in width.

Beyond this alluvial belt, and running parallel to the rivers, are vast tracts of arid, sandy land referred to as "pine ridges" due to the presence of red pines. Further inland, the geography shifts first to "broken ridges," which are less elevated, and then to "cahoon ridges," characterized by deep rich soil and numerous palm trees. These are followed by broad savannas containing clumps of trees, where streams descending from the mountains wind in various directions.

## Mountain Ridges
The mountains of this region rise in a series of ridges that run parallel to the coast. The first of these are the Manatee Hills, which reach heights between 800 and 1000 ft. Located beyond the Manatee Hills are the Cockscomb Mountains, which reach elevations of approximately 4000 ft. Between the Sarstoon and the Hondo, no fewer than sixteen streams large enough to be classified as rivers descend from these mountains toward the sea.

## Regional Exploration and Geology
In 1879, Henry Fowler, the colonial secretary of British Honduras, explored the uninhabited country located between the coast south of Deep river and Garbutt's Rapids. This exploration revealed a landscape of undulating, open grasslands in the west, which provide fine pasturage, and forests in the east containing valuable timber. The elevation of this specific area varies from 1200 to 3300 ft.

Regarding mineral resources, traces of other minerals and auriferous quartz have been discovered in the region; however, they have not been found in quantities sufficient to justify the cost of mining. The flora, fauna, and geology of British Honduras are noted to be not materially different from those of neighboring regions in Central America.
