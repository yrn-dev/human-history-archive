# The Kilimanjaro (Chaga-Siha) Group

The Kilimanjaro (Chaga-Siha) group is a linguistic classification comprising the peculiar dialects spoken on Mount Kilimanjaro, Mount Meru, and Ugweno. This group is situated within the broader context of Bantu speech in East Africa.

## Geography and Geology of Mount Kilimanjaro
Mount Kilimanjaro is a great mountain in East Africa, located at 3° 5´ S. and 37° 23´ E. It is the highest known summit on the continent, consisting of a volcanic cone that rises from a plateau of approximately 3000 ft. to a peak of 19,321 ft. While isolated, it is one of several summits on the eastern edge of the equatorial African plateau; it is located some 25 m. due east of Mount Meru and about 200 m. south of Mount Kenya.

The mountain's major axis runs almost east and west, featuring two principal summits:
*   **Kibo:** The higher of the two, Kibo is a truncated cone with a nearly perfect extinct crater, representing a more recent period of volcanic activity. Its lava slopes are covered by an ice-cap reaching depths of some 200 ft., which forms genuine glaciers in the ravines. The highest point of the mountain is located on the southern rim of the crater and is named Kaiser Wilhelm Spitze.
*   **Mawenzi (Ki-mawenzi):** Standing at 16,892 ft., Mawenzi is the ancient core of a former summit. Its rock has been rendered jagged by denudation, creating precipices and stupendous walls. Unlike Kibo, Mawenzi has no permanent ice-cap, though snow occasionally appears in patches.

The two peaks are separated by about 7 m. and connected by a saddle or plateau at an altitude of approximately 14,000 ft. Other features include the Shira ridge to the south-west of Kibo and a group of rugged cones of recent origin in the north-west.

## Vegetation and Fauna
Kilimanjaro exhibits well-defined zones of vegetation. The lowest slopes are arid with scant scrub. Between 4000 and 6000 ft. on the south side, the slopes are cultivated and well watered. The forest zone begins on the south at roughly 6500 ft. and extends to 9500 ft., though it is narrower in the north and nearly disappears in the driest north-west quarter. 

In the alpine zone, flowering plants such as *Senecio* and tree lobelias extend up to 15,700 ft. on the sheltered south-west flank of Mawenzi; elsewhere, vegetation beyond 13,000 ft. grows only in dwarfed patches. The flora and fauna of the upper zone are akin to those found on other high African mountains, including Cameroon.

## Human Habitation and History
The southern slopes of the mountain, specifically between 4000 and 6000 ft., constitute the well-peopled country of the Chaga, which is divided into small districts. Historically, evidence suggests that a belated Hottentot or Hottentot-Bushman group once existed in the district between Victoria Nyanza and Kilimanjaro before being driven south by Bantu tribes.

Local natives believe the summit of Kilimanjaro is composed of silver. This belief has led to conjectures that Aristotle's "Silver Mountain" was a reference to Kilimanjaro, though other theories suggest the Ruwenzori.

European discovery of the mountain began around 1845 when Arab traders brought reports of snow-covered mountains to Zanzibar. In 1848, Johannes Rebmann of the Church Missionary Society discovered Kilimanjaro. His accounts were initially met with incredulity by professional geographers but were later confirmed by Baron Karl von der Decken (1861 and 1862) and Charles New (1867). Further exploration was conducted by Joseph Thomson (1883) and Sir H. H. Johnston (1884). Dr. Hans Meyer conducted four expeditions and achieved the first ascent to the summit in 1889.

## Political and Strategic Significance
In the partition of Africa, Germany secured Kilimanjaro in 1886, despite British company treaties made with native chiefs in 1884. A German government station was established at Moshi on the southern side of the mountain.

During the war, the area served as a difficult northern frontier. The enemy colony was protected by an almost impassable mountain system, with a narrow gap of four or five miles between Kilimanjaro and the Pare Mountains. The enemy utilized this gap to establish extensive fortifications surrounded by impassable swamps and dense forests. These forces were driven out in March 1916, and by the end of that month, the Kilimanjaro-Meru areas were conquered. This period was marked by a severe rainy season where ceaseless rain—sometimes three inches in twenty-four hours—turned valleys into lakes and dissolved roads into mud.
