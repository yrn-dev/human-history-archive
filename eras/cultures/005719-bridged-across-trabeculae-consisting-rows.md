# Trabeculae and Related Structures

In biological and anatomical contexts, the term trabeculae refers to structural supports or partitions found in various organs and tissues, ranging from the skeletal framework of the head to the internal architecture of the heart and lymphatic system.

## Chondrocranium and Cranial Trabeculae
The chondrocranium is a cartilaginous trough that serves to protect the brain, situated in front of the vertebral column. This structure is composed of two primary portions: the parachordal portion and the praechordal portion. The praechordal portion is developed from a pair of lateral cartilaginous rods known as the *trabeculae cranii*. 

The parachordal portion develops from a cartilaginous rod (parachordal cartilage) on each side of the notochord, which may represent a fused row of dorsal arcualia. As development progresses, the separate trabeculae and parachordals connect to form a primitive cranium. This trough-like structure is nearly complete ventrally and laterally but remains open dorsally. Additionally, cartilaginous capsules for the auditory and olfactory organs, as well as a varying number of neural arches, become connected or fused with the cranium.

## Muscular Trabeculae of the Heart
In the heart, trabeculae appear as muscular developments within the walls of the ventricle, which acts as the main pumping apparatus. The nature of these trabeculae varies across different species:

*   **Lower Forms:** The muscular trabeculae are separated by wide spaces that contain the majority of the blood.
*   **Teleostomes:** The trabeculae are so enlarged that the ventricular wall takes on a compact character, reducing the spaces to small, scattered openings on the inner surface.

## Trabeculae in the Lymphatic System
Within the lymphatic glands—pink, generally bean-shaped bodies that act as filters for lymphatic vessels—trabeculae serve as structural dividers. Each gland is enclosed in a fibrous capsule. From this capsule, trabeculae extend toward the center of the gland, where they interlace and break up to form a network. 

This arrangement distinguishes the medullary region from the cortical region of the gland. The intervals between these trabeculae are nearly filled with lymphoid tissue. Adjacent to the trabeculae are lymph paths or sinuses, which are crossed by the reticular stroma of the lymphoid tissue, functioning as a mechanical sieve to entangle foreign particles. Furthermore, blood vessels enter the gland at the hilum and are distributed along these trabeculae.
