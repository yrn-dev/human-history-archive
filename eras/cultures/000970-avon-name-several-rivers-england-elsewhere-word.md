# AVON

The name Avon is applied to several rivers in England and other regions. Etymologically, the word is Celtic, appearing as *afon* in Welsh, *aon* in Manx, and *abhuinn* (pronounced *avain*) in Gaelic. It is radically identical to the Latin *aqua* and *amnis*, as well as the Sanskrit *ap*, all meaning water. This root is found throughout the Celtic areas of Europe, appearing in various forms such as *Evan*, *Aune*, *Anne*, *Ive*, *Auney*, and *Inney* in the British Islands; *Aff*, *Aven*, *Avon*, and *Aune* in France and Brittany; *Avens* and *Avenza* in Italy; *Avono* in Spain; and *Avia* in Portugal. Additionally, the terminal syllables of several Latinized French river names, including the *Garumna*, *Matrona*, and *Sequana*, appear to originate from the same word. The root is also evident in names such as *Doab* and *Punjab*.

## The Lower or Bristol Avon

The Lower Avon, also known as the Bristol Avon, originates on the eastern slope of the Cotteswold Hills in Gloucestershire. It collects waters south of Tetbury and east of Malmesbury, flowing in a wide curve east and south past Melksham and Chippenham. At Bradford-on-Avon, the river turns abruptly west, receiving the waters of the Frome from the south before entering a narrow valley containing Bristol and Bath. 

The river's total length is approximately 75 miles, with distances of 10 miles from Bradford to Bath, 12 miles from Bath to Bristol, and 8 miles from Bristol to the mouth. The total fall of the river is between 500 and 600 feet, though it is 235 feet from Malmesbury. Its drainage area covers 891 square miles. The river eventually enters the estuary of the Severn at Avonmouth.

Below Bristol, the river passes through the Clifton Gorge, characterized by wooded cliffs and the Clifton suspension bridge. Due to the disfigurement of these cliffs by quarries, an "Avon Gorge Committee" was appointed in 1904 to report to the Bristol corporation on preserving the area. The Kennet and Avon Canal follows the river closely from Bradford to Bath, where it joins the river via a descent of seven locks.

## Bristol Harbour and Trade

The Avon serves as one of England's most important commercial waterways, providing access to the port of Bristol. In 1809, Bristol harbour was created by converting a branch of the Frome and the Avon into "the Float" through the formation of two basins and a new channel for the Avon. The floating harbour has a water area of about 85 acres and four dry docks.

The port extends from the mouth of the river and the Severn estuary up to Hanham Mills on the Avon. While the city docks have a depth of 22 feet, the docks at Avonmouth are accessible to the largest vessels. The Royal Edward dock at Avonmouth, constructed by the corporation starting in 1902 and opened by King Edward VII in 1908, features a lock 875 feet long and 100 feet wide. Portishead dock on the Somerset shore covers 12 acres.

The port maintains significant trade with the West Indies and America. Primary imports include cattle, hides, timber, ore, oils, fruit, and grain, while exports consist of salt, tin, cotton goods, manufactured oils, and machinery. Major steamship companies operating here include Dominion and Elder Dempster.

## The Upper Avon

The Upper Avon, also referred to as the Warwickshire or "Shakespeare" Avon due to its association with Stratford, is an eastern tributary of the Severn. It rises near Naseby in Northamptonshire and flows for about 100 miles before joining the Severn below Tewkesbury in Gloucestershire. 

The river flows south-westerly to Rugby, then west and south-west to Warwick, where it receives the Leam. It continues south-westerly past Stratford-on-Avon, receiving the Arrow from the north and the Stour from the south, before passing Pershore and Evesham. The valley is broad, particularly through the Vale of Evesham, where it is flanked by the wooded slopes of the Arden district to the north and the Cotteswold Hills to the south.

The river is locked and supports a small trade up to Evesham (28 miles from Tewkesbury). While locks from Evesham to Stratford (17 miles) are decayed, weirs and mill-dams provide navigable reaches for pleasure boats. The total fall of the river is approximately 500 feet, with 230 feet from Rugby and 120 feet from Warwick. The river is noted for its abundance of coarse fish.

## Other Occurrences and Geological Terms

Other rivers named Avon in Great Britain include:
* A stream in Devonshire flowing from Dartmoor to the English Channel.
* A stream in South Wales with its mouth at Aberavon in Glamorganshire.
* Tributaries of the Forth, the Spey, and the Clyde in Scotland. In Banffshire, the Avon is one of the chief streams, and the region features Glen Avon and Loch Avon (located 2,300 feet above sea level).

In geology, "Avonian" is a term proposed by Dr. A. Vaughan in 1905 to describe Lower Carboniferous rocks in the Bristol Avon gorge. The Avonian stage corresponds to the "Dinantien" designation but utilizes different substages based on local conditions and zonal fossils. These include the Clevedonian substage (displayed near Clevedon in Somerset) and the Kidwellian substage (developed near Kidwelly in Carmarthenshire).
