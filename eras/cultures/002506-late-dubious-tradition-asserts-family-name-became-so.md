# Traditions Regarding Family Names and Origins

The historical record contains various traditions concerning the origins and evolution of family names, ranging from claims of ancestral nobility to assertions regarding the abandonment of names due to social discredit. These accounts often vary in their perceived reliability, with some being classified as trustworthy and others as dubious or mythical.

## The Baliol and Baillie Tradition
A tradition described as late and dubious suggests a specific reason for the evolution of the Baliol family name. According to this account, the name became so discredited because of the pusillanimous conduct of Edward and John Baliol. As a result of this discredit, the owners of the name allegedly abandoned it in favor of the form Baillie.

## The Polo Family Origins
The origins of the Polo family are subject to several differing accounts, some of which are noted as having been drawn largely from the imagination. One Venetian Antiquary claimed that Lucius Polus, a companion of Prince Antenor of Troy or King Venetus, was the progenitor of the family when they settled on the northern shores of the Adriatic. Another theory deduces the family's origin from Paolo, the first Doge (Paulus Lucas Anafestus of Heraclea, A.D. 696).

More trustworthy traditions, though still regarded as traditions, state that the Polo family came from Sebenico in Dalmatia during the 11th century. Records indicate the family had taken seats in the Great Council of the Republic before the end of that century. Specific mentions include Domenico Polo in a grant of 1094, Pietro Polo in an act from the time of Doge Domenico Michiele in 1122, and another Domenico Polo in an acquittance granted by the Council and Doge Domenico Morosini in 1153. However, the ascertained genealogy of the Traveller begins only with his grandfather in the early 13th century.

## The Bassarab Dynasty
The Bassarab family formed a dynasty that ruled Walachia from the beginning of its history until 1658. While the origin of the name remains unexplained, it is closely connected to the province of Bessarabia. Tradition holds that a member of the family came from Transylvania and settled in Tirgovishtea and Campulung, leading to the foundation of the Walachian principality.

The dynasty eventually split into two rival factions: the descendants of the brothers Dragul and Dan. The line of Dragul became known by the nickname Drakul (meaning "devil"), a term likely given by the rival line. This name persisted due to the fiendish cruelty associated in popular legend with Vlad Tsepesh (1456-1476) and Vlad Drakul (1433-1446).

## Ancestral Traditions in Denmark and Japan
In Denmark, tradition attributes the acquisition of Jutland and Skaane to Dan, the eponymous ancestor of the Danes. The Danish royal family of Skiöldungar is said to descend from Skiöldr, the son of Othin and husband of the goddess Gefjon. Anglo-Saxon tradition also identifies Scyld (Skiöldr) as the ancestor of the English and Danish royal families, describing him as a child of unknown origin who arrived in a rudderless boat.

In Japan, traditional faith centered on ancestor worship, where each great family possessed its own *uji no Kami* (deity). Over time, these deities were supplemented by Buddhism, and shrines of the *uji no Kami* were transformed into *uji-tera* (uji temples). Examples of this include Onjo-ji, known as Otomo no uji-tera (erected by Otomo Suguri), and Kofukuji, called O-Nakatomi no uji-tera (built by Fuhito and Kamatari).

## Reliability of Traditions
Historical analysis often distinguishes between factual records and local tradition or myth. For instance, the belief that Hipparchus was the eldest son of Peisistratus and the reigning tyrant when killed by Aristogiton is noted as a popular mistake. Some accounts, such as those in the Platonic *Hipparchus*, are regarded as historical romance designed to present the "tyrant" family as patrons of learning. Similarly, the claim that Lycurgus obtained poems from the descendants of Creophylus is dismissed as purely mythical.
