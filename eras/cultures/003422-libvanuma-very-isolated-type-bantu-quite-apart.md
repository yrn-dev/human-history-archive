# Lihuku-Kuamba and the Bantu Languages

The Lihuku-Kuamba group consists of separate and somewhat peculiar Bantu dialects located in the lands to the south and south-west of Albert Nyanza, specifically within the Mboga country. This group is categorized within the broader Western group of Bantu speech, which includes the languages of the Northern and Central Congo, Cameroon, and the Semliki river.

## Lihuku and Kuamba Dialects
Lihuku, also known as Libvanuma, is characterized as a very isolated type of Bantu. Despite being in close juxtaposition with the Uganda-Unyoro groups, it shows no special affinity to them. Similarly, its alliance with Kuamba, spoken on the north and west slopes of Ruwenzori, is not very close. 

Lihuku and Kuamba both demonstrate a marked relationship with languages found on the Aruwimi and northern Congo, though this relationship is more evident in their vocabulary than in their grammar. Additionally, Lihuku shows affinities with degraded Bantu dialects, such as Ki-bira, found in the Ituri-Aruwimi forests.

## General Characteristics of Bantu Languages
Bantu languages are noted for a striking homogeneity, which distinguishes them from the linguistic confusion found in Africa north of the Bantu borderland. A defining feature of these languages is the pronominal-prefix structure. 

The phonetics of true Bantu languages follow specific rules:
* Vowels are of the Italian type, and obscure sounds such as *ue* and *oe* are absent.
* Every word must end in a vowel, although terminal vowels may be dropped, absorbed by a terminal consonant (usually a nasal), or elided in rapid pronunciation in some modern dialects of South, West, and Eastern Equatorial Africa.
* Consonants are not doubled, except when two *d*'s, two *r*'s, or two nasals meet due to the elision of a labial or vowel.
* No two consonants appear together without an intervening vowel, with the exception of sibilants, labials, or nasals.

## Origins and Migration
The Bantu mother-language is believed to have originated in the heart of Africa, in the region between the western Nile, Congo, and Shari watersheds. Linguistic evidence suggests that a powerful tribe of negroes speaking this mother-language pushed forcibly into the southern half of the continent. While some estimates place this exodus no more than 3,000 years ago, other linguistic research suggests a date closer to 2,000 years ago. 

This dating is based on the common root *kuku* (and its variations such as *ngoko*, *nkuku*, or *nsusu*) used for the domestic fowl. Because the domestic fowl reached Africa via Egypt during the Persian occupation (no earlier than 500 to 400 B.C.), the Bantu must have acquired the bird before leaving their original home.

Prior to the Bantu invasion, the regions they entered were populated by various groups:
* The Atlantic seaboard was occupied by West Coast negroes.
* The center was inhabited by Forest Pygmies and negroes of a low type.
* The East African coast and eastern shores of Victoria Nyanza were populated by Hottentot-Bushman and Nilotic-negro groups.
* From the Cape of Good Hope north-west to Lakes Nyasa and Tanganyika, the population consisted of Nilotic-negro, Forest-negro, Bushman, and Hottentot.

The Bantu swept over nearly all these areas, absorbing or assimilating the majority of these preceding populations.

## Physical Diversity
The speakers of Bantu languages do not belong to a single, definite physical type. While there is some resemblance among certain tribes—such as the Ba-tonga, the people of the Great Lakes, the Zulu-Kaffirs, the Ova-herero, the Babangi of the upper Congo, and the Awemba—there is significant diversity in physical characteristics and head shape. 

Some Bantu speakers are dwarfish and belong to the Forest Pygmies group. Others show relationships to Hottentots, while some are indistinguishable from black West African negroes. In the north, some are of Nilotic or Gala (Galla) origin.

## Classification of Bantu Speech
The domain of Bantu speech is broadly divided into four great sections:
1. The languages of the East Coast and Great Lakes, extending to the Zambezi basin.
2. The South-Central group, including Bechuana-Zulu.
3. The South-West group, ranging from the Angola-Congo coast and Damaraland to the southern Belgian Congo.
4. The Western group, encompassing Cameroon and Central and Northern Congo languages, as well as the Lihuku-Kuamba group of the Semliki river and Albert Nyanza.

Within these groups, certain dialects are considered more archaic. These include the speech of Ruwenzori, Mount Elgon, Uganda, Unyoro, and the north coast of Tanganyika, as well as the Subiya speech of the western Zambezi.
