# Teutonic Languages

The Teutonic languages, also referred to as the Germanic branch, are a group of tongues belonging to the Indo-Germanic (Indo-European) family. This linguistic branch is associated with the Teutonic stock, a race whose Aryan descent is generally recognized as beyond dispute. This group includes the Angles and Saxons, as well as the peoples and languages of modern-day Germany, Holland, Denmark, Iceland, England, the Scandinavian peninsula, and the colonies of England.

## Linguistic Classification and Origins
The parent language of the Teutonic branch was Indo-European, spoken by the primitive Aryan race. While the exact original home of this race is not certainly known, current tendencies suggest it may have been located within Europe. From its initial point of origin, the language gradually diffused across much of Europe and portions of Asia.

In the early seventh century, the Anglo-Saxons and the Franks were able to mutually understand one another's speech, as both languages were derived from the same Teutonic root.

## Comparative Study and Grammar
The development of a comparative Teutonic grammar was first clearly grasped at the beginning of the 18th century by the Englishman George Hickes, who partially implemented this conception in his work, the *Thesaurus*. Further valuable contributions to the comparison and history of these languages were later made by Ten Kate in Holland.

A significant advancement in the field was the *Deutsche Grammatik* by Jacob Grimm. Grimm's work was based on purely philological research, utilizing a vast mass of dictionaries, grammars, and text-editions collected by previous generations of humanists. Although Grimm did not initially intend to include all Teutonic languages, he determined that a comprehensive understanding required the inclusion of several elements:
* **Gothic:** Postulated as a necessity for understanding Old High German.
* **Low German dialects (including English):** Necessary for understanding the later stages of German.
* **Scandinavian literature:** Deemed too rich to be ignored.

The first part of Grimm's *Grammar*, published in 1819, focused on the inflections of these languages and advocated for an historical study of the German language over the quasi-philosophical, a priori methods common at the time.

## Common Vocabulary and Etymology
Many words are common across various Teutonic languages, appearing in different forms. These shared terms provide insight into the linguistic connections between the branch's members.

### Postulated Forms and Roots
Certain words have specific postulated original forms. For example, the word "book" exists in all Teutonic languages with variations in gender and form. The postulated original form is a strong feminine *Boks*, which is believed to have meant a writing-tablet. This is most obviously connected to the old English *boc*, meaning a beech tree.

Other common terms include:
* **Ground:** The Old English *grund* (German *Grund*, Dutch *grond*) is common to Teutonic languages but lacks cognates outside the group. Its primary meaning refers to the lowest part or bottom of something.
* **Doom:** Derived from the Old English *dom*, this term is common to Teutonic languages and originally referred to that which is set up or ordered, such as a law, enactment, or a judge's legal decision.
* **Lot:** Originating as a Teutonic word (Old English *hlot*, Dutch *lot*, German *Loos*, Danish *lod*), it was later adopted into Romanic languages. While the meaning of the root *hleut* is unknown, the word primarily referred to an object—such as a pebble, bean, or wooden disk—cast to decide matters by chance or divine guidance.
* **Knife:** Appearing in various forms (Old English *cníf*, Dutch *knijf*, Swedish *knif*, German *Kneif*), the ultimate origin of this word is unknown, though some suggest a connection to the root of "nip."
* **Calf:** Common to Teutonic languages (German *Kalb*, Dutch *kalf*), referring to the young of the *Bovidae* family.

## Cultural and Literary Connections
The shared linguistic heritage of the Teutonic peoples extends beyond basic vocabulary to broader cultural expressions. An example of this is the extraordinary cycle of poetry associated with the name of Attila, which is found in different forms across almost all Teutonic languages.
