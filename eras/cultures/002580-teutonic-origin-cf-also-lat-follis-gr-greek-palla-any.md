# Teutonic Linguistic and Cultural Influence

The term "Teutonic" appears across various archival records to describe the linguistic origins of numerous English words and the cultural concepts associated with the peoples from whom these terms derive. These records highlight connections between Teutonic languages, other Indo-European branches such as Sanskrit, Latin, and Greek, as well as the persistence of Teutonic folklore in modern terminology.

## Etymology and Linguistic Cognates

Many fundamental English words are identified as being Teutonic in origin or common to Teutonic languages. The word "doom" (Old English *dom*) is a term common to these languages, originally meaning that which is set up or ordered, derived from the original meaning of "place." This root is linked to the Greek *themis* (from the stem of *tithemi*). From this origin, "doom" evolved to mean a law, an enactment, or the legal decision of a judge, eventually extending to mean the day of judgment or adverse decrees of fate. The derivative verb "deem," meaning to deliver a judgment or hold an opinion, also appears in various old Teutonic forms and is preserved in the title "deemster," used for the two judges of the Isle of Man.

Other common Teutonic words include "bore," in the sense of piercing or drilling (comparable to the German *bohren*), which shares an Indo-European root with the Latin *forare* and the Greek *pharos* (plough). The word "ball" (Middle English *bal*) is also considered probably cognate with "bale" and Teutonic in origin, though it is compared to the Latin *follis* and Greek *palla*.

## Social Structure and Kinship

The archive contains discussions regarding the origin of the word "king." Max Müller notes a general agreement that the English *cyning* and the Sanskrit *ganaka* share a common root, the same root that produced the Greek *genos* and the English words "cyn" or "kin." There is a scholarly distinction regarding whether *cyning* is strictly the same word as *ganaka* or an independent formation created after the Teutons separated from the common stock. O. Schrader suggests that the *chuning* (King) is essentially the *chunni* (Kin) personified. This is further illustrated by the Avestan *léod*, where the masculine form means "prince" and the feminine form means "race," similar to the Latin *gens*.

## Folklore and Superstition

Teutonic influence is evident in the terminology of dreams and nightmares. The termination of the word "night-mare"—referring to an oppressive or terrifying dream—is derived from a word appearing as *mar*, *maer*, or *mara* in various Teutonic languages. In these traditions, a *mara* was a goblin believed to sit on the chest of a sleeper to cause such dreams. This Teutonic root also appears in the French word *cauchemar*, combined with *caucher* (to tread or trample upon).

## Trade and Material Culture

The archive provides various examples of terms shared across Romanic and Teutonic languages due to trade and cultural exchange:

*   **Marchpane (Marzipan):** This sweetmeat made of sugar and pounded sweet almonds is common in most European languages. While the German form is *marzipan* and the English is *marchpane*, Italian (*marzapane*) is believed to be the source from which the word passed into other languages.
*   **The Mark:** While the modern silver coin of the German empire is a distinct word and not of Teutonic origin, the "mark" as a measure of weight for gold and silver was common throughout western Europe. In England, the mark was a money of account introduced in the 10th century through the Danes. It was initially equal to 100 pennies, later becoming 160 pennies (or 2/3 of a pound sterling) after the Norman Conquest.
