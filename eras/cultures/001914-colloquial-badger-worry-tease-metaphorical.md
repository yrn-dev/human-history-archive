# The Etymology and Usage of "Badger"

The term "badger" appears in historical records as a name for a specific animal, a type of itinerant trader, and as a metaphorical colloquialism.

## The Animal
The badger is the common name for animals within the typical genus *Meles* or the Musteline subfamily *Melinae*. The name likely originates from "badge" (a device) due to the markings on the animal's head, or it may be identical to the French term *blaireau*, which is used for both the animal and the trader.

The European badger (*Meles taxus* or *M. meles*) is characterized by a grey upper hue, black underparts, and a white head with black stripes on each side. It typically measures between 25 and 29 inches in length with an 8-inch tail. These animals are nocturnal, solitary, and generally quiet and inoffensive. They live in burrows and are omnivorous, feeding on roots, fruits, insects, frogs, small quadrupeds, bird eggs, and beech-mast. They are also known to dig up wasp nests to consume larvae.

Physiologically, the genus *Meles* is noted for a lower jaw articulated to the upper jaw via a transverse condyle locked into a long cavity of the cranium, making jaw dislocation nearly impossible and allowing the animal to maintain a tenacious hold. Other species include the Persian badger (*M. canescens*), the Chinese badger (*M. chinensis*), the white-tailed badger (*M. leucurus*), and the Japanese badger (*M. anakuma*). In North America, the American badger (*Taxidea americana*) and the Mexican badger (*T. berlandieri*) occupy a similar ecological role, though the American species appears more carnivorous.

## Historical Sport and Colloquialisms
While generally inoffensive, the badger becomes a formidable antagonist when driven to bay due to its tough hide and muscular power. This led to the practice of "badger-drawing," a cruel sport formerly popular in Great Britain. In this activity, a "badger-ward" kept the animal in a large box. Bets were placed on how many times a dog—typically a bull-terrier—could "draw" (pull) the badger out of the box within a set time limit.

Another method of baiting involved digging a hole approximately one yard long and four feet deep. A stake was driven into the deep end, and the badger was fastened to it by a chain passed through its split tail, allowing the animal to move only to the other end of the hole where dogs were set upon it. These practices, along with bull-baiting and bear-baiting, were prohibited around the middle of the 19th century.

The colloquial use of the verb "to badger," meaning to tease or worry, is a metaphorical derivative of these baiting practices. Similarly, the phrase "drawing a badger" is used in a figurative sense.

## The Itinerant Trader
Separate from the animal, "badger" is a term of uncertain derivation—possibly from *bagger*, referring to a hawker's bag—used to describe a dealer in food. These itinerant dealers purchased goods such as corn, victuals, butter, cheese, or fish in one location to sell them in another, functioning similarly to a huckster or hawker.

The trade was subject to various English statutes:
* **1552 Statute:** Badgers were specifically exempted from penalties regarding regrating, forestalling, and engrossing, provided they were licensed by three justices of the peace in their county.
* **1562-1563 Statute:** Because many entered the trade to "leave their honest labour" and "live easily," new restrictions were enacted. Badgers were required to be householders of three years' standing in their licensed county, enter into recognizances not to forestall or engross, and obtain licenses valid for only one year.
* **1844 Act:** This legislation abolished the offence of badgering and repealed previous statutes related to the trade.

Despite these legal changes, the term remained in common use in country districts.

## Related Breeds and Uses
The dachshund, also known as the badger hound, is a German breed originally developed for hunting and baiting badgers. It is characterized by a long, cylindrical body, short thick silky coat, and crooked legs.

Historically, badger skins with the hair attached were commonly used for the furniture of pistols when the animals were more abundant.
