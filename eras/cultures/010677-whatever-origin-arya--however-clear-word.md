# ARYAN

The term "Aryan" has been employed by various philologists in a wide range of significations. In modern scholarship, it is used uniformly to indicate the Eastern branch of the Indo-European peoples as a whole. Within this group, the compound "Indo-Aryan" refers specifically to the portion of the Eastern branch that settled in India, while "Iranian" refers to those who remained in Persia and Bactria. The term "Aryo-Indian" is sometimes utilized to distinguish this stock from other groups, such as the Dravidians, who also inhabit parts of the Indian peninsula.

## Linguistic Definitions and Usage
Max Muller significantly influenced the public's understanding of the term through his works on comparative philology. Muller used "Aryan" as a convenient short term for the entire body of languages more commonly known as Indo-Germanic or Indo-European. Correspondingly, he used "Aryas" as a general term for the speakers of these languages. 

Muller explicitly stated that his use of "Aryas" was devoid of ethnological signification, asserting that the term refers simply to those who speak an Aryan language, regardless of their blood, color, hair, skull, or anatomical characteristics. According to Muller, the grammar of the language is the only predicate of an Arya; thus, even the "blackest Hindus" may represent an earlier stage of Aryan speech and thought than the "fairest Scandinavians." He characterized ethnologists who spoke of "Aryan race" or "Aryan blood" as "sinners," comparing them to linguists who would speak of a "brachycephalic grammar."

## Etymology and Early Usage
The word *arya*- is associated with dignity and was a name the Eastern Indo-European peoples were proud to use. It appears in early Sanskrit writings, as well as in Zend (*airya-*) and Old Persian (*ariya-*), indicating its use as a national name in Persia, Bactria, and India. 

Within Sanskrit, scholars distinguish between two forms: *arya*, the origin of "Aryan," and *arya*, an epithet for deities frequently found in the *Rig Veda*. Additionally, *aryas* may be the genitive of *ari*, meaning "pious, devoted, active." 

The exact origin of the word remains a subject of debate. While Max Muller suggested a connection to the Sanskrit word for earth (*ira*), this is considered far from certain. Spiegel noted that while the word can be connected to a root *ar-*, there are several such roots with different meanings and no certain criterion to decide between them. A proposed connection to *Erin* (Ireland) by Pictet is regarded as impossible. However, the word may share an origin with the *Ario-* found in Celtic and Germanic names, such as *Ariovistus*. The connection to the name of Armenia (*Armina-* in Old Persian) is of uncertain origin.

## Linguistic Characteristics
The Aryan group is distinguished from other Indo-European languages by four primary characteristics:
1. The confusion of original long and short *e* and *o* with the original long and short *a* sound.
2. The representation of the short schwa-sound by *i* (e.g., *pita* for "father").
3. The transformation of original *s* into *s* after certain consonants, *i*, and *u*.
4. A genitive plural suffix *-nam* for stems ending in a vowel, borrowed from stems ending in *-n* (e.g., Sanskrit *asvanam*, Zend *aspanam*).

Distinctions also exist between Sanskrit and Iranian. In Iranian, the Aryan voiced aspirates (*gh, dh, bh*) are confused with original *g, d, b*. Aryan breathed aspirates (*kh, th, ph*) generally become spirants in Iranian. Additionally, Aryan *s* becomes *h* initially before vowels in Iranian, similar to Greek (e.g., Sanskrit *sapta* becomes Zend *hapta* and Greek *hepta*). One striking difference is the numeral "one": Sanskrit uses *eka-*, while Zend uses *aeva-* and Old Persian uses *aiva-*.

## Migration and History
The Aryan people's occupation of the Iranian table-land is undocumented, as the people possess no tradition of a time before they held those territories. Regarding India, while the Hindus have no tradition of their invasion, it is certain they are not indigenous and must have entered from the other side of the Hindu Kush. 

By approximately 1000 B.C., the period of their earliest literature, they were settled in the Indus valley, having pushed through the Kabul valley. Professor E.W. Hopkins argues that the *Rig Veda* was composed in the district around Umballa, citing descriptions of the dawn gleaming over the waters before the wind arises as evidence that the people were then west of the great rivers.

## The Arya Samaj
In a modern context, the Arya Samaj is a movement established by Dayanand, who founded the first *Samaj* in Bombay in 1875. Dayanand chose the epithet "Arya" because he considered it more dignified than the term "Hindu," which he viewed as slightly contemptuous. The movement appealed to educated natives who were dissatisfied with current Hinduism but opposed to foreign religious influence, offering a view of India's original faith that harmonized with European civilization's ethics and modern science. By 1900, the movement had over 92,000 members, though it split into the *Mansi* (flesh-eating) and *Ghasi* (vegetarian) sections.
