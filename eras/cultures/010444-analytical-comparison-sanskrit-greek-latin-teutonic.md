# Analytical Comparison of the Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and Teutonic Languages

The "Analytical Comparison of the Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and Teutonic Languages" is an essay authored by the German philologist Franz Bopp. Published in London in 1820 within the *Annals of Oriental Literature*, the work expanded upon the grammatical research Bopp had previously conducted. While his first book had focused specifically on the verb, this essay extended his analytical approach to all parts of grammar.

## Franz Bopp and the Development of Comparative Grammar
Franz Bopp (1791–1867) was a German philologist born in Mainz. His interest in the "sacred language of the Hindus" was stimulated by the lectures of Karl J. Windischmann and the influence of Fr. Schlegel's 1808 work, *Uber die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier*. To pursue these interests, Bopp studied in Paris for four years starting in 1812, where he utilized the Imperial library's collection of Sanskrit manuscripts and books from the Serampore and Calcutta presses.

In 1816, Bopp published *Uber das Conjugationssystem der Sanskritsprache in Vergleichung mit jenem der griechischen, lateinischen, persischen und germanischen Sprache*. In this initial work, Bopp did not seek to prove the common parentage of Sanskrit, Persian, Greek, Latin, and German—as that fact had already been established—but rather aimed to trace the common origin of their grammatical structures.

Bopp's academic career progressed with a period in London, where he became friends with Wilhelm von Humboldt (then the Prussian ambassador) and provided him with instruction in Sanskrit. In 1821, Bopp was appointed to the chair of comparative grammar and Sanskrit at Berlin.

## The Comparative Grammar and Methodology
Bopp's primary scholarly achievement was his *Comparative Grammar* (*Vergleichende Grammatik des Sanskrit, Zend, Griechischen, Lateinischen, Litthauischen, Altslavischen, Gothischen, und Deutschen*), published in six parts between 1833 and 1852. This work was preceded by a series of monographs in the *Transactions of the Berlin Academy* (1824–1831) titled *Vergleichende Zergliederung des Sanskrits und der mit ihm verwandten Sprachen*.

The objectives of Bopp's *Comparative Grammar* were threefold:
1. To describe the original grammatical structure of the languages as deduced from their intercomparison.
2. To trace their phonetic laws.
3. To investigate the origin of their grammatical forms.

Bopp considered the first two objectives to be subservient to the third. His work evolved over time; for instance, Old-Slavonian was included in the comparisons from the second part of the *Comparative Grammar* onwards, and a revised German edition published between 1856 and 1861 incorporated Old-Armenian.

## Etymological and Linguistic Affinities
Comparative linguistic research has identified specific cognates across these language groups. For example, the English word *cyning* and the Sanskrit *ganaka* are agreed by most to come from the same widely spread root as the Greek *genos* and the English *kin* or *cyn*. Some scholars, such as O. Schrader, suggest that the *chuning* (King) is the personification of the *chunni* (Kin).

Further etymological comparisons show that the Latin *rex* corresponds to the Sanskrit *rajah* (originally meaning "steersman"), whereas the Teutonic word for king corresponds to the Sanskrit *ganaka*, meaning "father of a family," "father of a clan," or "father of a people." Additionally, the etymological affinities between the Sanskrit *dhuma* and the Greek *thyô* and *thyos*, as well as the Latin *fuffio* and *funus*, are well recognized.

## Related Philological Contributions
Other scholars have contributed to the study of these languages and their histories. Jacob Grimm authored *Geschichte der deutschen Sprache*, which attempted to determine the oldest national history of the Teutonic tribes by analyzing the German language and its relations to the languages of the Scythians, Thracians, and Getae.

In the United States, James Bradstreet Greenough provided the first courses in comparative philology and Sanskrit at Harvard between 1772 and 1880. He also co-authored *A Latin Grammar for Schools and Colleges, founded on Comparative Grammar* (1872) with Joseph A. Allen.
