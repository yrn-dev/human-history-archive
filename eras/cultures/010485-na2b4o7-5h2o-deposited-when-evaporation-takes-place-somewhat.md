# Na2B4O7.5H2O (Octahedral Borax)

Na2B4O7.5H2O is the chemical formula for the pentahydrate of borax, a substance known in commerce as "jewellers'" or octahedral borax. It is one of two primary commercial forms of borax (sodium pyroborate or sodium biborate), the other being the "common" or prismatic borax (Na2B4O7.10H2O).

## Chemical Properties and Production
The pentahydrate, Na2B4O7.5H2O, is deposited from a supersaturated aqueous solution of borax when evaporation occurs at somewhat high temperatures. Alternatively, it can be produced by dissolving borax in water until the solution reaches a specific gravity of 1.246 and then allowing that solution to cool. The pentahydrate is specifically deposited at temperatures between 56 deg. C. and 79 deg. C. If the temperature falls below 56 deg. C., the decahydrate (ordinary borax) is deposited instead.

## Physical Characteristics and Industrial Use
Octahedral borax is preferred over the prismatic form for fluxing or soldering because its crystals fuse more easily and are less likely to split when heated. When borax is fused, it can dissolve many metallic oxides to form complex borates, which frequently exhibit characteristic colors.

In the process of soldering, borax is used because solder only adheres to metal surfaces that are untarnished. A small amount of borax is placed on the metal surface and heated with a soldering iron to remove any superficial film of oxide. Beyond soldering, the substance is utilized in the glazing of linen, glass-making, and the glazing of pottery.

## Natural Occurrence and Mineralogy
Borax is found as a natural product in several regions, including Peru, Canada, Transylvania, and Tibet. In Tibet, crude material was historically exported to Europe as *tincal* for the production of boron salts and pure borax. Tibetan deposits include *sohaga* (impure borax) in the lake-plain of Pugha on the Rulangchu, where saline efflorescence occurs through cycles of rain, snow, and evaporation. Purer material, known as *chu tsale* or water borax, is found at the lakes of Rudok and Tengri Nor.

In California, a large mass of crystallized borax is found in the bed of Borax Lake near Clear Lake, which is suitable for use by assayers without preliminary purification. Large isolated crystals, some weighing a pound and reaching 7 inches in length, have been found at Borax Lake in both San Bernardino and Lake counties.

## Commercial Sourcing and Synthesis
While natural deposits exist, the primary supply of borax is derived from the boric acid of Tuscany. This is processed in a reverberatory furnace by fusing the boric acid with half its weight of sodium carbonate; the resulting mass is cooled and extracted with warm water. Another synthetic method involves adding boric acid gradually to sodium carbonate dissolved in steam-heated, lead-lined pans, concentrating the solution until borax crystallizes. Borax can also be prepared by fusing naturally occurring calcium borate with a required quantity of soda ash in a finely divided state, followed by water extraction and concentration.

The boron found in salt lake solutions is likely supplied by solfataras and hot springs of volcanic origin. These deposits, formed by evaporation from marshes, salines, and lakes, typically consist of mixtures of various alkaline salts (such as chloride, sulphate, and sodium carbonate), gypsum, and borates.
