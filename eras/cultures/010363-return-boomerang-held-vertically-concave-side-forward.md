# Boomerang

The boomerang is a missile weapon utilized by the Australian aborigines and other peoples. The term is derived from a native name used by a single tribe in New South Wales, referred to by Captain King in 1827 as "the Port Jackson term." Contrary to some beliefs, the word is not onomatopoeic, as the weapon whistles in the air rather than "booming." Additionally, it has been erroneously linked to the *womera*, or spear-thrower.

## Types and Distribution
There are two primary types of boomerangs: the return boomerang and the non-return (or war) boomerang. While both types are found throughout most parts of Australia, similar weapons have been noted elsewhere. General Pitt-Rivers indicated that the return form was used in ancient Egypt, and a closely resembling weapon exists in North-East Africa, where it evolved into metal throwing knives. A boomerang-shaped instrument capable of returning is also found among the Dravidians of South India, though it remains uncertain if these Egyptian and Indian versions truly resemble the Australian return boomerang. In Arizona, the Hopis (Moquis) utilize a non-return form.

## Physical Characteristics
Both types of boomerangs share a general sickle-shaped form and are typically constructed from wood, although ivory or steel is used in India. The dimensions are generally proportional, with the thickness being approximately 1/6th of the breadth, and the breadth being 1/12th of the length. Lengths vary from 6 inches to 3 or 4 feet.

The return boomerang typically measures 2 to 3 feet in length and weighs about 8 oz. While it may have two straight arms meeting at an angle between 70 and 120 degrees, in Australia it is always curved at an angle of 90 degrees or more. Its cross-section is asymmetrical, featuring a convex upper side and a flat or nearly flat lower side. A critical feature of its flight is the "skew," where the arms are twisted 2 or 3 degrees from the plane running through the center of the weapon.

The non-return boomerang is similar in construction but possesses a skew in the opposite direction.

## Flight and Operation
The return boomerang must be thrown with the right hand. It is held vertically with the concave side facing forward and thrown in a plane parallel to the ground, imparting as much rotation as possible. Its flight pattern typically follows these stages:
* **Initial Flight:** It travels straight for 30 yards or more with nearly vertical rotation.
* **Curvature:** It then inclines to the left, rising in the air and lying over on its flat side.
* **Return:** After describing a circle with a diameter of 50 yards or more, it returns to the thrower.

Some observers note that the weapon can return after striking an object, and it is possible to strike the ground without interrupting the return. Australian natives can achieve throws of 100 yards or more before the leftward curve begins, with the weapon rising up to 150 feet in the air and circling five times before returning.

The non-return type is normally thrown like the return type to achieve immense distances. However, it can be made to return in a nearly straight line if thrown at a 45-degree angle. While accurate measurements of Australian throws are unavailable, an English throw of 180 yards has been recorded, which is significantly further than the same thrower's 70-yard throw with a cricket ball.

## Usage
The different types of boomerangs serve different purposes. The war boomerang is a deadly weapon in the hands of an expert, and the lighter hunting boomerang is also considered effective. The return boomerang is primarily used for killing birds or as a plaything; however, it is noted that the return form can often be as dangerous to the thrower as it is to the intended target.
