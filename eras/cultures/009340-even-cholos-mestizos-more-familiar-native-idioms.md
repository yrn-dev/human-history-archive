# Language and Education in Bolivia

## Linguistic Composition
In Bolivia, the linguistic landscape is divided between the dominant minority and the native majority. Spanish serves as the language of the dominant minority, while the native population primarily speaks Quichua, Aymara, and Guarani. A significant portion of the Indian population does not understand Spanish and resists efforts to force the language upon them.

The *cholos* (mestizos) exhibit a specific linguistic pattern where they are more familiar with native idioms than they are with Spanish. This phenomenon is also observed in certain regions of Paraguay and Argentina.

## Demographics and Social Structure
The population of Bolivia is composed of Indians, Caucasians of European origin, and *mestizos*, who are a mixture of the two races. There is also a very small percentage of Africans who are descendants of slaves from the colonial era. 

According to a roughly-taken census from 1900, the total population was 1,816,271 (which included the Litoral department, now part of Chile). The breakdown of this population was as follows:
* Indians: 50.7%
* Mestizos: 26.8%
* Whites: 12.8%
* Unknown: 9.4%
* Negroes: 0.3%

A later official estimate in 1904 placed the population at 2,181,415. The Indian population, estimated at 920,860, includes the "civilized tribes" of the Andes—primarily Aymaras south of Lake Titicaca—and the tribes of the Chiquitos and Mojos regions. The latter were organized into industrial communities by Jesuits in the 17th century. Additionally, the Chiriguanos, a tribe of the Guarani race, inhabit the southern Bolivian plain.

The white population, numbering 231,088, consists largely of descendants of early Spanish adventurers seeking mineral wealth, along with some Portuguese Americans from Brazil and Spanish Americans from neighboring republics. The *mestizos* number 486,018, outnumbering the white population by more than two to one.

## Educational System and Infrastructure
Bolivia maintains a free and compulsory school system, though progress in the provision of education has been limited, and only a small percentage of the population is literate. In 1901, official estimates recorded 733 primary schools with 938 teachers and 41,587 pupils. The cost of maintenance was estimated at 585,365 bolivianos, averaging approximately 14.07 bolivianos per pupil.

School enrollment is low, with only one in 43.7 of the population enrolled, a figure significantly lower than the one in 10 ratio found in Argentina. While nearly half of the schools are maintained by the national government, the Church, or private means, they are largely under the control of municipalities. Overall, the Church practically supervises all educational matters.

## Higher and Specialized Instruction
In 1901, there were 14 institutions of superior instruction and 13 of secondary instruction. Secondary instruction is managed by the universities, while general public instruction is directed by a cabinet minister.

Universities are located in the following cities:
* Sucre (Chuquisaca)
* La Paz
* Cochabamba
* Tarija
* Potosi
* Santa Cruz
* Oruro

All of these institutions provide instruction in law. Theology is taught in the first four listed, and medicine is taught in the first three. Of these, only the universities at La Paz and Sucre (which dates back to colonial times) are considered sufficiently well-equipped to merit the title of university.

Specialized education is provided through several other institutions:
* A military academy in La Paz.
* An agricultural school at Umala (La Paz department).
* A mining and civil engineering school in Oruro.
* Commercial schools in Trinidad and Sucre.
* Various mission schools.

The total appropriation for educational purposes in 1901 was 756,943 bolivianos.
