# River Dynamics and Geographic Distributions

## Physical Geography of River Mouths
In physical geography, a bar is defined as a ridge of silt or sand that crosses an estuary. These formations may be raised above sea-level by wave action or remain underwater, and they typically serve as impediments to navigation. A bar is formed when a river enters a tidal sea; at this point, the rate of flow is checked, causing the material the river carries in suspension to be deposited in a shifting bar that crosses the channel from bank to bank. If the channel is only partially closed, this feature is referred to as a "spit."

Bars can also be produced exclusively by tidal action in narrow gulfs or estuaries, such as Port Adelaide, where tides sweep loose sand back and forth and deposit it where the water's motion is checked. An example of this occurs at Nahant Bay, Massachusetts, where Lynn Beach forms a ridge that separates the bay from Lynn Harbor and connects Nahant to the mainland via a bar.

## River Systems of Asia
In Asia Minor, rivers are generally of limited importance. Many are mere torrents or flow through deep rocky gorges, and only a few are navigable for short distances from their mouths. These rivers often disappear into swallow holes, known as *duden*, after cutting deeply into the plateau's limestone formation. The longest river in Asia Minor is the Halys (Kizil Irmak), which exceeds 600 m in length. Other significant rivers flowing to the Black Sea include the Boas (Churuk Su), the Iris (Yeshil Irmak), and the Sangarius (Sakaria). Rivers flowing into the Sea of Marmora include the Rhyndacus and the Macestus, while the Granicus flows to the Sea of Marmora and the Scamander flows to the Dardanelles.

In India, the Indus is the chief river of the west, flowing south in a tortuous course into the Arabian Sea through several mouths, including the Khori, Sir, and Bhitiara creeks. The Nerbudda flows eastward for 700 m before falling into the Gulf of Cambay, where it forms the alluvial plain of Broach. The Tapti also falls into the Gulf of Cambay and is considered the most useful of the Gujarat rivers commercially. In Behar, the Ganges and its northern tributaries—including the Sone, Gandak, Ghagra, Mahananda, and Kusi—are navigable by large country boats throughout the year.

## European and American River Systems
In France, the Dordogne rises on the Puy-de-Sancy and flows 295 m to unite with the Garonne, forming the Gironde estuary. The influence of high tides reaches as far as Pessac, 100 m from the ocean. In England, the Yorkshire Derwent collects drainage from the North Yorkshire moors and is tidal up to Sutton-upon-Derwent.

In Ireland, the rivers of Kerry include the Blackwater, which forms part of the boundary between Cork and Kerry, and the Flesk, which becomes the Laune. The Feale, Gale, and Brick rivers join to form the Cashin, a short tidal river that enters the Shannon estuary.

In Central America, the San Juan drains Lake Nicaragua and flows 95 m to the Colorado Mouth in Costa Rica. On the Pacific coast, rivers are generally rapid and prone to sudden floods. The Tempisque river enters the Pacific at the Gulf of Nicoya, where its alluvial deposits tend to silt up the shallow inlet.

## Tidal Influence and Navigation
Tidal action significantly affects various river systems. In the district containing the Meghna, Arial Khan, and Haringhata (also known as the Baleswar, Madhumati, or Garai), all rivers are subject to tidal action from the Bay of Bengal to the south and the Meghna to the north. The Meghna, which carries the combined waters of the Ganges and Brahmaputra, expands into a large estuary with many islands, such as Dakshin Shahbazpur, before entering the Bay of Bengal.

Navigation varies by region and season. The Arial Khan is navigable year-round by large native cargo boats, with its width expanding from 1700 yards in the dry season to between 2000 and 3000 yards during the rains. Similarly, the Haringhata is navigable by large sea-going ships as far up as Morrellganj. In India, the Nerbudda is navigable by small boats in all seasons and by vessels of 30 to 50 tons during the rains.
