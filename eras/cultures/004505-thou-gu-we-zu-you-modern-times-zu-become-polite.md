# Personal Pronouns in Various Languages and Texts

The study of personal pronouns reveals significant variations in grammatical structure, the evolution of politeness, and the application of address across different linguistic systems and historical texts.

## Basque Pronouns and Numerals
In the Basque language, the personal pronouns include *ni* for "I," *hi* for "thou," *gu* for "we," and *zu* for "you." In modern usage, *zu* has evolved into a polite form of "thou." To express a true plural "you" (referring to more than one person), the pluralizing sign *k--zuek* is suffixed. 

The third-person pronouns function as demonstratives: *hura* or *kura* ("that"), *hau* or *kau* ("this"), and *ori* or *kori* ("this" or "that"). These demonstratives can also serve as articles, such as in *gazt-en-or* ("this younger one") or *andre-ori* ("this lady at some distance"). Other forms found in verbal inflexions include *d*, *it*, and *t* for "I" or "me." For example, *d-akus-t* translates to "it see I" (I see it), and *d-arrai-t* means "it follows me."

The Basque language uses *buru* ("head") to express the reflective "self." While a relative pronoun does not exist, a verbal participle with the ending *n* is used instead (e.g., *doa*, "he goes"; *doana*, "he who is going"). However, modern Basque has adopted the interrogatives *zein* and *zoin* as relatives, imitating Spanish or French. Common interrogatives include *nor* ("who"), *zer* ("what"), and *zembait* ("how much"). Indefinite pronouns include *bat* ("one"), *batzu* ("several"), *bakotch* ("each"), *norbait* ("some one"), *hanitz* or *hainitz* ("much"), and *elkar* ("both").

The Basque numeral system is vicesimal. For instance, 34 is expressed as *hogoi ta hamalaur* ("twenty and fourteen"). The numbers one through ten are *bat*, *bi*, *hiru*, *lau*, *bortz* or *bost*, *sei*, *zazpi*, *zortzi*, *bederatzi*, and *hamar*. Twenty is *hogoi* or *hogei*, forty is *berrogoi* (twice twenty), and one hundred is *ehun*. There is no genuine word for a thousand.

## Pronoun Evolution in Other Languages
In certain other languages, personal pronouns have lost their old nominatives, replacing them with new nominatives derived from the oblique base. In polite conversation, singular forms in the first and second persons have fallen into disuse. Instead, plurals are used honorifically for the singular, similar to the English use of "you" for "thou." New plurals are then formed from the new singular (old plural) bases.

In one specific language (A.), the old singular of the first person is retained, and the old plural continues to function properly. Examples of pronouns in the Bg. language include *mui* (old) and *ami* (modern) for "I"; *tui* (old) and *tumi* (modern) for "thou"; *se* or *tini* for "he"; *e* or *ini* for "this"; and *o* or *uni* for "that." Interrogatives include *je* or *jini* ("who"), *ke* ("who?"), and *ki* ("what?"). Other forms include *kon* ("what" as an adjective), *keha* ("anyone"), *kichu* ("anything"), and *kona* ("any"). In the O. language, the words for "I" and "thou" are *ambhe* and *tumbhe*.

## Pronouns in Sacred and Historical Texts
The use of "thou," "you," "we," and "I" appears frequently in historical and sacred narratives to define relationships and commands.

### The Qur'an
The Qur'an presents various interactions using these pronouns. In the dialogue between Abraham and his father, Abraham addresses his father as "O my father," questioning why he worships that which neither sees nor hears nor avails him. Abraham tells his father, "knowledge has come to me which has not come to you," and urges him to follow him to a right path. He warns his father not to serve Satan, describing Satan as a rebel against Allah the Most Gracious or the Beneficent, fearing that a penalty might afflict his father and make him a friend or comrade of the devil. The father responds by asking Abraham, "Dost thou hate my gods?" and threatens to stone him if he does not forbear.

Other passages involve commands and warnings. Allah tells Adam and his wife to dwell in the Garden and enjoy its goods as they wish, but commands them not to approach a specific tree to avoid harm and transgression. Satan later whispers to them, claiming their Lord only forbade the tree to prevent them from becoming angels or beings who live forever.

In other accounts, a mortal is challenged to bring a sign if he is of the truthful. A she-camel is presented with a right to water, and the people are told they also have a right to water on an appointed day, with a warning not to touch her with harm lest the penalty of a Great Day seize them.

The texts also record dialogues regarding wealth and judgment. One individual asks another why he did not say "Allah's will (be done)" upon entering his garden, noting that while he may be seen as inferior in wealth and children, the Lord may provide something better.

Regarding faith and judgment, the texts mention those who ask if they shall receive rewards and punishments after they die and become dust and bones. In addresses to the faithful, the command is given to stand steadfast as commanded and to judge justly, stating, "Allah is our Lord and your Lord: for us (is the responsibility for) our deeds, and for you for your deeds."

### Herodotus
In the writings of Herodotus, Croesus addresses his son, stating that the son has partly prevailed with him through his judgment about a dream, and therefore allows him to go to the chase. Croesus also addresses Adrastos the Phrygian, reminding him that when Adrastos was struck by misfortune, Croesus cleansed him and received him into his house. Croesus asks Adrastos to protect his son during the chase and encourages Adrastos to become famous through his deeds, as it is an inheritance from his fathers.
