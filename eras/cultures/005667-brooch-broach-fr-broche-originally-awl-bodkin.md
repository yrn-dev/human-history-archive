# BROOCH

The term brooch (also spelled broach) refers to a clasp or fastener used for dress. It is characterized by a pin and is equipped with a catch or loop at one end and a hinge or spring at the other. The word is derived from the French *broche*, which originally referred to a bodkin or an awl.

## Etymology and Related Terms
The word "brooch" is a doublet of "broach." While the former refers to the dress fastener, "broach" (from the French *broche* and Medieval Latin *brocca*) denotes various pointed instruments. These include bodkins, spits for roasting meat, and wooden needles used in tapestry-making. In a technical context, a "rimer" or broach is a tool used with a wrench to smooth or enlarge holes. 

The use of such pointed instruments to tap casks led to the phrase "to broach a barrel." This linguistic root further extends to the term "broker," which according to the *New English Dictionary* comes from the Latin *brocca* (spike) and *broccare* (to broach). This evolved through the Old French *vendre à broche*, meaning to retail wine from the tap, eventually leading to the general sense of mercantile dealing.

## Historical Types and Styles
Brooches have appeared in various forms across different cultures and eras, often serving as both functional fasteners and decorative ornaments.

### Classical and Early European Forms
In Greek and Roman sculptures, the circular medallion brooch is commonly depicted as a stud used to fasten a cloak on the shoulder. This style is also found in Etruscan deposits. In Roman provinces, these circular brooches were numerous and frequently decorated with enamel, paste, or inlaid stone. Another early variety featured animal forms; in early versions, the animal was a decorative appendage, but in later styles, the animal formed the body of the brooch with a pin attached underneath.

Between the 5th and 8th centuries, medallion, animal, and T-shaped brooches were found in Frankish cemeteries. These were crafted from bronze, silver, or gold and adorned with enamel, filigree, or precious stones, though the pins were almost always made of iron. Scandinavian T-shaped brooches began similarly to Frankish styles but grew more massive over time, incorporating intricate devices and animal forms, possibly influenced by Irish missionaries.

### Celtic and Viking Styles
The Celtic group is distinguished by a penannular ring and a long pin. The ring was inserted through a hole at the head of the pin and could be partially turned after the pin was thrust through the material, allowing the brooch to function as a buckle. These were typically made of silver or bronze and featured engraved or chased "dragonesque" or interlaced designs, similar to 7th, 8th, and 9th-century illuminated Celtic manuscripts. The Hunterston brooch, found at Hawking Craig in Ayrshire, is a prominent example.

Viking hoards found on Scottish soil have yielded immense silver brooches with pins reaching 15 inches in length and penannular rings ending in large knobs resembling thistle heads.

### Medieval and Highland Variations
In the medieval period, brooches often took the form of a simple flat circular disk with an open center and a pin equal in length to the disk's diameter. These were sometimes inscribed with talismanic or religious formulae. Highland brooches followed this circular form but featured a broader disk and a smaller central opening. They were decorated with foliage and interlacing patterns arranged in geometrical spaces, occasionally including animal figures.

## Cultural and Archaeological Contexts
In ancient Greece, the Argives and Eginetans established a custom of making their brooches half as large again as the standard size of the time, offering them specifically in the temples of certain goddesses.

Archaeologically, silver brooches have been recovered from "brochs" and other sites in Scotland. Examples of such antiquities are housed in the museum at Dunrobin Castle in Sutherlandshire.

## Related Textile Terms
The Latin *broccus* is also related to the French *brocher* and *brocarts*, implying a form of broaching or stitching. This led to the term "brocades" for fabrics woven to look as if they have been stitched. Historical examples include:
*   **Siculo-Saracenic weavings:** 11th and 12th-century specimens where animal and bird heads were broched with gold thread.
*   **Imperial robes:** A sumptuous weaving of gold threads and coloured silks produced at the Hotel des Tiraz in Palermo for Emperor Henry IV (1165-1197).
*   **English wardrobe accounts:** A 1480 entry for King Edward IV describes "satyn broched with gold."
