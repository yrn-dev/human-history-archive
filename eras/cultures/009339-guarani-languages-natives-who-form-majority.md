# Guarani

## Overview and Distribution
Guarani is a language spoken by native populations in South America. In Bolivia, it is identified as one of the languages of the natives, who constitute a majority of the population. Within the southern region of the Bolivian plain, the Chiriguanos—a detached tribe of the Guarani race—reside, having drifted westward. Additionally, the use of native idioms, including those of the Guarani, is noted in some parts of Paraguay and Argentina.

## Linguistic Context in Bolivia
In Bolivia, the linguistic landscape is divided between a dominant minority that speaks Spanish and a native majority. Along with Quichua and Aymara, Guarani serves as a primary language for the native people. A significant percentage of the Indian population does not understand Spanish, and some actively resist efforts to force the language upon them. This linguistic divide extends to the *cholos* (mestizos), who are more familiar with native idioms than with Spanish.

## Social and Educational Factors
The prevalence of native languages like Guarani is linked to the state of education in Bolivia. Despite having a free and compulsory school system, progress has been limited, and only a small percentage of the population is literate. As of 1901, school enrolment was low, with only one student for every 43.7 people. 

The total appropriation for educational purposes in 1901 was 756,943 bolivianos. While the national government, private means, and the Church maintain nearly half of the primary schools, all educational matters are practically under the supervision of the Church.

## Demographic Composition
The population of Bolivia consists of Indians, Caucasians of European origin, and *mestizos* (a mixture of the two), with a very small percentage of Africans. According to a roughly-taken census from 1900, 50.7% of the population were classed as Indians. This group includes the civilized tribes of the Andes—such as the Aymaras south of Lake Titicaca—as well as those from the Chiquitos and Mojos regions. The latter were organized into industrial communities by the Jesuits in the 17th century. Additionally, the 1900 census estimated that there were 91,000 wild Indians living in the forest regions.
