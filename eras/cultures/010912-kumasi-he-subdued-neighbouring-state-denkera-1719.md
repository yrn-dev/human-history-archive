# Kumasi

Kumasi is the capital of the Ashanti kingdom and serves as the distributing centre for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti. Historically, the city's development and status were closely tied to the expansion of Ashanti power and its subsequent conflicts with British forces.

## Early History and Development
Osai Tutu is regarded as the real founder of Ashanti power. He is credited with either building or greatly extending Kumasi. Under his leadership, the Ashanti empire expanded through conquests to the east and west, including the subduing of the neighbouring state of Denkera in 1719, as well as the Banna and Gaman (Mahommedan) countries. 

At the height of its prosperity, from the middle of the 18th to the middle of the 19th century, Kumasi was a city of high culture. It featured numerous broad, regular streets, with a main avenue measuring 70 yards in width. The city included a large market-place to the south-east and a "Spirit House" located in a grove of trees, which served as the place of execution. The royal palace was constructed of red sandstone and was believed to be modelled after Dutch buildings found at Elmina. In the first quarter of the 19th century, the population of Kumasi is estimated not to have exceeded 20,000, partly because Kintampo, located further north, served as the commercial capital and meeting-place for several caravan routes.

## British Conflicts and Destruction
Kumasi was the site of several major British military interventions. In 1873, following tensions over the transference of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British and the captivity of Europeans—including members of the Basel mission such as Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer and his family—an Ashanti force invaded the British protectorate. In response, Sir Garnet Wolseley led an expedition to Kumasi. On February 6, 1874, Wolseley's forces blew up the red sandstone royal palace.

Following this destruction, the city experienced a marked decline. While a new royal palace was eventually built, it was made of clay rather than brick, and wide stretches of grass-grown country appeared within the former town limits. Further damage occurred in 1896 when the British destroyed several of the largest and most ancient houses in the priestly and royal suburb of Bantama by fire.

## The Rebellion of 1900
After the deportation of Prempeh, no successor was appointed to the Ashanti throne. Kumasi was administered by chiefs under the guidance of a British resident, Captain Donald W. Stewart, and a fort was established and garrisoned by the Gold Coast constabulary. 

In 1900, a serious rebellion broke out involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes. The conflict was precipitated by Governor Sir F. Hodgson's demand that Ashanti chiefs pay 4000 oz. of gold annually and his reproaches regarding the "golden stool," which the Kumasi had hidden since 1896. On April 29, 1900, Kumasi warriors attacked the fort in force but were repulsed. A garrison of 700 men, including Lagos and Gold Coast constabulary, eventually held the fort.

The siege ended in July 1900 when a relief expedition led by Colonel James Willcocks fought through dense bush and mire. After a bayonet charge by Yoruba troops against the main rebel stockade on July 15, the relieving force entered Kumasi. The city was found in a state of ruin, with putrid bodies and burnt-down houses surrounding the fort.

## Economy and Infrastructure
Kumasi is a vital commercial hub. Its trade is supported by several modes of communication:
* **Rail and Road:** A railway from Sekondi, completed in 1903, passes through the gold-bearing regions to reach Kumasi. Additionally, a 141-mile wide road connects the city to Cape Coast.
* **Traditional Routes:** Ancient caravan routes extend from Kumasi to inland trading centres, and forest travel is often conducted via narrow paths or by palanquin borne by four carriers.
* **Communications:** The city is connected to the Northern Territories and coast towns via telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service.

The region's economy is based on various vegetable products, most notably the rubber tree (*Funtumia elastica*), kola nuts, and tobacco in the north. Since 1905, cocoa plantations have been established, particularly in the eastern districts. Gold mining is prominent in the western districts; in 1905, the gold output from Ashanti was 68,259 oz., valued at L254,790, with much of the gold coming from the Obuassi mines. Revenue for the region is derived from liquor licences, government land rents, caravan taxes, and contributions from gold-mining companies.
