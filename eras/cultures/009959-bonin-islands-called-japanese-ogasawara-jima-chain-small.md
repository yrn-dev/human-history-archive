# Bonin Islands

The Bonin Islands, known in Japanese as Ogasawara-jima, are a chain of small islands belonging to Japan. They stretch nearly due north and south, located a little east of 142 E. and between 26 deg. 35' and 27 deg. 45' N. The archipelago is situated approximately 500 miles from the mainland of Japan.

## Geography and Topography
According to Japanese investigations, the chain consists of twenty islands with a total superficies of 28.82 sq. m. and a coastline measuring 174.65 m. Only ten of these islands are of appreciable size. Listed from north to south, they are:
* Muko-shima (Bridegroom Island)
* Nakadachi-shima (Go-between Island)
* Yome-shima (Bride Island)
* Ototo-jima (Younger-brother Island)
* Ani-shima (Elder-brother Island)
* Chichi-jima (Father Island)
* Haha-jima (Mother Island)
* Mei-jima (Niece Island)
* Ani-jima (Elder-sister Island)
* Imoto-jima (Younger-sister Island)

The islands are volcanic in nature. Port Lloyd, the chief anchorage located on Peel Island (Chichi-jima), was observed by Commodore Perry in 1853 to have likely been the crater of a volcano. Perry noted that the surrounding hills were thrown up from this crater and that the harbor entrance was a fissure through which lava once poured into the sea. There are no mountains of any considerable height on the islands.

## Nomenclature
European geographers have historically divided the islands into three groups: the Parry Islands in the north, the Beechey Islands in the center, and the Coffin or Bailey Islands in the south. 

In 1827, Captain Beechey assigned several European names to the islands and their features. He called Chichi-jima "Peel Island," Ototo-jima "Stapleton Island," and Ani-jima "Buckland Island." Additionally, he named a large bay on the south of Peel Island "Fitton Bay" and a bay on the south-west of Buckland Island "Walker Bay."

The Japanese name *Bunin-jima* (which foreigners corrupted into "Bonin") refers to the islands being without inhabitants (*bu* meaning without, *nin* meaning inhabitants).

## History and Settlement
Japanese annals state that the islands were discovered toward the end of the 16th century and added to the fief of a Daimyo, Ogasawa Sadayori, which provided the name Ogasawara-jima. However, effective occupation was not maintained. In 1635, communications with the islands ceased entirely due to a Japanese government veto on the construction of sea-going vessels. Communication was briefly and fitfully restored in 1728 by a representative of the Ogasawara family, but was interrupted again until 1861.

In 1830, a small party consisting of a Dane, two American citizens, and two British subjects sailed from the Sandwich Islands to Port Lloyd, accompanied by some Hawaiian natives. This group settled on Peel Island (Chichi-jima) and hoisted the British flag. 

By 1853, when Commodore Perry visited, the population of Peel Island was thirty-one, consisting of four Americans, four English, one Portuguese, and the remainder being natives of the Ladrones and the Sandwich Islands. Perry strongly urged the establishment of a United States coaling station at the site. By 1875, Russell Robertson reported that the colony had grown to sixty-nine people, though only five were "pure whites." Robertson described the settlers as living comfortably on cultivated land, but noted they lacked laws, religion, education, and a system of government. The settlers spoke English and considered themselves a British colony.

## Administration and Modern Status
In 1861, an attempt was made to establish a Japanese colony at Port Lloyd, though it was unsuccessful. That same year, the British government renounced all claims to the islands, recognizing Japan's right of possession. 

Following this transition, the islands became duly administered and established regular steam communication. The population grew to approximately 4,500.
