# The Orthodox Churches of the Balkans

## Ecclesiastical Structure and Governance
The majority of the Christian population in the Balkan Peninsula belongs to the Orthodox Church. Nominally, the oecumenical patriarch at Constantinople serves as the head of this church, holding precedence over all other ecclesiastical dignitaries. However, in reality, the Greek, Montenegrin, Servian, and Bulgarian churches are autocephalous.

The Bulgarian church occupies an exceptional position within this structure. Its spiritual chief, known as the exarch and based in Constantinople, maintains control over Bulgarian prelates located both in the kingdom of Bulgaria and in European Turkey. In contrast, Greek prelates situated in Bulgaria remain subject to the patriarch.

## Religious and Political Intersections
In eastern Europe, religious and political matters are intimately linked. The Turkish government recognizes the heads of various religious communities as the sole representatives of the Christian population. These leaders supervise Christian schools and hold seats on local administrative councils.

Because no difference of dogma divides the various branches of the Orthodox Church, the efforts by these branches to establish separate organizations within Turkish dominions are attributed exclusively to political motives. For example, the Servian and Rumanian movements have historically been at a disadvantage compared to the Bulgarian movement due to their lack of a separate ecclesiastical organization; the Serbs and orthodox Vlachs in Turkey have traditionally owed allegiance to the Greek patriarchate.

To address this, the governments of Belgrade and Bucharest sought the recognition of Servian and Vlach *millets*. The Rumanian *millet* was recognized by the Porte via iradé on May 23, 1905. The Servians sought the restoration of the patriarchate of Ipek and the appointment of a Servian metropolitan at Usküb. While the Servians succeeded in obtaining the nomination of Mgr. Firmilian, a Servian, to the archbishopric of Usküb in 1902 with Russian aid, their broader aims for a separate organization have not been fully realized.

## Other Christian Denominations
While the Orthodox Church is the largest, other Christian faiths are present in the region:
*   **Roman Catholic Church:** This church includes approximately 21% of Bosnians, some of the Gheg tribes in Albania, the Serbo-Croats of Croatia-Slavonia and Dalmatia, a small number of Greeks in the islands, and a few Bulgarians in Macedonia and the kingdom. In the Montenegrin seaport of Dulcigno, there is a Roman Catholic cathedral and an ancient Latin church.
*   **Uniate Church:** A "uniate" church exists among a certain number of Bulgars at Kukush in Macedonia and other locations; this group accepts the dogma and authority of Rome while preserving other traditions.

## Demographics and Cultural Context
The religious landscape of the Peninsula was heavily influenced by the Turkish conquest, which led to numerous conversions to Islam. The total Moslem population of the Peninsula is approximately 3,300,000. Conversion rates varied by nationality: more than half of the Albanian nation and 35% of the inhabitants of Bosnia and Herzegovina adopted Islam. Conversions were less frequent among Greeks—where followers of Islam are almost confined to Crete—and Bulgars, among whom the Mahommedans (known as Pomaks) are numerically insignificant.

Culturally, the influence of the church extended to education. Until recently, Greek and Turkish were the only officially recognized or systematically taught languages in Balkan lands under Turkish rule. Due to the privileges acquired by the Greek church and the intellectual superiority of the Greeks, Greek became the language of the Christian upper classes.
