## Linguistic Structures in Bantu Languages
In the Bantu languages, the verbal root typically terminates in the vowel *a*. This terminal vowel can be modified to change the grammatical function of the word. For example, changing the terminal vowel to *e* forms the subjunctive tense, while changing it to *i* can provide a negative sense in specific tenses.

This mechanism of changing the terminal vowel, sometimes in conjunction with adding a personal prefix, allows for the creation of nouns from verbs. In Luganda, the verbal root for "to pardon" is *senyua*. By modifying the terminal vowel, the word for "a pardon" or "forgiveness" becomes *ki-senyuo*, and "a pardoner" is expressed as *mu-senyui*. Similarly, in Swahili, the root *patani[vs]a* (meaning "conciliate") can be transformed into *mpatana[vs]i* ("conciliator") or *upatani[vs]o* ("conciliation").

## Teachings on Pardon in the Qur'an
The Qur'an presents a vision of life where Allah is described as Pardoning, Forgiving, Clement, and Most Merciful. The text outlines several contexts regarding pardon and forgiveness:

### Divine Forgiveness
The text states that for those who are weak and oppressed—including children, women, and men who lack the means or a way to escape—there is hope that Allah will pardon them, as He blots out sins and forgives repeatedly. Furthermore, the text notes that Allah is "Oft-Forgiving" toward those who forsake their homes in His cause and die as refugees for Allah and His Messenger.

### Human Conduct and Repentance
The archive's sources emphasize the importance of repentance and sincerity. Those who repent, amend their ways, hold fast to Allah, and remain sincere in their religion are associated with the believers, for whom Allah grants a mighty reward. The text also suggests that Allah does not chastise those who believe and are grateful.

Regarding interpersonal conduct, the text teaches that Allah does not love the public utterance of hurtful or harsh speech, except in cases where a person has been wronged or injustice has been done. It encourages the act of forgiving evil or covering evil with pardon, noting that Allah is Powerful and Pardoning.

### Guidance for Leadership
The text describes the mercy of Allah as the reason for dealing gently with others. It warns that being stern, fierce, or hard-hearted would cause people to disperse. Consequently, the guidance provided is to pardon others, ask for forgiveness on their behalf, and consult with them in affairs of moment.

## Historical and Mythological Accounts
Pardon is documented in various historical and narrative contexts:

### Classical Narratives
In the account of Glaucos, the son of Epikydes, Glaucos attempted to rob others by using an oath. After being warned by a prophetess about a nameless, handless, and footless son of "Oath" who destroys the house of those who offend, Glaucos entreated the god to pardon him. Despite his request and his subsequent act of returning the money to the Milesians, the archive records that Glaucos was eventually utterly destroyed and rooted up from Sparta.

### Political and Military History
Historical records mention specific instances of legal and political pardon.
