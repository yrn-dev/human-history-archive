# Acer, Carpinus, Corylus, and Coniferae

## Acer (Maples)
The genus *Acer*, belonging to the order *Acerineae*, consists of approximately sixty species native to Asia (particularly China, Japan, and the Himalayas), North America, and Europe. These plants are generally trees characterized by long-stalked, palmately lobed leaves that grow in opposite arrangements. Their flowers may appear in fascicles before the leaves, or in panicles or racemes appearing with or after the leaves. Some flowers are imperfect, featuring aborted pistils or stamens. The resulting fruit is a two-winged "samara."

The common maple (*Acer campestre*) is the only species indigenous to Great Britain, though it also occurs in northern Asia, the Caucasus, and northern Europe. It is frequently found in hedgerows, typically reaching heights of 20 to 30 feet. Its leaves are generally less than 2 inches across with five blunt main lobes. The wood of *A. campestre* is highly valued as a fine-grained, compact material that takes a high polish, making it celebrated since antiquity for use in tables. Additionally, its roots provide knotted wood for cabinet work, its young shoots are used in France as whips, and the wood serves as an excellent fuel for charcoal. Another species, *Acer Pseudoplatanus* (common sycamore), produces fruit that divides into two mericarps. In North America, the large leaved maple (*Acer macrophyllum*) is noted as an important tree.

## Carpinus and Corylus
*Carpinus betulus*, the common hornbeam or yoke-elm, is a member of the natural order *Corylaceae*. It is indigenous to Europe, Asia Minor, and temperate parts of western Asia. In Europe, it ranges as far north as 56 and 55 degrees N. lat., and is common in the hedges and woods of southern England and Wales. The tree features a smooth, light grey bark and a flattened trunk that appears to be composed of several united stems. Its elliptic-ovate leaves are doubly toothed, hairy below, and opaque.

The common hornbeam can reach heights of 40 to 70 feet and is characterized by a closer, rounder head than the beech. While rarely grown for timber, it is highly valued for hedges; Evelyn noted in 1664 that it creates the "noblest and the stateliest hedges" for parks or gardens due to its deciduous leaves and ability to withstand clipping. Its fruit is a green, angular "nut" about 1/4 inch long, resembling a small chestnut and enclosed in 3-lobed leafy bracts. The tree thrives in moist, stiff, clayey soils but does not flourish in gravel or chalk.

Other related species include *Carpinus americana* (also known as *C. caroliniana*), the American hornbeam or water beech. A closely allied genus is *Ostrya*, which includes *Ostrya vulgaris* (common hop-hornbeam) from southern Europe and *Ostrya virginiana* from America, the latter of which is called ironwood due to its tight, close-grained, hard wood.

## Coniferae
The Coniferae are classified as gymnospermous plants, meaning their seeds are "naked" and lack a true pericarp. In these plants, the endosperm is formed prior to fertilization. Many Coniferae are polycotyledonous, possessing more than two cotyledons arranged in a whorl; for example, the larch, spruce, fir, and pine have been observed to have between six and fifteen linear cotyledons.

Various species of Coniferae are found in different global regions. In the mountains of northern Persia and Asia Minor, they are found alongside *Acer*, *Carpinus*, *Corylus*, *Fagus*, *Ulmus*, and *Quercus*. Notably, *Fagus* is the only genus among these that is not found in the Himalaya. In Canada, important Coniferae include the Douglas fir (*Pseudotsuga Douglasii*), which is a principal timber of commerce reaching 300 feet in height, as well as the western larch (*Larix occidentalis*), white spruce (*Picea alba*), Engellmann's spruce (*Picea Engelmanii*), Menzies's spruce (*Picea sitchensis*), white mountain pine (*Pinus monticola*), black pine (*Pinus murrayana*), yellow pine (*Pinus ponderosa*), giant cedar (*Thuya gigantea*), yellow cypress or cedar (*Thuya excelsa*), and western hemlock (*Tsuga mertensiana*).
