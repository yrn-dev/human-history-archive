# The Gobi Desert and Central Asia

The central area of the Asian continent, bounded to the north and north-west by the Yablonoi Mountains and their western extension in the Tian-shan, to the south by the northern face of the Tibetan plateau, and to the east by the Khingan range, constitutes the great desert of central Asia known as the Gobi. This vast interior region is characterized as an almost waterless space where local drainage is lost to swamps or deserts.

## Geography and Regional Divisions
The Gobi's eastern portion is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia, while its western portion forms eastern or Chinese Turkestan. The region is defined by a central depression that extends westwards through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan, beginning on the east of the Gobi. To the west of Kashgar, this depression is limited by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol, of which the Sarikol serves as the eastern face.

## Elevation and Topography
The Gobi is a high-altitude desert; it appears likely that no part of the region is less than 2,000 ft. above sea level. However, the level of the central depression—which was once a vast inland sea—probably never exceeds 2,000 ft. above sea level between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the sources of the Hwang-ho. Modern research suggests that in central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, the land may actually be below sea level.

Other specific elevations in the surrounding and intersecting areas include:
* **Kashgar and Yarkand:** The plain ranges from 4,000 to 6,000 ft.
* **Northern Mongolia:** Elevations are between 4,000 and 6,000 ft.
* **Route to Kiakhta:** No portion of the route across the desert from the Chinese frontier to Kiakhta falls below 3,000 ft.

## Surrounding Mountain Ranges
The central area is enclosed and traversed by several significant mountain systems:

**The Yablonoi and Khingan Ranges**
The Yablonoi range continues in a direction that divides the waters of the river Lena (flowing into the Arctic Sea) from the river Amur (falling into the North Pacific). The basin of the Amur and its affluents constitutes Manchuria. From the north of Manchuria, the Khingan range extends southward to the Chinese frontier near Peking. The region west of the Khingan range is almost rainless and inclines toward Mongolia.

**The Tian-shan and Ala-tau**
Extending from the western end of the Yablonoi range on the 115th meridian is a mountainous belt reaching the extremity of Pamir. The most important of these is the Tian-shan, or Celestial Mountains, which form the northern boundary of the Gobi. Located between 75 deg. and 95 deg. E. and 40 deg. and 43 deg. N., some of its summits are said to exceed 20,000 ft. The principal towns and cultivated districts of central Asia are situated along the foot of this range. The Ala-tau, a prolongation of the Tian-shan, rises to 14,000 or 15,000 ft. and acts as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes.

**The Altai**
Located near the 50th parallel, the Altai rises to 10,000 or 12,000 ft. This range separates the waters of western Siberia's great rivers from those collecting in Kalka, Dzungaria, and the lakes of north-west Mongolia.

## The Continental Divide
The region is part of a great central water-parting or divide that stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and Elburz to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, eventually reaching the Bering Strait. While some parts of this divide, such as the Altai, Khangai, and Tian-shan, reach great heights, a considerable portion remains below 5,000 ft. South of this divide, the land drops to the central depression of the Gobi.
