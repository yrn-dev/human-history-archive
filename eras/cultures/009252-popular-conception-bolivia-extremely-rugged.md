# Bolivia

## Physiography and Geography
While the popular conception of Bolivia is that of an extremely rugged mountainous country, three-fifths of the nation consists of gently undulating forest regions, great swamps, flooded bottomlands, and low alluvial plains, including the Chiquitos region. Roughly two-fifths of the total area is comprised of the Andean cordilleras, which cross the south-west corner and project east toward the Brazilian highlands. These cordilleras consist of two great parallel chains with flanking ranges and spurs, enclosing deep ravines and great elevated plateaus.

The western range, the Cordillera Occidental, forms the western rampart of the *alta-planicie* (great Bolivian tableland). This plateau extends 500 miles long and about 80 miles broad, covering approximately 40,000 square miles. It is divided into the *puna* in the north and the "desert of Lipez" in the south. The plateau has an average elevation of about 12,650 feet near Lake Titicaca, though it descends about 1,000 feet toward its southern extremity. The highest peak of the plateau is Cerro de Tahua at 17,454 feet, while overlooking peaks to the west include Sajama (21,047 feet), Huallatiri (21,654 feet), and Parinacota (20,918 feet).

In the lowlands, the extreme south contains the Bolivian Chaco and the *llanos* (open grassy plains) of Manzo. To the north, the llanos of Guarayos and Mojos occupy a region once covered by the ancient Mojos Lake. This area contains rich agricultural districts and open plains used for cattle-raising since the era of Jesuit missions. The lower slopes of the Andes toward the north-west are covered in heavy forests and are capable of sustaining a large population.

## River Systems
Bolivian river systems are divided into three distinct regions: the Central Plateau, La Plata, and the Amazon. The Amazon system covers more than one-half of the republic, consisting of rivers flowing into the Madeira. The two principal rivers are the Beni and the Mamore. 

The Mamore rises on the north-east slopes of the Sierra de Cochabamba and flows north for about 600 miles; three-fourths of this course is navigable. The Guapay (or Grande) is considered the main stream above its confluence with the Mamore. It rises in the Cordillera Oriental, skirts the Llanos de Chiquitos, and follows a tortuous, non-navigable course of over 700 miles. Other significant tributaries include the Yacuma, which drains a region of swamps and lakes, as well as the Secure, Chapare, and Manique.

## Flora and Fauna
The most notable animals in Bolivia are the Camelidae: the llama, alpaca, vicuna, and guanaco. These animals inhabit the steppes of Patagonia and the arid plateaus of the Andes. The llama and alpaca were domesticated before the discovery of America, while the vicuna and guanaco remain wild. 

The llama is utilized as a pack animal and for coarse wool used in native garments. The alpaca is highly prized for its fine wool, a staple export, though the animal is difficult to rear. The vicuna is celebrated for wool used in costly *ponchos* (blanket cloaks). The guanaco is hunted for its skin to make robes or rugs, though the slaughter of both the guanaco and vicuna is reducing their numbers. The rearing of llamas and alpacas is an industry managed exclusively by Indians.

Regarding forest products, the most profitable industry was rubber-collecting, which began between 1880 and 1890. Before the transfer of the best rubber forests to Brazil in 1903, the annual output reached nearly 3,500 metric tons. The rubber-producing *Hevea* is still found on the Madre de Dios and Beni. Additionally, Bolivia produces cinchona bark for quinine sulphate; however, this industry is destructive and the forests are diminishing.

## Political and Diplomatic History
In the late 19th century, Bolivia experienced political instability, with President Melgarejo deposed in January 1871 by Colonel Augustin Morales. Morales was murdered in November 1872 and succeeded by Colonel Adolfo Ballivian, who died in 1874.

Diplomatic tensions existed with Chile regarding territorial and commercial rights. A treaty in 1866 established the 24th parallel as the boundary of Chilean territory and granted Chile a half share of customs and tax-free mining and export rights between the 23rd and 24th parallels. In 1870, Bolivia granted an Anglo-Chilean company the right to work nitrate deposits north of the 24th parallel for $10,000. Discontent over Chilean capital in the Tarapaca district led Bolivia to enter a secret agreement with Peru on February 6, 1873, for mutual defense and the preservation of territorial integrity against possible Chilean encroachment.

Later, on May 18, 1895, a treaty was signed at Santiago between Chile and Bolivia to ensure Bolivia's free access to the sea. Chile agreed that if it acquired permanent sovereignty over Tacna and Arica, it would transfer them to Bolivia for an indemnity of $5,000,000 silver. If this failed, Chile bound itself to cede $5,000,000 silver and the roadstead (*caleta*) of Vitor or another analogous port.
