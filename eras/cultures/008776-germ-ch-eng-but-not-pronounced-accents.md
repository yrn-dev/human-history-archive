# Phonetics and Orthography of Selected Languages

The study of phonetics and orthography across various languages reveals diverse systems for representing sounds, utilizing diacritical marks, accents, and specific consonant rules to guide pronunciation.

## Bohemian (Czech) Pronunciation
The Czech (Cech) alphabet is identical to the English alphabet, with the exception of the letters q, w, and x. To distinguish variations in pronunciation, diacritical marks are used, a system originated by John Huss.

### Vowels and Accents
Vowels (a, e, i, y, o, u) are generally pronounced as they are in Italian, though "e" is pronounced as the English "ye" in "yet," and "ou" is pronounced as the English "oo." Accents placed on vowels serve to lengthen them.

### Consonants
While several consonants (b, d, f, k, l, m, n, p, r, v, z) follow English pronunciation, others differ:
* **g**: Pronounced as the English "g" in "gone."
* **s**: Pronounced as an initial English "s."
* **n**: Pronounced as the Spanish "n" (as in *canon*).
* **r**: Pronounced as "rsh."
* **s**: Pronounced as "sh."
* **z**: Pronounced as "zh" (similar to the French "j").
* **c**: Pronounced as "ts" or "ch."
* **ch**: Pronounced as the German "ch."
* **j**: Pronounced as the English "y," though it is not pronounced when it appears before d, m, or s.
* **k**: Pronounced as "g" when it appears before "d."
* **v**: Pronounced as "f" when it appears before k, p, s, or t.
* **H**: Always pronounced. At the end of words or before k and t, it sounds like the German "ch"; in other positions, such as in *bahno*, the pronunciation is softer.

Additionally, accents placed on "d" and "t" function as softening marks.

## Indo-Aryan Vernaculars
Phonetic rules vary across the Indo-Aryan languages, particularly regarding stress-accents and vowel pronunciation.

### Bihari
In Bihari, the stress-accent cannot fall on the last syllable of words with more than one syllable. Instead, the accent typically falls on the last long syllable. If no long syllables exist, the accent is moved back as far as possible, but no further than the syllable before the antepenultimate. A secondary accent may occur on the penultimate syllable if followed by an "imperfect" vowel, or on the first syllable if it does not hold the main stress. When the letter "a" follows an accented syllable, it is only half pronounced.

### Bengali, Assamese, and Oriya
These three languages share a common parent and several features. Oriya generally preserves standard Indo-Aryan accentuation, rarely placing the stress syllable further back than the antepenultimate. In contrast, Bengali throws the accent as far back as possible, which leads to contracted forms in colloquial speech where the first syllable is strongly accented. Assamese follows the accentuation of Bengali.

Regarding vowel sounds:
* **a**: In all three languages, "a" can sound like the "o" in "hot." In Oriya, the Sanskrit vowel "r" is pronounced "ru," whereas elsewhere it is "ri."
* **Bengali specifics**: A final vowel preceded by a single consonant is generally not pronounced (though in Bengali, a final "a" is freely sounded in verbs and adjectives). A medial "a" may sound like the first "o" in "promote." The syllable "ya" following a consonant may sound like a short "e" (e.g., *vyakti* as *bekti*) or an "a" sound (e.g., "bank" written as *byank*).
* **Assamese specifics**: The vowel "e" is always short.

### Standard Dialect (Sanskrit-based)
In the standard dialect, vowels match those of Sanskrit. Final short vowels (a, i, u) have disappeared in prose pronunciation, except in some local dialects. A medial "a" following an accented syllable is pronounced very lightly. When a long a, i, or u precedes an accented syllable, it is usually shortened.

## Other Phonetic Systems

### Greek and Map Nomenclature
In the Alexandrian practice of Greek, accents were added to words to aid correct reading and prevent doubt. Following an older system, accents were not written on the last syllable; if the accent fell there, a grave accent was placed on the preceding syllable.

For map nomenclature, the Royal Geographical Society suggests a system where vowels sound as in Italian and consonants as in English. In this system, the acute accent is the only one used, denoting the stressed syllable. The diphthong "ai" is pronounced as in "aisle," "au" as "ow" in "how," and "aw" as in "law."

### English Phonetic Markers
Certain phonetic guides use specific markers for English sounds:
* **j**: Used for sounds in "join," "judge," "germ," and "ginger."
* **ch**: Used for "church" or "batch."
* **_ch_**: Used for "loch" or "clachan."
* **Short marks**: A short mark over italic vowels (ă, ĕ, ŏ, ŭ) indicates an obscure or slurred sound, such as the "u" in "current" or the "o" in "sailor."
