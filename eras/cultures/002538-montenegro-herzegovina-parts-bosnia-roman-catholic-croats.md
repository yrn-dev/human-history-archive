# Montenegro, Herzegovina, and the Roman Catholic Croats

The regions of Montenegro, Herzegovina, and parts of Bosnia are located in the north-western portion of the Balkan Peninsula. These areas, along with Dalmatia and the southern half of Croatia-Slavonia, are part of a broader region historically referred to as Illyria. This region extends from Fiume to Durazzo along the eastern shore of the Adriatic and inland as far as the Servian Morava and the Danube.

## Ethnology and Migration
The current ethnographic composition of the peninsula was significantly altered by a great Slavonic immigration that began in the 3rd century A.D. and lasted for four centuries. During this period, the Byzantine Empire held nominal or actual possession of the regions south of the Danube. In the 7th century, Serbo-Croats invaded the north-western regions, which included Montenegro, Herzegovina, Bosnia, Croatia, and northern Albania. These immigrants appropriated old Roman colonies on the Adriatic coast and either assimilated or expelled the native Illyrian population. The Illyrians are represented in Dalmatia by the slavonized Mavro-Vlachs or Morlachs.

By the 12th century, a distinction had been well established by early Byzantine chroniclers between the Croats of the north-west and the Serbs of the more central region. This distinction was not based on racial or linguistic differences, but rather developed over time into a strong religious difference.

## Religious and Cultural Divisions
The religious landscape of the region was shaped by the influence of different civilizations. Along the Dalmatian and Croatian coasts, a well-developed Latin civilization existed, maintained through constant intercourse with Italy. Under this influence, Serbo-Croatian immigrants were converted to the Roman Catholic Church. Consequently, Roman Catholic Croats predominate in north-western Bosnia and Dalmatia.

In contrast, the wild and mountainous interior of the peninsula offered few rivals to the Byzantine Church. As a result, the Orthodox creed prevailed in these areas. The Orthodox Serbs inhabit Montenegro, Herzegovina, parts of Bosnia, the kingdom of Servia, and Old Servia (including north-western Macedonia and Novibazar).

## Political Administration and Conflict
In 1909, the population and area of these political divisions were recorded as follows:
*   **Bosnia and Herzegovina (Austria-Hungary):** 1,568,092 people over 19,696 sq. m.
*   **Montenegro:** 311,564 people over 3,255 sq. m.
*   **Dalmatia (Austria):** 591,597 people over 4,923 sq. m.

Following the Congress of Berlin, Austria undertook the occupation and administration of Bosnia and Herzegovina to prevent them from falling under Russian influence or remaining under Turkish control. This occupation began on July 29 with 60,000 troops under General Philippovich, eventually increasing to 200,000 men to crush strenuous resistance from Mahommedans in the mountainous terrain. Although the Porte recognized the occupation in 1879, the sovereignty of the sultan was still acknowledged.

The transition to Austrian civil administration was marked by tension. Christian populations were dissatisfied that they were still required to pay rent to Mahommedan begs. Additionally, difficulties arose between members of the Greek Church and Roman Catholics. In 1881, disturbances that began in Dalmatia spread across the frontier into Herzegovina, requiring a military expedition to restore order.

## Geography and Infrastructure
Dalmatia, a crownland of the Austro-Hungarian empire, serves as a coastal boundary for these regions. It is bounded to the south and south-east by Montenegro and Herzegovina. The Herzegovinian frontier reaches the sea only for a total distance of 14.5 miles near Castelnuovo and Klek.

Communications in the interior are often defective, with some areas accessible only via rough mountain roads. However, critical railway lines link Dalmatia with Herzegovina, terminating at Metkovic, Ragusa, and Castlenuovo on the Bocche di Cattaro. Cattaro serves as a primary outlet for the commerce of Montenegro, while the Narenta river is used by vessels to carry the majority of Herzegovina's sea-borne trade.

The economy of the coastal and adjacent regions relies on fishing, farming, shipbuilding, the distillation of spirits (specifically maraschino), and the pressing of oil and wine. These areas engage in a lucrative transit trade with Montenegro, Turkey, and Bosnia and Herzegovina, exporting hides, wax, honey, oil, spirits, wine, bay salt, lignite, and asphalt.
