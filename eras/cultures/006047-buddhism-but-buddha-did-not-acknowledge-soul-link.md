# Buddhism

Buddhism is a religious and ethical system founded by the historical Buddha, whose family name was Gotama. He was the son of Suddhoda, a chief of the Sakiya tribe, one of the republican clans in India. According to Buddhist theory, a "Buddha" appears periodically in the world to preach the true doctrine; once this teaching is corrupted or lost over time, it remains absent until a new Buddha appears.

## Core Doctrines and the Concept of the Soul
A central and distinctive feature of original Buddhism is its systematic rejection of the "soul" in the ordinary sense. This position is so fundamental that it is placed at the forefront of Buddhist expositions. The Buddha is stated to have devoted his very first discourse to his first converts to this subject. This rejection is further detailed in the *Dialogues of Gotama* and the *Kathavatthu*—the latest book in the canon—which devotes a vast amount of space to the question of the soul. Similarly, in the conversations between the Buddhist teacher Nagasena and the Greek king Menander, Nagasena convinces the king that the soul does not exist.

Despite rejecting the soul-theory of the Jains, the pantheistic speculations of the pre-Buddhistic *Upanishads*, and the ritualistic magic of the brahmin schools, the Buddha retained a belief in transmigration. Because he did not acknowledge a soul, the link of connection between one life and the next was identified as the influence exercised upon a future life by a desire felt in a previous one. 

This belief is tied to the ethical conception of *Karma*, which posits that a person's physical advantages or social position are the results of actions in a previous birth. For example, blindness is attributed to the "lust of the eye" in a previous birth, while unusual hearing powers are attributed to a previous love for listening to the preaching of the law.

## Spread and Influence in India
In India, Buddhism flourished for centuries alongside Brahminism. However, the priestly caste of the Brahmins, the most privileged caste in the Indian social body, consistently opposed the open teachings of the Buddha. Over time, the Brahmins undermined Buddhist influence, leading to a decay of the faith within India as the cults of Hinduism and a more rigorous caste system resumed their sway. 

Despite this internal decline, Buddhism was championed by figures such as Asoka, described as the greatest of kings, who is associated with a Pillar of Lions erected in Deer Park where the Buddha preached his first sermon. From India, the faith spread beyond the realms of caste to become predominant in Burma, Siam, China, and Japan.

## Introduction to Japan
The introduction of Buddhism to Japan involved several attempts. In 522, a Chinese bonze named Shiba Tachito arrived in Yamato, erecting a temple and enshrining an image of Buddha, though this initial effort produced no sensible impression. In 545, Song Wang Myong, the King of Kudara, petitioned the Yamato Court with a sixteen-foot-high image of Buddha. 

By A.D. 552, Buddhism was more formally introduced when the King of Kudara presented the Yamato Court with a gold-plated copper image of Buddha, several canopies (*tengai*), and volumes of sacred books via envoys including Tori Shichi (Nori Sachhi). A memorial accompanying these gifts described the doctrine as "most excellent," capable of transforming a man into a Bodhi and producing immeasurable retribution and fortune. While presented as a moral treasure, King Myong's motives were also political, as he sought to use the faith to bind Japan to his country for aid against Koma and Shiragi.

## Preservation and Texts
The preservation of Buddhist teachings involved the transmission of numerous texts and relics. The pilgrim Yuan Chwang returned to the Chinese capital, Singan, in 645 with hundreds of Buddhist books written in Sanskrit on trimmed leaves of palm and birch bark, as well as 150 authenticated relics of the Buddha.

Key texts and translations associated with the archive's records include:
*   The *Dhammapada* and *Sutta Nipata*
*   The *Vinaya Texts*
*   The *Jataka* (Buddhist Birth Stories)
*   The *Saddharma Pundarika*
*   The *Questions of King Milinda*

Notably, the Buddha is referred to in the texts as the Arahat, a title he always used for himself; the sources indicate he did not call himself "the Buddha," nor did his followers address him as such.
