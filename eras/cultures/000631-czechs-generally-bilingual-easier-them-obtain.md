# Czechs: Language, Politics, and National Identity

## Linguistic Capabilities and Employment
Educated Czechs are generally bilingual. This linguistic proficiency provides them with a professional advantage, as it makes it easier for them to obtain appointments in districts where knowledge of the Czech language is required. In contrast, there has been a growing resistance to the Czech language among Germans in Bohemia. While it was once common for children of German parents in Bohemia to learn Czech, this practice ceased after 1867. Many Germans have since made it a matter of principle, or a point of honor, not to learn the language. Consequently, Germans often view any official order requiring the use of Czech as a measure that excludes them from certain posts. This sentiment of contempt and hostility is most prevalent among the educated middle class, though it is less evident among the nobility and the clergy.

Beyond their native and German languages, Czechs are described as being literate in several languages. They demonstrate a high capacity for rapid learning; for instance, a Czech seeking to acquire a new language may dedicate a weekend to the task. This is exemplified by Czech officers and members of the National Assembly who sought to learn English specifically to understand the speeches of President Wilson, whom they rank alongside their own national leader, Masaryk.

## Political Aspirations in Bohemia
In Bohemia, the richest part of the Austrian dominions, over three-fifths of the population were Czech. Racial feeling in this region was supported by appeals to historic law. A significant political party, led by Rieger and Palacky, advocated for the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy in its fullest extent, to include Silesia and Moravia. This party insisted that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia at Prague, following the tradition of his predecessors, and sought a position within the monarchy similar to that of Hungary.

This movement was supported not only by Czechs but also by several great nobles of German descent, such as Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg (the cardinal archbishop of Prague), Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz, and Count Leo Thun. These nobles hoped that a self-governing kingdom of Bohemia would help preserve their power against the threats posed by German Liberals. While the Czechs also found support in Moravia from some nobles led by Count Belcredi, they faced opposition in Bruenn from a strong German party. In Silesia, the Germans held a considerable majority, and a large Polish element that did not support the Czechs led the diet to refuse Bohemian claims.

## Internal Political Divisions
The Czech political landscape was characterized by internal conflicts, particularly between the "Old Czechs" and a newer group known as the "Young Czechs" (or Radicals). The Young Czechs recalled Hussite traditions and sought universal suffrage, while rejecting alliances with the clergy and aristocracy. They aimed to join Germans in political reform and take their seats in the diet. This put them in direct conflict with Rieger, the leader of the Old Czechs, who favored a policy of passive opposition and maintained alliances with Feudalists.

These divisions were stark; twenty-seven members of the diet, led by Stadkowsky and Gregr, resigned their seats after being outvoted in the Czech Club. For a period of four years, these two Czech factions were as focused on opposing each other as they were on opposing the Germans.

## Military and Social Characteristics
During periods of conflict and displacement, Czechs have been noted for their discipline and determination. Czech soldiers, having left the Austrian army to avoid firing on their "brother Slavs," demonstrated a strong desire to reach France. Despite efforts by the Siberian Soviets and the Cossack General Semenoff to detain them—using fictitious floods on the Amur and reports of poor railway conditions—the Czechs maintained a stout resistance against those who attempted to disarm them.

Observers have characterized the Czech personality as being defined by an "enthusiastic good cheer" and a "determination" that allows them to win favor with others, from Russian peasant women to various tradespeople. They are described as "natural-born good mixers" who deal honorably and avoid ruffianism. In military contexts, they are noted for strict discipline and sharp salutes. It has been observed that while a Czech may be a Socialist in theory, in action he is a patriot.

## Presence in Siberia
The Czech presence in Siberia included a significant number of skilled workers. In Omsk, records indicated the residence of forty thousand Czech artisans. Their activities in the region included the establishment of infrastructure, such as cold storage cellars for meat. During their transit across Siberia toward the Pacific, Czechs were observed playing violins they had whittled themselves while in prison camps.
