# The Indian Peninsula and Ceylon

The Indian peninsula and the island of Ceylon (also known in ancient texts as Taprobana and Serendib) are regions characterized by diverse flora, fauna, and a complex history of administration and exploration.

## Geography and Flora
The vegetation of the Indian peninsula and Ceylon is primarily determined by climatic humidity. A strong contrast exists between the impenetrable, shady forests found on the west coast of the Indian peninsula, in Ceylon, eastern Bengal, and the Malay peninsula, and the more loosely-timbered districts of the north-western Himalaya and central India. In the Central Provinces and parts of Madras, loosely-timbered country is a prevailing feature, while the Tarai jungles at the foot of the eastern Himalaya are noted for their luxuriant growth and dense vegetation. In lowland areas, the landscape is either adorned with planted wood where highly cultivated or nearly completely desert where rain is absent.

The flora of the peninsula and Ceylon shows a distinct connection to eastern tropical Africa, evidenced by the identity of genera and families and the similarity of tropical forms. This is most remarkable in the mountain flora, where several species are believed to be identical to Abyssinian forms. Both the peninsula and Ceylon are characterized by the absence of cycads, conifers, and oaks, despite the fact that conifers and oaks are abundant to the north and east in mountains of similar elevations. 

Specific botanical orders developed more largely in south India include Ebenaceae, Jasmineae, Cyrtandraceae, Balsaminaceae, Dipterocarpaceae, and Aurantiaceae. The Balsaminaceae are particularly abundant in the peninsula but rare in the eastern region. While the genus *Nepenthes* is found in Ceylon and as far north as the Khasi hills, it is absent from the Indian peninsula and the Himalaya.

## Fauna
The regions are home to a variety of mammals. The typical Indian buffalo (*Bos bubalis bubalis*) is restricted in its wild state to Ceylon and India, inhabiting marshes and grass-jungles where the grass can exceed 20 feet in height. These animals are known to form covered pathways and wallow in mud. The Indian buffalo is distinguished from South African species by a more elongated, narrow head, wider separation of the horns, and hair on the fore-part of the back that is directed forwards.

The Bandicoot-rat (*Nesocia bandicota*), an Anglo-Indian name derived from the Telegu *pandi-koku*, is found throughout India and Ceylon, though it is especially common in the south of the peninsula. It measures 12 to 15 inches to the root of the tail, with a tail measuring 11 to 13 inches.

Other notable animals include the lion, found in part of India, and the leopard. The East Indian leopard is less dark and less full in fur than the Bengal variety. Bengal leopards have short, hard hair and their skins are used for saddle cloths and drummers' aprons in the Indian army.

## Historical and Political Overview
In antiquity, the island of Ceylon was known to the Romans as Taprobana or Serendib, though its real size was magnified by geographers and Ptolemy by more than fifteen times. Pliny records that during the reign of Claudius, a freedman who farmed Red Sea customs was driven by winds to the coast; after six months with the natives, the king of Ceylon sent an embassy to the emperor. Some historians, such as M. Letronne, suggest this freedman may have been an impostor. Additionally, the name *Diva gens* or *Divorum regio* was likely applied by ancients to the entire eastern coast of the Indian Peninsula, extending from Ceylon to the Canges.

In the 3rd century B.C., Asoka invaded Kalinga on the east coast of Madras. Following the horror of war, he renounced conquest and adopted Buddhism. During his twenty-eight year reign, he sent missionaries to Ceylon, Alexandria, Persia, and Kashmir. He also implemented public works, including the planting of shade trees, the digging of wells, the creation of hospitals, and the establishment of gardens for medicinal herbs.

In more recent history, Sir Edward Barnes served as the governor of Ceylon from 1824 to 1831. His administration saw the first census of the population, the introduction of coffee cultivation based on the West Indian system, and the construction of the great military road between Kandy and Colombo. Barnes later served as commander-in-chief in India from 1831 to 1853.

## Cartography and Surveying
A trigonometrical survey of British India began in 1800. By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, this resulted in a comprehensive system of maps. Topographical maps generally use a scale of 1:63,360, while revenue surveys for land settlement use 1:4,000. An *Indian Atlas* (scale 1:255,660), begun in 1827, includes the Malay Peninsula and Ceylon. While an official map of India (1:1,000,000) was published in 1903, Ceylon maintains its own surveyors-general.
