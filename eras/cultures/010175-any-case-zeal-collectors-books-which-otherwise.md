# Book-Collecting

Book-collecting is the act of bringing together books that possess an element of permanent interest in their form, contents, or the history of the individual copy, and which are either prospectively or actually rare. In this context, rarity is defined as being difficult to procure. While rarity is a significant factor, it is subordinate to interest; a book that lacks interest is of no concern regardless of its rarity. Conversely, if a book is perceived as being easily procurable, there is generally no reason to collect it. This anticipation of availability often prevents the collection of items that eventually become the rarest; for example, Horn-books are much rarer than First Folio Shakespeares. It has been suggested that the ultimate rarity of books varies in an inverse ratio to the number of copies originally printed.

## Evolution of the Practice
Book-collecting as it is understood today is essentially a modern growth, although adumbrations existed during the Roman Empire and toward the end of the Middle Ages. Historically, the love of books was tied to the enjoyment of literature. For instance, the *Philobiblon* (1345), attributed to Richard de Bury, focuses on the delights of literature, while Sebastian Brant later argued that possessing books was a poor substitute for learning. 

Modern book-collecting required a baseline abundance of books to read before it could begin. Even after the invention of printing reduced book costs by approximately 80%, collectors did not appear immediately. Many early owners of famous libraries were not collectors in the modern sense. Jean Grolier (1479–1565), for example, bought books to encourage the best printers of his era and to provide friends with Renaissance scholarship. Similarly, figures such as Archbishop Cranmer, Lords Arundel and Lumley, Henry, prince of Wales (1594–1612), and historian Jacques Auguste de Thou (1553–1617) assembled the best learned literature of their day, often binding them in handsome leather with heraldic badges and coats of arms.

## Types of Appeal and Value
Books that attract collectors generally fall into several categories of appeal:

*   **Associative Interest:** Books linked to famous events or people—such as kings, queens (notably Mary queen of Scots), poets, soldiers, or statesmen—possess an interest that defies analysis. This includes books bearing marks of being valued by the owner or connected to striking life incidents. Collectors also value a "pedigree" traced through a series of previous book-loving owners.
*   **Intellectual and Historical Value:** This includes works of historical value that have not been reprinted or whose original editions are more authentic than modern versions. Examples include Lord Crawford's collections of papal bulls and English, Scottish, and Irish proclamations, the Foxwell collection of early political economy books, and the philological library of Prince Lucien Bonaparte.
*   **Printing and First Editions:** First editions are valued for their positive superiority for study or for the associations they create. While the high prices paid during the era of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics are difficult to justify (as they are of little value as texts), the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces, such as the 1623 First Folio Shakespeare, has increased.
*   **Printing History:** Collectors often seek the first book printed in a specific town or works that help measure the quality of early printers. This zeal has preserved thousands of books that would otherwise have perished.

## Physicality and Preservation
The physical condition and presentation of a book are central to its value. This includes the binding and the use of book-plates. Jean Grolier used the legend *Portio mea domine sit in terra viventium* and his name on his bindings. Other notable examples include Tommaso Maioli, whose bindings featured atoms of gold in the leather, and Demetrio Canevari, who used dark green or deep red morocco with a colored cameo stamp of Apollo and a silver Pegasus.

Book-plates became a subject of study and collection around 1875, spurred by Lord de Tabley's 1880 *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates*. Styles are classified as early armorial (pre-Restoration), Jacobean (including the heavy decorative manner of the Restoration, Queen Anne, and early Georgian eras), Chippendale (rococo), and wreath and ribbon.

Regarding condition, the value of a copy is determined by its completeness, size, freshness, and absence of stains. Some dealers "complete" old books by adding leaves from other copies or editions. These meddlings can sometimes be detected by differences in paper color, height, watermarks, wiremarks, or the style of hand-added ornamentation.

## The Role of the Collector and Librarian
The pastime of the book-collector is essentially identical to the work of a museum curator or a librarian of a respectable age. While a library begins as a "literary workshop," it eventually becomes a storehouse of "dead stock" as older editions pass out of use. Critics and antiquaries then salvage books from this stock that remain "alive," creating a literary museum.

The financial aspect of the hobby is tracked in publications such as Slater's *Book-Prices Current* (published annually since 1887) and Livingston's *American Book Prices Current*. Prices are influenced by fashion, the rarity of the copy relative to the number of wealthy seekers, and the positive or associative interest of the book.
