# Christian and Heathen Interactions in History and Literature

The intersection of Christian and heathen traditions is documented across various historical, literary, and religious contexts, ranging from the adaptation of ancient legends to military conflicts and the evolution of genealogical records.

## Literary Adaptations and Romances
The transmission of stories between different religious traditions often involved significant modification. A notable example is the story of Josaphat, which shares a structure nearly identical to the story of the Buddha. Both narratives describe a prince educated in a secluded palace who, after encountering an old man, a man in poverty and disease, a dead body, and a peaceful religious recluse, abandons his princely state for an ascetic life in the jungle. The name Josaphat is considered a corruption of "Bodisatva," a title for the Buddha. This Christian version of the story may reflect 7th-century religious disputes in Syria and was later edited by a monk named John of Saint Saba. It eventually gained widespread popularity, appearing in the *Lives of the Saints* by Simeon Metaphrastes (c. 950), an Icelandic version for Prince Hakon in the 13th century, and a Tagala translation in Manila in 1712.

Similarly, Christian romancers adapted figures from other traditions, such as transforming Apollo into a patron of Mussulmans. In a parallel instance of religious re-characterization, the Persian poet Ferdousi depicted Alexander the Great as a Christian.

## Conflict and Conversion
Historical accounts detail violent encounters between Christian and heathen populations. In Spain, King Sigurd fought several battles against heathens who plundered Christian people. At the castle of Sintre, Sigurd killed every man who refused to be baptized, treating them as "God's foes." In Lisbon, a city described as being half Christian and half heathen, Sigurd gained another victory. He further campaigned westwards, capturing the town of Alkasse and killing so many inhabitants that the town was left empty.

In other contexts, the conversion to Christianity led to the decline of heathen minstrelsy. However, descendants of Woden maintained an interest in the achievements of their ancestors. Even by the 14th century, the deeds of Germanic heroes were still recited in verse, as evidenced by Chaucer's knowledge of "the song of Wade."

## Genealogical and Cultural Shifts
The adoption of Christianity altered how ancient peoples viewed their origins. Under the old heathen faith, the kings of the Saxons, Anglians, Danes, Norwegians, and Swedes traced their pedigree to Odin or his companions. Following the extinction of the worship of Odin, these genealogical lines were extended to Noah or Japhet. In this revised framework, Odin was degraded from a primitive source to a simple human member of the lineage.

Cultural tensions also existed among early converts. In Corinth, Christians faced difficulties due to the influences of the heathen world. The moral code of Judaism, which Christianity intensified—particularly regarding relations between the sexes—created strain for converts accustomed to the lower standards and naturalistic impulses of heathen systems.

## Theological and Philosophical Perspectives
Different authors have addressed the relationship between Christian virtue and heathenism. Some scholars have cautioned against becoming too immersed in the spirit of heathen authors or over-praising pagan heroes. Referencing St. Austin, it has been argued that true virtue cannot exist without true piety (the sincere worship of the true God) and that any virtue aimed at worldly glory is not true virtue.

Conversely, some perspectives suggest a broader divine plan. Inazo Nitobe expressed a belief that God created a testament for every nation and people, whether they be Christian, Heathen, Gentile, or Jew.

## Legends and Rituals
Certain legends blended Christian and heathen elements. The legend of the "Dry Tree" (known to heathens as *Kurru Thereck* or *Dirpe*) suggests that a tree remained green until the death of Christ, at which point it and all other trees dried. Prophecy associated with the tree claims that a Prince from the West will eventually win the Holy Land with the help of Christian men and sing a Mass under the tree, causing it to become green and bear fruit again, leading many Saracens and Israelites to turn to the Christian faith.

Additionally, some religious practices showed overlap. The Jewish feast of tabernacles was imitated in several heathen solemnities, and the rule established by Esdras against fasting on a festival day was adopted by Christians in the Apostolical Constitutions.
