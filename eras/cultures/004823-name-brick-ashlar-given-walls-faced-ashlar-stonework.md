# Brick-Ashlar and Ashlar Masonry

## Definition and Composition
The term "brick-ashlar" refers to walls that are constructed with a facing of ashlar stonework and a backing of brickwork. Ashlar, also spelled as ashler or ashelere, consists of hewn or squared stone and is generally utilized for the facing of walls. In medieval documentation, the term "clene hewen ashlar" is used to describe stone that has been finely worked or tooled, distinguishing it from faces that have been rough-axed.

## Structural Challenges and Stability
Brick-ashlar constructions are subject to an aggravated degree of unequal settling. This instability arises from the differing physical properties of the two materials used. The outer face consists of unyielding stone with very thin joints, which may occupy no more than one-hundredth of the wall's height. In contrast, the brick backing contains mortar joints that comprise approximately one-eighth of its height. Because mortar naturally yields to pressure and shrinks during the drying process, the wall is prone to failure or the bulging of the stone face.

To prevent this settling and ensure stability, specific construction methods are recommended:
* **Mortar Composition:** The mortar should be worked as stiffly as possible and composed of sand and Portland cement, with a large proportion of the cement.
* **Course Alignment:** The height of the stones used for the facing should be equal to an exact number of brick courses.

## Related Facing Techniques
Similar challenges in bonding occur when using other materials for facing. For example, it is common practice to back up stonework faced with Kentish rag rubble using bricks. Due to the irregularity of these stones, achieving a proper bond is difficult. To resolve this, cement mortar should be used throughout, and headers or bonding stones should be built in frequently.

In pure brickwork, stability is also a concern when facing bricks of a superior quality are used for the exterior while inferior bricks are used for the interior. A "pernicious" practice involves the use of "snapped headers," where heading bricks are cut in half. This creates a brickwork skin only 4½ inches thick with a straight mortar joint behind it, which destroys the proper bonding between the face and the back of the wall.

## Ashlar in Historical Architecture
Ashlar masonry has been employed in various historical contexts to achieve specific aesthetic and structural effects. In Cairo, the entrance gateways of the city—specifically the Bab-Zuwela, Bab-el-Futuh, and Bab-en-Nasr—rely on fine ashlar masonry for their massive and simple effect, with decoration limited primarily to ornamental disks.

In contrast, other structures in Cairo, such as the mosque of Ahmed Ibn-Tulun, were built entirely of brick. To provide these brick walls with a more monumental appearance, they were given a casing of stucco. This allowed Coptic craftsmen to enrich the imposts and archivolts with conventional foliage.
