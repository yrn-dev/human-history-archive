# Vegetation of the Dry Regions of Central Asia

The vegetation of the dry regions of central Asia is characterized by a specialized composition of plant life adapted to extreme climatic conditions. This region, which encompasses areas commonly referred to as Turkestan, Mongolia, western Manchuria, and the area between the southern border of Siberia and the temperate alpine zone of north China and the Himalaya, is described as almost rainless. It is subject to intense summer heat and winters of extreme severity.

## Botanical Characteristics
The vegetation in these dry regions is remarkable for the high relative number of salt plants, specifically *Salicornia* and members of the Chenopodiaceae family. Polygonaceae are also abundant in the region; the frequent occurrence of leafless forms among these plants gives the vegetation a very distinctive aspect. Additionally, peculiar forms of Leguminosae prevail.

These plant types, along with many other species found in the drier and southern regions of Siberia, or the colder desert tracts of Afghanistan and Persia, extend into Tibet. In Tibet, the combination of extreme drought and a nearly vertical, hot sun creates a summer climate that does not differ greatly from the plains of central Asia.

## Climatic Influences on Plant Life
The nature of the vegetation is heavily influenced by the region's geography and climate. The great elevations of the table-lands of Persia and Afghanistan, as well as the central part of Asia, tend to exaggerate the cold of winter. Conversely, the sterility of the surface—caused by low rainfall—increases summer heat. 

During the summer, solar heat accumulates on the dry surface soil. Because the soil is not moist or covered with dense vegetation, this heat cannot be released upwards through evaporation, nor can it be easily conveyed downwards. This cycle of intense heat and severe cold has resulted in plants and animals with a special character suited to these conditions, showing a closer alliance to the adjacent northern Siberian tract than to other bordering regions.

## Regional Variations and Comparisons
The botanical character of central Asia differs from other dry tracts in the continent. For example, the extremely dry and hot regions stretching from Arabia through Baluchistan and south Persia to Sind exhibit a uniformity of life that closely approaches the hot and dry regions of Africa. 

In contrast, the vegetation of the Nan-shan highlands presents a varied picture based on altitude and water availability. In the western portion, where valleys are dry, the slopes are devoid of vegetation, and only sparse bushes of *Salsolaceae*, *Nitraria*, and *Ephedra* grow on the dry clay and gravel soil. However, in the north-eastern Nan-shan, where streams run through gorges, the landscape is covered with vegetation, including deciduous trees such as birch, mountain ash, and Przhevalsky's poplar, as well as conifer forests (*Picea obovata*). Higher elevations in these gorges support willows, *Lonicereae*, *Spriaeae*, *Potentilla fruticosa*, and rhododendrons.

## Historical Changes in Vegetation
The plant life of central Asia and surrounding areas has undergone significant changes over the last 10,000 years. Following the recession of the last glacial age, a period of prairie-like or steppe conditions existed over the great plain of Europe. 

Evidence suggests that 3,000 to 4,000 years ago, the climate in western Asia and Europe was more genial, supporting a greater luxuriance of vegetable life than is found today. During this period, forests existed in the country now known as Western Turkestan and in south Russia, areas where deserts and steppes now prevail. This change coincided with the shrinkage of a great sea that once extended from the Balkan peninsula into central Asia and northward into central Russia. Later, between 1,500 and 2,000 years ago, the Aral-Caspian region likely became drier, and those seas smaller than they are currently.
