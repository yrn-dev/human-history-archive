# Borneo and the Surrounding Regions

## Geography and Topography of Borneo
Borneo is the largest and most western island of a northern group that extends between 110 deg. and 150 deg. E., reaching as far as Papua or New Guinea. The island is characterized by a rough and mountainous surface that is generally covered with forest, with the exception of the alluvial lands near the coast. While the ordinary elevations on the island do not exceed 5,000 or 4,000 ft., the highest mountain rises to nearly 14,000 ft.

Borneo is separated from the island of Celebes by the Macassar Strait. To the north and east lie other islands, including the Philippine Islands, which are situated between Borneo and southern China. The Philippine Islands are located between 5 deg. and 20 deg. N., and their highest land does not rise above 10,250 ft.

## Flora and Natural Characteristics
The vegetation of the Malayan Islands, including Borneo, largely resembles that of the hotter and wetter regions of India. However, the humidity and temperature uniformity lead to the predominance of specific tropical forms. In Borneo, rhododendrons occur, descending even to sea level. Additionally, some temperate forms of flora from Australia appear on the higher mountains of Borneo. This diffusion of Australian genera extends further north to the Philippines and even into southern China.

## Population and Settlement
The population of Borneo is described as small, rude, and uncivilized. Settlers from various surrounding countries have been attracted primarily to the alluvial lands near the coast. In the nearby island of Celebes, the chief town of the Dutch government is Macassar, located on the west coast of the southern peninsula. Macassar serves as a significant trade hub with a population of 17,925, including 2,618 Chinese, 940 Europeans, and 168 Arabs. The town consists of a Malay town located inland and a Dutch town and port known as Vlaardingen.

## Economic Activity and Resources
Borneo is a critical source of liquid fuel for the Far East. The development of Borneo oil-fields was led by Sir Marcus Samuel and the Shell Transport and Trading Company, which later amalgamated with the Royal Dutch Petroleum Company to control extensive wells in Dutch Borneo. These fields supply large quantities of liquid fuel to Japan, China, and as far as Cape Town via Singapore.

In the neighboring port of Macassar, annual trade amounts to approximately £1,250,000. This commerce consists mainly of valuable timber, spices, gums, copra, trepang, and coffee.

## Cultural and Historical Connections
There are theories regarding the migration of peoples from Borneo to other regions. Some authorities suggest that the Kumaso, a group inhabiting the extreme south of Japan (specifically the provinces of Satsuma, Osumi, and Hyuga), belonged to the Sow race of Borneo. These people may have reached Japan via the "Black Tide." Similarities in custom between the two groups include the use of ornamental tattooing, the use of shields decorated with hair, a fondness for dancing accompanied by singing and hand-clapping, similar hairstyles, and skill in creating bamboo articles, particularly hats.
