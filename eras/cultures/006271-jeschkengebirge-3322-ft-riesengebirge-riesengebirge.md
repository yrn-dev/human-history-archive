# The Riesengebirge and Jeschkengebirge

## Geographic Overview
The Riesengebirge, known in Czech as the *Kroknose* or the Giant Mountains, form a significant portion of the north-east frontier of Bohemia. From an orographic perspective, these mountains, along with other ranges of the Sudetes, serve as one of the three mountain ranges that border the Bohemian massif. The Sudetes comprise several distinct groups, including the Lausitzergebirge (2500 ft.), the Isergebirge—which features the Tafelfichte peak at 3683 ft.—the Jeschkengebirge, and the Riesengebirge.

The Jeschkengebirge reaches an altitude of 3322 ft. The Riesengebirge are among the highest mountains in central Europe, surpassed only by the Alps. The highest point within the Riesengebirge is the Schneekoppe, which attains an altitude of 5264 ft.

## Climate and Precipitation
The climate of Bohemia is generally healthy and continental, though it varies significantly across different districts. While the interior of Bohemia receives a mean annual rainfall of 18 inches, the Riesengebirge experience much higher levels of precipitation, with a mean annual rainfall of 40 inches. This is lower than the rainfall found in the Bohmerwald, where precipitation reaches between 60 and 70 inches.

## Military History
The geography of the Riesengebirge has played a role in regional military movements. During the campaign of 1745, the mountains served as a strategic barrier between opposing forces. In May of that year, Frederick lay in the camp of Frankenstein between Neisse and Glatz with approximately 65,000 men, while Prince Charles commanded 85,000 Austrians and Saxons positioned behind the Riesengebirge. Following the battle of Hohenfriedberg (also known as Striegau) on June 4, Prince Charles suffered a complete defeat and withdrew through the mountains. Frederick's subsequent pursuit was conducted methodically due to the barren and difficult nature of the country.
