# AXINITE

Axinite is a mineral composed of a complex aluminium and calcium boro-silicate that contains a small amount of basic hydrogen. Its chemical formula is $\text{HCa}_3\text{BAl}_2(\text{SiO}_4)_4$. Within the mineral's composition, the aluminium is replaced by ferric iron, and the calcium is replaced in varying amounts by manganese and ferrous iron.

## Nomenclature and Physical Properties
The mineral was named by R. J. Hauy in 1799, deriving the name from the Greek word *axine*, meaning "an axe," due to the characteristic thin, wedge-like form of its anorthic crystals. In 1792, J. C. Delametherie had previously named the mineral yanolite, meaning "violet stone," because the mineral rarely possesses a violet tinge. However, the usual colour of axinite is clove-brown.

Axinite has a specific gravity of 3.28 and a hardness rating of 6 1/2 to 7. These physical properties, combined with its transparency and colour, make the mineral suitable for use as a gemstone.

## Occurrences and Distribution
Axinite is found in several locations globally:
*   **France:** The best specimens are transparent, glassy crystals found in Dauphine at Le Bourg d'Oisans. These occur in a zone of chlorite-schists and amphibolite, appearing alongside quartz, prehnite, and albite. Crystals from this region are occasionally cut for use as gemstones.
*   **England:** It is found in hornblende-schists and greenstone at Batallack Head, located near St Just in Cornwall.
*   **Germany:** It occurs in diabase in the Harz.
*   **United States:** Small crystals have been found in Pennsylvania (Northampton county) and in Maine.
*   **Japan:** Large crystals of the mineral have been discovered here.

## Geological Classification
Axinite is distinguished from the boro-silicate tourmaline by its geological occurrence; while tourmaline is usually found in granite, axinite occurs in basic rather than acid eruptive rocks.
