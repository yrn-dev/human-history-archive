# BASSOON

The bassoon (French: *basson*; German: *Fagott*; Italian: *fagotto*) is a woodwind instrument featuring a double reed mouthpiece. As a member of the oboe family, it serves as the bass of the wood-wind section. The English and French names for the instrument are derived from its pitch, while the German and Italian names stem from a perceived resemblance to a bundle of sticks. This resemblance exists because the bassoon was the first instrument of its type to be doubled back upon itself.

## Evolution and Prototypes

The direct ancestor of the bassoon was the bass pommer, a straight instrument measuring 6 feet in length. During the 15th and 16th centuries, the unwieldy proportions of the bass pommer led to various attempts to create a more practical orchestral bass by doubling the long tube. This transformation resulted in the *fagotto*.

A transitional stage in this evolution involved the tenor pommer in C. To increase portability and reduce its size, it was constructed from a block of wood with a diameter more than double that of the pommer, featuring two bores that communicated at a flat bottom. This instrument—which possessed two keys and seven holes (six in front and one in back)—was known as the *dulceian*, *dolcian*, or *doucaine*, and was referred to in England as the *curtail*, *curtoll*, or *curtal*, and in France as the *courtaud*. Records from 1582 mention the *curtoll* alongside the *hobius* (hautboy) and drone.

The subsequent evolutionary step was the "double curtail," a converted bass pommer an octave below the single curtail. This instrument was identical in construction and pitch to the early *fagotto* in C. A description from a manuscript written before 1688 describes the double curtail as being twice the size of the single version and played eight notes deeper, consisting of two pipes fixed within one thick bass pipe.

## Origins and Early Makers

The origin of the bassoon is a subject of historical debate. J. J. Quantz credited France with transforming the pommer into the bassoon. However, other evidence suggests the instrument may not be of German origin. While Sigmund Schnitzer of Nuremberg was renowned in the 18th century for making *fagotte* of pure intonation and extraordinary size, records from 1549 describe him simply as a *Pfeifenmacher und Stadtpfeifer* (pipe-maker and town piper), suggesting he was a skilled craftsman rather than the inventor of the doubled-tube form.

The name *fagotto* is presumed to have been bestowed in Italy, possibly as a satirical reference to the *doucaine*. A 1426 document indicates that Louis Willay, a maker from Bruges, sold *doucaines* to Philippe le Bon as a gift for Nicolas III, the marquis of Ferrara.

Separate from the standard bassoon was the *phagotus* of Afranio, a canon of Ferrara, described in a 1539 work by his nephew, Theseus Ambrosius Albonesius. This instrument, first built by Ravilius of Ferrara, was a mechanical curiosity consisting of two twin columns with keys and finger-holes, sounded by a bellows or bag-pipe. Mersenne classified this as a different kind of *fagotto* related to the Neapolitan *sourdeline* (a complex musette), noting that it only spoke when the keys were open.

## Musical Use and Development

The bassoon has been utilized by most great masters, with the exception of Handel. Its use in classical music includes:
* **Beethoven:** Employed the bassoon extensively in his symphonies, writing original and beautiful independent parts.
* **Bach:** Included parts for two bassoons in his mass in B min.
* **Mozart:** Composed a concerto in Bb for bassoon and orchestra (Kochel, No. 191).
* **Weber:** Wrote a concerto for bassoon in F (op. 75) scored for full orchestra.

Technical developments continued into the 19th century. At the instigation of Wagner, the maker W. Heckel of Biebrich-am-Rhein added an extra key to the instrument, which extended its compass downwards to A.
