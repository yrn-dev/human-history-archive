# History of the Ashanti

The Ashanti first became known to Europeans in the early 18th century, primarily through their successful wars against kingdoms bordering maritime territory. The foundation of Ashanti power is attributed to Osai Tutu, who is considered the real founder. He is credited with either building or significantly extending the capital, Kumasi, and expanding the empire through conquests to the east and west. His military achievements included the subduing of the neighboring state of Denkera in 1719, as well as the Banna and Gaman (Mahommedan) countries. Following Osai Tutu's defeat and death in 1731, his successor, Osai Apoko, continued to make acquisitions toward the coast.

## Expansion and Early British Contact

In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina became king. An ambitious leader, he sought to open communications with white nations. Around 1807, a conflict arose after two chiefs of the Assin, defeated by the Ashanti, sought refuge with the Fanti people on the coast. When the Fanti refused to surrender the fugitives, Osai Tutu invaded their country, defeating them and driving them toward the sea.

The Ashanti reached the coast near Anamabo, where a British fort was located. Despite mediation efforts by the governor, the town was destroyed and 8,000 inhabitants were slaughtered. The Ashanti lost over 2,000 men and were unable to storm the English fort, though the garrison was reduced from twenty-four to eight men. A truce was eventually reached. Colonel G. Torrane, the governor of Cape Coast (1805–1807), concluded an agreement acknowledging the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland. Torrane surrendered half of the fugitives in Anamabo fort to the Ashanti—one of whom was put to death—while most of the remaining fugitives were sold as slaves by Torrane and his council. Additionally, the governor agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

## Conflict and Diplomacy in the 19th Century

The British government later sought to cultivate relations with the kingdom, sending Joseph Dupuis as consul to Kumasi. Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast in January 1819 and reached Kumasi on February 28, 1820. He drew up a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory. However, this treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and the British government in London, which assumed direct control of British settlements in 1821.

Hostilities resumed under Sir Charles M'Carthy, the first crown-appointed governor, who supported the Fanti. On January 21, 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles at Essamako. The Ashanti force of 10,000 men overwhelmed the 500 men under Sir Charles; the governor and eight other Europeans were killed, and Sir Charles's skull was used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. Osai Tutu Quamina died on the day of this victory and was succeeded by Osai Okoto.

A period of chronic warfare followed until the Ashanti suffered a major defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826. Consequently, Ashanti power over coast tribes declined. In 1831, the king purchased peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, paying 600 oz. of gold and sending his son as a hostage to Cape Coast.

## The War of 1873 and Annexation

Tensions escalated again in the 1870s due to two primary causes. The most significant was the April 2, 1872, transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British. The Ashanti viewed the Elmina as their subjects and resented the British occupation of what they considered Ashanti territory; previously, the king had received an annual "custom-note" payment from the Dutch. A secondary cause was the Ashanti captivity of four Europeans—including members of the Basel mission, Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife, infant son, and Mr. J. Kuhne, as well as French trader M.J. Bonnat—who had been captured in 1869 and taken to Kumasi.

On January 22, 1873, Ashanti forces invaded the British protectorate, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition led by Sir Garnet Wolseley. Upon landing on October 2, Wolseley found the Ashanti forces decimated by fever and smallpox.

Following the later deportation of King Prempeh, no successor was appointed. Captain Donald W. Stewart was installed as British resident at Kumasi. While other states in the confederacy kept their tribal systems, Kumasi was administered by chiefs under British guidance. On September 26, 1901, Ashanti was formally annexed to the British dominions.

## The Rebellion of 1900 and Administration

Despite the administration, a serious rebellion broke out in 1900 involving the Kokofu, Adansi, and Kumasi tribes. The conflict was sparked by Governor Sir F. Hodgson's demand that chiefs pay 4,000 oz. of gold annually and his demand for the "golden stool," which the Kumasi had hidden since 1896. In April 1900, Kumasi warriors attacked the fort in Kumasi, where Sir F. and Lady Hodgson and Basel missionaries had taken refuge. The rebels were eventually repulsed by reinforcements from the Gold Coast and Lagos constabularies.

Under the 1901 order in council, the governor of the Gold Coast oversaw the territory, represented by a chief commissioner. The administration aimed to respect native laws unless they were injurious to the natives or incompatible with British sovereignty. By 1906, Chief Commissioner F.C. Fuller reported that suspicion of British motives was decreasing, as natives increasingly utilized British civil and criminal courts.

## Geography and Economy

The capital, Kumasi, is situated in a clearing of dense forest on a low rocky eminence. The town's name refers to the okum trees found in its streets. The city features a fort built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner. Geographically, the region is defined by the Black Volta and Volta rivers to the north and east, and the Tano and Bia rivers to the west. Lake Busumchwi is regarded as the sacred lake of the Ashanti.

The economy is based on gold mining—particularly from the Obuassi mines—and agriculture. Key products include rubber, kola nuts, tobacco, and gum copal. Since 1905, cocoa plantations have been established in the eastern districts. A railway connecting Sekondi to Kumasi was completed in 1903. Revenue for the administration is derived from liquor licenses, caravan taxes, government land rents, and contributions from gold-mining companies.
