The archive contains records of various games and sporting competitions, ranging from card and tile games to billiards and athletic sports. These records detail the rules, equipment, and historical competitive matches associated with these activities.

## Billiards and Related Table Games

Billiards is categorized into several forms, including "billiards proper" and French and American billiards. 

### Billiards Proper
Billiards proper is typically played between two opponents or two teams of two for 100 or more points. Scoring is based on hazards and cannons: red hazards earn three points, while white hazards and cannons earn two points each. Players take alternate turns, continuing a "break" until they fail to score. Certain errors result in points for the opponent, such as "coups" (running a ball off the table or into a pocket without hitting another ball), which score three points, and ordinary misses, which score one point. Foul strokes, such as hitting a ball twice or playing with the wrong ball, terminate the turn but do not award points to the opponent.

### French and American Billiards
This version is played on a pocketless table. While the English table features six pockets, the pocketless variety is the only kind used in France, though it is also found in America. For match purposes, the table measures 10 ft. by 5 ft., whereas private houses and clubs typically use 9 ft. by 4 1/4 ft. or 8 ft. by 4 ft. tables. The balls measure between 2 1/4 and 2 3/8 inches; the larger size is designated as "match" size. Because these balls are heavier and larger than English balls, the cues are more powerful to allow for effects such as "side" and masses. In this game, only cannons (referred to as "caroms" in America or "caramboles" in French) are played, each worth one point.

### Championship Billiards and Table Specifications
Between 1870 and 1885, championship matches were played on "Championship Tables" with pockets measuring 3 inches at the "fall." In contrast, ordinary tables used 3 5/8-in. or 3 3/4-in. pockets. By the end of the 19th century, the Billiard Association introduced "Standard Association Tables," which adopted the 3 5/8-in. pocket for all matches and featured a rounder cushion shoulder to require greater accuracy. 

Notable championship contests include a match in 1899 between Roberts and Dawson for 18,000 up, level. This match was distinct because it used ordinary pockets rather than three-inch pockets; Roberts won by 1814 points. Later, Stevenson and Diggle contested a game of 9000 points, which Stevenson won by 2900. Stevenson eventually became the incontestable English champion by the beginning of 1907.

## Dominoes

Dominoes include various forms such as Block and Draw games, All Fives (Muggins), All Threes, and Threes-and-Fives.

### Block and Draw Games
In these games, dominoes are shuffled face down. The lead is decided by drawing the highest card or, in some rules, by a doublet. Players usually draw seven cards, leaving a "stock." To play a card is to "pose." The leader typically poses their highest domino because the loser is determined by the number of pips remaining in their hand. Doublets are placed crosswise (*a cheval*). In the Draw game, a player who cannot match the pose must draw from the stock until a match is found, provided two cards remain in the stock. A player wins the hand by posing all their cards and calling "Domino!", scoring the pips remaining in the opponent's hand. A full game is generally 100 points.

### All Fives (Muggins) and Variations
In All Fives, players take five cards. Points are scored if the leader poses a double-five, six-four, five-blank, or three-two. Additionally, points (5, 10, 15, or 20) are scored if a player makes the sum of the end pips reach those totals. At the point of domino, the winner scores the multiple of five nearest to the pips in the adversary's hand. All Threes follows the same rules but aims for multiples of three. Threes-and-Fives awards one point for each three or five made at the ends.

## Card Games

The archive notes several card games, including Basset and games involving "builds."

### Basset
Basset (or Bassette) is a French card game for five people using a 52-card pack. Believed to be of Venetian origin, it was introduced to France by the ambassador Justiniani in the second half of the 17th century. It is played between a banker and several punters, and is now considered practically obsolete.

### Build and Trailing Rules
In certain card games, a player who makes a "build" must take it in on their next play. Only one build may be on the table at a time per player. "Double Builds" cannot be increased. In partner play, partners may take in each other's builds. "Trailing" involves laying down a card without matching or building; players typically use small cards for this, avoiding aces, tens, and deuces. High cards are generally saved for the end of the game.

## Other Sports

### Base-Ball
Base-ball is the national summer sport of the United States and is also popular in Japan and Canada. Some sources suggest it derives from the English game of rounders, while others claim it originated in Cooperstown, New York, in 1839, devised by Abner Doubleday. Early variations included "One Old Cat," played by three boys, and its modifications "Two," "Three," and "Four Old Cat." Town-ball was a further development, with the Olympic Town-Ball Club organized in Philadelphia in 1833. Organized club matches began near New York, with the Washington Baseball Club founded in 1843. The Knickerbocker Baseball Club drew up the first regular code of rules in 1845. The first National Association was organized in 1858, featuring a larger, "livelier" ball and a requirement for the pitcher to deliver a full toss.

### Tennis (Aces)
Matches are typically played for 15 or 21 aces, though ladies' singles are played for 11. A rubber consists of the best of three games. Games of 21 aces are reserved for handicap contests or matches decided by a single game. Tossing determines who chooses the service or the ends. Sides change ends at the start of the second and third games. In the third game, sides change when the leader reaches 8 (in a 15-ace game) or 6 (in an 11-ace game).
