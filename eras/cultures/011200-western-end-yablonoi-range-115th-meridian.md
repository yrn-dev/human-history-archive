# Central Asian Mountain Systems and Plateaus

## The Yablonoi and Eastern Asian Ranges
The Yablonoi range serves as a significant geographical divider in eastern Asia, separating the waters of the river Lena, which flows through Siberia into the Arctic Sea, from the waters of the river Amur. The Amur river and its affluents constitute the basin of Manchuria. To the east of the Yablonoi range, the Stanovor range follows the southern coast of Asia's eastern extremity, extending from Kamchatka to the borders of Manchuria at the 135th meridian (latitude 55 deg. N.), reaching heights of 4,000 to 5,000 ft. Further south, the Khingan range stretches from north Manchuria to the Chinese frontier near Peking. This range divides the drainage into the Amur and Yellow Sea to the east from the almost rainless region of Mongolia to the west.

## The Central Mountain Belt and the Gobi
From the western end of the Yablonoi range on the 115th meridian, a mountainous belt extends in an irregular line to the extremity of Pamir. This system is broken into several branches that enclose isolated drainage areas with no outflow, resulting in the formation of numerous lakes. 

The most prominent of these ranges is the Tian-shan, or Celestial Mountains, located between 40 deg. and 43 deg. N. and between 75 deg. and 95 deg. E. With some summits said to exceed 20,000 ft., the Tian-shan forms the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. The few towns and principal cultivated districts of central Asia are situated along the foot of this range. The Ala-tau, a prolongation of the Tian-shan, rises to 14,000 or 15,000 ft. and acts as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes, which have an elevation of approximately 5,000 ft. 

Further north, near the 50th parallel, the Altai range rises to 10,000 or 12,000 ft., separating the lakes of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria from the great rivers of western Siberia. West of the Altai, a line of elevation continues to the Ural Mountains, dividing the drainage of south-west Siberia from the plains northeast of the Aral Sea.

## The Gobi Desert and Mongolian Plateau
The Gobi is a great central Asiatic desert bounded by the Khingan range to the east, the northern face of the Tibetan plateau to the south, and the Yablonoi Mountains and Tian-shan to the north and north-west. The desert is divided into eastern Turkestan (western part) and south Mongolia (eastern part). No part of the Gobi is believed to be less than 2,000 ft. above sea level. Specific elevations include:
* **Kashgar and Yarkand:** 4,000 to 6,000 ft.
* **Northern Mongolia:** 4,000 to 6,000 ft.
* **Route between Kiakhta and the Chinese frontier:** No portion is below 3,000 ft.

In the north-east, the Nan-shan Highlands overlook Tsaidam. This region consists of parallel mountain ranges running from the north-west to the south-east, with longitudinal valleys at altitudes of 12,000 to 14,000 ft. These highlands contain lakes such as Khara-nor (13,285 ft.) and Koko-nor (9,970 ft.). The Nan-shan highlands are fringed on the north-west by the Da-sue-shan border range, a continuation of the Astin-tagh, which reaches 12,200 to 13,000 ft. in its passes.

## The Western Kuen-lun and Pamir
The Pamir highlands are separated from the East Turkestan lowlands by the double border-ridge of Sarik-kol (comprising the Muztagh or Kashgar range and the Sarik-kol range). The Muztagh-ata peak reaches 25,780 ft. East of the 77° E. meridian (Yarkand or Yarkent), this double border-ridge continues as the Kuen-lun proper. The northern range includes the Keriya, Khotan, and Kilian (or Kiliang) mountains, while the southern range includes the Ullugh-tagh, Sughet, and Raskem (or Raskan) mountains. 

These ranges reach elevations of 19,000 ft., with some peaks 2,000 to 2,500 ft. higher. High-altitude passes include the Sughet-davan (17,825 ft.), Kyzyl-davan (16,900 ft.), and Sanju-davan (16,325 ft.). The Kuen-lun system is characterized by deep gorges, such as the Khotan-darya (between the Kilian and Sughet mountains) and the Yarkand-darya (separating the Sughet range from the Karakorum Mountains).

## Western Asian Plateaus and the Suliman Hills
A vast plateau exists west of British India, covering an area comparable to Tibet. Its boundaries extend from the Arabian Sea and Persian Gulf in the south to the Black Sea and Sea of Marmora in the north. This region encompasses Armenia, Asia Minor, Iran (Persia), Baluchistan, and Afghanistan. 

In northern Baluchistan, the Suliman hills are a dominant feature, with an axis slightly east of the 70deg E. meridian. A notable feature is the Takht-i-Suliman (Solomon's throne), a high tableland approximately 8,000 ft. above sea level composed of coral limestone 4,000 to 5,000 ft. thick. The tableland is bounded by steep ridges, with the peak of Kaisargarh reaching 11,300 ft. and a shrine (zirat) located at 11,070 ft. This plateau is home to juniper and chilghosa (edible) pine.
