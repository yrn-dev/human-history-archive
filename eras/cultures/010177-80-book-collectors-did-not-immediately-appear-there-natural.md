# Book-Collecting

Book-collecting is defined as the process of bringing together books that possess an element of permanent interest in their contents, form, or the history of the individual copy, and which are either prospectively or actually rare. In this context, rarity is understood as being difficult to procure. While rarity is often emphasized in popular views, it is subordinate to interest; a book that is rare but devoid of interest is of no concern to the collector. Conversely, the anticipation that a book will always be easily procurable acts as a restraint on collecting. This dynamic explains why certain items, such as Horn-books, are much rarer than First Folio Shakespeares. It has been suggested that the ultimate rarity of books varies in an inverse ratio to the number of copies originally printed.

## Historical Development
Although precursors to the practice existed during the Roman Empire and toward the end of the Middle Ages, book-collecting as it is currently understood is a modern growth. In the medieval period, the love of books was focused on the delights of literature, as seen in the *Philobiblon* (1345) attributed to Richard de Bury. Later, Sebastian Brant argued that possessing books was a poor substitute for actual learning.

The emergence of modern collecting required a sufficient supply of books to read. Even after the invention of printing reduced the cost of books by approximately 80%, book-collectors did not appear immediately. Many early book-owners were not collectors in the modern sense. For example, Jean Grolier (1479-1565) acquired books to encourage the best printers of his era and to provide friends with the latest Renaissance scholarship, rather than out of antiquarian instincts. Similarly, figures such as Archbishop Cranmer, Lords Arundel and Lumley, Henry, prince of Wales (1594-1612), and historian Jacques Auguste de Thou (1553-1617) gathered the best learned literature of their day, often binding them in handsome leather with heraldic badges and coats of arms.

## Motivations and Types of Appeal
Books appeal to collectors through various imaginative and intellectual channels:

**Associational Interest:** Collectors are drawn to books associated with famous events or people, such as kings, queens (notably Mary queen of Scots), poets, soldiers, or statesmen. Interest is heightened if the book bears marks of being valued by the owner or is linked to a striking incident in their life. Additionally, a "pedigree" traced through a long series of book-loving owners increases a work's regard.

**First Editions:** The desire for first editions stems partly from their superiority for study and partly from the associations they possess or create. While the high prices paid during the era of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics are difficult to justify—as they are often of no value as texts—the prestige of first editions of French and English masterpieces, such as the 1623 First Folio Shakespeare, has increased immensely.

**Intellectual and Historical Value:** Collectors may seek works of historical value that have not been reprinted or whose original editions are more authentic than modern versions. Examples include Lord Crawford's collections of papal bulls and English, Scottish, and Irish proclamations, the philological library of Prince Lucien Bonaparte, and the Foxwell collection of early books on political economy.

**Imaginative Interest:** Books that caused the persecution of authors or were condemned to be burnt hold a specific imaginative interest, though this has appealed more to writers than to collectors.

## Physicality and Forgery
The physical form of a book is often a primary focus. Jean Grolier, treasurer of France in 1545, bound many of his books with the legend *Portio mea domine sit in terra viventium* and the name *Io Grolierii et Amicorum*. Other notable binders include Tommaso Maioli, whose books featured atoms of gold in the leather, and Demetrio Canevari, who used dark green or deep red morocco with a colored cameo stamp of Apollo and a silver Pegasus. Geoffrey Tory, printer to Francis I of France, also designed fine bindings.

The high value placed on rare books and autographs has encouraged forgery. This includes spurious letters attributed to popular figures such as Nelson, Burns, and Thackeray. Notable literary inventions include the forgeries of Chatterton and Ireland, as well as a mid-19th-century fabrication of letters by Shelley and Byron that included seals and postmarks.

## Bibliographical Literature
The tools used by collectors have evolved. Early "omnivorous" collectors relied on general manuals such as Brunet's *Manuel du libraire* (first published 1810), W.T. Lowndes' *Bibliographer's Manual of English Literature* (1834), and J.G.T. Graesse's *Tresor de livres rares et precieux* (1859-1867). In modern times, these general manuals have been replaced by more detailed handbooks focusing on special books.
