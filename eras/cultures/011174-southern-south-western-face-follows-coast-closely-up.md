# The Southern and South-Western Face of the Asian Plateau

The southern and south-western face of the extensive plateau located west of British India (excluding Syria and Arabia) is a geographical feature that closely follows the coastline. This region is part of a high table-land that encompasses provinces such as Armenia, Iran (or Persia), Baluchistan, and Afghanistan, as well as parts of Asia Minor.

## Geographic Boundaries and Topography
The plateau's southern border is defined by the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Sea, and the Tigris, extending westward to the north-east angle of the Levant. The southern and south-western face specifically follows the coast closely from the mouth of the Indus up the Persian Gulf. 

Moving further west, this face is formed by a mountain scarp that flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris. This scarp rises in many locations to 10,000 feet and extends through Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian. Beyond this point, the boundary turns along the north-eastern angle of the Mediterranean and the Taurus range.

## Regional Climate and Rainfall
The geography of the region significantly influences the patterns of summer rains in south Asia. The heaviest rainfall typically occurs where mountain lines directly face vapour-bearing winds, such as the west coast of the Malay peninsula and the Western Ghats of India. In contrast, the southern border of Baluchistan, west of Karachi, experiences a cessation of rains. This is attributed to the projection of the south-east coast of Arabia, which limits the length of the coastline exposed to the south-west monsoon air current and restricts the breadth of that current.

In Sind and along the Indus, rainfall is irregular and very small because there are no obstacles in the path of the vapour-bearing winds. Consequently, these winds carry uncondensed rain further north to the Punjab, where it falls on the western Himalaya and the outer ranges of Afghanistan.

## Related Asian Highlands
The plateau's eastern flank is marked by a line of hills located a short distance from the Indus, between the Himalaya and the river's mouth, roughly on the 72nd meridian. While these hills generally do not exceed 5,000 feet, some summits reach 10,000 feet or more. 

To the north east, the Afghan table-land meets Tibet and the Himalaya, forming a continuous mountain mass between the 34th and 36th degrees North and the 71st and 72nd meridians. An unbroken mountain range stretches from the intersection of the 36th parallel and 71st meridian, extending north east toward the Tibetan plateau and west toward the Caspian. The north eastern section of this range is of high altitude and serves as a divide between the headwaters of the Oxus (which flow to the Aral Sea) and the headwaters of the Indus and its tributary, the Kabul, which flow south into the Arabian Sea. The western portion of this range was known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons.
