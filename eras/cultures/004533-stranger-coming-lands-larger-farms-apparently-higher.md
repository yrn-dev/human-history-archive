# Agricultural Practices and Regional Variations

The appearance and success of agricultural systems vary significantly based on geography, climate, and the adaptability of the people working the land. While some regions may appear poor to an outside observer, they often possess underlying stability or a capacity for evolution in response to economic shifts.

## Regional Adaptations and Migration
In the Basque Provinces and the Pays Basque, agriculture may seem poor to a stranger accustomed to higher cultivation and larger farms. However, the region's old scattered homesteads reflect a sense of security that has been absent in other parts of Spain. The Basques have demonstrated a strong ability to adapt their farming to new conditions, a process aided by the presence of the courts at Biarritz and San Sebastian. Following the decline of self-sufficient village industries due to the rise of machinery and external manufacture, the Basques emigrated to agricultural regions in the Americas. This movement has contributed to a period of high prosperity for the Basque Provinces and the Pays Basque, potentially establishing a new Eskuara and Eskual-herria in the lands where they are valued as immigrants.

## Livestock and Land Use in Europe
Agricultural focus often shifts based on soil quality and altitude. In north Derbyshire, the harsh climate and poor soil of higher altitudes make grain growth impossible, leading to a prevalence of heath pasture and sheep farming. In these upland districts, limestone walls built without cement or mortar are used instead of hedges. Conversely, southern Derbyshire produces heavy crops of cereals, turnips, and wheat, while barley is grown in the east and around Gresley and Repton to supply breweries in Burton. The Trent valley is largely dedicated to dairy-farming and cattle-feeding, with permanent pasture expanding into arable land and Derbyshire cheeses being exported to London or sold at fairs in Derby and Ashbourne.

In other regions, climate dictates the livestock. In areas where mountains and the sea create a moist climate unsuitable for cereals, dairy-farming is common. The Kerry breed of cattle—small, black or red animals with upturned horns—are highly regarded for their milk and flesh. A larger cross-breed known as the "Dexter" also exists, though it lacks the fine qualities of the Kerry. On higher mountain ridges, goats and sheep share the pasturage, while cattle remain on the lower slopes.

In certain districts, such as those where the mean yearly temperature is 48 deg. F. and annual rainfall is 53 inches, there has been a shift in livestock. Heavy cattle, once dominated by the Galloway breed, have been largely replaced by Ayrshire dairy cattle and shorthorns. Sheep breeding has also expanded; Cheviots occupy the higher hilly country, while half-bred lambs (a cross of Leicesters or other long-woolled rams and Cheviots) are kept on richer low-lying pastures.

## Specialized Farming and Land Management
Fruit cultivation is a significant industry in East and Mid Kent, where farms feature cherry, apple, pear, plum, and damson trees, as well as strawberries, raspberries, currants, and gooseberries. Some cultivators specialize exclusively in fruit, particularly between Canterbury and Rochester and near London. Advanced growers use systematic spraying and grease-banding on apple trees to combat insects and select varieties based on flavor, size, and keeping qualities to compete with imports from Canada and the United States.

In Bulgaria, peasant proprietorship is universal, with small freeholds averaging 18 acres. A notable social structure is the *zadruga*, a patriarchal house-community where family groups—sometimes numbering several dozen people—live and work according to communistic principles under a house-mother (*domakinia*) and house-father (*domakin* or *stareïshina*). Additionally, associations of market gardeners (*taifi* or *gradinarski druzhini*) numbering over 10,000 people migrate in the spring to cultivate gardens near towns, dividing the profits upon their return in autumn.

## Global Farming Trends and Infrastructure
In the higher regions settled by Europeans in Africa, there is a focus on vegetables, particularly large crops of potatoes, as well as coffee, wheat, barley, and oats. The uplands are well-suited for herds and flocks, including Merino sheep introduced from Australia. The soil is generally a light reddish loam, though the eastern plains feature a heavy black loam.

In the Americas, the introduction of barbed wire initially faced humanitarian opposition, but tests regarding its economy and humanity eventually silenced these objections. It became the standard fencing for American farmers, particularly in the west, though since 1899, meshed wire field fencing has also become common. While Argentina and Australia use barbed wire on a large scale, they utilize more plain wire; Argentina specifically uses a strong high-carbon oval fence wire to meet its specific conditions.
