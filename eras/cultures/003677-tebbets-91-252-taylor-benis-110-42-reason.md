# Legal Practice and the Bar in the United States

## Structure of the American Bar
In the United States, each state maintains its own bar, which is composed of all attorneys-at-law residing within that state who have been admitted to practice in its courts. Generally, once an attorney is admitted to one court, they are permitted to practice in all courts within that state. 

Separate from the state bars, each of the United States courts maintains its own bar. An attorney practicing in a state is not permitted to practice in a court of the United States unless they have been admitted to that specific court or to a court of the same class in another circuit or district. Admission to the Supreme Court of the United States requires a special admission and oath. This process is conducted via motion for any individual who is in good standing at their state bar and has practiced for three years in the highest courts of their state.

## Bar Associations and Professional Standards
Many states, as well as some counties and cities, have local bar associations. These are voluntary organizations consisting of bar members who wish to associate for the purpose of advancing and guarding the standards of the legal profession. While these associations lack official recognition, they possess considerable influence in shaping and recommending procedure and legislation regarding the judicial establishment. 

Additionally, these associations serve a functional role in promoting or instituting proceedings to expel or discipline attorneys deemed unworthy of the bar. Some of these associations maintain valuable libraries. On a national level, the American Bar Association was established in 1878; it consists of over 3,500 members of similar position and character from various states. Certain associations also publish annual volumes of transactions.

## Regulations and Legal Contracts
Attorneys are subject to the laws of the state in which they practice. These laws may include specific prohibitions on advertising; for example, the New York Penal Code (1902 s. 148a) forbids attorneys from advertising for divorce cases, a regulation upheld in the case of *People v. Taylor* [Colorado] (1905, 75 Pac. Rep. 914).

Regarding financial arrangements, it is common practice throughout the United States for lawyers to enter into contracts for "contingent fees," where the attorney receives a percentage of the recovered amount. The courts uphold such contracts and do not consider them champertous, although they will be set aside if the bargain made with the client is found to be unconscionable, as seen in *Deering v. Scheyer* [N.Y.].
