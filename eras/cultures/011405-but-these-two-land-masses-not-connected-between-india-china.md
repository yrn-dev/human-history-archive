# Geological and Civilizational Separation of India and China

## Geological History and Land Masses
Historically, the land masses of India and China were not connected. Between these two regions lay a broad belt characterized by well-developed marine deposits of Tertiary and Mesozoic age. This intervening sea, referred to by Suess as the Tethys and by Neumayr as the central Mediterranean sea, reached its maximum extension during the Upper Cretaceous period, at which time its waters covered most of western Asia and slightly encroached upon the Indian land. The Eocene sea was similarly extensive.

The region's geography was defined by two ancient land masses: Angaraland to the north and Gondwanaland to the south. The Indian peninsula was a fragment of Gondwanaland, which was once united with the south of Africa. Evidence shows that the peninsula of India has remained land since at least the Carboniferous period, as indicated by the absence of marine fossils except near its borders and the presence of terrestrial and freshwater deposits of the Gondwana series. Conversely, the northern land mass, stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the south of China, has also been land since the close of the Palaeozoic era.

The union of these old continents occurred after the Eocene period, following the main elevation of the Himalayas. This elevation is evidenced by the presence of nummulitic limestone at heights of 20,000 feet. The formation of the Himalayas and other central Asian mountain chains isolated portions of the former central sea and eventually led to the elevation of the entire region. However, Gondwanaland did not persist; the section between South Africa and India sank beneath the waves during Tertiary times.

## Physical Barriers and Early Civilizations
The physical separation caused by the mountain masses of Central Asia, the Indian north-west frontier, and deserts created a distinct environment for the development of civilization. In China, a civilization developed and spread from the slopes of the Kuen-lun mountains and the then-fertile valley of the Tarim, moving into the valleys of the Yang-tse-kiang and the Hwang-ho.

This early civilization was Mongolian in character and developed spontaneously and unassisted. Until after the time of Alexander the Great, there were few traces of Semitic, Aryan, or Hamitic influence, as these were separated from China by wild nomadic tribes, deserts, and mountains. While some writers suggest a connection to ancient Sumeria, the vast obstacles of mountain and desert between the lower Euphrates and the Tarim valley make migration or interchange of peoples unlikely.

## Chinese Social and Political Structure
Chinese history is characterized by a specific approach to land ownership: the practice of dividing a man's landed possessions among all his sons. This prevented the rise of a class of great land-owners or permanent estates common in other countries. Instead, land was typically held in small, intensively cultivated freeholds. When repeated division rendered a plot too small to sustain a person, it was sold to a neighbor, and the former owner moved to a great town to become a wage-earning worker.

These town populations, often possessing almost no property, provided the recruitment pool for the Chinese government's soldiers and the gang labor required for building walls and canals. In the era before the Christian era, the role of slaves and war captives in China was smaller than in more westerly records.

## Cultural Development and Governance
Chinese civilization reached its culmination during the Tang period in the seventh century A.D. For over two thousand years until the nineteenth century, the civilization was marked by conservatism and a belief in its own general superiority. This era produced a profusion of poetry, art, and "astonishing cookery." While the empire experienced dynastic changes, rebellions, famines, and two great invasions that placed foreign dynasties on the throne of the Son of Heaven, the fundamental order—including the classics, the mandarins, the examinations, and habitual life—remained stable.

Intellectually, a divide existed between the north and south. North China, centered around the Hwang-ho River, was Confucian in spirit—upright, official-minded, and conservative. South China, centered around the Yang-tse-Kiang, was Taoist—artistic, sceptical, and experimental. This tension was evident during the "Age of Confusion" in the sixth century B.C., when the Chow dynasty was enfeebled. Eventually, the power of Ts’in became predominant, and Shi-Hwang-ti became the "First Universal Emperor" around the time of Asoka in India.

## External Relations and Exploration
China's maritime activity was limited; ships rarely put to sea, and when they did, their furthest adventures were typically to Borneo or India. Despite this, China's influence spread steadily into Mongolia, Manchuria, Korea, Nepal, Tibet, Siam, Cambodia, and Annam.

Interactions with India occurred through individual journeys and diplomatic missions. In 629 A.D., a Buddhist named Yuan Chwang traveled from the capital of Tai-tsung, Singan, to India. He returned in 645 A.D. and wrote a classic account of his travels, providing insights into Central Asia, India, and China during the seventh century. Additionally, records note the arrival of Muhammad’s envoys at Canton in 628 A.D.
