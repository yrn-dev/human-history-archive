# Afghanistan and the Southern Slopes of the Himalaya

## Geography and Topography
Afghanistan is part of an extensive plateau in western Asia that covers an area as large as Tibet, though it exists at a lower altitude. This high region is nearly conterminous with Baluchistan, Iran (or Persia), Armenia, and the provinces of Asia Minor. The eastern flank of this table-land is defined by a line of hills located a short distance from the Indus, between the Himalaya and the river's mouth, generally not exceeding 4,000 or 5,000 feet in elevation, though some summits reach 10,000 feet or more.

The north-eastern portion of the Afghan table-land forms a continuous mass of mountain with Tibet and the Himalaya between the 71st and 72nd meridians and 34 and 36 degrees North. An unbroken mountain range extends from the intersection of the 71st meridian and 36th parallel; one side reaches the crest of the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau, while the other extends west toward the Caspian. This high-altitude north-eastern range separates the headwaters of the Oxus, which flow to the Aral Sea, from the headwaters of the Indus and its tributary, the Kabul. These latter rivers unite below Peshawar and discharge into the Arabian Sea. The western part of this range was known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons.

In the region of the Hindu Kush, a mountain system connects the southern abutment near Bamian with the water-divide north of Herat. This area is characterized by a series of mountains and plains where the value of Herat and the Pamirs as bases of aggression has been assessed.

## Climate and Rainfall
Afghanistan and the southern slopes of the Himalaya are part of a temperate climatic belt that extends from the Mediterranean across to northern China and Asia Minor. Due to its elevation, this zone experiences summers that are hot and winters that are severe, though neither is extreme. Rainfall in this belt is sufficient to maintain vigorous vegetation.

In Afghanistan specifically, the rainfall is influenced by the south-west monsoon. Vapour-bearing winds carry uncondensed rain past Sind and the Indus, eventually falling on the outer ranges of Afghanistan and the western Himalaya.

## Flora and Vegetation
The vegetation of Afghanistan and the southern slopes of the Himalaya shows a marked similarity to that of southern Europe. The flora of northern Afghanistan approximates that of the contiguous western Himalaya. The evergreen oak of southern Europe, *Quercus Ilex*, is found in forests as far east as the Sutlej. In the higher regions of Afghanistan and Persia, thistles and Boraginaceae are abundant, along with gigantic Umbelliferae such as *Prangos*, *Peucedanum*, *Bubon*, *Dorema*, *Galbanum*, and *Ferula*, some of which extend into Tibet.

In the less elevated parts of Afghanistan and Baluchistan, the flora resembles that of the hotter Mediterranean region and similarly situated parts of southern Persia. This includes an abundance of aromatic shrubs, while trees are rare and mostly restricted to *Celtis*, *Pistacia*, *Dodonaea*, poplars, and the date palm. Dry hills in these areas are covered by prickly forms of *Astragalus* and *Statice*.

Regarding the Himalaya, vegetation varies by region:
*   **Eastern Himalaya:** Vegetation is most abundant and varied, with luxuriant forests extending to 12,000 feet. Below 8,000 feet, plantains, bamboos, *Pandanus*, *Calami*, and other palms are abundant. Epiphytal orchids are frequent between 4,000 and 10,000 feet.
*   **Western Himalaya:** The climate is drier and forests are less dense. Warm slopes are covered with rhododendron, oaks, or *Pinus longifolia*. A thick forest of deciduous trees is almost universal between 8,000 and 12,000 feet. The cedar or deodar is primarily indigenous west of the sources of the Ganges, with these western forms increasing in number as one advances toward Afghanistan.

## Zoological Zones
The geographical limits of the equatorial zoological zone generally coincide with the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn. However, this boundary undulates; it sweeps north through Persia and Afghanistan to the southern ranges of the Himalayas before following the course of the Yang-tse-Kiang.

## Historical Routes
The region has served as a corridor for significant journeys. One recorded route involved traveling southward through the Khyber Pass and Peshawur, and returning via a southern route that crossed the Pamirs from Afghanistan to Kashgar. This path followed the line of retreat used by the Yueh Chi seven centuries prior, passing through Yarkand and along the slopes of the Kuen Lun.
