# Guipuzcoa

Guipuzcoa is one of the three Basque Provinces (*Provincias Vascongadas*) located in north-eastern Spain. Along with Alava and Biscay (or Vizcaya), it forms a triangular territory bounded by France and the Bay of Biscay to the north, Navarre to the east, and the provinces of Logrono, Burgos, and Santander to the south and west. Of the three Basque Provinces, Guipuzcoa is the only one described as being wholly Basque.

## Political History and Governance
The political status of Guipuzcoa shifted between dependencies on Navarre and Castile before it was definitively united to Castile in the year 1200. Despite this union and the subsequent integration into the crown of Spain, the Basque Provinces remained a "land apart" (*tierra apartada*). They maintained their own customs and laws, known as *fueros*, which the Spanish kings were required to swear to observe and maintain. 

The autonomy of the region was such that royal orders, Spanish legislation, or decrees of the Cortes held no force within the Provinces unless they were countersigned by the juntas. The juntas of the provinces acted in common to some extent, using a seal engraved with the word *Iruracbat* ("The Three One") as a symbol of their unity. Guipuzcoa demonstrated its independent diplomatic capacity in 1481 when it alone proposed a treaty of friendship, peace, and free trade with England, which was signed in Westminster on March 9, 1482. This tradition of autonomy continued, with the Basques making their own treaties with France and England, and being mentioned separately from Spain in the treaty of Utrecht in 1713. This system of independence lasted until 1833.

## Religion and Church-State Relations
The people of Guipuzcoa are characterized as highly religious and conservative in their practices, yet they resisted being "priest-ridden." They consistently opposed efforts by the crown to impose the authority of Spanish bishops. In Guipuzcoa, bishops were excluded from ecclesiastical patronage; instead, the appointment of priests was handled by the king, the nobles, or the municipalities. Alternatively, priests were elected by the people or chosen through competitive examination.

The region preserved primitive Christian traditions forgotten elsewhere in the West, including the earliest form of Christian marriage and the order of deaconesses. The feast of Corpus Christi, instituted by Pope Urban IV in 1262, is recorded in Basque almanacs as *Phesta-berria* (the New Feast). 

The Basques maintained a strict boundary between religious authority and civic life. Priests were not permitted to interfere with dances or games. Furthermore, when the Spanish bishops forbade drama across Spain in 1757, the cortes of Navarre compelled the king to withdraw that order.

## Notable Figures and Industry
Guipuzcoa is the birthplace of Ignatius de Loyola (1491–1556), the founder of the Society of Jesus. Born at the castle of Loyola on the river Urola near Azpeitia, he was the youngest of thirteen children and the son of Beltran, lord of the noble houses of Oñaz and Loyola. Before his religious life, he served as a page at the court of Isabella and Ferdinand and later served Antonio Maurique, duke of Nagera, following a career in arms.

Economically, the province is known for its specialized manufacturing. The swords of Mondragon in Guipuzcoa were renowned even before those of Toledo. Additionally, the town of Eibar has long functioned as a small-arms factory. In the 19th century, Senor Zuloaga revived the artistic practice of inlaying steel and iron with silver and gold in Eibar.
