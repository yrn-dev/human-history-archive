# Sughd

Sughd, also known as Sogdiana in antiquity, was a celebrated and flourishing district of Central Asia. Geographically, the region is situated in a fertile plain watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus rivers. It is bordered by mountain ranges that extend as far west as Samarkand before diminishing into low hills north of Bokhara. In the early middle ages, the region was also referred to as Ma wara 'l Nahr (or Ma-vera-un-nahr), a term translated in classical titles as Transoxiana.

## Historical Governance and Conquests
The foundation of the capital of Sogdiana is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, the region became part of the Seleucidae empire, sharing a similar history with Bactria. Later, the Yue-chi nomads moved from the east into the valley of the Oxus, establishing themselves as a territorial power. By approximately A.D. 45, the dominions of the Yue-chi king, Kadphises I, extended from Bokhara to the Indus.

Control of the district eventually returned to Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand. Following the Arab conquest of Persia between 640 and 642, Islam was introduced and rapidly became the dominant faith. 

In the 11th century, Malik Shah, the third ruler of the Persian Seljuk dynasty, crossed the Oxus and subdued the lands watered by the Oxus and Jaxartes. In 1216, Mahommed Shah Khwarizm subdued Bokhara, but this control was seized by Jenghiz Khan in 1220. While Jenghiz Khan's conquest wasted the country, prosperity partially returned under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent. Mongol possession continued until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand.

## The City of Bokhara
Bokhara (Bokkara-i-Sherif) is the capital of the state of Bokhara, located on the left bank of the Zarafshan and the Shahri-rud irrigation canal. The city is enclosed by a stone wall 8 meters long and 28 feet high, featuring semicircular towers and eleven gates. The current city was begun in A.D. 830 on the site of an older city; it was destroyed by Jenghiz Khan in 1220 and subsequently rebuilt.

The city is a major center of trade and religion in Central Asia. Its bazaars specialize in carpets, metal wares (particularly copper), silk stuffs, and Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins. Its population is estimated between 50,000 and 100,000, including an ancient colony of Jews.

Architecturally, Bokhara is noted for its 360 mosques and 140 *madrasas* (theological colleges), where nearly 10,000 pupils are educated. Notable structures include the 16th-century Mir-Arab mosque and the Mejid-kalyan (or Kok-humbez) mosque. Adjacent to the latter is a 203-foot brick minaret used until 1871 to execute state criminals by throwing them from the top. The principal square is the Raghistan, which contains a citadel built on a 45-foot artificial eminence. This citadel houses the palace of the emir, the prison, water-cisterns, and the homes of chief functionaries.

## The City of Samarkand
Samarkand served generally as the seat of government for Sughd. Along with Bokhara, it was a key node in the intellectual community of the Arabized world. By the 9th century, scholars in Samarkand corresponded with learned men in Bagdad, Cairo, and Cordoba. 

The city is also associated with the life of Mahommed ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari (810-872), the Iranian-born author of the most widely accepted collection of traditions (*hadith*) from Mahomet. Al-Bukhari traveled from Egypt to Samarkand to collect and test traditions. He eventually died in banishment at Kartank, a suburb of Samarkand.

## Learning and Religion
Bokhara has long been a primary seat of Islamitic learning and religious life. Even during the Mongol invasions of the 13th century, when other Moslem cities like Samarkand were laid waste, Bokhara remained independent and continued its role as a center of study. Persian mysticism also spread to the city during the middle ages.

Bokhara remains the principal book-market of Central Asia. Although many rich *madrasa* libraries were lost to fire, confiscation by emirs, or scattering, rare books are still found in private libraries, attracting bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan.
