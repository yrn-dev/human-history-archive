# Autographs

The existence of autographs—documents written in the handwriting of their composers—must necessarily have been coeval with the invention of letters. While such documents may possibly exist among the early examples of writing in the East, including the clay tablets of Babylonia and Assyria and the early papyri of Egypt, certain cultural practices made their production less common. In the East, the practice of employing professional scribes to write the body of documents and using seals for authentication (the "signum") almost precludes the existence of autographs. For example, when records state that King Darius "signed" a decree or that Jezebel wrote letters in the name of Ahab, it is understood that professional scribes performed the writing and the king's seal provided the authentication, serving as an equivalent to the signatures used by western nations.

## Early and Classical Examples
Certainty regarding the existence of autographs is found primarily in the Roman and Ptolemaic periods of Egyptian history. Due to the dry atmosphere of Egypt, an abundance of papyrus documents has been preserved, including personal documents and original letters from the 3rd century B.C. and later. These records demonstrate that writing was widespread among both lettered and unlettered individuals.

In Greece and Italy, most ancient writings perished. However, some waxen tablets and charred papyrus rolls were recovered from Pompeii and Herculaneum. These are particularly valuable because Roman law required the actual subscriptions or attested marks of the persons involved; consequently, many of these tablets contain autograph signatures of witnesses and principals attached to legal deeds, alongside seal impressions.

## Middle Ages and Early Modern Period
Autographs of famous men from the early middle ages are rare or difficult to identify, with the exception of sovereigns. While some documents claim to be autographs—such as a charter at Canterbury stated to be written by Dunstan—the existence of duplicates suggests they may be copies. Nevertheless, some confirmed autograph manuscripts exist, including the chronicles of Sigebert of Gembloux, Robert de Monte, and Ordericus Vitalis. Other examples include the writings of the English chronicler Matthew of Paris (found among the Cottonian MSS), documents in the hand of William of Wykeham in the British Museum, and the writings of the historian Joinville in French archives. By the 15th century, the Paston Letters provide numerous autographs of historical figures from the Norfolk family.

From the 16th century onward, autograph documents became plentiful. However, some notable figures remain underrepresented. William Shakespeare is a primary example; save for two signatures on property conveyances in Blackfriars and three signatures on the sheets of his will, there is no vestige of his handwriting. Other signatures attributed to him in books are dismissed as imitations or forgeries, such as those produced by W. H. Ireland.

## Autograph Collecting
The practice of collecting autographs for their own sake originated in the Low Countries and Germany, primarily among university students. They collected signatures and inscriptions from friends in small oblong pocket volumes known as *alba amicorum*. The British Museum holds a collection of these, with the earliest (Egerton MS. 1178) beginning in 1554.

In England, the taste for autograph collecting developed later. Earlier collections, such as that of Sir Robert Cotton or the collection of Philippe de Bethune (Comte de Selles et Charost) in the reign of Louis XIV, were gathered for historical or literary interest rather than as specimens of handwriting. Once the fashion was established, collectors began seeking autographs of both living and deceased persons. While many such collections were eventually dispersed, the collection of Alfred Morrison remains intact and is documented in a sumptuous catalogue.

## Modern Definitions and Facsimiles
In a restricted modern sense, "autographs" often refers specifically to the signatures or documents of persons of notoriety or eminence. Because private documents are less likely to survive, the earliest surviving autographs in this sense are typically the signatures of great officials and royal personages attached to important public deeds.

There are numerous published collections of autograph facsimiles. In England, these include works by J. Thane (*British Autography*), J. G. Nichols, C. J. Smith, and various publications by J. Netherclift and his son. Other official publications include facsimiles of national manuscripts from Ireland, Scotland, and from William the Conqueror to Queen Anne.
