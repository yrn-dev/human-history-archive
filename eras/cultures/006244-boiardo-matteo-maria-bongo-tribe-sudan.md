# BOIARDO, MATTEO MARIA AND BONGO (TRIBE OF SUDAN)

## Matteo Maria Boiardo
Matteo Maria Boiardo was an Italian poet and count born into a noble and illustrious house. While his family was established at Ferrara, they were originally from Reggio. Boiardo was born at Scandiano, a seignorial estate of his family near Reggio di Modena. Sources differ on his birth date, with Tiraboschi citing approximately 1434 and Mazzuchelli citing 1420.

Boiardo received an education at the university of Ferrara, where he became a doctor in law and philosophy. During his studies, he acquired a strong knowledge of Latin, Greek, and Oriental languages. He enjoyed the favor of Duke Borso d'Este and the subsequent duke, Hercules, at the court of Ferrara. This relationship led to several honorable appointments; in 1478, he served as the governor of Reggio. By 1481, he was elected captain of Modena and was reappointed as the governor of the citadel and town of Reggio. He died in 1494, though the specific month of his death is unknown.

## Literary Works of Boiardo
Boiardo is best known for his great poem, *Orlando Inamorato*, which is regarded as one of the most important works in Italian literature. It serves as the first example of a romantic epic that acted as a model for later works, specifically influencing Ariosto's *Orlando Furioso*. While some writers, including Gravina and Mazzuchelli, suggested that Boiardo modeled the poem after Homer's *Iliad*—comparing the siege of Paris to Troy and the character of Angelica to Helen—the actual subject matter is derived from the *Fabulous Chronicle* of the pseudo-Turpin. Aside from principal characters such as Roland, Oliver, and Charlemagne, the detailed plot differs from the *Fabulous Chronicle*.

Boiardo typically composed his works while retiring to his estates, such as Scandiano, rather than within the court precincts. He frequently included descriptions of the environs of his chateau in his poetry. Additionally, he drew the names of his heroes—including Agramant, Sacripant, Gradasse, and Mandricardo—from the names of his own peasants, as he felt their "uncouthness" was appropriate for Saracen warriors.

Boiardo did not finish *Orlando Inamorato* during his lifetime. The poem was printed at Scandiano in the year following his death under the supervision of his son, Count Camillo. A Latin letter from Antonia Caraffa di Reggio prefixed to the poem is dated June 1495. A second edition was printed in Venice before the year 1500, followed by two further reprints in that city.

## The Bongo Tribe of Sudan
The Bongo (also known as Dor or Deran) are a tribe of Nilotic negroes inhabiting the south-west portion of the Bahr-el-Ghazal province in Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. They are likely related to the Zandeh tribes of the Welle district. Historically, the Bongo lived in numerous independent and peaceful small communities. G.A. Schweinfurth, who lived among them for two years, stated that their population numbered at least 300,000 before the arrival of dervishes and slave-raiders around 1850. Their numbers were greatly reduced by these groups until the Sudan government established effective control between 1904 and 1906, allowing the population to recuperate. Under the Sudan government, they have returned to managing their own affairs.

### Physical Characteristics and Dress
The Bongo are characterized as being of medium height and thick-set, with black hair and a red-brown complexion described as being like the soil of their residence. Schweinfurth noted that their heads are nearly round, possessing a higher cephalic index than any other African race known to him. In later life, Bongo women incline toward steatopygia; Schweinfurth observed that this fat deposit, combined with a worn tail of bast, gave the women the appearance of "dancing baboons" while walking.

Traditionally, Bongo men wore loin-cloths and dozens of iron rings on their arms to create a form of armor. Women wore a girdle with an attached tuft of grass. In modern times, both sexes largely use cotton cloths for dress. Tribal ornaments include plugs or nails passed through the lower lip; women often wear a disk several inches in diameter in the lower lip, a ring or straw in the upper lip, a ring in the septum, and straws in the *alae* of the nostrils.

### Culture and Diet
Unlike other Negroes of the upper Nile, the Bongo are not significant cattle-breeders and instead focus on agriculture. Their primary crops include durra, sesame, tobacco, and sorghum. Their diet consists of fungi, tubers, and fruits found in the region. They eat almost every type of reptile, insect, beast, and bird, with the sole exception of the dog. They do not despise flesh regardless of whether it is putrid or fresh, and they are known to drive vultures from carcasses.

The Bongo are known for their well-built huts, which can sometimes reach a height of 24 feet.
