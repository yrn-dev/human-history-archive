# The Geography and Administration of the Arabian Peninsula and Adjacent Tracts

## Physical Geography and Climate
The peninsula of Arabia, along with Syria—which serves as its continuation to the north-west—is characterized by some of the hottest and driest conditions found in Baluchistan and Persia. Much of this region consists of low mountains reaching altitudes of 3,000 to 4,000 feet, featuring narrow valleys with scanty vegetation. However, areas near the coasts, where rainfall is greatest, exhibit higher fertility. In northern Syria, the mountains of Lebanon reach approximately 10,000 feet, and the increased water supply makes the land more productive.

The northern part of this tract is conterminous with the plain of Mesopotamia, which reaches a maximum elevation of about 700 feet above the sea. Geologically, Syria, Arabia, and the peninsula of India are regions of faulting or quiescence rather than folding, where Upper Palaeozoic, Mesozoic, and Tertiary beds lie almost horizontally upon the oldest rocks.

## Flora and Fauna
The rainless regions of south-western Asia possess a flora that is continuous with the desert flora of northern and eastern Africa. This botanical tract extends from the coast of Senegal to the 75th meridian East, encompassing the peninsula of Arabia, south Persia, Afghanistan, Baluchistan, and the shores of the Persian Gulf. Its western limit reaches the Cape Verde Islands, with partial representation in Abyssinia.

Zoologically, the extreme south-west of the continent—including Arabia, Palestine, and southern Persia, and extending to Sind and Baluchistan—constitutes a separate district. This area belongs to the Ethiopian region, which also covers Africa south of the Atlas. The extremely dry and hot tracts from Arabia through Baluchistan to Sind show a considerable uniformity in life forms, closely resembling those of neighboring hot and dry African regions.

## Political Status and Social Structure
Nominally, the whole tract is subject to Turkey, with the exception of south eastern Arabia. Despite this nominal subjection, the population is practically independent to a significant extent. In the more arid districts, people lead a freebooting, nomadic, and pastoral life under the leadership of petty chiefs. Conversely, in more fertile tracts where external commerce is established and agriculture is more profitable, the population is settled in towns.

## Regional Profiles: Asir and Yemen
In western Arabia, the district of Asir lies between 17° 30' and 21° N. and 40° 30' and 45° E. It is bounded by the Red Sea to the west, Hejaz to the north, Nejd to the east, and Yemen to the south. Asir is divided into two primary zones:
*   **The Tehama:** A lowland zone 20 to 30 miles wide along the coast that is hot and barren. Key locations include the chief port, Kanfuda, as well as Marsa Hali and El Itwad.
*   **The Mountainous Tract:** An area with an average altitude between 6,000 and 7,000 feet. This region is populous and fertile due to regular rainfall and a temperate climate. Its well-watered valleys produce dates and cereals. Notable areas include the Wadi Taraba—home to a town surrounded by gardens and palm groves—and the district of Besha. The Zahran district, located on the crest of the main range, includes the large market town of Makhwa, which exports grain to Mecca.

Historically, Arabia has been a land of nomads, except for the fertile strip of the Yemen in the south.

## Historical and Cultural Influence
Arabia is recognized as the land of origin and headquarters of the Semitic peoples. From this region, waves of nomads migrated north, east, and west, influencing early civilizations. These migrations included the establishment of the Arameans in Syria (with Damascus as their capital), the settlement of the Babylonians and Assyrians, the conquest of Egypt by the Hyksos, and the partial conquest of the "Promised Land" by the Hebrews. Additionally, Chaldeans from Eastern Arabia settled in the southern Sumerian lands.

In terms of religious influence, Islam is paramount in Arabia, Turkey, Persia, and Afghanistan. The faith is characterized by the fusion of religious and secular power, where the Moslem Church functions as a Moslem state. This state is characterized by polygamy, slavery, and the theoretical equality of Moslems, who maintain a political status superior to non-Moslems.
