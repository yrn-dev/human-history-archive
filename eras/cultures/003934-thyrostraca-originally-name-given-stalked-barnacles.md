# Thyrostraca

Thyrostraca, also known as Cirripedia, is a division of Crustacea that includes organisms commonly referred to as barnacles. Within this group, there are two primary forms: the stalked barnacles and the sessile barnacles.

## Classification and Characteristics
The name Thyrostraca was originally applied to the stalked barnacles, identified by C. Darwin as *Lepadidae*. These organisms typically attach themselves in great numbers to objects floating in the sea, such as drift-wood, and are noted as being one of the primary agents responsible for the fouling of ships' bottoms during long voyages.

In contrast, the sessile barnacles, which Darwin identified as *Balanidae*, are known as "acorn-shells." These are found in myriads on all coasts, where they encrust rocks between tide-marks.

Historically, the true biological affinities of barnacles were not immediately understood. Due to their external appearance, they were initially classified with the Mollusca. Their correct classification as Crustaceans was recognized after Vaughan Thompson demonstrated in 1830 that they develop from a typically Crustacean larva that is free-swimming. This biological reality is described by Huxley as a barnacle being a Crustacean that is fixed by its head and uses its legs to kick food into its mouth.

## Relationship to Other Crustaceans
Within the broader category of Crustacea, the Thyrostraca (or Cirripedes) are distinguished from the Entomostraca and the Malacostraca. There is also an intermediate group whose exact position remains a subject of dispute among scientists. This group is referred to by different names depending on the authority: Claus calls it the Leptostraca, while others categorize it as the Nebaliacea (within the Malacostraca) or as a subdivision of the entomostracans called Phyllocarida (according to Packard and Sars). This intermediate group currently comprises the single family *Nebaliidae*.

## Medieval Myths and Heterogenesis
From at least the 12th century, a persistent myth in medieval natural history concerned the origin of the barnacle or bernacle goose (*Bernicla branta*), a winter visitor to Britain. Because the Arctic nesting-places of these birds were unknown at the time, it was fabled that the geese originated from the shell-like fruit of a tree growing by the sea-shore.

Some versions of this story claimed the shell grew like a mushroom on rotting timber in the sea—a description that clearly refers to barnacles of the genus *Lepas*. This belief is an example of "heterogenesis," a theory suggesting that the offspring of an organism can be entirely different from the parent, potentially giving rise to a different species, genus, family, or even a different kingdom (such as an animal giving rise to a plant).

An illustrated account of this "barnacle-geese" fable was printed in an early volume of the Royal Society of London, describing how buds of a tree near the sea produced barnacles which, upon falling into the water, developed into geese. Modern science views this as an imaginary embroidery based on the fact that barnacles attach to submerged timber and that a specific species of goose is called the bernicle goose. While some modern proponents of heterogenesis have attempted to apply the theory to microscopic plants and animals, serious observers using modern microscopic technical methods have found these cases to be examples of parasitism.
