# Materials Employed in Construction

The materials used in construction vary widely depending on the intended purpose of the structure, ranging from the fabrication of small utilitarian objects and furniture to the erection of large-scale industrial works and permanent buildings.

## Building and Architecture
Throughout different periods of architectural style, the fundamental elements of solid edifices have remained consistent, consisting of bricks, stones, and mortar. While modern workmen possess a scientific understanding of the properties of these materials that exceeds that of medieval craftsmen, the core components remain the same.

In specific urban environments, such as London, building acts govern the materials used in dwelling-houses; notably, these laws forbid the use of timber-framed buildings. In certain cases, such as London flats or combined shop and dwelling-houses exceeding 1,000 square feet in area, stairs and corridors must be constructed from fire-resisting materials. Conversely, timber buildings are permitted in parts of New York.

Alternative construction methods include the use of steel dovetailed sheets fixed between small steel stanchions and plastered with cement on both sides. This method, which is fire and burglar proof, was used for the Newgate prison cells in London, the British pavilion at the 1900 Paris Exhibition, and buildings in India, Venezuela, and South Africa.

## Asphalt and Bituminous Materials
Asphalt, which is typically black or blackish brown and often brittle at ordinary temperatures, serves several construction purposes. It is used as a water-excluding coating for concrete floors, a "damp-course" in building, and in the manufacture of roofing-felt. Asphaltic concrete is also used to create foundations for high-speed engines, dynamos, and steam-hammers.

For the construction of roadways, asphaltic or bituminous limestone is used, sourced from locations such as the department of Ain (Seyssel), the canton of Neuchatel (Val de Travers), and near the city of Hanover (Limmer). Because rock containing more than 11% bitumen cannot be used satisfactorily for street pavements, richer and poorer varieties are mixed to achieve a bitumen proportion of 9 to 10%. The richer rock is utilized as asphalt "mastic" for roofs, floors, and footpaths.

## Industrial and Heavy Construction
The construction of bridges, girders, and boilers relies chiefly on steel and iron. These works primarily employ rolled sections and plates. Boilers, which must withstand high pressures and temperatures, require materials that combine strength when heated with high ductility. This is achieved through the selection of iron or steel with a high percentage of elongation and the application of hot and cold bending or temper tests.

## Basket-work and Wicker-work
The materials used in basket-work are numerous, with certain species of willow providing the largest supply. While willows are grown extensively across Europe, those of English growth—specifically from Nottinghamshire, Leicestershire, and the valleys of the Trent and Thames—are noted for their leathery and tough texture. 

Other materials used in wicker-work include:
* **Cane or Rattan:** The stem of *Calamus viminalis* or allied species, used whole or as skains. Since 1880, "cane-pith" (the central pith) has been used for finer work and furniture.
* **Rush and Straw:** Often colored and used since approximately 1880.
* **Wood and Nails:** Increasingly used in the construction of tables, chairs, and other basket-work furniture.

Historically, these materials were used for larger constructions. Early settlers in western Europe and Rome built huts of osier work plastered with clay. Similarly, soldiers' shields were fashioned from wicker-work, sometimes covered with hides; Xenophon noted that exiled Greeks at the Peiraeus used whitened osiers for shields. Boats were also constructed from these materials; some were covered in animal skins, while those used on the Tigris and Euphrates were round and covered with bitumen.
