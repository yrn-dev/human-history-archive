# Consonants in Various Languages and Texts

The study of consonants varies significantly across different linguistic systems, ranging from the phonetic guides of European and Asian languages to the structural composition of ancient sacred texts.

## Czech Pronunciation
In the Czech (Cech) alphabet, the letters b, d, f, k, l, m, n, p, r, v, and z are pronounced as they are in English. The letter g is pronounced as the English g in "gone," and the initial s is pronounced as the English initial s. However, several other consonants follow distinct rules:
* **S, Z, and R:** The letter s can be pronounced as "sh," z as "zh" (similar to the French j), and r as "rsh."
* **N and V:** The letter n is pronounced as the Spanish n (as in *canon*), and v is pronounced as f when it appears before p, s, t, or k.
* **C, CH, and J:** The letter c can be pronounced as "ts" or "ch," while ch is pronounced as the German ch. The letter j is pronounced as the English y, though it remains unpronounced before m, s, or d.
* **K and H:** When k appears before d, it is pronounced as g. The letter h is always pronounced; it sounds like the German ch at the end of words or before t and k, and is softer in other positions, such as in *bahno*.

## Dutch Consonants and Evolution
The development of Dutch consonants shows a transition from Middle Dutch to Modern Dutch. In Middle Dutch, final consonants were sharpened and graphically represented. In Modern Dutch, the soft consonant is written more frequently than it is sounded to maintain agreement with inflexional forms (for example, Middle Dutch *dach* became Modern Dutch *dag*, following the plural *dagen*).

The gutturals in Dutch include h, k, g, and ch. The letter h is pronounced as in English, and k was pronounced like the English k (though Middle Dutch sometimes used c instead of k). The letter g is a soft spirant not used in English; in Middle Dutch, this was also indicated by gh. The combination ch is pronounced as the German ch (without the i-sound). When ch combines with s to form sch at the end of a syllable (such as *vleesch*), it loses its sound, and the resulting s-sound is not purely dental. As an initial consonant, sch is pronounced nearly as sg (as in *schip*), except in Saxon and Frisian dialects, where the old consonant sk (as in *skool* or *skip*) is retained.

## Hebrew and the Old Testament
The original text of the Old Testament was composed using an alphabet that contained no vowels, similar to the alphabets of the Phoenician, Aramaean, and Moabite neighbors. Consequently, the text consisted of consonants only. While this text is complete and intelligible in itself, it is also ambiguous.

Over time, changes occurred in the text. Certain consonants were inserted to express vowel sounds; for example, the consonant h was used for the vowel a, and the consonant corresponding to w expressed u or o. Evidence from Semitic inscriptions, the Septuagint, and internal comparisons suggests that while some authors used these vowel consonants, many were added later by editors and transcribers. Between the 5th and 9th centuries A.D., Jewish scholars invented accents and vowel signs. These form a separate text of interpretation that is unintelligible on its own and only becomes clear when read with the original consonants of the Old Testament.

## Consonants in Bantu and Basque
In Bantu languages, no two consonants may appear together without an intervening vowel, with the exception of sibilants, labials, or nasals. Furthermore, consonants are not doubled, except when a labial, two d's, two r's, or two nasals meet due to the elision of a vowel.

Basque utilizes the Roman character (French or Spanish) and features thirty-eight simple consonants. "Wet" consonants, such as *ny*, *dy*, and *ty*, are found in some dialects. Basque does not allow the doubling of consonants, and in current speech, most soft consonants are dropped. Additionally, the letter r cannot begin a word; for instance, *rationem* is written as *arrazoin*.

## Phonetic Concepts and Other Systems
In the study of prosody, assonance is defined as the riming of words in the accented vowel and subsequent sounds, specifically excluding the consonants. This is considered an imperfect form of rhyme. While Romance languages often prefer the correspondence of vowels, Teutonic languages generally show a preference for consonants.

In the transcription of Japanese words into English via Hepburn’s rule, consonants are treated as they are in English.
