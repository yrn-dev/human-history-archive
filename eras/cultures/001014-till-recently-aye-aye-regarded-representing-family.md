# Aye-Aye

The aye-aye (*Chiromys* or *Daubentonia madagascariensis*) is a Malagasy lemur described as the most aberrant and remarkable of its group. The term "aye-aye" is of uncertain meaning, though it may be an exclamation.

## Classification and Taxonomy
Until recently, the aye-aye was considered to represent its own distinct family, the *Chiromyidae*. However, this classification was revised following the discovery that the animal's inner ear structure resembles that of other lemurs from Madagascar. Because it differs from all other members of that specific group, it is now best classified as a subfamily, the *Chiromyinae*, within the family *Lemuridae*.

## Physical Characteristics
The aye-aye possesses a superficial resemblance to a dusky-colored, long-haired cat with unusually large, naked eyes. Its physical features include:
* **Head and Face:** A broad, rounded head with a short face.
* **Hands and Feet:** It has large hands with long, thin fingers ending in pointed claws. The third finger is specifically noted for its extreme slenderness. The foot features a large opposable great toe with a flat nail, while the remaining toes have compressed, pointed claws.
* **Coat and Tail:** The animal has a long, bushy tail. Its general color is dark brown, consisting of a woolly under-coat and long, rather loose outer fur.
* **Anatomy:** It possesses two teats located in the inguinal position.

## Habitat and Behavior
The aye-aye is completely nocturnal and typically lives alone or in pairs, primarily inhabiting bamboo forests. It constructs large, ball-like nests made of dried leaves, which are lodged in the fork of large tree branches with an opening on one side.

Observations of captive specimens indicate that the aye-aye feeds mainly on juices, particularly from sugar-cane. It uses strong incisor teeth to tear open the hard, woody circumference of the cane stalk. Additionally, it is reported to eat certain species of wood-boring caterpillars. To obtain these, the animal cuts into their burrows with its teeth and extracts them using the claw of its attenuated middle finger.

## History of Discovery
The aye-aye was discovered by Pierre Sonnerat in 1780. The specimen Sonnerat brought to Paris remained the only known example of the species until 1860. Since that time, many more specimens have been obtained, including one that lived for several years in the gardens of the Zoological Society of London.
