# Brittany

Brittany (French: *Bretagne*), formerly known as Armorica, is an ancient province and duchy of France located on the north-west peninsula. It corresponds nearly to the departments of Ille-et-Vilaine, Morbihan, Côtes-du-Nord, Finistère, and Lower Loire. The region is popularly divided into Lower (Eastern) and Upper (Western) Brittany.

## Geography and Environment
The peninsula has a superficial extent of 30,000 square kilometres (approximately 18,630 English sq. m.) and reaches a maximum length of 250 kilometres (about 155 English miles) between the Atlantic Ocean and the English Channel. The territory is divided into two distinct zones: an inland zone and a maritime zone. 

The center of the province features two plateaus. The northern plateau is dominated by the Monts d'Arrée, while the southern plateau continues into the Montagnes Noires. These ranges do not exceed 1,150 feet in height, though their wild nature gives them the appearance of high mountains. Parts of these plateaus are covered with *landes*, which is unproductive moorland. 

The coastline is rocky, lined with islets and reefs, and is particularly indented along the English Channel. River mouths form deep estuaries, and most waterways are of little value due to their torrent-like character, with the exception of the Vilaine river basin, which flows through Rennes.

## Early History and Roman Period
Exact knowledge of Brittany prior to the Roman era is unavailable, though the primitive populations left numerous megalithic monuments, including cromlechs, menhirs, and dolmens. In 56 B.C., the Romans destroyed the fleet of the Veneti. In 52 B.C., the inhabitants of Armorica joined the Gauls in a great insurrection against Caesar, who finally subdued them in 51 B.C. Roman civilization remained established in the region for several centuries. During this time, the people of Armorica were described by some sources as a mobile, undisciplined, and rebellious population.

## Celtic Migration and the Middle Ages
In the 5th century, Celtic inhabitants of Britain emigrated to Armorica to escape the Saxons and Angles. These immigrants populated much of the peninsula and founded monasteries, which aided in clearing the wild and barren land. The newcomers, who were converted to Christianity, established the counties of Domnonée, Léon, Cornouaille, and Vannes. 

A powerful aristocracy emerged, utilizing serfs or villeins to cultivate estates. The Celts engaged in a prolonged struggle against Frankish kings. While Louis the Pious appointed a native chief named Nomenoë to lead Brittany, Nomenoë eventually rebelled against Charles the Bald. In 846, Nomenoë forced Charles to recognize Brittany's independence.

The region faced Northmen invasions during the late 9th and early 10th centuries. They were driven back on several occasions, first by Salomon (who died in 874) and later by Alain, count of Vannes (who died in 907). A decisive victory over the Northmen was eventually achieved by Alain Barbetorte (who died in 952).

## The Duchy and Union with France
Over time, ducal power strengthened and curbed the influence of feudal nobles. However, the death of John III in 1341 without a direct heir triggered a war of succession between the houses of Montfort and Blois that lasted until 1364. This conflict was an incident of the Hundred Years' War, with the houses of Montfort and Blois supporting England and France, respectively. John of Montfort was recognized as Duke John IV in 1364, though his reign was troubled by a struggle with Olivier de Clisson.

Subsequent dukes, including John V, Francis I, Peter II, and Arthur III, saw a remarkable development of ducal authority, which included the creation of a standing army and the levying of hearth taxes (*fouages*). Francis II fought against Louis XI during the War of the Public Weal and later against Charles VIII during "The Mad War" (*La Guerre Folle*). Following the death of Francis II, the king of France invaded and forced Francis's daughter, Anne of Brittany, to marry him in 1491.

The definitive annexation of the duchy was settled by a contract in 1532 under Francis I, who married Claude, the daughter of Anne and Louis XII. This contract guaranteed the liberties and privileges of Brittany. The province retained its own estates until the Revolution, although the royal power frequently attempted to reduce these privileges, leading to vigorous resistance in the 18th century, particularly between 1760 and 1769.

## Economic Development
Historically, industrial pursuits were limited primarily to a few seaport towns. In 1457, Bretagne's maritime presence was notable, with forty-two vessels represented in the harbor of Bruges.

In the 19th century, Brittany emerged from its long isolation through the development of other means of communication and railways. Agriculture saw remarkable progress during this period; market-gardening expanded along the coast, cereals were grown in fertile districts, and many *landes* were cleared and converted into excellent pasturage.
