# Oviparous Species and Egg Incubation

Oviparous organisms are those that produce eggs. This biological characteristic is found across various classes of animals, including arachnids, insects, amphibians, reptiles, and birds, each employing different methods for the protection, transport, and hatching of their offspring.

## Arachnids and Insects
The book-scorpion (or false scorpion), a minute arachnid of the order Pseudoscorpiones and class Arachnida, is oviparous. These creatures, which are found in temperate and tropical regions, often live under stones, in vegetable detritus, or beneath tree bark; some species also inhabit human dwellings, such as furniture and books. After the eggs are laid, the mother carries them attached to the lower surface of her body. The young remain with the parent until they have acquired their definite form and are capable of shifting for themselves.

Among insects, the reproductive processes vary significantly. In the genus *Apis*, worker bees may lay unfertilized eggs that result in males (drones). The queen bee lays fertilized eggs in cells intended for workers or queens, and unfertilized eggs in drone-cells. While some researchers have claimed that the food supplied to larvae and "sex-producing glands" in nurse-workers determine whether a fertilized egg becomes a queen, worker, or male, others support the view that parthenogenesis is a normal factor in their life cycle.

In parasitic relationships, such as that of the *Stelis* larva and the *Osmia leucomelana*, the female *Stelis* lays her eggs at the bottom of a food-mass earlier than the *Osmia* lays its eggs on the surface. Because the *Stelis* larva is older and larger, it eventually attacks, kills, and consumes the *Osmia* larva over a period of one to two days.

## Amphibians and Reptiles
The life history of amphibians, including newts, tritons, and frogs, demonstrates a continued dependence on water for reproduction. Their eggs must be developed in sunlit water. The resulting tadpoles possess branching external gills; as the creature develops legs and its tail is absorbed, it begins using lungs, and the gills vanish.

In contrast, reptiles possess eggs protected from evaporation by a tough egg case. This adaptation allows the young to breathe using lungs immediately upon hatching, removing the necessity for the reptile to spend any stage of its life cycle in water.

## Avian Reproduction and Mythology
In the natural world, birds utilize various methods of incubation. In France, professional hatchers known as *Couveurs* use hen-turkeys in dark rooms kept at constant temperatures. These birds are placed in boxes filled with vegetable materials like straw, heather, or cocoa-fibres and are induced to sit on dummy eggs made of plaster of Paris before fresh eggs are substituted. A single hen-turkey can cover approximately two dozen eggs.

Historical and mythological accounts also describe unique avian behaviors. The phoenix, a sacred bird described by the people of Heliopolis, is said to appear every five hundred years. According to these accounts, the phoenix is similar in size and outline to an eagle, with red and gold feathers. It is said to convey its father to the temple of the Sun (Helios) in Egypt by forming an egg of myrrh. The bird hollows out this egg, places its father inside, and plasters it over with more myrrh so that the weight remains the same as the original egg.

## Artificial Incubation
Human efforts to hatch eggs without the presence of a hen have been recorded in various cultures. In Egypt, an artificial fecundity was developed using ovens heated to a temperature proportional to the natural warmth of a hen. This process typically occurs from the end of December to the end of April, as the heat in other months is too violent. Upwards of three hundred thousand eggs are placed in these ovens, with about ten days spent heating the ovens and nearly ten days for hatching. While some ancient accounts from Pliny suggest the Egyptians used warm dung rather than ovens, modern travelers have confirmed the use of ovens.

Modern mechanical incubation, such as the Cornell incubator in New York, focuses on the regulation of ventilation to balance fresh air and moisture. This is managed by monitoring the rate of evaporation, measured by the size of the air-space within the egg-shell on the 1st, 5th, 10th, 14th, and 18th days. On the 18th day, lukewarm water is placed in moisture pans and the ventilating slide is opened wide.
