# Samarra and Beyond: Navigation and Local Craft

## Navigation of the Tigris
The Tigris river serves as a primary artery for transport, though its navigation is characterized by significant uncertainty and difficulty. Between Bagdad and Korna, the river flows through an absolutely flat landscape composed of alluvial soil, which makes the river prone to changing its channel. During flood seasons, the surrounding country can transform into a huge lake, making it extremely difficult for navigators to locate the channel. Conversely, during the dry seasons of autumn and winter, there is a persistent danger of grounding on shoals and flats that constantly shift.

These natural challenges are compounded by human activity. Inhabitants along the eastern bank of the river frequently dig new canals for irrigation. These canals reduce the volume of water in the river and further contribute to the shifting of its channel.

## Steam and Sailing Vessels
Steam communication exists between Bagdad and its port, Basra, which is located on the Shatt el-Arab. This route is serviced by two lines of steamers—one Turkish and one English. British steamers were first introduced to the Tigris in 1836 following an expedition by Colonel F. R. Chesney. While a British gunboat is stationed before the residency and British steamers are permitted to navigate the river, only two maintain a weekly connection with Basra, a frequency that is inadequate for the freight traffic between the two cities. The Turkish service utilizes more numerous vessels, but these are poorly handled, inadequately equipped, and small, leaving them as an option only for those unable to secure British steamers.

Above Bagdad, the use of steamers ceases. In this region, the river is navigated by sailing vessels of 30 tons or more, which travel to Samarra and beyond.

## The Kufa and Local Craft
In the immediate environment of Bagdad, the characteristic craft used for local service is the *kufa*. The *kufa* is a circular boat constructed of basket-work and covered with bitumen. These vessels are often large enough to transport a dozen men and five or six horses. The use of these boats dates back to the remotest antiquity throughout the region and they are frequently depicted on old Assyrian monuments. Another ancient form of river transport used in the region is the raft known as the *kellek*.

## Samarra and Regional Pilgrimage
Samarra is identified as one of the four great centres of pilgrimage for Shi'ite Moslems within the vilayet, alongside Nejef, Kerbela, and Kazemain (a suburb of Bagdad). These sites are visited annually by tens of thousands of pilgrims arriving from the surrounding regions as well as from India and Persia. Some of these pilgrims bring their deceased to be buried in the vicinity of the sacred tombs.

Historically, the region has dealt with endemic health issues. While cholera remains endemic in some parts of the vilayet, the bubonic plague—which was also endemic before 1875—was stamped out by energetic government measures. However, the plague has reappeared in recent years, apparently introduced by pilgrims from Persia or India.
