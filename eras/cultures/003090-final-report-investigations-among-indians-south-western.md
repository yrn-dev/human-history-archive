# Final Report of Investigations among the Indians of the South-western United States

The *Final Report of Investigations among the Indians of the South-western United States* is a two-volume scholarly work published between 1890 and 1892. It was authored by Adolph Francis Alphonse Bandelier, an American archaeologist born in Bern, Switzerland, on August 6, 1840.

## Authorship and Academic Context
Adolph Francis Alphonse Bandelier emigrated to the United States as a youth and, after 1880, dedicated his career to ethnological and archaeological research. His primary focus was on the Indians of the south-western United States, as well as Mexico and South America. Bandelier began his regional studies in New Mexico, Arizona, and Sonora, Mexico. Through this work, he became a leading authority on the history of the region and, alongside F. H. Cushing and subsequent researchers, a primary authority on its prehistoric civilization.

During his research in the south-western United States, Bandelier's work was associated with the Hemenway Archaeological Expedition. His findings were published in the *Papers* of the Archaeological Institute of America, American Series, where his various contributions—including the *Final Report*—constitute volumes i. through v.

## Related Works and Research
The *Final Report of Investigations among the Indians of the South-western United States* is part of a broader body of work by Bandelier regarding the Americas. Other related publications include:

*   *Contributions to the History of the South-western Portion of the United States carried on mainly in the years from 1880 to 1885* (1890).
*   *Historical Introduction to Studies among the Sedentary Indians of New Mexico, and Report on the Ruins of the Pueblo of Pecos* (1881).
*   *Report of an Archaeological Tour in Mexico in 1881* (1884).

Bandelier also produced three specific studies for the Harvard University Peabody Museum of American Archaeology and Ethnology between 1877 and 1879, focusing on the ancient Mexicans' social organization, mode of government, tenure and distribution of lands, and their art and mode of warfare. Later in his career, starting in 1892, Bandelier shifted his focus to Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador, working for the American Museum of Natural History and Henry Villard of New York. In this later period, he challenged various historical myths, specifically those regarding the Inca civilization of Peru.

## Regional Historical Context
The south-western region investigated by Bandelier has a history of exploration and indigenous settlement. In the 16th century, Spanish explorer Francisco Vasquez de Coronado led an expedition starting in February 1540 to find the "Seven cities of Cibola," which are now identified as the Zuni pueblos of New Mexico. This expedition included Spanish footmen, over 250 horsemen, and several hundred friendly Indians. 

During this period, exploring parties visited the Moki (or Hopi) country of Tusayan in north-eastern Arizona, and Garcia Lopez de Cardenas described the Grand Canyon of the Colorado. Coronado's forces also traveled along the Rio Grande and penetrated as far as central Kansas in search of "Quivira," an Eldorado mentioned by a plains-Indian slave.

Additionally, the region has been home to various tribes, such as the Kiowas. The Kiowas, the sole representatives of the Kiowan linguistic stock, formerly ranged across New Mexico, Colorado, and Indian Territory (Oklahoma) around the Canadian and Arkansas rivers. After raiding settlers in western Texas, they were placed on a reservation in Indian Territory in 1868, eventually settling in Oklahoma with a population of approximately 1,200.
