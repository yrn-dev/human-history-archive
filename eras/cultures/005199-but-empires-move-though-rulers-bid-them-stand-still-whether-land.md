# Empires and Imperial Administration

The history of global empires is characterized by the movement of frontiers, the establishment of administrative controls, and the varying methods used by ruling powers to maintain authority over subject populations.

## Roman Frontiers and Fortifications
The Roman Empire expanded its reach through strategic military engineering and the movement of its borders. Around 142, the successor to Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, advanced the frontier from the Tyne and Solway to a narrower isthmus between the Forth and Clyde, which measured 36 miles across. This area had been previously fortified by Agricola. 

Under the direction of General Lollius Urbicus, the Romans constructed a continuous rampart with a preceding ditch, a connecting road running from end to end, and approximately a dozen fair-sized forts. These forts were built either abutting the wall or positioned close behind it. Excavations conducted between 1891 and 1893 confirmed that the rampart was constructed using regularly laid sods. 

Among the excavated forts, Bar Hill covers three acres—nearly five times the size of Agricola's earlier fort on the same site—and featured turf ramparts, wooden barrack-rooms, and stone structures including a bath, storehouse, and headquarters. Castle Cary, covering nearly four acres, utilized massive, well-dressed masonry for its ramparts. In contrast, Rough Castle near Falkirk was significantly smaller but noted for its earthen and turf-built ramparts, ravelins, and defensive pits similar to the *lilia* used by Caesar at Alesia.

## Greek and Spartan Imperialism
In ancient Greece, the nature of power shifted from the loose, unsettled society of the heroic age—where political power was concentrated in hereditary kings—to more structured imperial systems. 

The Spartan empire, particularly following the battle of Ægospotami, was characterized by the use of standing instruments of control known as decemvirs and harmosts (some of whom were Helots). These individual rulers were often viewed as more oppressive than the empire itself. For example, cities under Spartan rule suffered under native decemvirs like Kritias and Spartan harmosts such as Aristodemus or Aristarchus.

This differed from the Athenian empire, which did not appoint resident Athenian harmosts, garrisons, or native rulers like the Thirty. While subject cities of Athens bore definite burdens enforced by their own governments, they had access to the popular Athenian Dikastery. This court served as a harbor of refuge where subject cities could seek redress against misconduct from other cities or individual Athenian wrong-doers.

## Asian and Eastern European Empires
The Mongol Empire, established by Chinghiz, once extended from the Gulf of Scanderoon and the borders of Poland to the Yellow Sea and the Amur. While the Great Kaan remained the nominally supreme head, the empire eventually split into several monarchies led by the descendants of Chinghiz's four sons: Tuli, Okkodai, Chaghatai, and Juji.

Hulaku, the third son of Tuli, became the practically independent ruler of Armenia, Mesopotamia, Babylonia, and Persia, though he continued to use Chinese seals of state and stamp the Great Kaan's name on his coins. Other regional powers included the Seljukian Sultans of Iconium (whose dominion was titled Rúm), who became bondsmen of the Ilkhans, and Barka, son of Juji. Barka, the first prince of the House of Chinghiz to turn Mahomedan, reigned on the Volga steppes from the city of Sarai.

## Persian and Japanese Administration
The Persian Empire under King Darius was vast, encompassing 127 provinces. Its administration involved a hierarchy of princes, rulers of the Medes, and toparchs from regions as distant as Ethiopia and India.

In Japan, the extent of administrative control varied by era. While it is commonly said that the Kamakura Bakufu controlled the entire country, this is a modification of the truth; the whole sixty provinces did not pass under a single feudal ruler until the Tokugawa days in the seventeenth century. The Muromachi period, however, extended its theoretical influence over the entire empire, with the exception of the Chokodo estates, though its military supremacy was less complete than that of the Kamakura.
