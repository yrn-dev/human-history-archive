# The Truly Tropical Flora of Eastern India and Adjacent Regions

The truly tropical flora found in the wetter and hotter regions of eastern India is continuous with the vegetation of the islands and peninsula of Malaya. This floral distribution extends along the lower ranges of the Himalaya, though it becomes less marked and is restricted to lower elevations as it moves westward, a transition caused by increasing winter cold and diminishing rainfall.

## Distribution and Regional Variations
The vegetation of the Malayan Islands largely mirrors that of the hotter and wetter regions of India. However, due to more uniform humidity and temperature, certain tropical forms predominate in the Malayan area that are not as conspicuous in India. Additionally, the proximity of the Australian continent has allowed for the partial diffusion of Australian plant types into the Malayan Islands, though these are not seen in India. Examples of these include the casuarina, which is abundant on the mountains of eastern Java.

In the perennially humid regions of the Malayan peninsula and the western archipelago, dense forests prevail. These forests are characterized by the presence of thorny cane, a palm of the genus *Calamus*, which reaches its greatest development in this part of Asia. Similar forest flora extends through the mountains of eastern India to the Himalaya, ascending to elevations of 6,000 to 7,000 feet in the east and 3,000 to 4,000 feet in the west.

## Botanical Composition and Key Species
The chief trees of the humid Malayan and eastern Indian forests belong to several orders, including:
*   Dipterocarpaceae, Meliaceae, Sapindaceae, and Terebinthaceae
*   Clusiaceae, Ternstroemiaceae, and Leguminosae
*   Laurels, oaks, figs, Sapotaceae, and nutmegs
*   Dilleniaceae

Bamboos, palms, *Dracaena*, and *Pandanus* are also abundant. Certain Leguminosae—specifically *Acacia, Butea, Dalbergia, Bombax, Bauhinia, Skorea, Nauclea, Lagerstroemia*, and *Bignonia*—along with some palms and bamboos, are better equipped to resist the excessive drought and high temperatures of the northern Indian hot months (April to June). These species extend beyond the tropic, providing a tropical appearance to the forests at the extreme northern border of the Indian plain.

Other notable plants include the liquidambar, which is a conspicuous tree in Java, and rhododendrons, which occur in Sumatra and Borneo, descending to sea level. The genus *Nepenthes* is specially developed in the Malayan area, extending from Madagascar to New Caledonia; it is found in Ceylon and as far north as the Khasi hills, though it is absent from the peninsula of India and the Himalaya.

## Regional Comparisons and Connections
There is a distinct connection between the flora of Ceylon and the Indian peninsula and that of eastern tropical Africa. This is evidenced by the identity of genera and families, as well as a similarity in mountain flora, with several species believed to be identical to Abyssinian forms. A further link is the shared absence of cycads, conifers, and oaks in both the peninsula/Ceylon and eastern tropical Africa, despite the fact that conifers and oaks are abundant in the north and east at similar elevations.

In contrast, the Balsaminaceae are very abundant in the peninsula but rare in the eastern region. Orders such as Cyrtandraceae, Ebenaceae, Jasmineae, Dipterocarpaceae, and Aurantiaceae are more largely developed in south India than elsewhere, though few of these contain as many as 100 species peculiar to India.

## General Characteristics of South-Eastern Asia
The region of south-eastern Asia, encompassing the Malayan peninsula, the islands, and India (from the 65th to the 105th meridian), contains an estimated 12,000 to 15,000 indigenous plant species. The most numerically important orders are:
*   Leguminosae, Rubiaceae, and Orchidaceae
*   Compositae, Gramineae, and Euphorbiaceae
*   Acanthaceae, Cyperaceae, and Labiatae

While the region is a heterogeneous mix of alpine, temperate, and tropical plants, the tropical forms are dominant. The Indian flora is noted for providing a more general and complete illustration of the world's chief natural families than any other country. Orchidaceae are universally present in specially large proportions, whereas Compositae are comparatively rare.
