# The Stomochord and Anatomy of the Proboscis Region

In the anatomy of certain worm-like animals known as Balanoglossus, the region involving the proboscis, its neck, and the subsequent body segments contains specialized supporting and nervous structures. These animals are characterized by a tongue-shaped muscular proboscis used for burrowing through sand, which works in conjunction with a buccal segment, or collar, to effect progression.

## The Stomochord
The stomochord is a complex structure that originates as an outgrowth from the anterior dorsal wall of the collar-gut. It extends forward through the neck and into the basal (posterior) region of the proboscis, where it terminates blindly within the proboscis-coelom. While it is an integral part of the gut, the stomochord no longer assists in alimentation. Its lumen has become more or less vestigial, and its epithelium undergoes hypertrophy and vacuolar differentiation.

This organ has been metamorphosed into a resistant supporting structure. Due to its mode of origin from the gut-wall and its skeletal properties, the term "hemichorda" was introduced as a phyletic name for the class Enteropneusta. The stomochord is not a simple diverticulum; it possesses a ventral convexity, known as the ventral caecum, and paired lateral pouches. In species of *Spengelidae*, the stomochord features a long capillary vermiform extension in the front.

## The Nuchal Skeleton
Associated with the stomochord is the nuchal skeleton, which is a non-cellular laminated thickening of the basement-membrane. This skeleton underlies the portion of the stomochord located between the orifice into the throat and the lateral pouches. The body of the nuchal skeleton is situated within the narrow neck of the proboscis.

At the point where the stomochord opens into the buccal cavity, the nuchal skeleton bifurcates into two cornua. These cornua pass obliquely downwards and backwards, embedded in the wall of the throat. They often create projecting ridges that bound a dorsal groove of the collar-gut, which maintains continuity with the wall of the stomochord. These skeletal cornua serve a vital function by keeping the mouth permanently open and preventing it from collapsing.

## Nervous System and Epidermal Layers
The epidermis of these organisms contains a diffuse nervous layer with special thickenings in specific regions. In the neck of the proboscis, the fibrous layer is greatly thickened. Other intensifications of this layer are found along the ventral and dorsal middle lines of the trunk, extending to the posterior end of the body.

A circular commissural thickening follows the posterior rim of the collar, providing a connection between the ventral and dorsal nerve-tracts. The dorsal epidermal nerve-tract continues forward into the ventral wall of the collar nerve-tube. Additionally, numerous motor fibres extend from the ventral surface of the collar nerve-tube to the subjacent musculature, though these fibres are not aggregated into roots.

## Coelomic Divisions and Pore-Canals
The body cavity, or coelom, is divided into three distinct portions separated by septa:
1. The proboscis-coelom (first body-cavity).
2. The collar-coelom (second body-cavity).
3. The truncal coelom (third body-cavity).

The first two cavities communicate with the exterior via ciliated pore-canals located at the posterior end of their respective segments. The collar-pores are constant and are used to inhale water to make the collar turgid during movement. The proboscis-pores are more variable; they may be reduced to a single pore on the left side, unite into a common median orifice, be occluded, or remain vestigial. While the truncal coelom is generally not provided with pore-canals, vestiges have been described in the genus *Spengelia* near the anterior end of the truncal coelom.
