# The Sacred Books of the East and La Magie dans l'Inde antique

## Overview of the Sacred Books of the East
*The Sacred Books of the East* is a collection of religious and philosophical texts edited by F. Max Muller. The series serves as a resource for the study of Eastern traditions and has been utilized by various scholars of Oriental studies. One notable contributor to the series was the French author and antiquarian James Darmesteter (1849–1894). Darmesteter, who was a teacher of Zend at the Ecole des Hautes Etudes and a professor at the College de France, edited the *Zend Avesta* for *The Sacred Books of the East*.

## The Zend Avesta and Iranian Tradition
The *Zend Avesta* provides insights into the ancient inhabitants of Iran and their social and religious structures. According to the text, the ancient inhabitants were divided into a fourfold system consisting of priests, warriors, agriculturists, and artificers. Additionally, the text describes a sevenfold division that corresponds to the seven amschespands, who are the servants of Ormuzd. These divisions are presented not as inventions of Zoroaster, but as traditions originating from the golden age of Diemschid (or Jemshid).

The priestly caste, known as the Magi, was further subdivided into three levels:
*   **Herbeds**: The disciples.
*   **Mobeds**: The masters.
*   **Destur Mobeds**: The complete masters.

Only the Destur Mobeds were permitted to read the liturgies of Ormuzd. They held the exclusive authority to predict the future and were the only ones to carry specific sacred objects, including the *barsom* (a bunch of twigs), the *havan* (a cup), and the *costi* (a girdle).

## Connections Between Persian and Indian Traditions
There are noted connections between the religions of Persia and India. The ceremony of Homa in the Persian tradition corresponds to the Soma ceremony in the Hindu tradition. Furthermore, the Zend term *baresma* is thought to be linked to the "Brahma" or sacred element, symbolized by a bunch of kusa grass known as veda.

Scholar Haug, in his *Tract on the Origin of Brahmanism*, argued that the division of Ahura Mazda's followers into Vastrya, Rathaesvas, and Atharvas in the *Zend Avesta* is precisely equivalent to the three superior castes found in India. Haug also asserted that the rule requiring only the sons of priests (Atharvas) to become priests remains in effect among the Parsis. However, other sources, such as the Book of Daniel, suggest that the Magi may have been an elective body rather than a hereditary one.

## Scholarly Perspectives on the Zend Avesta
James Darmesteter provided significant philological and historical commentary on the *Zend Avesta*, including a complete three-volume translation published between 1892 and 1893 in the *Annales du musee Guimet*. Darmesteter challenged common beliefs regarding the age of the texts; he argued that the extant versions were more recent than generally assumed, placing the earliest texts in the 1st century B.C. and the majority of the material in the 3rd century A.D.
