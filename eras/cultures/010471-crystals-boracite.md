# Crystals of Boracite

Boracite is a mineral of particular scientific interest due to its optical anomalies. First observed in 1787, it is found in considerable numbers as small crystals with sharply defined faces, embedded in anhydrite and gypsum within the salt deposits of Luneburg in Hanover.

## Physical and Chemical Properties
Chemically, boracite is a chloride and magnesium borate with the formula $\text{Mg}_7\text{Cl}_2\text{B}_{16}\text{O}_{30}$. It is insoluble in water, though it is soluble in hydrochloric acid. The mineral's hardness is 7 on the Mohs scale, and it has a specific gravity of 3.0. 

The crystals range from transparent to translucent and possess a vitreous lustre. While they are typically white or colourless, they are frequently tinged with green, yellow, or grey. Boracite is subject to slow alteration upon exposure because the magnesium chloride absorbs water; this altered form of the mineral is known as parasite. Additionally, a variety known as stassfurtite occurs as nodules in the salt deposits at Stassfurt in Prussia. This massive variety appears as compact, white or greenish material resembling fine-grained marble when found in the carnallite layer, but is yellowish or reddish, soft, and earthy when found in the kainite layer.

Some specimens contain a small amount of iron. When half of the magnesium is replaced by ferrous iron, the resulting iron-boracite is called huyssenite.

## Crystallography and Habit
In external form, boracite crystals are cubic with inclined hemihedrism, sharing the same symmetry as tetrahedrite and blende. The habit of the crystals varies depending on whether the cube, the tetrahedron, or the rhombic dodecahedron predominates. In some instances, penetration twins are observed with a tetrahedron face serving as the twin-plane.

As noted by R.J. Hauy in 1791, these crystals are markedly pyroelectric. When a cube of boracite is heated, four of its corners become negatively electrified while the four opposite corners become positively electrified. In crystals where the rhombic dodecahedron predominates, the larger, bright tetrahedral faces are located at the antilogous poles, and the smaller, dull tetrahedral faces are at the analogous poles; the latter become positively electrified upon heating.

## Optical Anomalies and Internal Structure
While boracite appears to follow cubic symmetry, examination under polarized light reveals that it is doubly refracting, a phenomenon first observed by Sir David Brewster in 1821. Thin sections of the mineral show a division into definite areas that are optically biaxial and the presence of twin-lamellae. 

By cutting sections in specific directions, it can be demonstrated that a rhombic dodecahedral crystal is actually composed of twelve orthorhombic pyramids. The bases of these pyramids coincide with the dodecahedral faces of the pseudo-cubic crystal, and their apices meet at the center.

These optical characters change when the crystals are heated. At a temperature of 265 degrees, the crystals suddenly become optically isotropic. Upon cooling, the complex internal structure reappears.

## Theoretical Explanations
Various theories have been proposed to explain these anomalies. Some observers suggest they are the result of internal strains or alteration. Another theory posits that the crystals originally grew as truly cubic at temperatures exceeding 265 degrees. 

Current evidence suggests there are two distinct crystalline modifications of the boracite substance: an orthorhombic (or monoclinic) modification that is stable at lower temperatures, and a cubic modification that is stable above 265 degrees. This is considered strictly analogous to silver iodide (iodyrite), which has rhombohedral and cubic modifications at different temperatures. However, while silver iodide is found in nature in both rhombohedral and pseudo-cubic forms, boracite has only been encountered as pseudo-cubic crystals.
