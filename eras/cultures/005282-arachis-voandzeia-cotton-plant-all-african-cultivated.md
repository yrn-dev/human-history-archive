# African Cultivated Plants and Ground-Nuts

The agricultural landscape of various African regions is characterized by a diverse array of cultivated plants, ranging from commercial fiber and oil crops to a variety of cereals. These products are grown across different terrains, including the savannahs, park-like divisions, and coastal regions.

## African Cultivated Cereals and Grains
In British Central Africa, the region is largely composed of savannah or park-like divisions, with the landscape being well-watered and covered in vegetation. The principal cultivated cereals found in this area include:
*   _Sorghum_
*   _Pennisetum_
*   _Eleusine_
*   Maize
*   Rice
*   Wheat (which is cultivated primarily by Europeans)

Similarly, in the coast regions and by the shores of Victoria Nyanza, rice and maize, along with other grains, are raised in large quantities. In these coastal areas, cultivation is mainly conducted by Indian immigrants or natives, though European-owned plantations are also numerous.

## Ground-Nuts (_Arachis_ and _Voandzeia_)
Among the vegetable products of commercial interest in British Central Africa are ground-nuts, specifically those of the genera _Arachis_ and _Voandzeia_. 

### Botany of _Arachis hypogaea_
The ground-nut, also known as the earth-nut, monkey-nut, pea-nut, or Manilla nut, is the fruit or pod of _Arachis hypogaea_ (of the natural order Leguminosae). It is an annual plant with a diffuse habit, featuring a hairy stem and two-paired, abruptly pinnate leaflets. 

The plant is distinguished by its unique fruiting process. After the flower withers, the stalk of the ovary elongates and bends downward, forcing the young pod underground. Consequently, the seeds mature below the surface. The resulting pods are stalked, cylindrical, and oblong, measuring approximately one inch in length. The thin reticulated shell contains one or two irregularly ovoid seeds. While it is considered a legume, it is noted that the fruit of _Arachis_ does not dehisce.

### Cultivation and Use
Originally native to South America, _Arachis hypogaea_ is now extensively cultivated in subtropical and tropical countries, preferring light sandy soil. It is a prolific plant, capable of yielding between 30 and 38 bushels of nuts per acre. 

The seeds are utilized in several ways:
*   **Food:** When fresh, they are eaten in tropical countries and are compared in taste to almonds. When roasted, they serve as a substitute for chocolate. In America, they are consumed widely as "pea-nuts," while in England, they are known as "monkey-nuts," primarily among poorer children.
*   **Oil:** The seeds yield a large quantity of oil used for medicinal purposes, as a curry or fish oil, and for lamps by natives.
*   **Fodder:** The leaves are very similar to clover and serve as excellent cattle feed.

## Cotton and Other Commercial Crops
The cotton plant is a significant cultivated crop in several African regions. In the coast region and near Victoria Nyanza, cotton is cultivated alongside tobacco. In other areas, the industry extends beyond cultivation to include cotton ginning.

Other notable cultivated plants and vegetable products in these regions include:
*   **Tobacco:** Grown in both native and introduced varieties.
*   **Coffee:** While wild coffee is reported in some mountainous districts, the coffee cultivated by European settlers is introduced from abroad.
*   **Rubber:** Derived from apocynaceous plants and various species including _Ficus_, _Landolphia_, _Carpodinus_, _Clitandra_, and _Conopharygia_.
*   **Palms:** Six species of palms are present, including the oil palm. In coastal regions, coco-nut palm plantations produce high-quality copra.
*   **Other Products:** The _Strophanthus_ pod is harvested for a valuable drug, and the bark of mangrove trees is exported for tanning. In certain inland districts, the beans of the castor oil plant are a lucrative trade item. Sugar-cane is also cultivated by natives in various places.

## Regional Agricultural Variations
In the higher regions of some territories, European settlers focus on vegetables, producing very large crops of potatoes. They also grow barley, oats, wheat, and coffee. To improve fodder for livestock—including Merino sheep introduced from Australia—settlers have introduced ryegrass, lucerne, and clover.

The soil varies by region, consisting of heavy black loam in eastern plains and light reddish loam over much of the rest of the country, which is generally easy to cultivate.
