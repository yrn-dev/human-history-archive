# Turki Tribes of Western Mongolia

## Regional Characteristics and Lifestyle
The population of Mongolia is essentially nomadic, with wealth based on herds of camels, horses, sheep, and horned cattle. In western Mongolia, the region is occupied by Turki tribes described as being among the least civilized of human beings, characterized by extreme cruelty and barbarity. This environment is marked by a severe climate with great extremes of cold and heat. Rainfall is rare and occurs in small quantities, resulting in a landscape that is primarily a hard stony desert, with occasional areas of blown sand.

Settled villages and towns are scarce in this region. They are found almost exclusively along the slopes of higher mountains where melting snow provides streams for irrigation or where rain falls more abundantly. These specific locations are the only areas where cultivated lands exist; beyond these zones, trees are hardly seen.

## Ethnic and Linguistic Affiliations
The Turki peoples are part of a broader group of eastern steppes and deserts inhabitants—including the Hunnish, Mongolian, and Tartar peoples—who are considered akin in race, language, and way of life. The Turks are Mahommedans and utilize letters derived from Persia. Their tribes extend to the Caspian, up the Oxus to the borders of Persia and Afghanistan, and into Russia under the name of Kirghiz. Their language is spoken across a large portion of western Asia.

Historically, the predatory habits of the Turkish, Mongolian, and Manchu populations of northern Asia led to irruptions into Europe and other parts of the continent. In the context of the Mongolian family, the Chinese branch differs significantly, as they are a settled people of traders and agriculturists with a distinct monosyllabic, uninflected language.

## Notable Tribes and Historical Connections
One significant group is the Ḳungurat tribe, whose name is derived from the Turki *Kongur-At*, meaning "Chestnut Horse." The seat of the Ḳungurats was located near the Great Wall. This tribe was highly influential within the house of Chinghiz, providing more wives to its princes than any other tribe. Notable members included Burteh Fujin, the mother of Chinghiz's four heirs and his favorite wife. Other princes who took wives from the Ḳungurats included Kúblái (including Jamúi Khátún), Chagatai, Hulaku, Mangku Kaan, Abaka, Ahmed Tigudar, Arghun, and Ghazan. The name Ḳungurat is also applied to a tribe of the Uzbeks of Western Turkestan and a town of Khiva near the Sea of Aral.

Additionally, historical records mention the "Nine Tatars" (*Tokuz Tatar*) and "Thirty Tatars" (*Otuz Tatar*) in a Turkish inscription from A.D. 732 found on the river Orkhon. These tribes likely lived between the Uigúr or Oguz Turks to the west and the Kitan to the east.

## Political and Geographic Context
In north-west Mongolia, the district of Kobdo occupies the north-western corner of the region. It is peopled primarily by Mongols, as well as Khotons, Uryankhes, Soyotes, and Kirghiz. The town of Kobdo, founded by the Chinese in 1731 and pillaged by Mussulmans in 1872, serves as an important trade center between Peking and cattle-breeding nomads. The district is governed by a Chinese commissioner and a special Mongol functionary known as a *dzurgan*.

Further south and west, Turki influence extends to the principalities of Khotan and Yarkand, as well as the state of Khokand, through which the Jaxartes and Oxus rivers flow.

## Linguistic Influence and Misconceptions
The Turki language has influenced terminology in other regions. For example, the Kolis, a tribe or caste in Western India, may have derived their name from the Turki word *kuleh*, meaning "a slave," which some theories suggest evolved into the word "cooly" for agricultural laborers.

In historical accounts of Temujin (Chinghiz Kaan), a common misconception arose regarding his profession. Some sources, including Rubruquis, referred to him as a blacksmith. This error is attributed to the resemblance between the name *Temújin* and the Turki word for blacksmith, *Temúrjí*.
