# Douglas Fir and Western White Oak

The Douglas fir (*Pseudotsuga Douglasii*) and the western white oak (*Quercus garryana*) are among the significant tree species found in the forest growth of certain Canadian provinces. These regions possess a rich variety of timber, including other species such as the giant cedar (*Thuya gigantea*), western larch, white spruce, and red alder.

## Douglas Fir (Pseudotsuga Douglasii)
The Douglas fir serves as the principal timber of commerce within its province. It is a tree of substantial size, frequently reaching heights of 300 feet and diameters ranging from 8 to 10 feet. Due to the tough and strong nature of its wood, it is highly valued for use in building purposes and for the construction of ships' spars.

## Western White Oak (Quercus garryana)
The western white oak is listed as one of the more important trees of the province's forest growth. While it shares the region with the Douglas fir and the giant cedar—the latter of which rivals the Douglas fir in girth and is used for interior work and shingles—the western white oak is part of a diverse botanical landscape that includes the large leaved maple and various spruce and pine species.

## Regional Timber Industry
The demand for lumber from these forests is steady, extending beyond Canada to include markets in China, Australia, Africa, and South America. The industry is supported by approximately eighty sawmills of varying sizes. 

Government records indicate significant timber harvesting activity in the early 20th century. In 1904, the total amount of timber cut was 348,031,790 feet, consisting of 325,271,568 feet from provincial lands and 22,760,222 feet from Dominion government lands. By 1905, the volume of timber cut on provincial lands increased to 450,385,554 feet, and the cut on dominion lands also exceeded the totals seen in 1904.

## Other Oak Species and Uses
Beyond the western white oak, other species of the *Quercus* genus are noted for various economic and biological properties:
*   **Cork Oak (*Quercus Suber*):** Found in North Africa and southern Europe (principally Spain and Portugal), this evergreen oak reaches about 30 feet. Its outer bark is harvested every eight to ten years to produce cork, a material valued for its elastic and compressible properties.
*   **Quercitron Bark (*Quercus tinctoria*):** An oak tree from the North American States used to produce a yellow dyestuff from its inner bark. The coloring principle is known as quercitrin, which decomposes into quercetin.
*   **Other Varieties:** *Quercus Ilex* (evergreen oak) is found in southern Europe and as far east as the Sutlej. *Quercus coccifera* is a small shrub in the Mediterranean basin that serves as a food source for the scale-insect *Kermes ilicis*, used to create a crimson dye.

## Forest Giants in Tradition
Historical and fabulous accounts from other regions also describe "forest giants" of the oak variety. In the *Tsukushi Fudoki*, an oak in Chikugo is described as towering 9,700 feet high, with branches that shaded the peaks of Hizen and Higo. Another account from the *Konjaku Monogatari* describes an oak with a stem circumference of 3,000 feet.
