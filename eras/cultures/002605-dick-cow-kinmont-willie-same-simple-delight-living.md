# Dick of the Cow and Kinmont Willie

## Overview of Border Ballads
The songs of Dick of the Cow and Kinmont Willie are categorized as Border songs. These ballads are noted for possessing a "simple delight of living," a tone that is identified as being similar to the ballads of Klephtic exploits found in Greece, as well as the short Greek *Scolia* and their counterparts in France.

## Historical Accuracy and Composition
There is scholarly debate regarding the timing and accuracy of early popular poetry. Gaston Paris, an authority on the subject, suggested that such poetry was improvised and contemporary with the facts it described. However, when this view is applied to ballads such as "Kinmont Willie," "Dick of the Cow," "Jamie Telfer," "Jock o' the Side," and "The Bonny Earl o' Murray," it appears that the poets often possessed little knowledge of the events, or the knowledge they had was incorrect.

Evidence from contemporary despatches and letters indicates that the facts in these ballads are often distorted or confused. This suggests several possibilities regarding their origin:
* They may have been composed in a later generation based on oral traditions that were erroneous.
* They may have been defaced by the interpolations of reciters, as seen in the case of *The Queen's Marie*.
* Reciters may have been inspired by printed broadsheets or "stall copies."

A comparison between historical Border ballads—specifically those from 1595 to 1600—and Bain's *Border Papers* (1894-1896) supports the conclusion that these works are not strictly factual. Furthermore, ballads concerning the sons of Rob Roy remained mythopoeic as late as 1750. It is probable that the existing form of most Border ballads dates no earlier than the generation between 1603 and 1633, following the union of the crowns.
