# The Bronze Age and Peruvian Metallurgy

## Definition and Nature of the Bronze Age
The Bronze Age is the term used by archaeologists to describe a stage of human culture situated between the Stone Age and the Iron Age. It is characterized as a period when implements, utensils, and weapons were generally manufactured from bronze. Rather than having an absolute chronological value, the term marks a stage of civilization that most races are believed to have passed through.

The progression through these stages was not uniform across the globe. In many countries, the Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages overlapped. Evidence for this overlap is found in tumuli and sepulchral mounds, where implements from different ages have been discovered together. The duration and prevalence of the Bronze Age in any specific region were determined by the accessibility of the metals required to create the alloy. Consequently, in some lands, bronze remained a substance of extreme value until the Iron Age was reached.

## The Bronze Age in Europe and Asia
In Europe, the Bronze Age is believed by most authorities to have commenced between 2000 B.C. and 1800 B.C., though it may have started earlier in some countries. This period is characterized by specific designs of swords, daggers, knives, hammers, arrow-heads, gouges, and awls. The patterns of these arms are distinctly oriental, suggesting a theory that the culture originated in the East; this is further supported by the fact that handles for daggers and swords were often too narrow and short for the large-handed races of Europe.

Other characteristics of the European Bronze Age include:
* **Funerary Practices:** Cremation became the standard mode of disposing of the dead, replacing the burial practices of the Stone Age. Barrows and sepulchral mounds from this era were generally smaller and less imposing than those of the Stone Age.
* **Artifacts:** Excavations have revealed high-workmanship weapons, gold coronets, diadems, amulets, and elegant vases made of bronze and gold.
* **Art and Pottery:** Ornamentation typically consisted of concentric circles, bosses, and spirals. Some swords featured handles in the shape of a cross or tracery imitating the Svastika. Pottery was handmade, primarily consisting of cinerary urns between 12 and 18 inches in height.

In Asia Minor, the smelting of iron began even earlier than the transition that occurred in Europe approximately 3,000 years ago. In lower Mesopotamia, the Sumerians—a brownish people with prominent noses—discovered the use of bronze and built tower-like temples of sun-dried brick.

## Metallurgy in the Americas and Peru
The development of civilization in the Americas was largely a separate process. While American peoples achieved a high level of skill in dyeing, weaving, pottery, and stonework, their metallurgical path differed from that of the Old World.

Peruvians and Mexicans are noted for having remained in their Bronze Age in recent times. American metallurgy may have arisen independently or been brought by canoe-borne drifts of heliolithic culture. These peoples utilized copper, bronze, gold, and silver, but they did not progress to the use of iron.

## Peruvian Record-Keeping and Intellectual Development
In Peru, the development of writing did not follow the path of Egyptian hieroglyphics or the calendar-based Maya script of Yucatan. Instead, the beginnings of writing were superseded by a complex system of record-keeping using *quipus*. These were bundles of strings of various shapes and colors with knots tied in them. This code was used to convey orders and laws, although the art of reading *quipus* has since been lost. Additionally, Peruvians utilized counting-frames and created maps. Despite these achievements, there was no method for fixing or summarizing intellectual possessions to hand down knowledge and experience from one generation to another, which serves as the basis for science and literature.
