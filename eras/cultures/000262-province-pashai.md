# Province of Pashai

The Province of Pashai is described in the records of Marco Polo as a region located ten days' journey to the south of Badashan. It is characterized by a hot climate and is inhabited by a people of brown complexion who possess a peculiar language.

## Culture and Society
The inhabitants of Pashai are described as idolaters and are noted for being "pestilent" and "crafty." They are said to be great adepts in the diabolic arts and sorceries. The diet of the population consists of rice and flesh. 

The men of the province are noted for their adornments, wearing brooches and earrings made of silver and gold, which are set with pearls and stones.

## Geography and Etymology
The exact location and extent of the province have been a subject of analysis. While Polo places it south of Badashan, some interpretations suggest the term "Pashai" may have been used vaguely to cover the entire tract from the southern boundary of Badashan to the Kabul River and the Indus. It is noted that a journey of ten days from the old capital of Badakhshan could feasibly reach the town of Chitral, which some suggest represents Udyána.

Regarding the name, Sir H. Rawlinson indicated that "Pashai" might originally be connected to *Paresín* or *Aprasin*, the Zendavestian name for the Indian Caucasus. In the Babylonian version of the Behistun Inscription, this is equivalent to *Gadára* in Persian (or *Gandhára*), referring to the entire region between the Indus and Bactria.

## Historical and Tribal Context
The Pashai are reckoned among the aboriginal inhabitants of the country, distinguishing them from the Afghans. While Polo describes them as idolaters, later records indicate the Pashai tribe became Mahomedan. Their language is noted by Baber as one of a dozen spoken at Kabul during his time, and it is described as resembling the language of the Kafirs. Linguistic analysis suggests the language is Aryan and belongs to the "Indic" class, though it is not very close to the Siah-posh Kafir dialect.

The Pashais have been mentioned in various historical accounts:
* **Ibn Batuta:** Reached the Mountain Bashái (Pashai) after crossing the Hindu-Kúsh via a pass at the head of the Panjshir Valley.
* **Sidi ’Ali:** Mentioned the Pashais in the same vicinity in 1554.
* **Tribal Distribution:** The tribe is most numerous in the neighborhood of Panjshir, with other settlements on the left bank of the Kabul River between Jalalabad and Kabul, and in the hill-country around Nijrao. 

Additionally, *Pasha* and *Pasha-gar* are named as chief divisions of the Kafirs, which may represent Pashais who escaped or resisted conversion to Islam.

## Military History
The region is mentioned in the context of an incursion by a leader named Nogodar. Nogodar, a relative of Chagatai, led 10,000 horsemen through Badashan and a province called Pashai-Dir (and subsequently Ariora-Keshemur) on his way to India. The roads in these provinces were described as narrow and perilous, resulting in the loss of many horses and men. This route is characterized as being of a rugged and difficult nature.
