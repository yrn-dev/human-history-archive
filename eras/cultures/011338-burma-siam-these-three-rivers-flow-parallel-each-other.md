# Burma and Siam

Burma and Siam are regions of the Indo-Chinese peninsula characterized by high mountain ranges and great river systems. Burma, a province of British India, includes the former independent kingdom of Burma and British Burma, the latter of which was acquired through wars in 1826 and 1852. The province is divided into Lower and Upper Burma, with the latter annexed on January 1, 1886.

## Geography and Boundaries
Burma is situated east of the Bay of Bengal, extending from the Pakchan river at 9° 55' north latitude to the Naga and Kachin hills between the 27th and 28th degrees of north latitude. Its longitudinal span is roughly between the 92nd and 100th degrees of east longitude. The province reaches a maximum north-to-south length of almost 1,200 m and a maximum width of 575 m at approximately 21° north latitude.

The province's total area is estimated at 238,738 sq. m, consisting of Burma proper (168,573 sq. m), the Chin hills (10,250 sq. m), and the Shan States (59,915 sq. m). Burma is bounded to the north by the Mishmi hills, the dependent state of Manipur, and Chinese territory. To the south lie the Bay of Bengal and the Siamese Malay States. To the west are Chittagong and the Bay of Bengal. The eastern boundaries involve the Chinese Shan States, portions of Yunnan province, the French province of Indo-China, and Siam and the Siamese Shan (Lao) States.

The boundary between Burma and Siam is defined by the eastern watershed of the Salween basin, which separates the Lower Burma provinces from Siam.

## Hydrography and River Systems
The Indo-Chinese peninsula is defined by several great rivers: the Mekong, the Menam, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy. The valleys of these rivers form the primary portions of Burma, Siam (including Laos), and Cochin-China (including Cambodia and Tongking).

### The Irrawaddy and its Tributaries
The Irrawaddy is the most important river in Burma. It likely originates in unexplored regions where China, Tibet, and India meet. Its volume is primarily contributed by the N'mai and Mali rivers, which meet at latitude 25° 45'. The Irrawaddy is navigable for steamers throughout the year up to Bhamo, 900 m from the sea.

The Chindwin, the Irrawaddy's chief tributary in Burma, is navigable for 300 m from its junction at Pakôkku. The Chindwin rises south-west of Thama as the Tanai, flows north into the Hukawng valley, and then turns south. Another tributary, the Mogaung river, flows for 180 m before joining the Irrawaddy and is navigable for four months of the year as far as Kamaing. In the delta region, the Irrawaddy splits into several mouths, including the Pantanaw, Pyinzalu, Panmawaddy, Pyamalaw, and Ngawun.

### The Mekong and Salween
The Mekong river serves as a boundary between British interests in Burma and French interests in Tongking. Specifically, from approximately latitude 21° 45' southwards for some 200 m, the Mekong defines the limit of occupation between French and British subjects, separating Tongking from the Shan States.

The Salween and Mekong, along with the Yang-tsze-kiang, flow parallel to each other for approximately 300 m through narrow, precipitous troughs before the Yang-tsze turns eastwards north of the 25th parallel.

## Mountain Ranges and Topography
Burma is encircled on three sides by mountains. The Arakan Yomas extend northwards from Cape Negrais to join the Naga and Chin hills. The highest peak of the Arakan Yomas is Liklang, reaching nearly 10,000 ft. In the east, the Karen, Shan, and Kachin hills form a continuous barrier extending into China. The Kachin hills contain two peaks, Sabu and Worang, which rise to 11,200 ft.

Other notable elevations include:
* **Loi Ling** in the Northern Shan States: 9,000 ft.
* **Shwedaunggyi** in the Kumon range: 5,750 ft.
* **Popa**: An extinct volcano in the Myingyan district rising nearly 5,000 ft.

The Pegu Yomas are described as mere hills compared to the larger ranges. In the Minbu area, mud volcanoes occur, which are craters formed by the accretion of oily mud and gas discharges.

## The Shan States and Keng Tung
The Shan States comprise the eastern portion of the province. Keng Tung is the most extensive of these states, covering over 12,000 sq. m. It is bounded to the east by the Mekong river (bordering French Lao territory) and to the west generally by the Salween river. The state's terrain is predominantly mountainous, with peaks exceeding 7,000 ft and elevations rarely falling below 5,000 ft. 

Keng Tung's drainage is split between the Mekong basin (63%) and the Salween drainage area (37%). Key tributaries of the Salween in this region include the Nam Hsim and Nam Hka, while the chief tributary of the Mekong is the Nam Lwe.
