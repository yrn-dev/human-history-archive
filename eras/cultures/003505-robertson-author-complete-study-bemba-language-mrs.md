# Bemba Language and Related Bantu Dialects

The Bemba language, also referred to as Emba, is a Bantu language of typical structure and archaic form. It is categorized as an interesting genus of speech spoken in a region that encompasses the ground between Lake Mweru, the Upper Chambezi river, and the south and south-west coasts of Tanganyika.

## Linguistic Classification and Domain
The domain of Ki-bemba is extensive, including locally-modified dialects such as Ki-mambwe and Ki-lungu, which are found in South and South-East Tanganyika. Within the broader study of Bantu languages, the dialects of the Bemba country to the south-west of Tanganyika are considered among the most archaic dialects existing today.

## Related Bantu Groups
The Bemba dialects are closely allied with other linguistic groups in the region. To the north and north-west, the North Nyasa or Nkonde group comprises dialects of the north and north-west coasts of Lake Nyasa, including Iki-nyikiusa and Ici-wandia. This group also includes Ishi-nyi[chi]a of the Nyasa-Tanganyika plateau and may extend as far as the shores of Lake Rukwa (Ici-wungu) and the Fipa country (Iki-fipa). While Iki-fipa has affinities with western Victoria-Nyanza and Tanganyika languages, it remains part of this general geographic linguistic spread.

Other distinct groups in the vicinity include:
* **The Tumbuka section:** Occupying the area between the Upper Luangwa river and the western shores of Lake Nyasa, this section includes the languages of the A-tonga, Henga, and Tumbuka peoples.
* **The Kinga speech:** Located in the lofty Livingstone mountains, this language is sufficiently distinct from its neighbors to be classified as a separate group.
* **The Sango-Bena-Hehe-Sutu group:** This group extends south of the Unyamwezi domain, east of the Livingstone mountains, and west of the Ruaha river.

## Comparative Bantu Studies
The study of Bantu languages has been documented by various authorities. Father J. Torrend produced a *Comparative Grammar of the South African Bantu Languages* in 1894, which focused primarily on the languages of the Central Zambezi. Other significant contributions to the field include the works of Carl Meinhof, who collected vast amounts of information on existing languages in South-East and East Africa and the original structure of the Bantu mother-speech in his 1906 work, *Grundzuege einer vergleichenden Grammatik der Bantusprachen*.

Additionally, illustrative vocabularies have been provided in the works of Sir H. H. Johnston, specifically in *The Kilimanjaro Expedition* (1884), *British Central Africa* (1898), and *The Uganda Protectorate* (1902-1904). The classification of the Fernandian, Cameroon, and Congo groups of Bantu speech was carefully detailed in *George Grenfell and the Congo* (1908).

## Archaic Features of Bantu Speech
Beyond the Bemba country, other archaic Bantu dialects are found in Uganda, Unyoro, Ruwenzori, Mount Elgon, and the north coast of Tanganyika. Other primitive or archaic types include:
* **Subiya speech:** Found in the western Zambezi.
* **Nkonde and Kese dialects:** Located on the north-east and north coasts of Lake Nyasa.
* **Ci-bodzo:** An archaic type found in the Zambezi delta.
* **Oci-herero:** Spoken in Damaraland, this language contains typical features and many original Bantu roots, despite some strange divergencies.
* **Kimakonde:** A primitive Bantu tongue located on the east coast of Africa.
* **Ki-swahili:** The celebrated language of Zanzibar, which is primitive in its roots but not in its prefixes.

Historically, the Zulu-Kaffir language was regarded as the most archaic type of Bantu speech due to its preservation of certain characteristics of the hypothetical mother-tongue. However, it has since been deposed from this position following the discovery of the languages of the Great Lakes.
