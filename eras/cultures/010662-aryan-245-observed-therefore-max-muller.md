# Aryan

The term "Aryan" has been utilized by various philologists in a diverse and sometimes confusing array of meanings. In the context of comparative philology, the term is most widely recognized through the work of Max Muller, who employed it as a convenient short term to describe the entire body of languages more commonly referred to as Indo-Germanic or Indo-European.

## Linguistic and Ethnological Definitions
Max Muller specifically used the term "Aryas" to refer to the speakers of Aryan languages. In his 1888 publication, *Biographies of Words and the Home of the Aryas*, he defined Aryas as those who speak these languages regardless of their blood or color. According to Muller, calling a person an Arya predicates nothing about them other than the fact that the grammar of their language is Aryan.

Muller was careful to avoid any ethnological or anatomical signification in his use of the term. He asserted that "Aryas" refers neither to bones, hair, skull, nor blood. This definition applies equally to Romans, Germans, Greeks, Hindus, Celts, and Slaves. He noted that even the "blackest Hindus" represent an earlier stage of Aryan speech and thought than the "fairest Scandinavians." Muller argued that the blue-eyed and fair-haired Scandinavians may have adopted the language of their subjects or darker lords, or vice versa, but that the label "Aryan" remains strictly a linguistic designation. He characterized any ethnologist who attributed "Aryan race," "Aryan blood," or specific physical traits like eyes and hair to the term as a "sinner," comparing such a mistake to a linguist speaking of a "brachycephalic grammar."

## Etymology and Usage
The word has historical roots as a national name used not only in India but also in Persia and Bactria. In Sanskrit, it appears as *arya-* and *arya*; in Old Persian as *ariya-*; and in Zend as *airya-*. Within Sanskrit, a distinction is likely necessary between *arya* (the origin of "Aryan") and *arya*, which is frequently used in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet for deities. Additionally, *aryas* may be the genitive of *ari*, meaning "pious, devoted, active."

There are several scholarly debates regarding the word's origin:
* **Connection to Earth:** Max Muller asserted a connection to the Sanskrit word for earth, *ira*, though this is considered far from certain.
* **Root Origins:** Spiegel noted that while the word can be connected to a root *ar-*, there are multiple roots of that form with different meanings, leaving no certain criterion for a decision.
* **External Connections:** A proposed connection to *Erin* (Ireland) by Pictet has been deemed impossible. However, the word may share an origin with *Ario-*, found in Celtic and Germanic names such as *Ariovistus*. The origin of the name Armenia (Old Persian *Armina-*) remains uncertain.

## Classification and Related Languages
In linguistic classification, certain languages are identified as Aryan. For example, the languages of the Siah-posh Kafir and the Pashai tribe (the latter of which are now Mahomedan and considered aboriginal inhabitants of their country) are both classified as Aryan. Specifically, they are categorized under Professor Max Müller’s "Indic" class, although they are not very close to one another.

Philologists distinguish the Aryan group of languages from the Semitic group. Semitic languages—including Arabic, Hebrew, ancient Phoenician, ancient Assyrian, and Abyssinian—possess different root words, different ways of expressing relationship, and different fundamental grammatical ideas. Evidence suggests that Aryan-speaking and Semitic-speaking peoples were separated for thousands of years during early Neolithic times, living in "different worlds" with minimal intercourse. While both groups are classed as Caucasians, the original Semites are associated with the Mediterranean type, whereas the original Aryan speakers are associated with the Nordic race.
