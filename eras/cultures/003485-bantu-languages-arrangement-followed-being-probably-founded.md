# Bantu Languages

The Bantu languages constitute a great group of languages in Africa, extending from a short distance north of the equator to the southern extremity of the continent. The term "Bantu" (literally *Ba-ntu*) is the most archaic and widely used term for "men," "mankind," or "people" within these languages. It is a designation consecrated by usage, though it has faced criticism.

## Linguistic Structure and Features
The defining characteristic of the Bantu group is the governing of an unchangeable root by prefixes. The syllable *-ntu* originally meant "person" or "object" and is used as a relative pronoun meaning "he who" or "that which." When combined with different prefixes, it takes on various meanings: *muntu* means "a man," *bantu* means "men," *kintu* means "a thing," *bintu* means "things," and *kantu* means "a little thing."

The Bantu verb consists of a practically unchangeable root, which serves as the second person singular of the imperative. Various particles—which are reduced prepositions, adverbs, or worn-down verbs acting as auxiliaries—are prefixed and suffixed to this root. While the building of verbal roots into tenses and moods likely occurred independently across principal groups, this arrangement was probably founded on a fundamental system common to the original Bantu tongue.

One shared method for forming the preterite tense involves a particle such as *-ile* or *-ili*, likely connected to the root *li* or *di* (meaning "to be" or "exist"). For example, the Zulu word for love, *tanda*, becomes *tandile* (have loved/did love). In other dialects, the root *pa* (to give) becomes *pele* in the preterite.

## Classification and Distribution
The domain of Bantu speech is broadly divided into four great sections:
1. The languages of the East Coast and the Great Lakes, including the Zambezi basin.
2. The South-Central group, such as Bechuana-Zulu.
3. The South-West group, ranging from the Angola-Congo coast and Damaraland to the southern Belgian Congo.
4. The Western group, comprising Cameroon and Central and Northern Congo languages, and likely group No. 2 of the Semliki river and Albert Nyanza.

There are also small patches of languages spoken by a few villages that are emphatically Bantu but possess isolated characters. The family is noted for a striking homogeneity compared to the confusion of tongues found in Africa north of the Bantu borderland.

## Archaic Dialects and Evolution
The most archaic dialects today are found in Uganda, Unyoro, Mount Elgon, Ruwenzori, the north coast of Tanganyika, and the Bemba country. Other archaic types include the Subiya speech of the western Zambezi, the Nkonde and Kese dialects of Lake Nyasa, and Ci-bodzo of the Zambezi delta. The Oci-herero of Damaraland contains many original roots, while Kimakonde on the east coast is considered primitive. Ki-swahili of Zanzibar is primitive in its roots, though not in its prefixes. 

Previously, Zulu-Kaffir was regarded as the most archaic type, but it has been deposed from this position as the languages of the Great Lakes became known. Zulu-Kaffir exhibits recent deviations in phonetics and vocabulary and is unusually divergent from the typical Bantu in some features.

## Origins and Migration
The creation of the pronominal-prefix language type predates the exodus from the Bantu mother-land. While the exact birthplace is unknown, there are affinities in numerals (2, 3, 4, and sometimes 5) and a few verbal roots between Bantu and languages of the Gold Coast, lower Niger, Benue river, and North Togoland. Fula shows some resemblance in grammatical construction (using suffixes instead of prefixes) and possesses a similar form of concord.

Linguistic evidence suggests that not more than 3,000 years ago, a powerful tribe of negroes speaking the Bantu mother-language pushed forcibly from the heart of Africa—the region between the western Nile, Congo, and Shari watersheds—into the southern half of the continent. This group was physically allied to negroes of the southern Lake Chad and south-western Nile basins, though impregnated with the Caucasian Hamite.

The timeline of this migration is supported by the common root for domestic fowl, *kuku* (and its variants like *nkuku*, *ngoko*, or *nsusu*). Because the domestic fowl reached Africa through Egypt no earlier than 500 to 400 B.C., and took several hundred years to reach equatorial regions, the Bantu must have had the bird established before leaving their original home. This suggests the exodus began not more than 2,000 years ago.

As the Bantu swept across the continent, they assimilated or absorbed the preceding populations, which included Forest Pygmies, Hottentot-Bushmen, and Nilotic-negroes. Consequently, modern Bantu speakers exhibit great diversity in physical characteristics, including some who are dwarfish (Forest Pygmies) and others of Nilotic or Gala origin.
