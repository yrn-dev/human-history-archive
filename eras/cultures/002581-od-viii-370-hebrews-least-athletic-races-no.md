# Ball Games and Athleticism in Antiquity

## The Nature and Origins of the Ball
A ball is defined as any rounded body, specifically those with a smooth surface. These objects have been utilized as missiles, for games, or as descriptors for anatomical features such as the "ball socket" joints of the shoulder and hip, or the protuberances at the root of the big toe and thumb. Because rolling objects appeal to humans and animals alike, balls are considered an essential feature of nearly every game requiring physical exertion and are believed to date from the earliest times. Evidence of ball games is found in portrayals on Egyptian monuments and continues to be observed among the least advanced savage tribes.

## Ball Play in Greek Culture
In the works of Homer, ball play is depicted in the land of the Phaeacians. Nausicaa is described as playing with a ball and her maidens, while Halios and Laodamas performed ball play accompanied by dancing before Odysseus and Alcinous. 

Among the Greeks, games with balls (known as *sphairai*) were generally left to girls and boys. These activities were viewed as useful subsidiaries to more violent athletic exercises, serving as a means to render the body graceful and keep it supple. While regular rules for these games have left little trace, the *Onomastikon* of Pollux of Naucratis lists several forms:
*   ***Aporraxis***: The act of putting the ball on the ground using the open hand.
*   ***Ourania***: A game where the ball is flung into the air to be caught by two or more players.
*   ***Phaininda***: A game of catch involving two or more players that utilizes feinting to test the skill and quickness of the participants.

## Ball Play in Roman Culture
In Rome, ball games were viewed as an adjunct to the bath. These activities were graduated according to the health and age of the bathers. To accommodate this, a specific place called a *sphaeristerium* was typically set apart within the baths (*thermae*).

## Athleticism and the Hebrews
In contrast to the Greek and Roman traditions of physical exercise, the Hebrews are characterized as the least athletic of races. This is evidenced by the fact that there is no mention of the ball within their scriptures.

## Perspectives on Athletic Training
While some cultures embraced athletic contests, others viewed them with skepticism. In the Greek tradition, athletes such as Theagenês were highly distinguished, with some accounts claiming he won between twelve hundred and fourteen hundred prizes in various *agônes*. Other figures, such as Tisamenos, practiced for the "five contests" of the Olympic games, though it was later perceived that his specific destiny concerned martial rather than athletic contests.

However, some military leaders argued that the life of an athlete was fundamentally different from that of a soldier. The athlete's routine—consisting of regular exercise, frequent meals, long sleep, and intervals of rest—was seen as making them susceptible to being put out of condition by any change in routine. Conversely, the life of a soldier requires an adaptation to diversity and change, specifically the ability to endure a lack of sleep and hunger. Consequently, some commanders, such as Philopœmen, avoided wrestling and sought to bring athletic exercise into contempt, believing it unfitted the body for the struggles of battle.
