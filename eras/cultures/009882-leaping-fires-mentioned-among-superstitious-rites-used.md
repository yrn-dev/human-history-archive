# Leaping Over Fires in Superstitious Rites

The practice of leaping over fires has been documented across various cultures and eras as a component of superstitious rites, pagan customs, and exorcism rituals. These activities range from seasonal communal celebrations in Europe to specific spiritual cleansings in Central Asia.

## European Traditions and Pagan Origins
Throughout Europe, peasants have historically lit bonfires on specific days of the year, engaging in dancing around or leaping over the flames. This custom is traced back to the Middle Ages, with similarities to ancient usages suggesting the practice originated in the early days of heathen Europe.

The earliest evidence of these ceremonies in Europe is found in the efforts of Christian synods during the 7th and 8th centuries to suppress them as pagan practices. For example, the third council of Constantinople (A.D. 680), in its 65th canon, commanded an end to the "ridiculous and foolish" custom of people kindling fires before their houses and shops on new moons and leaping over them. Similarly, in A.D. 742, the Synodus Francica under Pope Zachary forbade "sacrilegious fires" known as *Nedfri* (or bonefires), as well as other pagan observations.

In antiquity, Ovid's *Fasti* describes leaping over fires as one of the superstitious rites used during the Palilia, a feast dedicated to Pales, the goddess of shepherds. During this festival, shepherds would light heaps of straw and jump over them as they burned.

## Rituals of Exorcism and Purification
Beyond seasonal festivals, leaping and passing through fire have been used in specific ritualistic contexts to ward off evil. In an account involving the exorcisers known as Shamans, a rite was performed for a man named Zemarchus. The Shamans placed baggage in the center and kindled a fire using branches of frankincense. While beating a drum and a gong and murmuring "barbarous words" in the Scythian language, the practitioners passed burning frankincense leaves over the baggage. During this process, the exorcisers became frantic and leaped about violently to exorcise evil spirits. The ritual concluded by leading Zemarchus himself through the fire to avert evil.

## Integration and Evolution of Bonfires
The use of bonfires evolved over time, and their presence in Christian festivals is viewed as a compromise made by the early Church with heathen traditions. In Cornwall, bonfires are lit on the eve of St. Peter's day and St. John the Baptist. The Cornish term for midsummer, *Goluan*, signifies both "festivity" and "light." These fires sometimes involve the burning of effigies or the pretense of burning a living person; some evidence suggests that human sacrifices were actually performed in bonfires in ancient times.

## Other Uses of Fire in Ritual and History
While leaping is a specific superstitious rite, fire has served various other ritualistic and functional purposes:
*   **Funeral Rites:** In the earliest age of the Yngling race, known as the Age of Burning, all dead were consumed by fire, and standing stones were raised over their ashes.
*   **Destruction and Alarm:** Bonfires have been used as signals of alarm, such as those used to warn England of the approach of the Armada, or for the immolation of proscribed books and heretics.
*   **Religious Festivals:** In Japan, historical rites include the "fire-subduing fete," alongside other ceremonies like the harvest festival and the "great purification" (Oharai).
