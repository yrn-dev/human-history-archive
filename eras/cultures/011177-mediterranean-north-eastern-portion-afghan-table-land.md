# The North Eastern Afghan Table-land and Surrounding Regions

The north eastern portion of the Afghan table-land is part of an extensive plateau in Asia located west of British India. This high region, which is nearly conterminous with Afghanistan, Iran (or Persia), Baluchistan, Armenia, and the provinces of Asia Minor, covers an area as large as Tibet, although it exists at a much lower altitude.

## Geography and Boundaries
The Afghan table-land is bounded to the south by the Arabian Sea, the Persian Gulf, and the Tigris, extending westward to the north-east angle of the Levant. Its northern border follows nearly 36 deg. N. to the southern shore of the Caspian and continues to the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmora. The eastern flank consists of a line of hills located a short distance from the Indus, between the mouth of that river and the Himalaya, approximately on the 72nd meridian. While these hills generally do not exceed 5,000 or 4,000 ft. in elevation, some summits reach 10,000 ft. or more.

The south-western face of the plateau follows the coast of the Persian Gulf from the Indus mouth. Further west, it is formed by a mountain scarp that flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris, rising in many points to 10,000 ft. This scarp extends through Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian, where it turns along the Taurus range toward the north eastern angle of the Mediterranean.

## The North Eastern Highlands
The north eastern portion of the Afghan table-land abuts on Tibet and the Himalaya. Between 34 deg. and 36 deg. N. and the 71st and 72nd meridians, these regions form a continuous mass of mountain. From the intersection of the 36th parallel and the 71st meridian, an unbroken mountain range stretches in two directions: one toward the north east, reaching the crest of the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau, and the other nearly due west toward the Caspian.

This north eastern range is characterized by great altitude and serves as a drainage divide. It separates the headwaters of the Oxus, which flow toward the Aral Sea, from the headwaters of the Indus and its tributary, the Kabul. These latter rivers unite below Peshawar and discharge southward into the Arabian Sea. The western section of this range, known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons, decreases in height west of the 65th meridian.

## Regional Territories and Borders
The Pamir extension of Afghan territory in the north-east reaches nearly 75 deg. E. The boundary follows the water-divide to the head of the Taghdumbash Pamir and is subsequently defined by the water-parting of the Hindu Kush. This boundary leaves the Hindu Kush near the Dorah Pass, dividing Chitral and Bajour from Kafiristan. It also separates sections of the Mohmands according to whether they fall under British or Afghan sovereignty, crossing the Peshawar-Kabul route at Lundi-Khana.

This demarcation creates a belt of independent territory—including Bajour, Swat, and part of the Mohmand territory north of the Kabul river—between Afghanistan and British India. This principle of neutral intervening territory is maintained along the eastern borders of Afghanistan down to the Gomal river. From the Gomal, Baluchistan serves as the intervening state between the two countries.

## Adjacent Districts and Landscapes
To the north-west of Afghanistan lies Badghis ("home of the winds"), a district situated between the Hari Rud and Murghab rivers, extending north to the edge of the Sarakhs desert. This region includes the Chul formations and was surveyed by a boundary commission in 1885.

In the vicinity of the Indus, the district of Bannu (comprising the Marwat and Bannu tahsils) lies north of the river. The valley of Bannu forms an irregular oval measuring 60 m. north to south and 40 m. east to west. The region is inhabited by the Bannuchis (a mongrel Afghan tribe), Marwats (a Pathan race), and Waziri Pathans.

Baluchistan, which also derives its name from its dominant race, extends from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea. It is divided into foreign territories and British Baluchistan. The latter includes tracts ceded by Afghanistan under the 1879 treaty of Gandamak. The territory comprises native states such as Las Bela and Kalat (including Kharan and Makran), as well as tribal areas held by the Bugti and Marri tribes.

## Climate and Environmental Factors
The table-lands of Afghanistan are not affected by the south-west monsoon. As air currents flow north over India, they are diverted and lose force, blowing as south winds up the Indus and as easterly or south-easterly winds over the north-eastern Gangetic plain. By the time these winds reach the northern extremity of the Indian plain, their northward velocity is almost entirely exhausted, preventing them from penetrating the Afghan table-lands or the ranges of the Himalaya.
