# The Noble Eightfold Path

The Noble Eightfold Path is a spiritual framework discovered by the Tathagata (Gautama) designed to lead practitioners toward peace, insight, higher wisdom, and Nirvana. It is presented as a "Middle Path," specifically positioned to avoid two extremes: the low and pagan ideal of devotion to attractions based on passions—which is described as ignoble and unprofitable—and the practice of asceticism, which is characterized as painful and unprofitable.

## The Four Noble Truths
The Eightfold Path is the practical application of the Four Noble Truths. The first truth is the Noble Truth as to suffering, which posits that birth, decay, disease, and death are painful. Pain is also found in union with the unpleasant, separation from the pleasant, and any unsatisfied craving. In summary, the five aggregates of clinging, or the conditions of individuality, are painful.

The second truth identifies the origin of suffering as "craving thirst," which causes the renewal of becomings. This craving may manifest as a desire for the gratification of the senses, a craving for prosperity, or a craving for a future life. The third truth is the passing away of pain, which occurs when no passion remains and one is emancipated from this craving thirst. The fourth truth is the way that leads to the passing away of pain, which is the Noble Eightfold Path.

## Elements of the Path
The path consists of eight elements, some of which are grouped together in certain descriptions:

*   **Right Views:** This is the first research of followers, emphasizing a stern examination of ideas and an insistence upon truth. It rejects tawdry superstitions and the belief in the transmigration of souls or an enduring individual soul.
*   **Right Aspirations:** This involves the suppression of evil desires, idle excitements, useless cravings, and low ideals. These are replaced by the cultivation of right desires and lofty aspirations. Examples include the wish not to injure any living thing, the desire for emancipation from sensuality, the attainment of love for others, and the promotion of right dispositions in one's own heart. This element does not seek the destruction of desire, but rather the change of desire; therefore, devotion to art, science, or the betterment of things is harmonious with Right Aspirations, provided they are free from the craving for fame or jealousy.
*   **Right Speech, Right Conduct, and Right Mode of Livelihood:** These three elements comprise the ethical application of the path.
*   **Right Effort:** This requires a keenly critical eye upon one's activities, as the path does not tolerate slovenly application or mere good intentions.
*   **Right Mindfulness:** This is described as a constant guard against lapsing into personal feeling or glory regarding one's actions.
*   **Right Rapture:** This element is aimed against witless gloryings and pointless ecstasies.

## Historical Application
The Eightfold Path was applied by a monarch who ruled a vast empire in peace. After joining the Buddhist community as a layman and later becoming a full member of the Order, he devoted himself to attaining Nirvana through the path. His career was distinguished by Right Aspiration, Right Effort, and Right Livelihood. His practical contributions included:
*   The founding of hospitals, public gardens, and gardens for medicinal herbs.
*   The organization of tree planting for shade and the digging of wells in India.
*   The appointment of officers to supervise charitable works.
*   The creation of a ministry for subject races and aborigines, and the provision of education for women.

This monarch also sought to educate his people in a common view of life and set up thirty-five surviving inscriptions rehearsing the simple, human teachings of Gautama. He further sent missionaries to spread these teachings to the Ptolemies, the Seleucids, Ceylon, and Kashmir.

## Origins and Transmission
The principles of the Noble Eightfold Path were first explained by the Buddha to five ascetics in the Deer-forest near Benares. Although the ascetics initially questioned how the Buddha could have won victory over the mind after abandoning his vows of bodily austerity, he explained the Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path as the "ambrosia of truth." While the initial discourse is simple, its implications are expanded upon extensively in the *suttas*.
