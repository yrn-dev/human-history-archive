# Royal Authority, Summons, and Revivals

The historical record contains various instances where individuals or institutions were summoned by royal or official authority, as well as cases where previous systems, ideas, or cultural movements were revived.

## Royal and Official Summons

Summonses have been utilized across different eras and cultures to compel attendance or service. In the case of the Italian musical composer Giovanni Battista Bononcini, royal and institutional summonses directed his career. In 1720, he was summoned to London by the Royal Academy of Music, where he produced several operas and received protection from the Marlborough family. Later, in 1748, he was summoned to Vienna for the purpose of composing music to honor the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle.

In ancient Sparta, the ephors exercised significant authority over the kings. The ephors presided over the Apella and Gerousia and were responsible for maintaining royal authority on the condition that the kings swore to rule according to the laws. The ephors possessed the power to try and imprison a king, who was required to appear before them at the third summons. In one instance, the Ephors summoned King Anaxandrides, the son of Leon, to advise him to put away his wife—who had borne him no children—and marry another to ensure the race of Eurysthenes did not become extinct.

In the context of the Welsh bards, the *Eisteddfodau* (congresses where famous bards met to contend in song) continued to be summoned by royal commission even after the conquest of Wales.

## Cultural and Institutional Revivals

The archive records several instances of the revival of literature, ideas, and social principles.

### Literary Revivals
In Belgium, there has been a revival of Flemish Literature since the separation of Belgium from the Netherlands in 1830. Similarly, the archive notes a group of poets associated with a revival who were born in Norway. This group included Nordahl Brun, Claus Fasting (who edited *The Critical Observer*), Christian H. Pram (author of the romantic epic *Staerkodder*), and Edvard Storm. These writers were fellow-students in Copenhagen and introduced a style distinct from that of the Danes, celebrating the mountains and rivers of Norway. While Norwegians claim them as the founders of Norwegian literature, they primarily appealed to the Danes.

### Intellectual and Religious Revivals
In the field of theology, J.L. Hug, a Roman Catholic professor at Freiburg, published *Einleitung in die Schriften des N. T.* in 1808. Hug is noted for introducing critical ideas that were not appreciated during his time but have since been revived. 

In a religious context, the archive mentions that the "patient principles" of the *Morale des Peres* were revived following the Reformation by the Quakers, the modern Anabaptists, and the Socinians.

## Limitations of Revival and Restoration

Not all previous systems could be revived. In Kyrênê, the equitable constitution established by Demônax—which included a triple classification into tribes—was broken up by the victory of the third Arkesilaus and the restoration of the Battiads. Because Arkesilaus the Third introduced a large change of property and a number of new colonists, the arrangements of Demônax could not have been revived after the Battiad dynasty was finally expelled, as they were rendered inapplicable to the subsequent city.
