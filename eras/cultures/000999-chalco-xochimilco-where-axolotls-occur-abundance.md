# Chalco and Xochimilco

Lakes Chalco and Xochimilco are bodies of water located near Mexico City. Situated approximately 10 feet higher than the Lago de Texcoco, these lakes are separated from the Texcoco by several hills. The geography of the region is characterized by high mountains that slope down toward the southern shores, featuring a belt of fertile pastures containing trees, shrubs, ravines, rocks, and small streams.

## The Axolotl Habitat
These two lakes are described as a "paradise" for the axolotl, a Mexican salamander of the genus *Amblystoma*. While axolotls are entirely absent from the Lago de Texcoco, they occur in abundance in Chalco and Xochimilco. In Lake Xochimilco, the water is characterized by powerful springs; however, away from these springs, the water appears muddy and dark, containing decomposing vegetable matter and suspended fresh matter. The environment teems with worms, *Daphniae*, larvae of insects, fish, and axolotls.

The axolotl finds numerous inducements to remain in these lakes, including a stable amount of water, a constant abundance of food, and innumerable hiding places located under banks, in the mud, and among the roots and reeds of floating islands scattered throughout the lakes. Although there are thousands of opportunities for the creatures to leave the water and move into the surrounding landscape, there is nothing to induce them to do so.

## Life Cycle and Characteristics
The axolotl is a larval salamander that can grow to a length of eleven inches. It possesses a plump, moderately long body with a low dorsal crest and a strongly compressed tail. Its head is large and thick, featuring a large pendent upper lip and small eyes that lack lids. The most distinctive feature of the axolotl is the presence of three large appendages on each side of the back of the head. These are external gills fringed with filaments, resembling black ostrich feathers, which the animal uses to breathe oxygen dissolved in the water. Its coloration is typically brownish grey, dark olive-grey, or blackish, marked with round dots or black spots.

The breeding cycle begins in early February. Native fishermen observe that the eggs are fastened to water plants, after which thousands of small larvae swarm. These larvae grow rapidly, and by June, they have developed into "big, fat creatures" ready for the market. In the later summer, the axolotls take to the rushes, and they become scarce during the autumn.

## Metamorphosis and the Genus *Amblystoma*
In the natural environment of Lakes Chalco and Xochimilco, axolotls are perennibranchiate, meaning they retain their larval features (such as gills) even after reaching sexual maturity. No perfect *Amblystomas* have been found in the vicinity of these two lakes, and the axolotls there have not been known to metamorphose or leave the water.

However, the axolotl is the larval form of an air-breathing salamander. The genus *Amblystoma*, established by J. J. Tschudi in 1838, includes about twenty species. One of the most widely distributed is *Amblystoma tigrinum*, which inhabits cooler parts of Mexico and the United States. This species typically does not exceed nine inches in length and is usually blackish or brown with yellow spots.

Research and experiments, such as those by Marie von Chauvin and observations at the Jardin des Plantes in Paris, have proven that the axolotl's ancestral course of evolution remains latent. While sexually ripe axolotls in the lakes appear incapable of changing, they can transform into *Amblystoma tigrinum* under certain circumstances. This metamorphosis involves the loss of the dorsal crest and gills, the development of movable eyelids, a change in dentition, and the appearance of yellow spots.

## Human Use and History
The axolotl has been known to the people of Mexico from the remotest times. It is regularly harvested from the neighboring lakes and brought to the Mexico market as an article of food, as its flesh is considered wholesome and agreeable. The naturalist Francisco Hernandez (1514-1578) referred to the creature by several names, including *atolocatl*, *lusus aquarum*, *piscis ludicrus*, and *axolotl*.

In the early 19th century, Humboldt brought the first specimens to Cuvier, who recognized that they were not related to permanent gill-breathers like *Proteus* or *Siren*, but were larval forms of air-breathing salamanders. Later, in 1863, thirty-four axolotls (including one albino) were sent to the Jardin d'Acclimatation in Paris by order of Marshal Forey. These specimens helped settle scientific debates regarding the animal's ability to breed in a branchiferous condition and its eventual capacity for metamorphosis.
