# Vegetation of the Higher, Cooler, and Less Rainy Ranges

The vegetation found in higher elevations—characterized by cooler temperatures and reduced rainfall—exhibits distinct patterns of uniformity and species distribution, often diverging significantly from the flora of lower, wetter tropical regions.

## The Himalayan Ranges and Tibetan Plateau

In the Himalaya, there is a clear transition in flora based on elevation and geography. While the hotter and wetter eastern regions possess tropical flora continuous with the Malayan islands, this becomes less marked as one moves westward, where winter cold increases and rainfall diminishes. The vegetation of the higher, cooler, and less rainy ranges of the Himalaya maintains a greater uniformity of character across the entire chain and more closely resembles European forms. At the greatest elevations, many alpine plants are found that are believed to be identical to species from the north Arctic regions.

As the terrain reaches the Tibetan plateau, the increased dryness causes the flora to assume many features of the Siberian type, including various Siberian genera and true Siberian species. Some of these Siberian forms extend into the plains of upper India and the rainy parts of the mountains. A notable feature of Tibet is the presence of assemblages of marine plants, which are frequently found growing near salt lakes at elevations of 14,000 to 15,000 feet above the sea.

## Regional Variations in the Himalaya

The distribution of plants in the Himalaya varies between the east and west:

*   **Eastern Region:** Vegetation is most varied and abundant. Luxuriant forest extends to 12,000 feet. Above this begins the sub-alpine region, where rhododendron scrub often covers the ground up to 14,000 or 13,000 feet.
*   **Western Region:** Due to a drier climate, forests are less dense and luxuriant. Warm mountain slopes are covered with rhododendron, oaks, or *Pinus longifolia*. Below 8,000 feet, forests are not commonly dense, except in some low-elevation secluded valleys. From 8,000 to 12,000 feet, a thick forest of deciduous trees is almost universal. Above this, the sub-alpine region continues up to 18,000 feet or more.

Certain species are geographically restricted; for example, tree-ferns and other tropical forms of the east do not reach west of Nepal. Conversely, the deodar or cedar is hardly indigenous east of the sources of the Ganges, with western forms increasing in number toward Afghanistan.

## High-Altitude Flora and Species Distribution

At various elevations, specific plant types prevail. Between 4,000 and 8,000 feet, epiphytal orchids are very frequent, sometimes reaching 10,000 feet. Below 8,000 feet, only one pine is found, while several other Coniferae appear above that height. In the lower levels, *Pandanus*, palms, bamboos, tree-ferns, and plantains are abundant. On the drier and less snowy slopes of Tibet, vegetation can ascend to above 18,000 feet.

In more rainy regions, herbaceous vegetation includes the Cyperaceae, Gramineae, ferns, and other Cryptogams, as well as the Scitamineae, Orontiaceae, and Orchidaceae. Some of these, along with certain trees, ascend into the temperate zones of the mountains, including *Osbeckia*, *Begonia*, various Scitamineae, Cyrtandraceae, and a few epiphytical orchids.

## Vegetation in Other High or Cold Regions

In other global regions, high-altitude or cold-climate vegetation follows similar patterns of adaptation:

*   **Greenland:** In the height of summer, vegetation is scanty in species but profuse in individual plants of the arctic type, similar to those on the highest British hills or in Lapland. There is no forest in the country. In the north, the ground is covered by creeping dwarf willows, crow-berries, mosses, and flowers such as the yellow poppy, pyrola, pedicularis, and andromeda. In South Greenland, vegetation is more abundant and less arctic, with dwarf birches rising a few feet in sheltered spots and willows growing higher than a man.
*   **Doubs (France):** In the highest region of this mountainous department, where snow lies for six to eight months a year, the land is partly barren. However, forests of fir trees occupy the less exposed slopes.
*   **Other Mountains:** In certain regions, higher mountains preserve distinct species that are surviving remnants of flora from a cooler period.
