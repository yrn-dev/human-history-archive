# The Shrine of Nabi Yusha

## Location and Description
Located approximately 3 miles west of Bagdad on the Euphrates road, the shrine and tomb of Nabi Yusha (also known as Kohen Yusha) is situated in or by a grove of trees. It serves as a site of monthly pilgrimage.

## Religious Significance and Beliefs
The shrine is venerated by two different religious groups, each attributing a different identity to the figure interred there:

*   **Israelite Tradition:** The shrine is a place of pilgrimage for the Israelites, who believe it to be the place of sepulture for Joshua, the son of Josedech. Joshua is identified as the high priest at the conclusion of the exilian period.
*   **Muslim Tradition:** The site is also venerated by Moslems, who identify the tomb as that of Yusuf (Joseph).

## Regional Context and Conflicts
The shrine of Nabi Yusha is one of four similar Israelite shrines located in Irak. The other three include:
*   The tomb of Ezra, located on the Shatt el-Arab near Korna.
*   The tomb of Ezekiel, located in the village of Kefil near Kufa.
*   The well of Daniel, located near Hillah.

A point of contention exists regarding the use of the site; the Israelites maintain the practice of burying their chief priests at this shrine. This right has been contested by Moslems at times. In 1889, an attempt by the Israelites to exercise this burial right resulted in a serious conflict between the two groups.

## Geography of the Surrounding Region
The shrine is located near Bagdad, the capital of the Turkish vilayet of the same name. Bagdad is situated on the river Tigris within an extensive desert plain. At this location, the Tigris and Euphrates rivers reach their closest proximity, separated by little more than 25 miles. 

The region between these two rivers is characterized by varying terrain. North of Bagdad, the area is an arid, waterless limestone steppe inhabited by roving Arabs. South of Bagdad, the land consists of alluvial soil deposited by the rivers. While this southern region possesses great potential for fertility, it is absolutely flat and subject to inundations during flood seasons, often leaving the surrounding areas of Bagdad underwater. For much of the remainder of the year, the land is either a parched desert or composed of lagoons and swamps.

## Transport and Communication
The shrine lies along the Euphrates road, which has historically been a significant line of communication. This route coincides largely with the present road from Bagdad to Aleppo and crosses the Euphrates at Thapsakus. 

Historically, this road acquired great importance during the period of the Seleukid kings (approximately 300–100 B.C.) as a means of communication between Seleukeia on the Tigris (located below Bagdad) and other cities founded by Seleukus Nikator and his successors in Asia Minor and Northern Syria, including Antioch, Laodikeia, Apameia, and Seleukeia in Pieria.
