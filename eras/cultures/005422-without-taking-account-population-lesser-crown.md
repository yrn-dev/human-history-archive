# Lesser Crown Colonies and Related Classifications

The term "lesser crown" appears in historical and geographical contexts to distinguish subordinate territories, ranks, or entities from their "greater" counterparts. In the context of the British Empire, this specifically refers to a category of colonies maintained for strategic purposes.

## British Lesser Crown Colonies
The British Empire was geographically distributed across five principal divisions: Canada, Australia, India, South Africa, and the United Kingdom. Because these divisions are separated by the three great oceans of the world, the empire required the acquisition of intermediate ports to serve as coaling stations on trade routes. This necessity determined the position of the lesser crown colonies, which were held primarily for commercial and military purposes.

Examples of these lesser crown colonies include:
*   **Eastern Route:** Aden, Ceylon, Gibraltar, Hong-Kong, Labuan, Malta, the Bermudas, and the Straits Settlements.
*   **Other Routes:** Ascension, the Falklands, Mauritius, Seychelles, St. Helena, Tristan da Cunha, and various groups in the western Pacific.

Additionally, the British annexed rocky islets in the northern Pacific to serve as telegraph stations for an all-British cable.

### Political and Social Hierarchies
In English history, those who held land directly from the crown via military service were all termed "barons." However, differences in importance led to a division between "greater" and "lesser" barons. By the time of Henry II, the *Dialogus de Scaccario* distinguished between greater and lesser baronies. By 1164, greater barons received special summons to the council, while the Magna Carta (1215) stipulated that lesser barons were to be summoned only through sheriffs. This distinction eventually restricted the privileges of peerage to the greater barons.

In diplomatic disputes, French ambassadors once argued that Christendom was divided into four great nations (Italy, Germany, France, and Spain), and that "lesser kingdoms" such as Portugal, Denmark, and England were merely comprehended under those divisions. The English countered this, asserting that the British islands should be viewed as a fifth coördinate nation.
