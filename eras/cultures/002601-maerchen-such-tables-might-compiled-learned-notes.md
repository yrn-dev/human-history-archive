# Maerchen

*Maerchen* is the German term for nursery tales or folk-tales. These stories are recognized as the immemorial inheritance of all European peoples, as well as the peoples of India, Slavonic regions, and Celts.

## Characteristics and Origins
The antiquity and origin of *Maerchen* are considered mysterious and unknown. Because they are genuine popular tales, it is regarded as futile to attempt to assign them to a specific author or a particular date. While the language of these tales alters automatically over centuries of oral recitation, their stock ideas and main incidents are of world-wide diffusion and dateless age.

## Comparative Study and "Story-Roots"
There have been efforts to analyze the "story-roots" of *Maerchen* through the creation of comparative tables. Von Hahn attempted to produce such tables, though the effort was not very exhaustive. It has been suggested that more comprehensive comparative tables regarding the identity of plot and incident could be compiled using the introductions and learned notes provided by Prof. Child in his 1898 work, *English and Scottish Popular Ballads*.

## Common Motifs and Elements
Certain features found in *Maerchen* are also mirrored in the ballad literature of various lands, including those of Italy, Portugal, Denmark, France, Greece, and Scotland. 

One such recurring element is the presence of talking-birds. While these are more common in *Maerchen* than in ballads, they remain a general feature that causes no surprise to the human characters within the stories. Examples of such birds in related folk traditions include:
* The omniscient popinjay found in Border minstrelsy.
* The wise birds whose speech is understood by exceptionally gifted Zulus.
* The wicked dove that whispers temptation in French folk-songs.
* The birds that introduce stories in Romaic ballads.
* The bird in *Water o' Wearies Well* that emerges from a bush to dine on water.

## Relationship to Ballad Literature
The study of *Maerchen* provides a framework for understanding the universal sameness of tone, legend, and primitive poetical formulae found in European ballads. Just as *Maerchen* are seen as a shared inheritance, many romantic ballads share common plots across different cultures. Examples of these shared plots include:
* **The Faithful Leman:** A story where a lord brings home a "braw new bride," but the original lover recovers his affection at the eleventh hour. This appears as *Lord Thomas and Fair Annie* in Scotland, *Skiaen Anna* in Danish, and twice in M. Fauriel's collection of Romaic songs.
* **The Feigned Death:** A plot involving a girl who pretends to be dead so she may be carried on a bier to meet her lover. This occurs in Scotland, as well as in the songs of Metz (collected by Puymaigre) and Provence (collected by Damase Arbaud). In the latter two regions, the story often includes a sequel where the lover attempts to murder his bride, but she is too cunning and drowns him.
* **The Symbolic Flora:** The motif of two rose trees, or a bush and briar, that meet and plait over the graves of unhappy lovers. In Provencal, passers-by observe this and say, "Diou ague l'amo Des paures amourous."
