# Physical Features, Flora, and Fauna of Selected Regions

The distribution of plant and animal life across the globe is closely dependent on climatic conditions. This relationship is evident in the diverse environments of Asia, Africa, the Americas, and the Arctic, where physical geography dictates the types of vegetation and wildlife present.

## African Regions: Ashanti and Benin

In Ashanti, a significant portion of the land is covered by almost impenetrable primeval forest. This environment is characterized by hardwoods and silk-cotton trees, with the bombax reaching heights exceeding 200 feet. The forest's "weird aspect" is largely created by lianas, or monkey-ropes, which hang in immense festoons from tree to tree; some species of these lianas produce a type of commercial rubber. While ferns are abundant and the mimosa grows to heights of 30 to 60 feet, the forest is described as oppressive in its monotony, with few flowers, birds, or beasts. However, areas surrounding towns are carefully cultivated, producing fruits, vegetables, yams, and grain. In the north-eastern districts, the forest transitions into a park-like landscape of plains with high coarse grass, dotted with small trees such as the dwarf date, fan palms, shea-butter, wild plum, and occasional baobabs.

The fauna of Ashanti includes leopards, various antelopes, many types of monkeys, and numerous venomous snakes, though the elephant is comparatively rare. Rivers are inhabited by crocodiles and two types of hippopotami—the ordinary and a pygmy variety. Parrots are the most characteristic birds, and insect life is abundant.

In Benin, the physical characteristics and flora are similar to the rest of southern Nigeria. The coastline consists of a huge mangrove swamp intersected by creeks. Moving inland to rising ground, the landscape is dominated by dense forests where mahogany and cotton trees are conspicuous.

## European and Central Asian Mountain Systems

The physical landscape of certain European regions is defined by major mountain chains. The Balkans run east and west through the heart of the country, while the Rhodope mountains form much of the southern boundary. The Balkans are the southern half of the anti-Dacian system, with the Carpathians forming the northern portion. These two ranges share similarities in flora, geological formation, and outline.

The Carpathians act as a protective wall, creating a milder climate for regions to the south. The vegetation on these mountains is divided into four distinct zones based on altitude:
*   **Beech:** Up to 4,000 feet.
*   **Scottish fir:** Up to 5,000 feet.
*   **Pine:** Above 5,000 feet, becoming dwarfed and disappearing at 6,000 feet.
*   **Lichen and moss:** Above 6,000 feet, where the rock is almost bare.

The High Tatra and Transylvanian Mountains possess a flora similar to the middle region of the Alps. Transylvania also features a remarkable sea-shore flora found in marshes, ponds, and salt-impregnated lakes. The fauna of the Carpathians includes lynxes, wolves, bears, and birds of prey, as well as a mollusc fauna with species found only in the Alpine region and the Carpathians. The slopes are used for pasturing sheep and cattle in great numbers.

## Northern and Central Asia

The flora of northern Asia is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, though species vary. Notable absences east of the Ural include all heaths and the oak. On mountains, the primary trees are birch, larch, and pine—the latter reaching a northern limit of approximately 70 deg. N. in Siberia. Lower ground is characterized by poplars, alders, and willow. 

A flora of essentially European character extends from the Mediterranean to the Himalaya along the warm temperate zone. While many European species reach the central Himalaya, they are fewer in the eastern parts. Conversely, Japanese and Chinese forms are most numerous in the eastern Himalaya and less frequent in the west.

## The Americas: Kentucky and Honduras

In Kentucky, the primeval state was well timbered. While the middle section has been cleared—making blue grass the dominant floral feature—extensive forests remain in the east and west. Eastern forests are characterized by cedar, pine, walnut, tulip-tree (yellow poplar), elm, chestnut, beech, maple, and oak. Western forests consist largely of sycamore, gum, tulip-tree, beech, walnut, chestnut, hickory, oak, ash, and cypress. Other abundant trees include black mulberry, buck-eye, cucumber, pawpaw, and locust, while strawberry, raspberry, and grape are native fruits.

Historically, Kentucky's fauna was vast, including buffaloes, elks, deer, panthers, wolves, bears, lynx, and a wide array of smaller mammals such as skunks, opossums, woodchucks, raccoons, squirrels, rabbits, musk-rats, minks, otters, foxes, and beavers. Birds included partridges, turkeys, ducks, and geese. Streams contained suckers, eels, mullet, sun-fish, buffalo-fish, perch, and trout. Currently, only a few bears, lynx, and deer remain in the mountain districts, and fish and small game populations have been greatly reduced.

In Honduras, the fauna and flora resemble neighboring Central American states. The country experiences two seasons: a dry season from November to May and a wet season from May to November.

## Greenland and Global Reptilian Distribution

Greenland's land mammals are more American than European. Species from America include the dog, the Eskimo, the reindeer, the white polar wolf, the banded lemming, and the musk-ox. Circumpolar forms include the polar fox, the Arctic hare, the stoat, and the polar bear. Among its 61 breeding bird species, eight are European-Asiatic and four are American, with the remainder being North Pacific, North Atlantic, or circumpolar. Its vascular plants (about 310 species) are split nearly equally between American and European-Asiatic elements, with fifteen species being endemic.

On a global scale, surviving reptiles—including lizards, alligators, crocodiles, turtles, tortoises (Chelonia), and snakes—are limited in distribution. These creatures require warmth year-round and cannot tolerate cold. This is a continuation of the "hothouse fauna" of the Mesozoic era, which existed alongside a hothouse flora and could not endure frosts.
