# Linguistic Studies and Philology

The study of languages, encompassing grammar, lexicography, and comparative philology, has been documented through various specialized works focusing on regional dialects, national languages, and the historical evolution of speech.

## African Languages
Research into African languages has frequently focused on the Bantu group. Dr. W. I. Bleek published a two-part, unfinished *Comparative Grammar of South African Languages* in 1869, while R. N. Cust provided a *Sketch of the Modern Languages of Africa* in 1882. Father J. Torrend’s 1894 *Comparative Grammar of the South African Bantu Languages* focused primarily on the Central Zambezi, though some of its deductions are considered erroneous.

Further classification of Bantu speech, including the Cameroon and Fernandian groups, was detailed in the 1908 work *George Grenfell and the Congo*. Carl Meinhof contributed extensive information on the original structure of the Bantu mother-speech and languages in South-East and East Africa through his 1906 *Grundzuege einer vergleichenden Grammatik der Bantusprachen* and essays in the *Mittheilungen des Seminaers fuer Orientalische Sprachen*.

Specific language resources include:
*   **Swahili:** Dr. L. Krapf’s *Swahili-English Dictionary* (1882) contains invaluable data on the language as spoken on the East African coast, including obsolete words. Bishop Steere also published handbooks and vocabularies regarding the coast-lands, preserving prefixes that are now passing out of use.
*   **Mang'anja:** The Rev. D. C. Scott produced *An Encyclopaedic Dictionary of the Mananja (Mang'anja) Language of British Central Africa* in 1891.
*   **Congo and Kimbundu:** The Rev. Holman Bentley wrote a *Dictionary of the Congo Language* (1891), and Dr. Heli Chatelain authored a *Grammar of Kimbundu* and *The Folklore of Angola*.
*   **Yao:** The Rev. Alexander Hetherwick wrote an *Introductory Handbook of the Yao Language*.

## Indian Languages
Philological work in India spans several major languages, often involving European missionaries and scholars.

### Bengali, Assamese, and Oriya
The first Bengali grammar and dictionary in a European language was Manoel da Assumpcam's *Vocabulario em Idioma Bengalla e Portuguez* (1743). N.B. Halhed wrote the first English Bengali grammar in 1778, but William Carey is regarded as the father of Bengali philology due to his *Grammar* (1801) and *Dictionary* (1825). Other notable works include the grammars of W. Yates (edited by T. Wenger), Syama Caran Sarkar, and J. Beames, the latter of which became the standard work in 1894 by incorporating colloquial material. Dictionaries by G.C. Haughton (1833) and J. Mendies (c. 1870) are also prominent.

For Assamese, Nathan Brown's *Grammar* (1848) remains widely studied. M. Bronson's *Dictionary* (1867) was a primary resource until it was superseded by Hem Candra Barua's *Hema-kosa* (1900). Regarding Oriya, grammars were produced by E. Hallam, T. Maltby, and J. Browne, while Sutton's dictionary (1841) is noted for its practical use.

### Marathi and Konkani
Marathi is noted for having the best dictionary of any modern Indian language, J. T. Molesworth's (2nd ed., 1857), and the best grammar by Navalkar (3rd ed., 1894). Early Portuguese students focused on the northern Konkan dialect and Konkani. Resources for these include J. F. da Cunha Rivara's *Grammatica da lingua Concani no dialecto do norte* (1858), A. F. X. Maffei's English grammar (1882) and dictionaries (1883), and Monsenhor S. R. Dalgado's *Konkan-Portuguese Dictionary* (1893).

## European and Middle Eastern Languages
### English and Germanic Philology
The study of English has seen significant advances. Early influential works include those by Edwin Guest, Richard Garnett, R.G. Latham, and G.P. Marsh. Other specialized studies include A.J. Ellis's *Early English Pronunciation* (1869) and Henry Sweet's *The History of English Sounds* (1874).

In the realm of Germanic languages, Jacob Grimm's *Deutsche Grammatik* is regarded as a model for science due to its comprehensiveness and method. Its first part (1819) treated the inflections of Gothic, Old High German, Low German dialects (including English), and Scandinavian languages. While his grammar was highly influential—shaping Diez's grammar of Romance languages—Grimm's attempt at a great German dictionary was less successful, as his historical tendencies and the alphabetical format hindered its scientific character.

### Kurdish
The Kurdish language, Kermanji, is an old Persian patois with Chaldaean influences in the north and Turanian elements in the south. Two major varieties are the Zaza, spoken in the western Dersim country and intermingled with Armenian, and the Guran, spoken in Kermanshah and Ardelan, which is free from Semitic intermixture and closer to Persian. Kurdish literature includes translations of Persian poets and books on the religious mysteries of the Ali-Illahis. Linguistic resources include the Rev. Samuel Rhea's grammar of the Hakkari dialect (1872) and a French-Kurdish dictionary completed by Ferdinand Justi in 1879.

## Classical and Comparative Methods
Thomas Hewitt Key (1799-1875), an English classical scholar and professor of comparative grammar, introduced the "crude-form" system (the uninflected stem of words) from Sanskrit grammar into the teaching of classical languages, as seen in his *Latin Grammar* (1846). In his 1874 work *Language, its Origin and Development*, Key supported the onomatopoeic theory of language origin.
