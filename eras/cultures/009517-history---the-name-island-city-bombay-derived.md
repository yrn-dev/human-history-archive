# Bombay

## Etymology and Early History
The name of the city and island of Bombay is derived from Mumba, a form of Parvati. Mumba was the goddess of the Kolis, a race of fishermen and husbandmen who are the earliest known inhabitants of the area, having likely occupied the island around the beginning of the Christian era. 

Originally, Bombay consisted of seven islands, referred to by Ptolemy as the *Heptanesia*. These islands were outlying portions of the territories held by various successive dynasties dominant in western India, including the Rashtrakutas, Chalukyas, Mauryas, and Satavahanas. During the Chalukya and Maurya periods (450-750), the principal location in the Bombay harbour was the city of Puri, located on Elephanta Island.

The first town established on Bombay Island was Mahikavati (Mahim). It was founded by King Bhima, who was likely a member of the house of the Yadavas of Deogiri, following a raid into the Deccan by Ala-ud-din Khilji in 1294. Mahikavati remained under Hindu rule until 1348, when it was captured by a Mahommedan force from Gujarat. The islands remained part of the kingdom (or province) of Gujarat until 1534, at which point Sultan Bahadur ceded them to the Portuguese.

## Geography and Urban Layout
Bombay City is the capital of the Bombay Presidency and serves as the chief seaport of western India, located at 18 deg. 55' N. and 72 deg. 54' E. The city is situated on an island of the same name, which is now connected to the mainland via causeways. The island—more correctly described as the Bombay Peninsula—consists of a plain approximately 3 miles broad and 11 miles long, flanked by two parallel lines of low hills.

The harbour is enclosed on its eastern side by a neck of land stretching south-west, which protects the water (ranging from 5 to 7 miles wide) from the open sea. At the south-west of the island lies Back Bay, a shallow basin about 2 miles wide and 3 miles long. The nucleus of the city is the fort, situated on a raised strip of land between the harbour and the head of Back Bay. The central plain of the town was previously subject to submersion by the sea at high tide before the construction of the Hornby Vellard embankment.

The city features spacious streets for European commerce and native bazaars with houses rising three or four storeys, characterized by elaborately carved pillars. Notable landmarks include the Taj Mahal hotel, built by the Tata family in 1904, and the suburb of Malabar hill, a high ridge with terraced houses.

## British Acquisition and Administration
The East India Company established its first settlement in the Bombay presidency in 1618 with a factory at Surat, protected by a charter from Emperor Jahangir. After an unsuccessful attempt by the English and Dutch to gain the island of Bombay in 1626, proposals to purchase it from the Portuguese were suggested in 1653. In 1661, the island was ceded to the English crown as part of the dower of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II.

Due to poor administration by crown officers and a low estimation of the acquisition in England, the crown transferred Bombay to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10. The Company was granted powers for the administration of justice and defence, including the enrollment of a European regiment. These fortifications deterred a Dutch attack in 1673.

The settlement faced various challenges in the late 17th century. It was isolated between the Mogul empire and the Mahrattas, and trade was constantly menaced by Malabar pirates. The Siddi admirals of the Moguls also claimed the right to use the harbour for winter quarters. Internal instability occurred in 1674 with a mutiny, and again in 1683 when Richard Keigwin, a naval officer, was elected governor by popular vote. Keigwin cited the "oppressions, extortions, and exactions" of the Company and declared his government under the authority of the crown. He reformed taxation and increased trade by admitting "interlopers" before surrendering the fort to Sir John Child upon receiving a royal order.

## Economic Development and Growth
Bombay was placed at the head of all Company possessions in India in 1687, though it became subordinate to Calcutta in 1753. Following the annexation of the Deccan and victory over the Mahrattas, the city entered a period of unrestricted development. Its growth was further fostered by Mountstuart Elphinstone in 1819. The population grew from 236,000 in 1838 to 821,000 by 1891. This expansion was driven by the foundation of cotton factories, the opening of the Suez Canal, and the completion of the first railway line in 1853.

The modern industrial life of Bombay is defined by the manufacture and growth of cotton. The city and island contain nearly two-thirds of the presidency's mills. By 1905, there were 432 factories in the presidency, the majority of which were cotton-related. Other manufactures include silk goods, though this industry has declined due to European competition.

Despite its commercial prosperity and monopoly of the cotton industry, Bombay's growth has been hindered by a lack of great waterways like the Brahmaputra or Ganges and less fertile surrounding land. The city also suffered from a financial crisis in 1866-1867 due to falling cotton prices after the American war, and later experienced significant population and trade declines due to plague during the decade following 1891.

## Regional Context
The southern division of Bombay includes the Kolaba (or Colaba) district. This district is characterized by the Western Ghats, rice cultivation, and salt marshes used for salt manufacture. The district takes its name from a small island off Alibagh, once a stronghold of the 18th-century Mahratta pirate Angria. This same island gave its name to Kolaba Point, the southern spur of Bombay Island that protects the harbour entrance. Kolaba Point houses an observatory, a lunatic asylum, barracks for a European regiment, and the terminus of the Bombay & Baroda railway.
