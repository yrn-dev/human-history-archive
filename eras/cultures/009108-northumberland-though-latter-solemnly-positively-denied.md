# Northumberland

Northumberland is a region in England characterized by a diverse history involving royal boroughs, prominent territorial families, and notable intellectual figures. Its geography includes the sea-coast, where the village of Bamburgh is located.

## Bamburgh and Regional Landmarks
Bamburgh, also known as Bamborough, is a village situated on the sea-coast in the Berwick-upon-Tweed parliamentary division of Northumberland. It is located 54 miles north of Newcastle and 2.5 miles east of the Belford station on the North Eastern railway. Historically, Bamburgh was a royal borough prior to the Norman Conquest and returned two members to parliament during the reign of Edward I.

The village is home to an ancient castle positioned on a perpendicular rock 150 feet high, accessible only from the south-east. While the castle buildings are of various dates from the Norman period, the Saxon chronicles attribute the first erection to King Ida of Northumberland. These structures include a massive keep and the remains of an apsidal chapel dedicated to St Peter. 

The village church is dedicated to St Aidan, who served as the bishop of Lindisfarne (or Holy Island) around 634. The church is a cruciform building of Early English date featuring a crypt beneath the chancel. The churchyard contains a monument to Grace Darling (1815-1842), who rescued members of the crew of the ship "Forfarshire" in 1838. Her father served as the keeper of the Longstone Lighthouse, located on an outer rock of the Farne Islands, which extend 6 miles north-eastward from the Bamburgh coast.

## Notable Families and Figures
The house of Grey of Howick is described as one of the most considerable territorial families in Northumberland. General Sir Charles Grey (1729-1807), a younger son of this house, had a career of active service spanning nearly half a century. He served on the staff of Prince Ferdinand of Brunswick during the Seven Years' War, where he was wounded at Minden, and later served in Pennsylvania and New Jersey against revolted colonists. He also captured Guadeloupe and Martinique. In 1806, he was created Viscount Howick and Earl Grey, having previously been Baron Grey of Alnwick. His eldest surviving son, Charles Grey, 2nd Earl Grey (1764-1845), was an English statesman born at Fallodon, near Alnwick.

Other figures associated with the region include:
* **John Horsley (c. 1685-1732):** A British archaeologist and author of *Britannia Romana, or the Roman Antiquities of Britain*. There are conflicting accounts of his origin; some suggest he was a native of Newcastle-on-Tyne, while others claim his father was a Northumberland Nonconformist. Horsley was educated at Newcastle and Edinburgh University. He opened a private school in Morpeth and delivered lectures on hydrostatics and mechanics in Newcastle, Alnwick, and Morpeth. He resided at Widdrington, where he recorded rainfall in 1722 and 1723.
* **Louis Dutens (1730-1812):** A French writer and former charge d'affaires at Turin. The duke of Northumberland provided Dutens with the living of Elsdon in Northumberland and appointed him as a tutor to his son.

## Legal and Social History
The region's history includes specific social customs, such as "contributed Ploughing Days," which are known locally as *Bone-daags*.

In the context of the English court, the earl of Northumberland is noted as a former lover of Anne, the queen. During the proceedings against Anne in May, the earl left the court due to a sudden illness. In an attempt to save herself, Anne informed Cranmer of a supposed impediment to her marriage with the king. According to some accounts, this impediment was a previous marriage to Northumberland; however, Northumberland solemnly and positively denied this claim. Despite his denial, a committee of ecclesiastical lawyers and the archbishop pronounced the impediment sufficient to invalidate the marriage on May 17.
