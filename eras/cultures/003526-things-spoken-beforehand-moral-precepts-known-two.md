# The "Things Spoken Beforehand" and Moral Precepts

In the context of early Christian rites, the phrase "things spoken beforehand" refers to a specific body of moral teaching. These are defined as the moral precepts known as the "two ways"—the way of life and the way of death. This teaching serves as the beginning of the tract and is considered older than the rest of the text, potentially dating back to the year A.D. 80.

## Early Christian Rites and Instruction
The "things spoken beforehand" are associated with the process of baptism. According to the archive's records, those performing the baptism and those being baptized are expected to fast, with the candidate specifically bidden to fast for one or two days prior to the rite. If neither oil nor other materials are available, the rite is performed by pouring water upon the head three times in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

Justin, writing in his first *Apology* (c. 140), describes the dedication to God as a process for those who are persuaded and believe the teachings to be true and undertake to live accordingly. These individuals are instructed to pray and entreat God with fasting for the remission of past sins. Following this, they are brought to water to be regenerated through washing in the name of the Holy Spirit, the Saviour Jesus Christ, and God, the Father and Lord of the universe.

## Precepts in the Traditions of Bani Israel
The archive contains records of divine precepts given to the sons of Israel. According to the account of Josephus, Moses brought the people, including women and children, to a mountain to hear God speak directly so that the energy of the message would not be diminished by human speech. These precepts were written on two tables.

The ten resulting commands are:
1. Worship only the one God.
2. Do not create images of living creatures for worship.
3. Do not swear falsely by God.
4. Observe the seventh day as a day of rest from all work.
5. Honor parents.
6. Abstain from murder.
7. Do not commit adultery.
8. Do not be guilty of theft.
9. Do not bear false witness.
10. Do not desire that which belongs to another.

Similarly, the Qur'an records that Allah chose Musa (Moses) above other men through messages and words. Tablets were ordained for him containing laws, clear explanations, and admonitions regarding all matters. Musa was commanded to hold these with firmness and to enjoin his people to hold fast by the best in the precepts.

## Moral Precepts in Greek and Roman Philosophy
The development of moral precepts in the Greco-Roman world varied significantly across different eras and figures:

*   **Homeric Era:** The archive notes that there was no law or morality in Homer, meaning there were no general principles of action or classifications of acts as right or wrong. While "Aidos" (shame, pity, or veneration) existed as a moral feeling, it was rudimentary.
*   **Socratic Ethics:** Sokratês is credited with the innovation of establishing an ethical theory, focusing on human happiness and security as the end goal. While some authors, such as Zeller, suggest this eudæmonistic basis is similar in principle to sophistical moral philosophy, others argue that the sophists never aimed to establish ethical theories. Sokratês also noted that exceptions exist for every separate moral precept.
*   **Gnomic Poets:** Poets such as Solon, Theognis, and Phokylidês utilized a moralizing vein. Solon mixed political and social morality, while Theognis produced a mixture of general precepts and personal passion. Phokylidês is remembered for a few maxims in verse.
*   **Marcus Aurelius:** The precepts of Marcus Aurelius are described as the "gospel of his life," serving as records of his own practice. His morality was characterized by self-denial, purity, and constancy in the face of misfortune.

## Moral Standards in Other Traditions
Beyond the Mediterranean, other systems of precepts provided moral frameworks for society. In Japan, the spirit of Bushido provided a moral standard for the entire population. Originally the "Precepts of Knighthood" for the elite, these aspirations eventually filtered down to the masses. This influenced the *otoko-daté* (natural leaders of democracy), who acted as guardians of popular rights and spokesmen for the working-men. This system, known as *Yamato Damashii* (the Soul of Japan), expressed the *Volksgeist* of the Island Realm.
