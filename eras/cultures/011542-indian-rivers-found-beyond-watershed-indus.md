# Indian Rivers and Hydrography

The river systems of the Indian region are characterized by vast drainage basins and a diverse array of aquatic life. Among the most significant of these systems are the Indus and the Ganges, which form a substantial part of the southern division of Asia's hydrographic basins.

## Major River Basins and Drainage
The hydrographic basins of Asia are divided into several sections. The southern division, which includes the Indian Ocean and Pacific drainage, covers a total area of 6,514,000 square miles. Within this division, the Indian Ocean drainage accounts for 2,873,000 square miles. 

Among the largest individual river basins in Asia, the Ganges and the Indus are prominent. The Ganges basin covers an area of 409,500 square miles, while the Indus basin encompasses 370,000 square miles. In the northeast, the former province of Assam comprises the valleys of the Surma and Brahmaputra rivers, separated by a mountainous watershed.

## The Indus River
The Indus is a major waterway noted for its navigability and geographical significance. In the District of Dera Ismail Khan, the river intersects the land from north to south and is navigable by native boats throughout its 120-mile course within that district. The river also serves as a strategic point for infrastructure; at Kushalgarh in the Kohat district of the North-West Frontier province, the Indus is bridged to allow the strategic frontier railway to extend from Rawalpindi to the Kurram and Miranzai valleys.

Historically, the Indus was a point of exploration for Dareios, who sought to discover where the river—described as the second river in the world to produce crocodiles—emptied into the sea. He dispatched Skylax of Caryanda, who sailed from the land of Pactyïke and the city of Caspatyros down the river toward the East and the sunrising. In the era of Alexander, the land beyond the Indus was organized into native kingdoms, specifically those of Porus and Taxiles, which functioned as client principalities.

## Regional Hydrography and Watersheds
The drainage patterns surrounding the Indus vary by region. In Baluchistan, mountain walls facing the Indus extend 300 miles south of the railway to Quetta and 250 miles north of it. In the northern Pathan district, drainage either flows north toward the Gomal or moves eastwards through the limestone axis of the hills to reach the Indus. In the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, drainage forms channels that cut deep gorges and flow east into the plains of Sind. Further south, streams either run in straight lines to the Arabian Sea north of Karachi or curve westwards into inland swamps.

## Aquatic Life of Southern Asian Waters
The fresh waters of southern Asia exhibit specific biological characteristics. They are largely inhabited by siluroids, carp, spined or simple eels, and climbing and walking fishes. The walking or climbing fishes, found in Africa and south-eastern Asia, are uniquely organized to breathe outside of water, allowing them to survive in regions of rain and periodical drought.

The *Cyprinidae* (carp) are widely represented in southern Asia and can reach sizes not seen in Europe; for example, a *Barbus* weighing 300 lb was taken from the Tigris. These fish are most developed in number and size within temperate mountain regions, while smaller species inhabit low-lying rivers and hotter regions. The *Clupeidae* (herrings) also occur in Asiatic waters and can ascend rivers for many hundred miles; the hilsa is a well-known Indian fish of this family.

Certain species are notably absent from Indian rivers. The *Salmonidae* are entirely absent from southern Asian waters. While trout are unknown in Indian rivers, they are found in streams flowing into the Caspian, beyond the watershed of the Indus.
