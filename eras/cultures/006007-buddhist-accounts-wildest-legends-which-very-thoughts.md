# Buddhist Legends and Romance Literature

The Buddhist tradition contains a variety of accounts and literary forms that utilize imaginative and spectacular descriptions to convey spiritual meaning. These range from the detailed legends surrounding the life of the Buddha to a specific genre of romance literature known as Avadana.

## Legends of the Buddha
Buddhist accounts of the life of Gotama include descriptions characterized by a high degree of poetry and imagination. One such instance is the description of a second struggle within Gotama's mind. The crisis of this event is surrounded by "the wildest legends," in which the thoughts passing through the mind of the Buddha are depicted as gorgeous descriptions of angels of light or darkness. While these exaggerations may appear absurd or childish to some, they are viewed by others as attempts to describe the indescribable, containing a depth of meaning for those who read between the lines.

Additionally, the legends of Mara are a subject of critical analysis in scholarly works, such as Windsisch's *Mara and Buddha* (1895).

## Avadana and Romance Literature
Avadana refers to a type of Buddhist romance literature. This genre is represented by numerous Sanskrit (Nepalese) collections. The primary examples of this literature include:
*   **Avadanasataka**: Also known as the *Century of Legends*.
*   **Divyavadana**: Known as *The Heavenly Legend*.

Although these works were written later than most canonical Buddhist books, they are held in veneration by the orthodox. In the context of Buddhism, Avadana occupies a position similar to that of the Puranas in Brahminism. The *Divyavadana* specifically serves as a collection of legends concerning important members of the order and the leading disciples of the Buddha over a period of three centuries. While the collection was put into its present form relatively late, it is noted to contain some very ancient fragments.

## Related Buddhist Texts and Doctrines
Beyond the legends, the history of Buddhism is documented in several other significant works. The *Netti Pakarana* (written in Pali) and the *Saddharma Pundarika* (written in Sanskrit) both date to the 2nd or 3rd century of the era. The *Netti Pakarana* discusses questions of importance to the Buddhist community and relies heavily on canonical works. The *Saddharma Pundarika* provides the earliest exposition of the later Mahayana doctrine. Later, the *Prajna Paramita* emerged as a treatise on the Mahayana system, which eventually replaced the original doctrines in India.

Other textual sources include the *Vinaya Texts*, the *Jataka*, and the *Samyutta*. One specific sermon mentioned in these texts is the *aditta-pariyaya*, which focuses on the lessons to be drawn from burning. The account of the Buddha's final journey is recorded in the *Mahaparinibbana Suttanta*.

## Philosophical Distinctions
The Buddhist "doctrine of the Noble (or Aryan) Path" presents a view of existence that differs from other Indian thought and European philosophy (such as that of Heracleitus). While other systems may attribute a "Being without Becoming" or an unchanging individuality to souls and gods, the Buddhist path considers such views to be erroneous. According to this doctrine, holding such a view would block the entrance to the Path.

## Influence and External Legends
The Buddhist emphasis on spectacular effect contributed to the spread of the religion in certain regions, such as Japan between 593 and 758. During this time, several female sovereigns aided the propagation of the faith. 

Tradition has woven Buddhist-style elements into local legends, such as the story of Empress Komyo. In one account, Komyo vowed to wash a thousand beggars; the final beggar appeared as a loathsome leper, and upon the completion of the task, the mendicant ascended to heaven with a glory of light radiating from his body. Another legend describes Komyo receiving a miniature golden image of the goddess of Mercy (Kwannon) in a dream, leading to the conception of a daughter who became Empress Koken. This narrative mirrors the Buddhist account of the Incarnation.
