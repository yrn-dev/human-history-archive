# The Pamirs and the Great Central Depression

The Pamirs and the associated regions of the great central depression constitute a critical geographical complex in Central Asia. This area is defined by a combination of high-altitude plateaus and vast, low-lying interior basins that have historically served as a boundary between the northern Siberian flats and the southern plains of India, China, and Siam.

## Geography and Topography
The Pamir region is situated at the north-western extremity of the elevated Tibeto-Himalayan mountain plateau, approximately at 39 deg. N and 73 deg. E. It possesses the characteristics of the highest regions of Tibet and is referred to by the Russians as "steppe." The Pamir plateau serves as a major hydrographic hub: the Oxus (Amu) flows west from this region, the Jaxartes (Syr) flows north through the Turki state of Khokand, and waters to the east flow past Kashgar toward the central desert of the Gobi.

The Pamirs are linked to a broader continental divide that stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and Elburz to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, eventually reaching the Bering Strait. To the west of Kashgar, the central depression is limited by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol, which forms the eastern face of the Pamir.

## The Great Central Depression
The great central depression extends laterally across Asia along the 40 deg. north parallel, beginning east of the Gobi desert and stretching through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. This depression, which is believed to have once been a vast inland sea, reaches from the foot of the Pamir plateau in the west through the Tarim desert to Lop Nor and the Gobi. 

The elevation of this depression between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the sources of the Hwang-ho generally does not exceed 2,000 ft. above sea level. Research indicates that in central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, the land may actually be below sea level. This region is characterized as a vast, almost waterless interior where local drainage is absorbed by swamps or deserts.

## Geological and Environmental Features
The region has been the subject of significant scientific investigation. Sven Hedin revealed the periodic shifting of the Lop Nor system, while both Hedin and Dr. M.A. Stein demonstrated that the sand-strewn wastes of the Turkestan desert once housed highly civilized centers of industry and Buddhist art.

In other areas, Russian scientists have investigated the depression westward of the Aral and Caspian basins. Evidence such as the discovery of shells 100 miles inland at altitudes of 140 to 280 ft. above the current Caspian level suggests that the Caspian Sea extended eastwards into Trans-Caspian territory during the post-Pleiocene age. This is further supported by the nature of the Kara-kum sands, which appear to be marine formations lacking the fluviatile deposits associated with the Oxus.

## Exploration and Mapping
Until the late 19th century, the highlands of the Pamirs and the central depression remained largely unknown to European geographers. Early pioneers included Robert Barkley Shaw and George Hayward, who arrived at Yarkand in 1868. In 1870, Sir Douglas Forsyth led a mission to Yarkand, during which Henry Trotter produced the first maps of Chinese Turkestan.

Further knowledge was gained through the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission (1884-1886), where Sir T.H. Holdich, Sir Adelbert Talbot, and Colonel St George Gore mapped the Oxus regions and Afghan Turkestan. During this period, Ney Elias crossed from China through Badakshan and the Pamirs, identifying the "Dragon Lake," known as Rangkul. Other notable explorations include:
* **Captain (Sir William) Lockhart:** Crossed the Hindu Kush into Wakhan.
* **Colonel Woodthorpe:** Verified the explorations of W.W. M'Nair regarding the conformation of the Hindu Kush.
* **Arthur Douglas Carey and Andrew Dalgleish (1885):** Added new data to the map of Asia by following the tracks of Prjevalsky.
* **Captain (Sir Francis) Younghusband (1886):** Crossed the Muztagh, the mountain barrier between Kashmir and China.

## Regional Classifications and Peoples
Geographers have identified several specific Pamir regions, including Pamir Alichur, Pamir Khurd ("Little"), Pamir Kalan ("Great"), Pamir Khargosi ("of the hare"), Pamir Sares, and Pamir Rang-kul. Additionally, the Kuen-lun (or Kwen-lun) mountain ranges extend from the Pamirs in the west to 113° E, separating the Tibetan plateau from the Takla-makan desert.

Historically, the regions stretching from the Danube and South Russia to the Pamir Plateau and the Tarim basin were inhabited by similar barbaric tribes. These peoples were largely nomadic, used Aryan languages, and were possibly Nordic in race. While they began mingling with Mongolian tribes in Central Asia, the Mongolian tribes were not yet prevalent in the region during the era of Alexander's wanderings. In the high peaks south-west of Kashgar, reports from the 1853 diary of a Mr. Gardiner describe the Keiaz tribe, who lived in caves, subsisted by hunting, and were said to be anthropophagous.
