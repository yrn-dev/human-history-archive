# The Caste System of India

The caste system in India is a complex social structure characterized by the fixation of class and the division of society into primary groups with specific functions and restrictions. While the system is often associated with the Brahmanic period of history—which concluded around 600 B.C. with the arrival of Sakya Muni—its origins and the justifications for its existence vary across different historical and religious texts.

## Primary Divisions and Functions
The social body was traditionally divided into four main castes, with a further group existing outside this structure. The first three castes are often referred to as the "twice born." These include:

*   **Brahmins:** The priests and teachers. Their assigned duties included reading the Vedas, teaching, sacrificing, assisting others in sacrifice, and giving or receiving alms.
*   **Kshatriyas:** The warriors. Their duties were to defend the people, sacrifice, give alms, and read the Vedas.
*   **Vaisyas:** A class comprising land-owners, money-lenders, merchants, and herdsmen.
*   **Sudras:** The fourth class. While Indian writers state their function was to serve the three "twice born" castes, other accounts, such as those by Yuan Chwang, describe them as tillers of the soil.

Outside of these four castes are the Pariahs. Over time, the primary divisions of the Kshatriyas and Vaisyas largely disappeared, while the Brahmins and Sudras subdivided into numerous exclusive minor castes, each requiring members to adhere to a specific group of associates and a definite way of living.

## Origins and Theoretical Justifications
Various contradictory accounts exist regarding the origin of caste. Some texts describe it as a late episode in creation, born from abstract principles, non-entity, the mortal Manu, or different parts of various gods. Other accounts suggest it was coeval with creation and existed in perfection during the Krita period before falling into sin.

The *Rigveda Samhita* contains the Purusha Sukta (the 90th Hymn of the 10th Book), which presents an allegory of the division of man: the Brahmana was his mouth, the Raganya (Kshatriya) his arms, the Vaisya his thighs, and the Sudra his feet. While some interpret this as a symbolic representation of social roles, others view it as a fanciful creation account.

Alternative theories suggest a racial origin, proposing that the first three castes were descendants of Vedic Aryan conquerors who established separations to prevent racial mixing with the conquered Sudras and Pariahs. In this view, Sudras were a previous wave of northern conquerors, and Pariahs were the original Dravidian inhabitants. Other perspectives suggest that the uniform conditions of the Ganges valley over many centuries simply stereotyped existing class differences.

## Social Regulations and Marriage
Caste is based on the social idea of assigning specific functions to certain classes and the religious idea of an "indelible stain" or impurity resting on certain individuals. This led to strict regulations regarding social intercourse, eating, drinking, and marriage.

Generally, a good marriage requires the parties to belong to the same caste but unconnected families. While women are generally prohibited from marrying beneath their own caste, the rules for men are slightly more flexible. For example, Manu permits the son of a Brahman and a Sudra mother to raise his family to the highest caste by the seventh generation. The three higher castes were permitted to intermarry with the caste immediately below them, though the resulting offspring typically took the lower caste or formed a new class.

A Sudra is not required to marry within his own caste or sect. For instance, a 1871 judicial decision by the privy council ruled that marriage between a Sudra of the Malavar class (land-owners) and a woman of the Vellala class of Sudras was lawful.

## The Role of the Brahmins
The Brahmins acted as the custodians of tradition and the sole teachers of the people, which helped sustain the caste system. Unlike many Western priesthoods, the Brahmins are an exclusive and reproductive class that accepts no recruits from other social strata.

Historically, the Brahmans asserted their supremacy through both religious and political means. While early Vedic periods saw non-Brahmans acting as priests and some Brahmans receiving instruction from Kshatriyas, later texts like the Puranas and the Laws of Manu emphasize the submission of Kshatriyas to Brahmans. Some historians suggest that ancient myths were manufactured by Brahmans to demonstrate their superiority in birth and divine favor.

## Terminology and the "Aryan" Concept
The term "Aryan" (Sanskrit *arya-*, Zend *airya-*, Old Persian *ariya-*) was used as a national name in India, Persia, and Bactria. Scholars use "Indo-Aryan" to describe the branch of Eastern Indo-Europeans who settled in India, distinguishing them from the Iranians. Within Sanskrit, a distinction is often made between *arya* (the origin of "Aryan") and *arya*, the latter frequently appearing in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet for deities.
