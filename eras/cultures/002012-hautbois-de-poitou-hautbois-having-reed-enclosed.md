# Hautbois de Poitou

The Hautbois de Poitou is a member of the hautbois family of instruments. Its primary distinguishing structural characteristic is that its reed is enclosed within an air-chamber, a design similar to the arrangement of reeds found in bag-pipes.

## Relationship with the Cornemuse
The Hautbois de Poitou was played specifically in concert with a particular variety of *cornemuse* (bag-pipe). This specific type of cornemuse differed from the solo or shepherd's variety in several key ways:

*   **Drone Configuration:** Unlike the shepherd's cornemuse, which featured both a large drone (*gros bourdon*) and a small drone (*petit bourdon*), the cornemuse played with the Hautbois de Poitou possessed only one drone. This drone could be tuned by drawing out the joint to alter its length.
*   **Reed Type:** The drone of this instrument utilized a double reed, similar to that of the chaunter, rather than the beating-reed used in the other variety of cornemuse.
*   **Chaunter Details:** The chaunter of this instrument featured eight holes. The lowest of these holes was covered by a key, which was enclosed within a perforated box.

## Historical Context and Usage
Records indicate the use of these instruments in the middle of the 14th century; deeds from the hand of King John exist creating charges for musettes de Poitou and four hautbois. The *musettes de Poitou* mentioned in these records are likely the same *cornemuses* used in concert with the Hautbois de Poitou.

The broader family of instruments to which the hautbois belongs underwent various evolutions during the 17th century. For example, the *haute-contre de hautbois* (also known as the cor anglais) was developed from the alto Pommer. In France, where the hautbois held a significant role in court music, these instruments were often provided with keys. By the end of the 17th century, the musicians of the *Grande Ecurie du Roi* included two players of the *haute-contre de hautbois*.

## Technical Comparisons
The design of the Hautbois de Poitou's enclosed reed aligns it with other bag-pipe-related instruments described by Pere Marin Mersenne. In contrast, the standard shepherd's cornemuse featured a chaunter with a double reed that was hidden within the stock. While the chaunter of the shepherd's cornemuse could be removed and played separately—allowing the player to increase the compass from C to C' up to E by overblowing—the Hautbois de Poitou is specifically noted for its air-chamber enclosure.
