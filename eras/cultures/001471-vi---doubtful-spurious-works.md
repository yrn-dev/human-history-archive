# Doubtful and Spurious Works

In the study of historical archives, the identification of spurious or doubtful works—texts falsely attributed to a specific author or containing forged elements—is a recurring challenge. This issue spans various disciplines, including classical oratory, music, poetry, and diplomatic history.

## Classical Oratory and Literature

The works of several ancient Greek figures have been subject to scrutiny regarding their authenticity. In the case of Demosthenes, more than half of the sixty-one extant speeches under his name are considered probably or certainly spurious. Among the deliberative speeches, those identified as spurious include *On Halonnesus* (attributed to Hegesippus), *On the Treaty with Alexander*, and the *Fourth Philippic*. Additionally, modern scholars have questioned the authenticity of *Philip's Letter*, the *Answer to Philip's Letter*, and *On the Assessment*. In forensic speeches, *In Theocrinem* and the two speeches *In Aristogitona* are regarded as spurious, with the latter being classified as rhetorical forgeries.

Similarly, the works of Lucian include more than 80 pieces, of which approximately 20 are of doubtful authorship or spurious. Lucian's writings center on the impossibility and absurdity of the various rival schools of pagan Greek philosophy, leading him to the conclusion that truth does not exist.

Other instances of textual doubt appear in the works of Andokides. The rhetor Dionysius pronounced the oration *De Pace* to be spurious, though subsequent study suggests it may be genuine despite containing erroneous factual allegations regarding times before the battle of Ægospotami. However, the fourth oration ascribed to Andokides, *Against Alkibiades*, is considered spurious. In the works of Lysias, the *Complaint of Slanders* is noted as certainly spurious. Furthermore, the *erotikos* found in Plato's *Phaedrus* has been regarded as Plato's own work, but some doubt this due to the closeness of the criticism applied to the text and the dialogue's preparation for a "verbally exact" recital. Conversely, a letter from Plato to Perdikkas, which recommends and praises Euphræus, is considered likely genuine, as there is nothing to prove it spurious.

## Poetry and Epic Works

The *Canzoniere* of Dante contains a mixture of genuine and spurious poems. While many canzoni, ballate, and sonnetti bear his name, some actually belong to Dante de Maiano. Others attributed to Aldighiero are referable to Dante's grandsons or his sons, Pietro and Jacopo, while some are ascribed to predecessors and contemporaries such as Cino da Pistoia. One specific collection by Fraticelli includes seventy-eight genuine poems, eight doubtful ones, and fifty-four spurious ones.

In Persian literature, the epic poem by Ferdusi consists of sixty thousand rhymed couplets. However, Captain Macan noted that he never encountered a manuscript containing more than 56,685 couplets, a figure that includes episodes and passages deemed spurious or doubtful.

## Musical Arrangements

The archive records instances where works attributed to one composer are actually arrangements or collaborations. Certain arrangements of 16 concertos generally described as being by Vivaldi actually include several by other composers. Some of these contain original additions, such as large cadenzas and new slow movements. Additionally, Bach is credited with providing finishing touches to cantatas by his uncle, Johann Ludwig Bach, including a complete "Christe eleison" inserted into a Kyrie.

## Forgeries and Spurious Imprints

Beyond authorship, the archive notes the existence of "spurious imprints" and piracies. In 1499, a Brescia printer used the fake imprint "Florentiae" for an edition of Politian to avoid a privilege granted to Aldus. During the 16th century, some English controversial books claimed to be printed in German towns or at the sign of S. Peter in Rome. 

Modern forgeries include type-facsimile imitations of high-value short pieces by A.C. Swinburne, George Eliot, and Tennyson, as well as the letter of Columbus announcing the discovery of the New World. These are often detected by the thickening of letters caused by photographic reproduction.

## Diplomatic and Historical Documents

The authenticity of certain historical patents and treaties has also been debated. A patent from Mahomet in favor of the Christians, published by Sionita in 1630, was viewed with skepticism by Hottinger and Mosheim, the latter inclining to believe it spurious. While Abulpharagius quotes a treaty between the "impostor" and the Nestorian patriarch, the validity of such documents remains a point of historical contention.
