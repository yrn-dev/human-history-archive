# Bahrein Islands

The Bahrein Islands are a group of islands located in the Persian Gulf, situated approximately 20 miles east of the coast of El Hasa and slightly south of the port of El Katif. El Katif, if correctly identified as the ancient Gerrha, has been historically celebrated as the starting point for caravans across Arabia and a mart for Indian trade.

## Geography and Composition
The largest island in the group is Bahrein, which measures approximately 27 miles from north to south and 10 miles in width. The landscape of Bahrein is characterized as a low, flat space of sandy waste, though it contains palm groves and cultivated oases of great beauty and luxuriance. In the center of the island rises Jebel Dukhan (the "mountain of the mist"), a rocky hill reaching a height of 400 feet.

The other islands in the group are of coral formation. Moharek is the second largest island; it is curved in shape, measuring about 5 miles long by 1/2 mile in breadth, and is located 1 mile north of Bahrein. Other islands in the group include Sitrah (4 miles long), Nebbi, Saleh, Sayeh, Khasifeh, and Arad (3/4 mile long). Of these smaller islands, only Arad has retained its classical name.

## Environment and Resources
The climate of the islands is humid and mild, though it is considered rather unhealthy. The soil is generally fertile, producing fruits—specifically high-quality citrons—as well as pot herbs and rice. Water is abundant. On Moharek, the water supply is likely unique, as it comes from springs that burst through beds below sea level with enough force to remain fresh despite the surrounding salt water.

The islanders rely on sea-weed and fish as their staple food, with various kinds of fish being abundant off the coast and inexpensive in markets. The surrounding sea is very shallow, which restricts access to native craft, and the harbor is closely enclosed by reefs.

## Population and Economy
The inhabitants are a mixed race of Persian, Omanite, and Arab blood. Physically, they are small and slender, and they are recognized in the ports of the Persian Gulf for their intelligence, activity, and industrial and commercial abilities. The islands feature some fifty villages, most of which are walled for protection; each village possesses its own cultivation and date groves.

Bahrein has served as the center for the pearl fishing industry in the Persian Gulf. Currently, about 400 boats are employed in these fisheries, and each boat pays a tax to the Sheik. The annual value of pearl exports from Linja is estimated between L30,000 and L35,000.

The capital town is Manameh, a narrow, long, and straggling town with approximately 8,000 inhabitants, most of whom belong to the Wahabi sect. Manameh is located at the northernmost point of Bahrein, overlooking the narrow strait that separates it from Moharek.

## History and Governance
There is little doubt that the Puni, or Phoenicians, emigrated northwards to the Mediterranean from these islands. In 1507, the Portuguese took possession of the islands, but they were driven out of their settlements in the area by Shah Abbas in 1622. Following this, the islands were a point of contention between Arabs and Persians until the Arabian tribe of the Athubis became their masters in 1784.

The current ruling race is the family of El Kalifa, and the present Sheik of Bahrein resides primarily at Moharek. This family was driven from the mainland, where they once held great possessions, by the Turks around 1850. In 1867, the Persians threatened Bahrein, and the Turks took control of it in 1875. In both instances, British interference successfully maintained Arab rule. The Bahrein islands currently exist under British protection.
