# Beliefs Regarding Wine and Possession

## The Concept of Spirits in Wine
In various historical and cultural contexts, the consumption of wine has been linked to the intake of souls or spirits. Some beliefs suggest that a soul or spirit within wine is what inspires those who become intoxicated. For example, old Egyptian kings avoided wine in libations and at the table based on the belief that vines grew from the rotting bodies of rebels who had fought against the gods. In this view, wine was considered the blood of these rebels; drinking it meant imbibing their souls, and the resulting frenzy of intoxication was interpreted as possession by those spirits.

## Medieval Beliefs and the Cardiac Demon
Similar concepts regarding possession and wine were held by other groups. Medieval Jews believed that a "cardiac demon" exists in wine, which takes possession of men who are drunken. This perspective aligns with a similar superstition that forms the basis of the Mahommedan prohibition against drinking wine.

## Parallel Practices of Abstention
The avoidance of wine is seen as parallel to the abstention from eating flesh. This pattern of avoidance is found among several groups, including the Nazirites, Rechabites, Pythagoreans, and Arab dervishes. 

The avoidance of flesh diets was often driven by the fear of absorbing the irrational soul of an animal, which was believed to reside especially in the blood. This led to rules against eating strangled meats, except during sacramental meals where the god inherent in the animal was partaken of. Additionally, Porphyry noted a widespread concern that human souls transmigrated into animals, meaning that eating meat could be akin to cannibalism. While some meat-eaters argued that killing animals was a pious duty to release these imprisoned human souls, others, such as the "lover of wisdom" who served as a priest of the universal God, abstained from animals to avoid polluting demons and inferior souls.

## General Demonology and Possession
In broader demonological contexts, demons are classified as spirits that enter into relations with humans. These may include ghosts, malevolent revenants, or human souls regarded as familiars. Possession is a central theme in these beliefs, where spirits may enter a victim's body to dominate the mind and body, inflict specific diseases, or cause various pains. 

Different cultures identified specific demons for different functions. For instance, the Malays recognized a demon for every known disease, as well as demons of wild-beast tracks and waterfalls. They also believed in a baboon demon that possessed dancers to enable wonderful feats of climbing. In other traditions, such as those in the Malay Peninsula, the blood of a murdered man could be used to secure a demon called a polong, which appeared as a diminutive female figure.
