# The Tibetan Plateau and the Kuen-lun System

The Tibetan plateau is a high-altitude region of Asia characterized by a distinct "ridge and furrow" arrangement of mountain ranges. Its northern edge is defined by a series of border-ridges, most notably the Kuen-lun Mountains, which serve as a tectonic "backbone" for the continent. These mountains are significantly older than the Tian-shan to the north or the Himalayas to the south.

## The Kuen-lun Mountain System
The Kuen-lun Mountains extend in a wavy line for nearly 2,500 miles from east to west. The system varies in breadth and elevation across its length; in the west, lateral compression has squeezed the ranges into a breadth of 150–200 miles, forcing summits to higher altitudes. In the east, the system spreads to a breadth of approximately 600 miles, resulting in ranges that are flatter, lower, and less folded.

While the crests of the component ranges reach altitudes of 21,500 to 22,000 feet, they generally lack the towering individual peaks found in the Himalayas, maintaining a more uniform altitude relative to the valleys that separate them. A defining characteristic of the plateau's northern edge is the "twinning" of its outermost border-ranges and intermont lake-basins.

## Western Kuen-lun and the Pamirs
In the west, the Pamir highlands are separated from the East Turkestan lowlands by a double border-ridge consisting of the Muztagh (or Kashgar) range and the Sarik-kol range. The eastern foot of this ridge descends into the Tarim basin (4,000–4,500 feet), while the western foot rises onto the Pamirs at 10,500 to 13,000 feet. Individual peaks in this region, such as Muztagh-ata, reach 25,780 feet.

East of the Yarkand meridian (77° E.), this double border-ridge continues as a succession of twin ranges. The northerly range includes the Keriya, Khotan, and Kilian (or Kiliang) Mountains, while the southerly range comprises the Ullugh-tagh, Sughet, and Raskem (or Raskan) Mountains. These ranges decrease in altitude from west to east, though they still reach elevations of 19,000 feet, with some peaks ascending an additional 2,000–2,500 feet. High-altitude passes characterize this rugged region, such as the Sughet-davan at 17,825 feet.

## The Astin-tagh and Northern Border Ranges
The Astin-tagh forms the outermost border-ridge on the north of the Tibetan plateau. Rather than a single chain, it consists of two parallel main ranges, expanding to three or four parallel ranges in the east and northeast of Tsaidam. It is considered by several judges to be a subsidiary branch of the main Kuen-lun range.

The Astin-tagh is characterized by severely weathered hard rock, forming rugged, pinnacled crests and eccentric masses. This contrasts with the rounded ranges found further onto the Tibetan plateau. The range's elevation decreases toward the east; passes in the Lower Astin-tagh range from 10,150 to 10,700 feet, while those in the Upper Astin-tagh range from 11,770 to 15,680 feet (Tash-davan).

## Central and Eastern Ranges
South of the Astin-tagh, the mountains rise in a series of steps or terraces. From the deserts of the Gobi and Takla-makan, each successive range is higher than the one to its north. This progression includes the Lower Astin-tagh, the Upper Astin-tagh, the Akato-tagh, the Chimen-tagh, the Ara-tagh, and the Kalta-alaghan, culminating in the Arka-tagh ranges, which maintain general altitudes between 16,470 and 17,260 feet.

The Kalta-alaghan is the culminating range of this section, featuring snow-clad peaks and high passes, such as the Avraz-davan at 15,700 feet. Unlike most northern Tibetan ranges, the Kalta-alaghan increases in elevation toward the east, where it merges with the ranges bordering Tsaidam.

To the southwest, the Nan-shan mountains consist of irregular chains separated by broad plains and lakes. These plains differ slightly in altitude from Tsaidam (8,800–9,000 feet). A higher terrace (12,000–13,500 feet) contains Ritter's range, which features extensive snow-fields and passes reaching 15,990 feet, and the Humboldt (or Ama-surgu) range.

## Koko-nor
Located in the extreme northeast of Tibet at an altitude of 9,975 feet, Koko-nor (also known as Kuku-nor or Tsing-hai) is a salt lake measuring 66 by 40 miles. It is situated between the Nan-shan Mountains to the north and the southern Koko-nor range (10,000 feet) to the south. The lake contains several islands, one of which houses a Lamaist (Buddhist) monastery. The lake is inhabited by fish and is frequently frozen for three months during the winter. The surrounding shores are inhabited by Tangut nomads.
