# BONZE

## Definition and Etymology
The term "bonze" is the European designation for members of Buddhist religious orders in China and Japan. It is derived from the Japanese word *bonzo*, which is likely a mispronunciation of the Chinese term *fan sung*, meaning "religious person." In a broader and looser application, the word is used to refer to all Buddhist priests within these two countries and their neighboring regions.

## Early Propagation of Buddhism in Japan
The arrival of Buddhist practitioners from China played a significant role in the introduction of the faith to Japan. In 522, a Chinese bonze named Shiba Tachito arrived in Japan and established a temple on the Sakata plain in Yamato. He enshrined an image of Buddha and attempted to propagate the faith. Tradition suggests that Shiba Tachito came from Liang, where the first Emperor of the Liang dynasty, Wu, was working to popularize the Indian creed. It is probable that he traveled via the Korean peninsula, though the specific facts remain obscure. This initial effort produced no sensible impression on the Japanese population, who showed no disposition to worship a foreign god at the time.

Later, at the close of the sixth century, a Chinese bonze at the monastery of Tientai taught a doctrine involving the "Buddhas of Contemplation" and a creed resembling a Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This teaching was brought to Japan two hundred years later by Dengyo, who established the Tendai sect on Mount Hiei near Kyoto. Dengyo adapted these Chinese meditative and disciplinary methods to fit pre-existing sects, creating a system of Japanese education. The Tendai system became the parent of many large sects (excluding the Shingon) and proposed that the native Shinto gods were manifestations of mystical beings, thereby embodying Shinto into a more universal system.

## Political and Intelligence Roles
Beyond religious duties, bonzes were sometimes utilized for political and strategic purposes. During the Kamakura period, the Bakufu regents—Yasutoki, Tokiyori, and Tokimune—were earnest disciples of Buddhism who built temples and assigned them to Chinese priests from the Sung and Yuan eras. While these priests influenced the art-canons and literature of the capital, the regents also used Zen-shu bonzes as channels for obtaining political information about China.

For example, Tokimune studied under Doryu (originally the Chinese priest Tao Lung). Following Doryu's death in 1278, Tokimune invited the famous bonze Chu Yuan (known in Japan as Sogen) from China. Sogen, who had narrowly escaped massacre by the Mongols while officiating at the temple of Nengjen in Wenchow, may have served as a medium of information between Kamakura and China. The temple Yengaku-ji was later erected for Sogen's ministrations.

## Contributions to Art and Culture
Chinese bonzes contributed significantly to the physical and aesthetic landscape of Japan. Tradition credits a Chinese bonze named Nei-issan (or Ichinei) with the planning of the first landscape garden in Japan. Arriving in Kyushu as a delegate from Kublai Khan under the name I Ning, he was initially banished to Izu before being invited to Kamakura by the Bakufu. He was assigned the temple Kencho-ji for his residence, where he designed a landscape garden that continues to be regarded as a model.

## Historical Interactions and Conflicts
The records of the archive note various interactions involving bonzes across different eras:
* **Diplomacy and Espionage:** In one instance, a person named Taitsu employed a Buddhist priest, but the bonze was imprisoned after his true character was detected.
* **Administrative Roles:** Buddhist priests, along with Shinto priests, were involved in the classification of the census during the Daika and Daiho periods.
* **Religious Conflict:** In 1587, during the invasion of Kyushu, the leader Hideyoshi questioned the vice-provincial of the Jesuits, Coelho. Among the five questions propounded by Hideyoshi was an inquiry as to why the Jesuits and their fellow propagandists had persecuted the bonzes and induced their disciples to overthrow temples. Coelho responded that the missionaries had never persecuted bonzes nor incited violence in their propagandism.
