# Domestic Fowl in Bantu Linguistic History

The study of the domestic fowl serves as a primary piece of linguistic evidence used to determine the history and migration patterns of the Bantu people. Because historical records are nearly absent, researchers rely on linguistic research, specifically the study of existing dialects and widely-spread roots common to the majority of Bantu languages, to reconstruct their past.

## Linguistic Evidence and the Root *Kuku*
A significant root common to a large proportion of Bantu languages is the term for the domestic fowl: *kuku* (appearing in various forms such as *nkuku*, *ngoko*, *nsusu*, *nguku*, and *nku*). This root recurs throughout Bantu Africa, with rare exceptions that are not found among the purest Bantu tribes. 

The specific variations of this word are found across diverse regions:
* **Zanzibar (Ki-Swahili):** *kuku*
* **Zulu:** *inkuku*
* **Uganda and the Victoria Nyanza (Luganda and others):** *enkoko*
* **Victoria Nile (Runyoro):** *enkoko*
* **Zambezi, Lakes Nyasa and Tanganyika, and most South African dialects:** *nkuku*
* **Congo:** *nkogo* and *nsusu*
* **Cameroon languages:** *lokoko*, *ngoko*, and *ngok*

Notably, among Negro races that do not speak Bantu languages, the name for the fowl is entirely different, even when those groups live in close proximity to the Bantu. The root *kuku* is believed to be remotely derived from the cry of the bird and bears a marked resemblance to several Persian words for "fowl."

## Migration and Chronology
The presence of the *kuku* root suggests that the Bantu people had the domestic fowl well established among them before they left their original home in the heart of Africa. This allows researchers to fix an approximate date for the southern exodus of the Bantu, estimated at not more than 2,000 years ago.

This dating is based on the known timeline of the bird's arrival in Africa. The domestic fowl first reached Africa through Egypt during the Persian occupation, which occurred no earlier than 500 to 400 B.C. It is estimated that it would have taken at least two hundred years for the bird to spread from people to people and tribe to tribe up the Nile valley before reaching the equatorial regions of Africa.

## Origins of the Domestic Fowl
The domestic fowl originated as an Indian jungle fowl. Up until approximately 1300 B.C., fowls were only jungle denizens in Burmah and India. It is probable that they were first domesticated in Burmah. Records indicate they reached China around 1100 B.C. and arrived in Greece via Persia before the time of Socrates.

In the context of early human civilization, the domestic fowl was a late addition to human cuisine. For example, the hen is not mentioned by Homer nor in the Old Testament, though an allusion to an egg appears in Job vi, 6. In the New Testament, the crowing of a cock is mentioned in the account of Peter's desertion of the Master.

## Comparative Biology and Terminology
In modern English usage, the term "fowl" is generally confined to domestic poultry, except when used in terms like "water-fowl" or "wild-fowl." This differs from the older Teutonic use of "fowl" as a generic name for feathered vertebrates, which has since been replaced by the word "bird."

Biologically, the domestic fowl is used as a reference for skeletal study. Its axial skeleton is divided into cervical vertebrae (between the skull and the first vertebra connected to the sternum), dorsals, pelvic vertebrae (fused with the iliac portion of the pelvis), and caudal vertebrae. In terms of size, the domestic fowl is used as a comparison for the kiwis of New Zealand, which are approximately the size of a large domestic fowl.
