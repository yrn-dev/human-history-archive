# Ridicule and Reflection in Human History

The act of laughing at others, whether as a form of mockery, a reaction to perceived folly, or a reflection on one's own life, appears across various historical and sacred records. These instances range from the ridicule of prophets and believers to the personal regrets of individuals facing the end of their lives.

## Mockery of the Believers and Prophets

Sacred texts record multiple instances where those who believed in divine revelation were met with laughter and derision from those who did not. In the account of the construction of the ark, the chiefs of the people laughed at the builder as they passed by him. In response, the builder stated that if they laughed at him now, he and his followers would likewise laugh at them. He warned that they would eventually discover upon whom a lasting doom and a disgracing chastisement would descend. Following this, the command came for the fountains of the earth to gush forth, and the builder was instructed to embark with his family—excluding those already judged—the Believers, and two of every kind of animal, male and female.

Similarly, records indicate that those guilty in sin used to laugh at believers. When passing them, the guilty would wink at one another in mockery and return to their own followers exulting and jesting, claiming that the believers were truly astray or in error. However, the texts teach that these mockers were not sent as guardians over the believers. It is presented that on the Day of Judgment, the roles are reversed: the Believers will laugh at the Unbelievers while gazing from thrones of dignity.

In another instance, when Moses commanded his people to sacrifice a heifer, they questioned if he was making a laughing-stock of them, to which he replied that he sought safety from Allah from being an ignorant fool.

## Laughter as a Reaction to Folly and War

Historical accounts describe laughter as a response to the absurdity of human behavior. In the records of Pompeius, a "ludicrous" event occurred in Libya. After some soldiers discovered a treasure, the rest of the army became convinced the area was full of hidden Carthaginian money. For many days, Pompeius could not lead his troops because thousands of soldiers were busy digging and turning up the ground. Pompeius spent this time laughing and watching the soldiers until they eventually grew tired and admitted their folly.

In the context of modern warfare, laughter is recorded as a reaction to the tension of the firing line. Soldiers, finding the environment unexpectedly quiet, laughed at the contrast between their months of training and the stillness of the fog-covered line, while noting that it would not be so quiet once they reached the land of the Boches.

## Personal Reflection and the Passage of Time

Laughter is also presented as a tool for self-reflection and a marker of regret. The life of Buckingham is described as one of incompleteness and waste of opportunities. In his younger, "gay moments," he wrote of the "wanton houres" that, as they passed, would turn back and laugh at him. This poetic imagery of time mocking the individual contrasts with his final recorded words before death, in which he lamented being a "prodigal" of time, which he identified as the most valuable of all possessions.
