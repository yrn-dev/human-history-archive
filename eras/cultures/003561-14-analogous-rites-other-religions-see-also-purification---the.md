# Analogous Rites in Other Religions

The study of religious rites reveals numerous parallels across different cultures and eras, particularly regarding purification, initiation, and the conceptualization of spiritual rebirth. These analogous practices often involve the use of physical elements—such as water, fire, and air—to achieve a state of ritual purity or to mark a transition in a person's spiritual status.

## Purification and Lustration
Purification rites are found across a wide spectrum of global religions, often predating formal theology. In the Babylonian-Assyrian religion, as in all religions, practices such as ritualistic purification, the sprinkling of water, animal sacrifice, and libations represent a religious layer older than theoretical theology.

Among the Greeks and Romans, these methods are categorized under the term "lustration." Greek tradition distinguishes between *katharsis* (the need for human nature to purify itself from guilt before associating with men or communing with God) and *hilasmos* (the voluntary expiation of guilt through revealed processes to avoid punishment). In Hellenic religion, the most significant guilt is associated with murder, though impurity also arises from touching a dead body, sexual intercourse, or witnessing a divine sign or prodigy. Roman purification often focused on expiating guilt following great disasters.

Methods of lustration include:
*   **Water:** Worshippers dipped their hands in holy water (*perirrhanterion* or *aqua lustralis*) before entering temples. Bathing was common before sacrifices, with salt-water considered more efficacious than fresh water. For example, the *mystae* of the Eleusinian mysteries bathed in the sea.
*   **Fire and Air:** Fire, sulphur, and torches served as powerful purifying agents. Purification by air was most frequent in Dionysiac mysteries.
*   **Earth and Nature:** Ceremonies also employed earth or branches of sacred trees, particularly the laurel.

In Japan, the "great purification" known as *Oharai* is performed twice annually, on the last day of the sixth and twelfth months.

## Initiation and Baptismal Rites
Baptismal rites, characterized by the use of water for initiation or cleansing, appear in several religious contexts. Early Christian Fathers noted that "the devil too had his sacraments," observing that *mystae* in Mithraic, Isiac, and Eleusinian rites used baptism during initiation. However, records indicate that baptism was imposed on proselytes and existed within Judaism long before the advent of Jesus.

Specific parallels exist between different cultural rites:
*   **Malay Rites:** A rite of purification by water common among the Malays involves turning to the west three times to renounce the Evil One, then turning to the east. This is exactly paralleled in certain Christian ceremonies.
*   **Jewish Traditions:** The concept of spiritual rebirth was familiar to the Jews and is mentioned by Philo of Alexandria. The Talmud states that a proselyte resembles a child and must bathe in the name of God. Additionally, 2 Kings v. 10 describes the Jordan River as a cleansing medium, and the cure of Naaman is viewed as a pre-figurement of Christian baptism.

## Concepts of Spiritual Rebirth and Regeneration
The idea of spiritual rebirth is a recurring theme in ancient religions. Beyond the baptismal contexts, other traditions describe an internal spiritual awakening. Jerome recorded a teaching from the Rabbi Baraciba stating that the "inner man" who rises within a person at the fourteenth year after puberty (at age 29) is superior to the man born from a mother's womb.

In a non-religious context, the philosopher Sokratês utilized a process described by Plato, Bacon, and Herschel as the "purification of the intellect." This *elenchus* served as an indispensable precursor to the attainment of positive science.

## Funeral Rites and Pollution
Analogous rites also extend to the treatment of the dead. Across various races—including the Egyptians, Babylonians, Hindus, Persians, Greeks, and Romans—funeral rites are often underpinned by the concept of pollution or taboo attaching to a corpse. These ceremonies are used to manage the relations between the living and the dead, reflecting shared human fears and hopes regarding the future of the deceased.
