# Study: Its Principles, Methods and History

The study of the Hebrew language and the Old Testament has evolved from a reliance on traditional texts and translations to the application of systematic critical methods and comparative linguistics. This progression has been shaped by the contributions of both Jewish and Christian scholars, as well as the development of philological tools.

## History of Hebrew Language Study
For a significant period, the study of the Old Testament was hindered by a lack of a critical text. Jewish study relied exclusively on an official Hebrew text that was scrupulously preserved after being fixed, likely in the 2nd century A.D., though it contained obvious corruptions. Conversely, Christian scholars—and Jewish Alexandrian scholars—were largely dependent on translations from the 5th to the 15th centuries. While the Greek Septuagint version may have reflected an earlier form of the Hebrew text than the one preserved by Jewish tradition, the limitations of the translators often kept Christian scholars further from the original meaning.

Early efforts in textual criticism were limited. Origen's *Hexapla* attempted to compare the Hebrew text, the Septuagint, and later Greek versions, though the work was only fragmentarily preserved. 

A shift occurred during the Renaissance as knowledge of the original languages grew. In 1504, Konrad Pellikan published a brief introduction to Hebrew, and in 1506, Johann Reuchlin published *De Rudimentis Hebraicis*, which included a Hebrew grammar and lexicon. The Complutensian Polyglott, printed starting in 1514 and published in 1522, provided the first Christian edition of the Hebrew text, though its editors placed the Vulgate at the center of the page to signify the Roman Church's position between the Greek Church and the synagogue. This accessibility allowed 16th-century translations, such as those by Tyndale (1530) and Luther (1523), to be made directly from the Hebrew.

## Evolution of Critical Methods
Until the mid-17th century, Christian scholars were dominated by Jewish traditions and methods. However, the 17th and 18th centuries saw the emergence of comparative linguistic study through the exploration of other Semitic languages. This was further systematized in the 19th century with the study of Assyrian and the work of scholars such as Driver, Stade, Noeldeke, Renan, Olshausen, Ewald, and Gesenius.

Biblical criticism eventually adopted critical methods used for other ancient literatures. For example, the textual criticism of classical literatures preceded the application of Higher Criticism to the Old Testament; Bentley's *Phalaris* (1699) and Niebuhr's *History of Rome* (1811) preceded the systematic Higher Criticism of the Bible and Ewald's *History of Israel* (1843-1859).

## Notable Scholars and Divergent Perspectives
The application of these methods led to significant theological and scholarly debates in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

**Charles Augustus Briggs (1841– )** was an American theologian and professor at Union Theological Seminary. He was tried for heresy in 1892 and suspended in 1893 due to views expressed in his inaugural address. His claims included that Moses was not the author of the Pentateuch, that Isaiah did not write half of the book bearing his name, and that many Old Testament predictions had been reversed by history. He also argued that reason and the Church could enlighten men apart from Holy Scripture and that errors existed in the original text of the Holy Scripture.

**William Henry Green (1825–1900)**, a professor at Princeton Theological Seminary, represented the orthodox wing of the American Presbyterian church. He employed a "conservative Higher Criticism" to defend traditional views. His works include *The Unity of the Book of Genesis* (1895) and *The Pentateuch Vindicated from the Aspersions of Bishop Colenso* (1863). Green also authored a *Grammar of the Hebrew Language* (1861, revised 1888), which improved upon the methods of Nordheimer, Ewald, Roediger, and Gesenius.

Other figures of note include Johann Philipp Baratier (1721–1740), a German prodigy who could translate the Hebrew Bible by age eight and translated Tudela's *Itinerarium* by age eleven.

## Hebrew Poetry and Literature
The Hebrews, a Semitic people in the hills behind the Philistine and Phœnician coasts, produced the Hebrew Bible, a collection of prophetic works, poems, histories, and books of wisdom.

One notable figure in Hebrew poetry was Immanuel ben Solomon (c. 1265–c. 1330), a Roman poet and friend of Dante. Immanuel's work reflects the influence of Dante and employs symbolical methods in his commentaries on Job, Psalms, and Proverbs. He is best known for *Mehabberoth*, a collection of 27 satires on Jewish life. His most popular work is a section titled *Hell and Paradise* (*ha-Tophet veha-Eden*), in which a figure named Daniel (possibly identified with Dante) guides the poet through realms of bliss and torture.
