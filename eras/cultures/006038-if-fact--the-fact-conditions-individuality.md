# Individuality and Its Conditions

The concept of individuality is examined across various disciplines in the archive, ranging from biological processes and evolutionary theory to philosophical and religious interpretations of the human condition.

## Biological and Evolutionary Perspectives
In the ordinary course of nature, living matter originates from pre-existing living matter. An individual living body is formed when a portion of pre-existing matter is detached and acquires an independent existence. This new form inherits the characters of its predecessor, including the ability to propagate itself through offshoots. Throughout its existence, an individual body undergoes continual modifications in size and form, constantly changing its substance until it eventually ceases to live and is resolved into highly oxidated compounds of its elements. These biological activities are conditioned by a need for heat within a limited temperature range and a dependence on moisture.

From an evolutionary standpoint, individuality is characterized by difference. Offspring both resemble and differ from their parents. Natural Selection, also referred to as the "Survival of the Fittest," is a deduction based on these facts of reproduction and individual difference. Within any species, some individuals are sturdier, stronger, or better suited to succeed in life than others. Consequently, those better equipped are more likely to survive, grow, and reproduce, while the weaker individuals—who are less able to fight enemies or obtain food—generally go under. If the conditions under which a species lives change, those individuals whose differences make them better adapted to the new conditions will reproduce more abundantly, causing the average of the species to change in a favorable direction over generations.

## Philosophical and Religious Interpretations
Different traditions offer varying views on the implications of being an individual. In Buddhist teaching, the conditions necessary to create an individual are the same conditions that necessarily give rise to sorrow. This is linked to the *Upadana Skandhas*, the five groups of constituent parts of every individual. The archive notes that as soon as an individual becomes separate, decay and disease begin to act upon it. Individuality is seen as involving limitation, which leads to ignorance, the source of sorrow. This results in unsatisfied craving, separation from the pleasant, and union with the unpleasant. Emancipation from this pain is found in a habit of mind—specifically, being free from the craving that originates these pains. This state of emancipation of the heart and mind is known as Arahatship.

Furthermore, while the Buddha did not acknowledge a soul, a connection between lives was found in the influence that a desire felt in a previous life exerts upon a subsequent one. This suggests that cravings in one life possess a power over future existence.

Other perspectives suggest that individuality finds its true meaning through a union with the divine. In this view, because God is One, every life has a unique place in a divine harmony. True individuality is not expressed in the present form of consciousness—which is seen as a fragment or a glimpse—but is attained in a subsequent life.

## Individuality in Social and Political Contexts
The relationship between the individual and the state is also a subject of historical analysis. In the context of ancient Rome, the preservation of the kind and the promise of happiness required a common political idea. This involved the state being viewed as the personal possession of each individual and the central fact of their scheme of duties. In early, smaller versions of the state, this was taught in the home; however, as Rome grew, a need arose for organized teaching of laws and history to maintain moral unity.

In the history of Athens, the role of personal energy is highlighted. Demosthenes argued that public misfortunes were the result of citizens being backward as individuals. He suggested that victory could be achieved if citizens individually shook off negligence and undertook their full share of personal duty in the common cause, citing the example of Philip, who rose from small beginnings through his own ability, perseverance, and personal energy.

## Epistemological Considerations
In English philosophy, specifically that of Butler, the universe is viewed as a system composed of individual facts. Butler posits that complete knowledge is impossible because one cannot have a thorough knowledge of any part of the system without knowing the whole. In this framework, reason is the natural light used to judge things, but it provides only probable conclusions rather than a completed system of knowledge. This leads to the conclusion that any one thing may be a necessary condition for any other.
