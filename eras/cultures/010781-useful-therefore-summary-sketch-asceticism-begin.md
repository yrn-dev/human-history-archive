# Asceticism

Asceticism is defined as the theory and practice of self-mortification and bodily abstinence, which is generally religious in nature. The term is derived from the Greek verb *askeo*, meaning "I practise," which led to the noun *askaesis* and the adjective *askaetikos*. This terminology employs a metaphor from the ancient *palaestra*, or wrestling-place, where victory was granted to those who had most effectively trained their bodies.

## Conceptual Basis and Development
While various technical terms of Greek philosophic asceticism—initially used by Neo-pythagoreans and Cynics and later adopted by Christians and Greek Israelites—were borrowed from athletic contests, these remained metaphors. True asceticism possesses a moral purpose, rooted in the eternal contrast between the proposition "This is right" and the proposition "That is pleasant."

The instinct for asceticism is likely as old as humanity. However, early religious practices often lacked lofty spiritual meaning, with certain things being regarded as holy that are considered trifling or obscene from a modern perspective. Consequently, primitive asceticism often included self-torture and abstentions that appeared valueless for the discipline of feelings or the training of character. Despite this, such non-moral restraints or *taboos* may have contributed to the development of the human faculty to prefer a larger good over immediate impulse.

## Primitive and Ritualistic Forms
In primitive forms of asceticism, the instinct is often over-developed but ill-informed, operating in ways that may be morally harmful or unessential. Aristotle, in his *Ethics*, characterized the barbarian's ideal of life as "the living as one likes," though in reality, the savage is more restricted by superstitious scruples and restrictions than the civilized man.

Practices observed among less advanced races, or surviving among those who have reached a level of genuine moral reflection, include:
*   **Physical Discomforts:** Sleeping on bare ground, nakedness, living in caves, vigils, and the avoidance of hair-cutting, clean raiment, and baths.
*   **Bodily Alterations:** Tattooing and the deformation of the feet, teeth, and skull.
*   **Abstinence:** Fasting, the denial of sexual instinct, and the abstention from specific drinks and meats.
*   **Vows:** Life-long vows of silence or silence observed during pilgrimages.
*   **Self-Inflicted Pain:** Scourging, branding, fire-walking, the wearing of hair shirts, cutting with knives, burial alive, and the use of hooks plunged into the skin to hang oneself or suspend weights from tender parts of the body.

These customs are often based on demonological beliefs or superstitions. For example, the priests of Cybele (the Galli) practiced self-mutilation as a magical ceremony intended to stimulate crops and fertilize the soil.

## Ethical Perfection and Religious Reform
When self-denial is consciously pursued as a method of ethical perfection, it is generally less rigorous and cruel than primitive forms. The role of great religious reformers—including Buddha, Moses, Mani, Mahomet, Paul, Luther, and St Francis—has been to direct the ascetic instinct toward higher aims, replacing ritual purity with true personal holiness.

## Historical Applications and Cultural Reactions
In the development of Christianity, the austere conception of the Kingdom of God taught by Jesus of Nazareth was eventually overlaid by a revival of the ancient sacrificial idea. This led to a *theocrasia* where Christianity absorbed elements from other cults, such as Mithraism, the cult of the Serapeum, and Judaism. These grafted elements included self-tormenting asceticism and the practice of the fasting, shaven priest.

While these adaptations made the religion more acceptable in Syria and Egypt, they were viewed with hostility by other groups. Arab nomads found these features disgusting, and Nordic barbarians of the North and West viewed the habits of shaven and robed priests, monks, and nuns as unnatural and queer, leading to a peculiar bias among early Northmen and Anglo-Saxons.

In the 2nd century, the disciples of Marcion practiced rigorous asceticism and the rejection of the Old Testament. These Marcionites lived in close communities ruled by presbyters and bishops. Marcionite impulses later influenced the rise of the Bogomils and Paulicians, as well as the antinomian and anticlerical sects of the 13th century and the western Cathari.
