# Polygamy in Historical and Cultural Contexts

Polygamy, the practice of having multiple spouses, has been documented across various civilizations, ethnic groups, and religious movements, with its legality and social acceptance varying significantly by region and era.

## Ancient Civilizations and Early Societies
In ancient Egypt, polygamy was permitted, though a specific restriction existed for priests, who were allowed to marry only one woman. Egyptian law ensured that children were considered legitimate and free regardless of whether the mother was a slave or a free woman. Additionally, Egyptian laws and religious traditions—influenced by the examples of the gods Osiris and Isis—authorized the marriage of brothers to sisters.

In contrast, historical Greece was characterized by a general absence of polygamy. This lack of the practice served as a negative characteristic that distinguished the Hellens from contemporary neighbors such as the Persians, Egyptians, Thracians, and Carthaginians.

Among the early inhabitants of Japan, polygamy was common across all social strata. While a Chinese record from the early Christian epoch suggested this was due to a surplus of women, the primary cause is identified as a desire for abundant progeny. Nobles typically had four or five wives, while commoners had two or three. Japanese society maintained a strict line between nobles and commoners, but this did not prevent noblemen from taking wives or concubines from inferior families. While some allege no distinction existed between wives and concubines, annals suggest that differentiation by rank was practiced and reflected in the status of the offspring.

## Central Asian and Steppe Peoples
Among the Mongols, the plurality of wives was not prohibited. In these arrangements, the first wife was the most respected and managed domestic concerns. The Buraets, who maintained old Mongol customs, and the Yakuts (of Eastern Turk lineage) practiced polygamy extensively, with the Buraets taking as many wives as they chose. A historical Mongol custom allowed a son to take any of his deceased father's wives upon succeeding him; while a modern Mongol writer notes this practice is now obsolete, it is preserved in proverbs. Herodotus ascribed a similar custom to the Scyths.

The Bulgars, a Turanian race of wild horsemen governed by *khans* and *boyars*, also practiced polygamy. This group eventually merged with the Slavonic population of Moesia after crossing the Danube in 679.

## African Societies
Among the Ashanti, a powerful nation that emerged in the 16th or 17th century in the impenetrable forests of West Africa, polygamy was practiced by all individuals who had the financial means to afford it.

## Religious and Legal Developments
### The Israelite Tradition
Historical records indicate that it was an immemorial custom among the patriarchs and forefathers of the Israelites to have more than one wife or a combination of wives and concubines. While this practice was not directly forbidden in the law of Moses, it is noted that it was not distinctly permitted in the law of Moses either. Some interpretations suggest that Moses suffered the practice of several wives due to the "hardness of their hearts," though it was not the original intent.

### Mormonism and United States Legislation
In the 19th century, the Mormon population in the United States faced significant legal opposition due to charges of polygamy, bigamy, and "celestial marriage." In Illinois, during the 1840s, these charges contributed to intense opposition and a civil war in Hancock County in 1844, leading to the murder of Joseph Smith.

In Idaho, the large Mormon population led to restrictive legislation. Between 1884 and 1885, county and precinct officers were required to take a test oath abjuring polygamy, bigamy, or celestial marriage; in 1888, three territorial legislators lost their seats for being ineligible under this law. An 1889 act forbade registration or voting for anyone who had practiced, taught, or encouraged polygamy since January 1, 1888, unless they took a renunciation oath and proved to the District Court that they had not engaged in such practices for two years. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld this legislation in *Davis v. Beason*. By 1893, the law was amended to remove the retroactive nature and the two-year clause, requiring only a present renunciation of polygamy.

### The Zionist Movement
John Alexander Dowie, the founder of "Zionism" and the Christian Catholic Apostolic Church in Zion, established Zion City on the west shore of Lake Michigan in 1901. In 1906, Dowie was deposed following a revolt in which he was charged with peculation and the practice of polygamy. This deposition occurred with the assent of his own son and wife.
