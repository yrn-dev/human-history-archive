# Avon and Related Celtic River Names

The term "Avon" is a Celtic word used as the name for several rivers in England and other regions. In the Celtic languages, it appears as *afon* in Welsh, *aon* in Manx, and *abhuinn* (pronounced *avain*) in Gaelic. This root is radically identical to the Latin *aqua* and *amnis*, as well as the Sanskrit *ap*, all of which mean water.

## Distribution in Europe
The root of the word "Avon" appears, though sometimes disguised, in a vast number of river names across the Celtic areas of Europe. 

In the British Islands, this is seen in forms such as *Evan*, *Aune*, *Anne*, *Ive*, *Auney*, and *Inney*. Within France, specifically in Brittany and other regions, the names *Aff*, *Aven*, *Avon*, and *Aune* appear. The root is further evidenced in Italy by the names *Avenza* and *Avens*, in Portugal by *Avia*, and in Spain by *Avono*. Additionally, the terminal syllable of many Latinized names for French rivers—including the *Garumna*, the *Matrona*, and the *Sequana*—appears to have originally been the same word. Outside of Europe, the root is visible in names such as *Doab* and *Punjab*.

## The Bristol Avon
In England, one of the principal rivers of this name is the Lower or Bristol Avon. It originates on the eastern slope of the Cotteswold Hills in Gloucestershire, collecting streams east of Malmesbury and south of Tetbury. The river flows in a wide curve through a broad upper valley, passing Melksham and Chippenham, before turning abruptly west toward Bradford-on-Avon. After receiving the waters of the Frome from the south, it enters a narrow valley containing Bristol and Bath.

Below Bristol, the river passes through the Clifton Gorge, characterized by wooded cliffs and the Clifton suspension bridge. The river eventually enters the estuary of the Severn at Avonmouth. The total length of the river, excluding minor sinuosities, is approximately 75 miles. Its drainage area covers 891 square miles, with a total fall between 500 and 600 feet.

## Commercial and Geological Significance
The Bristol Avon is a critical commercial waterway providing access to the port of Bristol. In 1809, Bristol harbour was created by converting a branch of the Frome and the Avon into "the Float," which involved the formation of two basins and a new channel for the Avon. The port extends from the mouth of the river and the Severn estuary up to Hanham Mills on the Avon. 

Significant infrastructure at the river entrance includes the Portishead docks on the Somerset shore and the Avonmouth docks. The Royal Edward dock at Avonmouth, opened in 1908 by King Edward VII, features a lock 100 feet wide and 875 feet long. This port facilitates extensive trade with the West Indies and America, exporting salt, tin, cotton goods, manufactured oils, and machinery, while importing ore, timber, hides, cattle, fruit, grain, and oils.

In the field of geology, the term "Avonian" was proposed in 1905 by Dr. A. Vaughan to describe rocks of the Lower Carboniferous age found in the Avon gorge at Bristol. The Avonian stage includes the Clevedonian lower substage (displayed near Clevedon in Somerset) and the Kidwellian upper substage (well developed near Kidwelly in Carmarthenshire).

## Other Occurrences in Great Britain
Beyond the Bristol Avon, the name appears in several other British locations:
* **England:** A stream in Devonshire that flows from Dartmoor to the English Channel.
* **South Wales:** A stream with its mouth located at Aberavon in Glamorganshire.
* **Scotland:** Tributaries of the Forth, the Spey, and the Clyde.
