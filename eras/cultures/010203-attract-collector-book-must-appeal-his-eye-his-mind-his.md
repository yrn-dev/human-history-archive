# The Principles and History of Book-Collecting

Book-collecting is defined as the process of bringing together books that possess an element of permanent interest in their form, contents, or the history of the individual copy, and which are either prospectively or actually rare. In this context, rarity is subordinate to interest; a book that is rare but devoid of interest is of no concern to the collector. Conversely, the anticipation that a book will always be easily procurable generally restrains the impulse to collect.

## The Three Appeals of the Book
To attract a collector, a book must appeal to the eye, the mind, or the imagination. Many famous works appeal to all three.

### Visual Appeal
A book may be beautiful due to its illustrations, its binding, or the harmony and perfection of its paper and print. In France, a strong attraction to fine bindings has historically existed, with 17th and 18th-century jackets often driving the high prices of French first editions and Elzevirs. In England, the appreciation for old bindings increased following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club. Sought-after English examples include embroidered bindings, blind stamped bindings, and those attributable to Samuel Mearne during the time of Charles II.

### Imaginative Appeal
Books that appeal to the imagination are often those associated with famous events or people. This includes works that belonged to great poets, soldiers, statesmen, or royalty—such as Mary, queen of Scots—particularly if the book bears marks of being valued by the owner or is linked to a striking incident in their life. Collectors also value a "pedigree," where a work's history can be traced through a series of book-loving owners. Other imaginative interests include books that caused the persecution of their authors or were condemned to be burnt, though this specific interest has appealed more to writers than to collectors.

The desire for first editions is partly driven by these associations or the imagination, and partly by the fact that they may be superior to reprints for study. However, some values are fanciful; for example, first printed editions of Greek and Latin classics were highly priced during the reign of George III despite having no value as texts and no link to the personality of their authors.

### Intellectual Appeal
Intellectual appeal is found in works of historical value, specifically those that have not been reprinted or whose original editions are more convincing and authentic than modern versions. Collectors in this field often act as both rivals and servants to historical students. Examples of this include Prince Lucien Bonaparte's philological library, the Foxwell collection of early books on political economy, and Lord Crawford's collections of papal bulls and English, Irish, and Scottish proclamations.

## Evolution of the Practice
Book-collecting as it is currently understood is a modern growth. While early forms existed under the Roman Empire and in the late Middle Ages, they differed from modern collecting. For instance, the *Philobiblon* (1345), attributed to Richard de Bury, focuses on the delights of literature rather than the acquisition of rare objects. Similarly, Sebastian Brant argued a century and a half later that possessing books is a poor substitute for learning.

Modern collecting required a surplus of books to be available. Even after printing reduced book costs by approximately 80%, collectors did not immediately appear. Early prominent book-owners, such as Jean Grolier (1479-1565), were not antiquarians; Grolier bought books to provide friends with Renaissance scholarship and to encourage the best printers. Other figures who assembled great libraries of learned literature in handsome leather jackets included Henry, prince of Wales, Archbishop Cranmer, Lords Lumley and Arundel, and the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou.

## Methods and Theories of Collecting
The "cabinet theory" of collecting became popular in the United States, England, and France as a response to the growing cost of books and limited house-room. Rather than maintaining a miscellaneous library representing every class of book, "cabinet" collectors aim for small, compact collections that epitomize their specific tastes and fit within a neat bookcase. This approach was carried out with skill by Henry Perkins, whose collection of early printing and manuscripts realized nearly £26,000 when sold in 1870.

Historically, general manuals like Brunet's *Manuel du libraire* (1810) and W.T. Lowndes' *Bibliographer's Manual of English Literature* (1834) provided the necessary information for "omnivorous" collectors. In the modern era, these have been replaced by more detailed handbooks focusing on special books.

## Notable Collectors and Bindings
Various collectors are noted for their specific tastes in bindings:
* **Jean Grolier:** Used the legend *Portio mea domine sit in terra viventium* and the name *Io Grolierii et Amicorum*.
* **Tommaso Maioli:** Used a similar legend to Grolier; his books are marked with atoms of gold in the leather irregularities.
* **Demetrio Canevari:** Used dark red or green morocco with a colored cameo stamp depicting Apollo driving a chariot with one black and one white horse toward a mountain with a silver Pegasus, surrounded by a Greek legend.
