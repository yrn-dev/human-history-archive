# Diffusion of Australian Flora and Fauna

The proximity of the Australian continent has facilitated the partial diffusion of Australian biological types into neighboring regions, particularly the Malayan Islands. This movement of species creates a distinct transition between the Asiatic and Australian biological realms.

## Botanical Diffusion in the Malayan Islands
In the Malayan Islands, the vegetation is primarily similar to the wetter and hotter regions of India. However, the stability of humidity and temperature allows for the predominance of certain tropical forms. Because of the proximity of Australia, Australian plant types—which are not found in India—have partially diffused into this area. 

One characteristic Australian form, the casuarina, is abundant on the mountains in the eastern part of Java. Additionally, some temperate forms from Australia are found on the higher mountains of Borneo. This extension of Australian genera is not limited to these islands; they reach as far as the Philippines and into southern China.

## Wallace's Line and Physical Separation
Modern science has identified a specific line of physical separation known as Wallace's line, named after the naturalist A.R. Wallace. This boundary follows the Straits of Macassar, the channel between Celebes and Borneo, approximately along 120 deg. E. 

This line serves as a biological marker:
* **West of the line:** Flora and fauna are essentially Asiatic in type.
* **South and east of the line:** The Australian element becomes distinctly marked and eventually predominant.

While the southern islands link the Asian continent to Australia and the Pacific islands, old geographers established no marked boundary in this quarter. However, the current scientific consensus recognizes the Straits of Macassar as the point where the Australian influence begins to dominate.

## Zoological Regions and Avifauna
The zoological provinces of Asia generally correspond with its botanical provinces. According to the divisions of Dr. Sclater, the south-eastern portion of Asia, including the Philippines, Borneo, Java, and Sumatra, forms the Indian region. In contrast, the Australian region includes Papua, Celebes, and other islands located east of Java beyond Wallace's line.

Regarding avifauna, there are significant differences between the Oriental and Australian areas. While many Indo-Malayan bird families extend across Wallace's line into the Australian realm, the reverse occurs to a much more moderate extent. Certain bird forms prevalent in the Indian Region are entirely absent in the Australian region, including:
* Bulbuls (*Ixidae*), specifically the peculiar sub-group *Phyllornithinae*.
* Vultures (*Vulturidae*).
* Barbets (*Megalaeminae*).
* Pheasants (*Phasianidae*).

Woodpeckers (*Picidae*) are also weakly represented; out of 400 species, only a very few cross the boundary to occur in the Moluccas, Celebes, or Lombok, remaining unknown elsewhere in the region.

## Characteristics of the Australian Continent
The Australian continent possesses distinct geographical and biological features. Its highest mountain is Kosciusco, located in the Australian Alps toward the south-eastern extremity of New South Wales, reaching a height of 7,328 ft. A nearby peak to the south, Mueller's Peak, stands at 7,268 ft.

The continent is home to unique marsupials, such as the koala (*Phascolarctus cinereus*). A member of the *Phascolmyidae* family (which also includes wombats), the koala is an ash-grey animal approximately 2 ft. in length. It inhabits the south-eastern parts of the continent, primarily residing in lofty eucalyptus trees. It feeds on tender shoots and buds, though it occasionally descends to the ground at night to search for roots. Due to its slow movements and arboreal habits, colonists have referred to it as the "native bear" or "native sloth."
