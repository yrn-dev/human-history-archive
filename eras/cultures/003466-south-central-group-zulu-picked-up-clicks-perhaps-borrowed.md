# The South-Central Bantu Group and the Zulu Language

The South-Central group is one of the four great sections into which the domain of Bantu speech is broadly divided. This group, which includes Bechuana and Zulu, is characterized by a striking homogeneity common to the Bantu family, though it possesses specific phonetic and structural developments.

## Classification and Geography
The South-Central group (b) is categorized alongside the languages of the Great Lakes and East Coast (a), the languages of the South-West (c), and the Western group (d). Within the broader Bantu classification, the Zulu-Kaffir group specifically occupies regions including Natal, Swaziland, the eastern portion of the Transvaal, the eastern half of Cape Colony, and parts of Rhodesia.

Due to the activities of Zulu raiders in the early 19th century, Zulu dialects—specifically Ki-ngoni (or Ci-nongi) and Tebele—were carried to the plateaus north-east of Lake Nyasa, as well as to Western Nyasaland and South-West Rhodesia. Additionally, Zulu dialects such as Ronga and Tebele have exerted a significant influence on the languages of Manika and the coast-lands of Inhambane and Beira, including Chopi and Ci-shangane.

## Phonology and the Acquisition of Clicks
In terms of phonology, the South-Central group is closely related to the East Coast and South-West groups. These three groups are distinguished from the Western group by their lack of the specific West African phonetic combinations *kp* and *gb*, which are characteristic of speech between the Guinea coast and the Upper Nile.

A distinctive feature of a small portion of the South-Central group, specifically Zulu, is the adoption of clicks. It is suggested that these clicks may have been borrowed from the Bushmen and Hottentots, who are identified as the aboriginal races of South Africa.

## Linguistic Characteristics and Relations
The Zulu-Kaffir language is noted for exhibiting marked deviations and changes in its phonetics and vocabulary, developments that are likely recent. Because of these features, it is unusually divergent from the typical Bantu structure in certain respects. While it was once considered the most archaic type of Bantu speech before the languages of the Great Lakes were studied, it has since been deposed from that position. However, it still preserves a few characteristics of the hypothetical Bantu mother-tongue.

In terms of its relationship to other Bantu languages:
* **Vocabulary and Phonetics:** Zulu is related in some phonetic features to group No. 42 and No. 43, though it otherwise stands largely alone in its developments.
* **Distant Relations:** It may have distant relations with groups No. 29 and No. 32.
* **Limited Affinity:** The Subiya and Tonga languages (group No. 32) evince only a slight relationship with the Zulu group.

## General Bantu Structural Features
As a member of the Bantu family, the languages of the South-Central group follow specific construction rules. They are agglutinative, meaning syntax is formed by adding prefixes (principally) and suffixes to a root, without the use of infixes. The root remains practically unchanging except for its terminal vowel, although the first or penultimate vowel or consonant may be modified by a preceding prefix, or the last vowel by a succeeding suffix.

An example of these structural forms found in Zulu-Kaffir is the use of *ngumuntu* and *ngabantu* (the latter evolving from *nga-ba-ntu*).
