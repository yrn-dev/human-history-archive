# Bolivia

## Geography and Terrain
While commonly perceived as an extremely rugged mountainous country, three-fifths of Bolivia consists of gently undulating forest regions, great swamps, flooded bottomlands, and low alluvial plains, including the Chiquitos region. The extreme south contains the llanos (open grassy plains) of Manzo and the Bolivian Chaco. In southern Santa Cruz and eastern Chuquisaca, there are low-lying plains and extensive swamps that are subject to periodical inundations, rendering them of little value for pastoral or agricultural use. However, some areas in this region remain above the floods and provide rich grazing lands. A significant challenge to this area is defective drainage, as streams possess currents too sluggish to remove water during the rainy season.

Between the Andes and the Chiquitos sierras lie the Llanos de Chiquitos, which feature a more diversified surface and higher general elevation. To the north are the llanos of Mojos and Guarayos, a region once covered by the great Mojos Lake. This area is traversed by numerous rivers, including the Beni, Madre de Dios, Yacuma, Guapay, San Miguel, Guapore, and Mamore. It contains extensive open plains where cattle-raising has been practiced since the era of Jesuit missions, as well as rich agricultural districts. The lower slopes of the Andes, particularly in the north-west, are covered with heavy forests and represent one of the richest districts in the country, capable of supporting a large population.

In the south-western corner of the Potosi department lies a portion of the Atacama Desert, an arid, saline, and barren region. The higher elevations here are known as the Puna de Atacama, a broken mountainous region with extinct volcanoes and peaks ranging from 7,000 to 13,500 feet. While the western slope is rainless and desolate, the eastern side sees occasional rainfall and streams from snow-clad peaks that produce slight fertility.

## Hydrography
The south-east drainage basin discharges into the Paraguay. Its only large river within Bolivia is the Pilcomayo, which rises on the east slopes of the Cordillera Oriental and flows through the Bolivian Chaco. Due to a sluggish current, its channels are often obstructed. In the sierra region, the Pilcomayo is joined by smaller tributaries such as the Mataca, Cachimayo, and the Pilaya (or Camblaya), the latter of which is formed by the San Juan and Cotagaita. 

Other notable streams in this region include the Tarija (or Rio Grande), which drains a small district south-east of the Santa Victoria sierra and flows into the Argentine Bermejo river. The Otuquis, formed by the Tucabaca and San Rafael, is lost in marshes 50 miles from the Paraguay. Additionally, the Parapiti rises on the eastern slopes of the Sierra de Misiones and flows north-east for approximately 150 miles before being lost in the marshy districts of the Bolivian plain.

## Flora and Fauna
Bolivia possesses a wide variety of birds and insects. The tropical valleys and plains are inhabited by the American ostrich (*Rhea americanus*) and the *jaburu* (or *bata*), a large stork reaching a maximum height of 8 feet and a wingspan of 8 feet 6 inches. The high sierras are frequented by eagles and condors of the largest size, while the common vulture is found throughout the country. The forests of the lowlands are home to toucans, araras, and parrots, while the warm valleys contain song-birds and birds with beautiful plumage. Humming-birds are found even at high mountain elevations.

The country benefited significantly from the introduction of European animals, including poultry, swine, goats, sheep, cattle, and horses, which serve as sources of wealth and food for much of the population. Mules are widely used as pack animals, though they are imported from Argentina. Additionally, the cochineal insect is found where cacti grow, and silkworms have been bred successfully in certain departments.

## History and Ethnography
The territory of the republic of Bolivia, named after the liberator Simon Bolivar, was originally part of the empire of the Incas of Peru. The Aymara (anciently known as Colla) are a tribe of South American Indians who formerly inhabited the valleys of the Andes and the area around Lake Titicaca. This region was considered a "holy land" by the Incas, who attributed the origin of all Quichua (Inca) civilization to it. The Aymaras possessed a considerable culture before being conquered by the Incas in the 13th and 14th centuries, as evidenced by the megalithic ruins of Tiahuanaco.

By the time the Spaniards arrived in the 16th century, the Aymaras had been under Inca domination for a long time and were in a decadent state. Despite this, they kept certain privileges, including their own language. Physically, the pure Aymara is characterized by a broad face, rounded forehead, black eyes, reddish complexion, and a short, thick-set build with great chest development. They are described as apathetic and sullen in disposition, similar to the Quichuas. Including half-breeds, the Aymara number about half a million in Bolivia and form the chief ethnical element of the country.

Following the Spanish conquest, natives faced oppression and tyranny, often in defiance of the Spanish home government's protective regulations. While some claim eight million Indians died due to forced labor in mines, this is considered a gross exaggeration; the population decline was primarily caused by alcohol and European epidemics. In 1780-82, the Inca Tupac Amaru led an abortive insurrection directed more against Creole tyranny than Spanish rule. The subsequent losses and distrust of colonial Spaniards led to a level of indifference among the Indians during the 1814 colonial revolt that eventually secured independence for the South American states.
