# The Bag-pipe

The bag-pipe is a musical instrument defined by its use of an air-reservoir (the bag) and its most characteristic feature: the fixed harmony of buzzing drones. These drones provide a ground bass that sounds without intermission.

## Construction and Mechanics
The bag-pipe consists of a bag, a blow-pipe or bellows, a chaunter, and one or more drones. The bag rests under the performer's arm, with the blow-pipe and chaunter positioned at opposite extremities and the drones pointing over the shoulder.

The drones produce sound via a beating-reed, which is created by making a cut partly across a piece of cane or reed near the open end and splitting it back toward a joint to create a tongue or flap. This reed is fixed in a socket within the drone, which then fits into the stock. When the performer applies pressure with their arm, air is forced from the bag into the drone-pipe, causing the reed's tongue to vibrate over the aperture and set the air column in vibration. Because the drone-pipe is a cylindrical tube with a reed mouthpiece, it possesses the acoustic properties of a closed pipe, producing a note corresponding to a pipe twice its length. Drones are typically tuned using sliding-joints.

## Methods of Inflation
Bag-pipes are categorized into two classes based on how the bag is inflated:
1. **Blow-pipes**: The performer inflates the bag using their mouth. Examples include the Calabrian bag-pipe, the Highland bag-pipe, and the ancient Greek *askaules* and Roman *tibia utricularis*.
2. **Bellows**: A small bellows, connected to the bag by a valved feed-pipe, is worked by the performer's arm or elbow via a ribbon or strap. This method is used in the *musette*, the modern Lowland bag-pipe, the Northumbrian (or Border) bag-pipe, and the modern Irish union-pipes.

## Historical Development
The origins of the bag-pipe are traced to remote antiquity. While not represented on known Egyptian or Assyrian monuments, evidence exists in ancient Persia, Greece, Chaldaea, and Egypt. In a mummy-case discovered in Turin, a pipe was found containing a straw beating-reed. The *arghoul*, a modern Egyptian instrument, utilizes a drone and chaunter but lacks a bag; this instrument appears in hieroglyphs (sounded *as-it*) and a mural painting in the Musee Guimet. 

In Persia, terracotta figures of musicians from the 8th century B.C. found at Susa depict players with bags under their arms and curved chaunters. Later historical records include 13th-century Spanish illuminated manuscripts, such as the *Cantigas di Santa Maria*, which show three bag-pipes, including one with four long drones and two chaunters. A 15th-century Spanish manuscript in the British Museum also depicts bag-pipes, including one with a curved chaunter and two drones (one conical and one cylindrical).

## Regional Varieties and Types
### European Varieties
*   **Germany**: The *Sackpfeife* or *Dudelsack* existed in five sizes described by Michael Praetorius. These include the *Grosser Bock* (double-bass), which featured a single long cylindrical drone and a chaunter both ending in curved ram's horns; the *Bock* (pitched a fourth higher in C); the *Schaeferpfeife* (two drones in B flat and F); the *Huemmelchen* (two drones in F and C); and the *Dudey* (treble, with three drones in E flat, B flat, and E flat).
*   **France and Italy**: The *cornemuse* and *chalemie* were used before the *musette*. One type of *cornemuse* used by shepherds featured a leather bag, a large drone (*gros bourdon*) and a small drone (*petit bourdon*), both using beating-reeds. A second type, played with *Hautbois de Poitou*, used a double reed for its single drone. The *musette*, evolved during the 15th century, replaced long drones with a compact barrel droner containing parallel channels and double "hautbois" reeds. These were regulated by slides called *layettes*.
*   **Italy**: The *surdelina* or *sampogna* (also called *musette de Naples*) featured a complicated construction with two chaunters and two drones, each with ten keys, all using double reeds and bellows inflation.
*   **British Isles**: The Northumbrian bag-pipe is distinguished by a chaunter stopped at the lower end, rendering the pipe silent when all holes are closed. The 18th-century union-pipes (modern Irish bag-pipe) utilize bellows, a chaunter with eight keys for a chromatic scale in two octaves, and three regulators (drones with keys) played by the elbow.

### Specialized Instruments
The Magdeburg bag-pipe, mentioned by Praetorius, featured two chaunters in one stock, allowing the performer to play simple two-part melodies. The Calabrian bag-pipe uses a goatskin bag with the hair left on and employs double reeds for both its two drones and two chaunters.

## Tuning and Scales
The tuning of the bag-pipe is often non-diatonic. In some traditions, the chaunter's notes are not strictly in tune with each other, and the instrument cannot be transposed. Some experts suggest the natural scale of the chaunter most closely corresponds to the Arab scale of Zalzal. In certain configurations, three drones are tuned to A, with the largest two octaves below the chaunter's A and the two smaller drones one octave below.
