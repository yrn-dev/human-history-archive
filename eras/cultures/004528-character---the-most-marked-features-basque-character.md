# The Basque People

The Basques are a sturdy and independent-spirited people currently residing on the north and south slopes of the Pyrenees, numbering approximately 600,000 in Europe. They are characterized by a distinct cultural and linguistic identity that differs fundamentally from the surrounding Aryan languages.

## Character and Social Traits
The most prominent features of the Basque character include an obstinate conservatism, a pride of race, and intense self-respect. Historically, the claim that all Basques were noble was a reality in practice and within the *fueros* and laws of Spain. This status allowed every Basque freeholder, known as a *vecino*, to prove his nobility, which rendered him eligible for any office.

The Basques are not a town-dwelling race; their villages typically consist of only a few houses, with the population living in scattered habitations. Because they do not fear solitude, they have proven to be excellent missionaries and emigrants.

## Military and Maritime History
Basques are renowned as splendid seamen. They were early leaders in whale fishing in the Bay of Biscay and were the first to establish cod-fisheries off the coast of Newfoundland. They also participated fully in the colonization of the Americas. Evidence of this migration is found in the abundance of Basque names among older colonial families and the publication of Basque newspapers in Los Angeles, California, and Buenos Aires.

In military contexts, Basques are described as splendid marchers who possess power of endurance and tenacity, traits that the Romans had previously noted in the Celtiberians and Iberians. While they are considered better in defense than in attack, the turning-point in both Carlist wars was the failure to take Bilbao.

## Legal Status and Gender Roles
In the tenures of property and civil institutions, women held a very high legal position. Ancestral property was inherited by the eldest born child, regardless of whether they were a girl or a boy; this practice was observed among the peasantry as well as the higher classes. Under the *fueros*, insults committed against a woman, or in the presence of a woman, were punished more severely than similar offenses occurring between men.

Despite this high legal standing, Basque women worked as hard as, or harder than, men. Historical accounts highlight the robust appearance of women who loaded and unloaded ships in Bilbao and those who worked as rowers on the Bidassoa.

## Language and Linguistics
The Basque language is a fully developed tongue, though its structural character is entirely different from the Aryan languages. It possesses no graphic system of its own and utilizes the Roman character (either French or Spanish), with dotted or accented letters used in modern writing to indicate specific sounds. The alphabet varies by dialect. Prince L. L. Bonaparte identified thirty-eight simple consonants and thirteen simple vowels, noting that some dialects contain "wet" consonants (such as *ny*, *dy*, and *ty*) and nasal vowels.

The language is characterized by several specific rules:
* Consonants cannot be doubled.
* Most soft consonants are dropped in current speech.
* Words cannot begin with the letter *r* (for example, *rationem* is written as *arrazoin*).

Basque utilizes a highly developed postpositional system instead of declension. The definite article *a* (plural *ak*) is a postposition. Other principal suffixes include *k* (singular nominative agent and plural mark), *n* ("in" and "of"), *i* ("to"), *z* ("by"), *ik* ("some"), *ko* ("of" or "from"), *tik* ("from"), *kin* or *gaz* ("with"), *gatik* ("for the sake of"), *gana* ("towards"), and *ra* or *rat* ("at," "into," or "to").

## Ethnology and Origins
The origins of the Basque people and their language have been a subject of significant scholarly debate. Some philologists suggest a remote link to the Hamitic languages through the Berber language of North Africa. Others believe Basque is a specialized survivor of a widely extended group of pre-Hamitic languages, possibly related to speech vestiges found in the Caucasian Mountains.

Ancient remains and toponymy suggest the Basque speech and people were once distributed much more widely across Spain. Classical scholars noted that ancient Spanish place-names transcribed by Greek and Latin writers—such as *Iliberis* and *Mondiculeia*—bear a strong resemblance to actual Basque names like *Iriberry* and *Mendigorry*. While many students accept that a people speaking a language akin to Basque once extended throughout Spain and perhaps into Sardinia and Sicily, some Basque scholars deny this, and a conclusive racial or linguistic connection to the Celtiberians or Iberians has not been proved.
