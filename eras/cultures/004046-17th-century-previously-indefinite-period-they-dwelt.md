# The 17th Century

The seventeenth century is characterized in historical records as an "interregnum" in the progress of mankind toward worldwide unity. It was an age defined by division and separated nationalities, lacking a single ruling unifying idea. During this time, the dream of Christendom was fading, and the impulse of the empire had declined to the point where the Emperor was merely one of several competing princes. Despite the jostling of developing powers and the waste of policies and wars, the people of Europe experienced increasing prosperity, aided by the enlargement of human resources resulting from the geographical discoveries of the sixteenth century.

## Regional Conflicts and Political Shifts
In Central Europe, there was a steady recovery from the devastation caused by the Thirty Years War. However, specific regions continued to suffer; for example, the town of Baden was severely impacted by various combatants. In 1643, the French pillaged Baden, and in 1689, they laid the town in ashes. Baden had served as the residence of the margraves from the 14th century until the close of the 17th century.

In Asia, the region of Kulja (also known as New Kulja, Manchu Kulja, or Ili) is believed by some, such as Grum-Grzimailo, to have been conquered by the Oirads at the end of the 16th or the beginning of the 17th century. The Oirads maintained control of this territory until 1755, when it was annexed by the Chinese.

In Africa, the Barotse people (originally known as Aalui) occupied the extensive plain through which the Zambezi passes starting approximately at the commencement of the 17th century. This occupation spanned the reigns of twenty-two successive paramount chiefs. Prior to this, for an indefinite period, they had lived on the Kabompo river, located 200 miles to the northeast of their later country. A section of the tribe that did not migrate remained on the Kabompo river under the name Balokwakwa (men of the ambuscade), though they were formerly known as Aalukolui.

## Linguistic Evolution: The Seventeenth-Century Transition
The period between 1611 and 1689 is referred to as the "Seventeenth-Century Transition" regarding the English language. The year 1611, which coincides with the end of Shakespeare's literary work and the appearance of the Authorized Version of the Bible, marks the close of Tudor English. From this point, the language became modern in structure, style, and expression.

The transition continued until approximately the revolution of 1688, after which literary spelling became fixed and the language became modern in form. This era also saw the disappearance of many words derived from Latin during the 16th and 17th centuries. The vocabulary of this period became more composite due to the development of physical sciences, which required new terminology from Greek and Latin. Additionally, commercial, artistic, and social contact led to the adoption of words from modern European languages, particularly Dutch, Portuguese, Italian, and French.

## Arts and Material Culture
The 17th century produced a variety of distinct artistic and furniture styles across Europe:

*   **English Furniture:** Examples from the 17th century include oak gate-legged tables and carved oak sideboards and court cupboards.
*   **French and Dutch Furniture:** The middle of the 17th century saw the production of ebony carved cabinets with interiors decorated in coloured woods and inlaid ivory. Later, during the period of Louis XIV, Andre Boulle executed an ebony armoire designed by Berain, featuring brass, other metals, and tortoise-shell panels. Late Louis XIV period works also include pine commodes featuring "Boulle" work with marquetry of ivory, mother-of-pearl, ebony, tortoise-shell, brass, and green-stained bone.
*   **Swiss Furniture:** Walnut tables with expanding leaves were produced during this century.
*   **Textiles:** "Polish carpets," often made of silk pile for ornaments and gold and silver thread for the ground, were created in a manner similar to early 17th-century Brusa fabrics. These carpets, likely made in Damascus or Constantinople, frequently featured 17th-century Turkish ornament, such as arabesques and long curved serrated leaves.

In France, the town of Luxeuil-les-Bains retains architectural remnants from this era, including various buildings and a curious 17th-century organ loft in an abbey-church, which is supported by a colossal figure of Hercules.
