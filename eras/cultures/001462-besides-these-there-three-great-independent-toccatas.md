# The Organ Works of J.S. Bach

The organ compositions of Johann Sebastian Bach comprise a diverse array of collections and independent works, ranging from structured pedagogical sets to large-scale architectural pieces.

## Collections of Organ Music
Bach's organ music is organized into several distinct groups, separate from the material found in the third part of the *Klavier-Uebung*. These collections include:
*   Six sonatas.
*   Four groups consisting of six organ preludes and fugues each.
*   *Das Orgelbuechlein*, a collection of short choral-preludes. This work was carefully planned, with the titles of intended chorales written on the blank pages of the autograph, though it was not half executed. Had it been completed, it would have been a larger volume than the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier*.
*   Eighteen larger chorale-preludes, a group that includes Bach's final composition.
*   The six "Schuebler" chorales, which were all arranged from movements of cantatas.

Additional choral-preludes fill one *Jahrgang*, while other organ works occupy two more.

## Independent Works and Unclassified Compositions
Beyond the primary collections, Bach composed a Passacaglia and three great independent toccatas. 

There are also two significant instrumental works that remain unclassified. The first is *Das musikalische Opfer*, a volume of compositions based on a theme provided to Bach by Frederick the Great; this volume contains various puzzle-canons, two great fugues, and a trio for figured bass, violin, and flute. The second is *Die Kunst der Fuge*, which consists of a progressive series of fugues based on a single subject. Written in open score as abstract studies, these works—with the exception of the extreme contrapuntal *tours de force*—are of great musical value and written in an admirable clavier style.

## The Toccata Form
In the hands of Bach, the toccata was transformed into one of the most plastic and noble of musical forms. Historically, the toccata (from the Italian *toccare*, meaning to try the touch) was characterized by running over the keyboard before bursting into full harmony and settling into a fugue. In the works of Buxtehude and his predecessors, these fugues often collapsed quickly, leading to a process of quasi-extemporization and a subsequent new fugue, which created a deceptive sense of organic unity.

Bach improved this form by maintaining the motion of the fugue to create a convincing climax. His approach to the toccata varied:
*   **Architectural Style:** In the organ toccata in C, disjointed and exaggerated introductory runs gradually fill out to build "grand piles of musical architecture."
*   **Canonic Style:** In the toccata in F for organ—described as the greatest of all toccatas—the work is developed on an enormous scale using long, smooth canonic passages with a definite theme, followed by a fugue that is unusually quiet given its size.
*   **Organized Themes:** In the toccata at the beginning of the E minor clavier-partita, the introductory runs take the form of a rounded-off group of contrasted themes. In this piece, the fugue is developed leisurely and is as long as a normal large-scale fugue by the time it reaches its central episode. At this point, introductory material returns and leads to a recapitulation of the introduction in the current key. Rather than following with a counter-development of the fugue (as seen in the clavier-toccata in C minor), Bach expands the restatement of the introduction and returns the harmonies to the fundamental key.
