# English Language

The English language, in its historical sense, encompasses the speech of the English people from their initial settlement in Britain to the present day. It is categorized by historians and philologists into three primary stages: Old, Middle, and New (or Modern) English.

## Historical Evolution and Stages
The development of English is viewed as a continuous evolution where living speech changes in definite directions. Because of an abundance of written materials, this transition appears gradual, making it difficult to draw distinct lines between successive stages.

The chronological divisions of the language are as follows:
*   **Old English (Full Inflections):** This period is subdivided into Early Old English (500–850), Typical Old English or Anglo-Saxon (850–1000), and Late Old English/Old English Transition (1000–1150).
*   **Middle English (Levelled Inflections):** This stage includes Early Middle English (1150–1250), Typical Middle English (1250–1400), and Late Middle English/Middle English Transition (1400–1485).
*   **Modern English (Lost Inflections):** This is divided into Early Modern English or Tudor English (1450–1611), Transitional Modern or 17th Century English (1611–1689), and Current English (1689 onward).

Some scholars have historically treated the oldest inflected stage as a separate language called "Anglo-Saxon," with the transition to the later stages termed "Semi-Saxon." This distinction is based on the practical difference between the two; for example, three-fourths of the Anglo-Saxon vocabulary perished and was reconstructed within 900 years. Due to differences in syntax and accidence, a modern Englishman might find it faster to learn to read and write French than to attain proficiency in Old English.

## The Rise of the Midland Dialect
Following a period where French was prominent, English steadily gained ground. By approximately 1350, French was so little known in England—even among the great families—that John Cornwal, a master of grammar, changed the teaching of grammar and construction from French into English. By 1362–1363, statute mandated that English replace French in court of law pleadings.

The midland dialect became the dominant form of the language for several reasons:
1.  It served as an intermediate dialect that was intelligible to those speaking the extreme northern and southern dialects, even when those two extremes could not understand each other.
2.  Its south-eastern form was the language of London, the center of commercial and political life and the location of the supreme law courts.
3.  It was the language used in the Wycliffite versions of the Holy Scriptures.
4.  It was the medium through which Chaucer elevated English poetry to a height of excellence.

By the end of the 14th century, the midland speech became the standard for general communication among Englishmen. The southern dialect became a "rustic patois," used by dramatists like Shakespeare for local coloring (such as Edgar's peasant disguise in *Lear*). While the northern English dialect also disappeared from English literature, it continued to develop in Scotland as a national language with a high literary culture during the 15th and 16th centuries.

## Linguistic Classification
English belongs to the Indo-European or Aryan family of languages, a group that covers nearly all of Europe and extends to India. This family includes languages such as French, German, Spanish, Italian, Greek, Russian, Armenian, Persian, and various Indian tongues. These languages share fundamental roots and grammatical ideas; for instance, the English word *father* corresponds to the Gothic *fadar*, German *vater*, Latin *pater*, Greek *pater*, and Sanscrit *pitar*. These variations often follow a specific law of variation known as Grimm’s Law.

## Academic Study and Philology
The scientific study of English in American colleges was pioneered by Francis Andrew March (1825–), who held the first chair of English language and comparative philology at Lafayette College in 1857. In 1870, March published *A Comparative Grammar of the Anglo-Saxon Language*, illustrating its forms through comparison with Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, Gothic, Old Norse, Old High German, Old Saxon, and Old Friesic.

The study of the language has seen immense advances through various works and societies. Early influential texts include those by Edwin Guest (1838), Richard Garnett (1835–1848), and R.G. Latham (1862). More recent contributions (late 19th and early 20th centuries) include works by Professor W.W. Skeat on etymology, Otto Jespersen on language progress, and Henry Bradley's *The Making of English* (1904). Research has also been disseminated through publications such as *Englische Studien*, *Anglia*, and the publications of the Modern Language Association of America.
