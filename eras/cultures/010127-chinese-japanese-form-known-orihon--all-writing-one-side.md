# Orihon

The *orihon* is a specific form of book used in China and Japan that serves as a structural link between the traditional roll and the modern book. It is characterized by a layout where all writing is placed on one side of a roll or strip of paper, while the opposite side remains blank.

## Development and Structure
The *orihon* evolved from a specific method of arranging manuscripts on rolls of papyrus, vellum, or paper. In this particular arrangement, writing was placed in short lines parallel to the length of the roll, with vacant spaces left on each side of every column or page of writing. While such rolls could be kept on two rollers—a method still utilized for the scrolls of the law in Jewish synagogues—they also allowed for a more convenient method of preservation.

By folding the roll backwards and forwards, using the vacant spaces between the columns of writing as the bends, the roll is transformed into a book. This folding process creates the *orihon*.

## Binding and Preservation
Once folded, *orihon* books are preserved in various ways. Some are simply protected by two boards. However, they are generally fastened together along one of the sides, which then serves as the back of the book. The earliest method of fastening these books involved "stabbing" the substance of the roll near the edge with holes and running a cord or fibre through them as lacing. This "stabbed" form of binding is recognized as the earliest method for keeping the leaves of a book together. While this method appears in a Coptic papyrus from approximately the 8th century found at Thebes, it is rarely used with papyrus because the material is too brittle to hold the threads securely.

## Context of Writing Systems
The *orihon* exists within a broader history of Chinese and Japanese writing. Japanese writing is derived from Chinese and utilizes a system of ideograms. In China, the writing system requires the mastery of a vast multitude of complex word signs and groupings to represent various meanings, a process requiring significant time and labour.

In Japan, the writing system evolved to include both borrowed Chinese ideographs and a phonetic script known as *kana*, which consists of forty-seven symbols formed from abbreviated Chinese ideographs. There are two varieties of *kana*:
*   **Katakana:** Referred to as "side kana" because the symbols are fragments or sides of whole Chinese ideographs.
*   **Hiragana:** A phonetic variety.

Both forms of *kana* were known before the close of the ninth century, though they were not widely used until the Heian epoch, and even then were used almost entirely by women.

## Cultural Significance of Script
In Japanese history, the visual quality of the script held high value. A perfectly formed ideograph was viewed with an appreciation similar to that of a pictorial masterpiece. This led to the emergence of celebrated figures such as the "Three Calligraphists" (Sampitsu)—Emperor Saga, Kobo Daishi, and Tachibana Hayanari. Emperor Saga's enthusiasm for Chinese learning was so great that he required all scions of the aristocracy to be instructed in the Chinese classics.

While the *kana* syllabary allowed for the development of Japanese-style prose (*wabun*), such as the preface to the *Kokin-shu* by Ki no Tsurayuki, the prestige of the ideograph remained high. Tsurayuki, for instance, presented his *Tosa Nikki* (Tosa Diary) as the work of a woman because he was reluctant to be identified with a book written in the *kana* syllabary.
