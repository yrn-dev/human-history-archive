# Ceylon

Ceylon is an island that has been known by several names throughout history, including Taprobana and Serendib. It is distinguished from the neighboring regions of British India primarily by its separate administration and the Buddhistic religion of its population.

## Geography and Flora
The island's topography is characterized by a highest point that rises to approximately 9,000 feet above sea level. The slopes of these mountains are densely covered with forest, while the lower levels possess a climate and cultivation similar to regions of the same latitude on the Malay peninsula.

The flora of Ceylon shows a distinct connection to that of the Indian peninsula and eastern tropical Africa, evidenced by the identity of various families and genera and the similarity of truly tropical forms. Notably, both the Indian peninsula and Ceylon lack oaks, conifers, and cycads, even as their mountains reach elevations where such plants are typically abundant to the north and east. The genus *Nepenthes* is found in Ceylon and the Malayan area (extending from Madagascar to New Caledonia) and as far north as the Khasi hills, though it is absent from the peninsula of India and the Himalaya.

## Population and Demographics
The population of Ceylon is composed of several racial groups. The most significant are the Sinhalese, numbering 2,000,000, and the Tamil, numbering 954,000. Other inhabitants include Arabs, Malays, Eurasians, Chinese, and others. According to archival records, the total population of the island is listed as 3,568,824.

## History and External Relations
In antiquity, the island was imperfectly known to the Romans. During the reign of Claudius, a freedman who farmed the customs of the Red Sea was driven by winds to the coast of Ceylon. After spending six months with the natives, the king of Ceylon—having learned of the justice and power of Rome—was persuaded to send an embassy to the emperor, though some sources suggest the freedman may have been an impostor. Ancient geographers, including Ptolemy, significantly magnified the size of the island, extending it as far as the equator and the vicinity of China. Some ancient writers also frequently confounded Ceylon with the island of Sumatra. Additionally, it is noted that Phœnician merchant-ships may have visited the island.

In the 19th century, Sir Edward Barnes served as the governor of Ceylon from 1824 to 1831. During his administration, he conducted the first census of the population and introduced coffee cultivation based on the West Indian system in 1824. He also directed the construction of various lines of communication, most notably the great military road between Kandy and Colombo.

## Religious Significance
Buddhism is the religion of the population of Ceylon. The island holds a position of spiritual reverence similar to that which Buddhists of Burma feel toward Ceylon. Historical accounts from the Mahawanso describe the consecration of the great Tope at Ruanwelli by King Dutthagamini in 157 B.C., an event attended by 280,000 priests from Káshmir.
