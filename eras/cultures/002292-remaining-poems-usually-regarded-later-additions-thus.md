# Analysis of Additions in Historical and Literary Works

The study of historical texts and epic poetry often involves identifying "additions"—sections of a work that may have been inserted after the primary composition. These additions are analyzed by critics to determine the original intent of the author, the date of the work's completion, and the influence of subsequent writers.

## Additions in Biblical Oracles
In the analysis of certain biblical poems, specifically those found in Num. xxiv, scholars have identified several later additions. While the Fourth poem (xxiv. 14-19) announces a king who may be David and is expected to conquer Moab and Edom, the remaining poems are generally viewed as later insertions. According to the *Oxford Hexateuch*, the three concluding oracles appear irrelevant to the primary subjects of Israel or Moab; it is suggested they were added to ensure the cycle reached a total of seven.

These later poems include:
*   **The Fifth (xxiv. 20):** Concerns the ruin of Amalek. Its date is uncertain, as Amalek may be a symbol rather than the historical entity.
*   **The Sixth (xxiv. 21 f.):** Describes the destruction of the Kenite state by Assyria. The date is uncertain because "Assyria" may refer to the ancient realm of Nineveh or the Seleucid kingdom of Syria.
*   **The Seventh (xxiv. 23 f.):** Mentions ships from the West attacking "Eber" and Assur, which may refer to Alexander the Great's conquest of Persia. Some doubtful emendations suggest this describes the ruin of Shamal in N.W. Syria.

## The Structure of the Iliad and Odyssey
In Grecian epic poetry, the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* present different structural characteristics regarding additions. The *Odyssey* is viewed by some as having a unity that is beyond the reach of current criticism, appearing as a single projection from the beginning to the death of the suitors. However, some critics suggest the *Nekuia* of book xi may contain additions, and the final book is likely the work of a different hand.

Conversely, the *Iliad* appears to be a "house built upon a plan comparatively narrow" that was later enlarged. The primary organization—essentially an *Achillêis*—consists of the first book, the eighth book, and books eleven through twenty-two. The twenty-third and twenty-fourth books are regarded as additions to the tail of this primitive poem. Furthermore, books two through seven, along with the tenth, are of a more comprehensive character that converted the *Achillêis* into the *Iliad*. While these additions are slightly more recent than the original, they belong to the same generation and state of society. Within the primary section, the shield of Achilles is considered a superadded amplification due to its disproportionate length and limited connection to the surrounding events.

## Attribution and Influence in 15th-Century Poetry
During the 15th century, several poems were long attributed to Chaucer that are now recognized as the work of others. These include the *Complaint of the Black Knight* (Lydgate), the *Mother of God* (Hoccleve), and *La Belle Dame sans merci* (a translation by Richard Ros). Other works, such as the *Assembly of Ladies* and the *Flower and the Leaf*, are noted for a freshness of spirit and love of beauty, and Professor Skeat believes the author may have been a woman.

The *Court of Love* is an example of the persistence of Chaucerian influence, as it is usually ascribed to a date as late as the middle of the 16th century. Although some attempted to attribute it to Scogan, this is considered impossible because the language is later than Scogan's time.

## Prose Romances and Metrical Stories
The transition from metrical stories to prose romances in the 13th century often involved the addition of material. Sir Walter Scott observed that prose authors, such as Rustician of Pisa and Robert de Borron, often took the subjects of old minstrels and recomposed the narratives. In doing so, they added characters and adventures, which often obliterated the original and authentic traditions hidden under "accumulated fable."
