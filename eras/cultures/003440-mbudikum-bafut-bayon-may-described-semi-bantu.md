# Bantu and Semi-Bantu Languages

The Bantu languages constitute a vast field of speech characterized by specific linguistic structures, most notably the use of pronominal-prefixes and a system of concord. The history of these languages is reconstructed primarily through linguistic research and the study of existing dialects, as historical records are nearly absent.

## Origins and Migration
The creation of the Bantu language type is considered a much more ancient event than the subsequent exodus from the Bantu mother-land. While the exact birthplace of the Bantu and the specific group of dialects from which it sprang remain a puzzle, linguistic evidence suggests the Bantu people left their primal home in the heart of Africa approximately 2,000 years ago.

This date is derived from the study of the root for "domestic fowl," which is *kuku* (and its variations such as *nkuku*, *ngoko*, *nsusu*, *nguku*, and *nku*) across the majority of Bantu Africa. Because domestic fowl reached Africa through Egypt during the Persian occupation (no earlier than 500 to 400 B.C.) and required several hundred years to reach equatorial regions, the widespread presence of this root indicates the Bantu had established the bird among themselves before their migration.

## Linguistic Characteristics
The Bantu verb is built upon a practically unchangeable root, which serves as the second person singular of the imperative. This root is modified by various prefixed and suffixed particles, which are often reduced prepositions, adverbs, or worn-down verbs acting as auxiliaries.

A defining feature of the Bantu verb is the terminal vowel *a*, which almost invariably ends the verbal root. Rare departures from this rule are typically the result of borrowed words from English, Portuguese, or Arabic. The terminal vowel can be changed to form different meanings: changing *a* to *e* forms the subjunctive tense, while changing it to *i* provides a negative sense in certain tenses. Additionally, nouns can be created from verbs by changing the terminal vowel and potentially adding a personal prefix. For example, in Luganda, the root *senyua* (to pardon) becomes *ki-senyuo* (a pardon) or *mu-senyui* (a pardoner).

The Bantu languages also employ a system of concord, involving the repetition of pronominal particles. This is seen in the structure of sentences where pronouns and descriptors agree with the subject.

## Regional Groupings and Dialects
The Bantu field is divided into various groups across Africa:

*   **Cameroon and West Africa:** The *Duala* group, found on the estuary and delta of the Cameroon river, is of a "purer Bantu type." Other groups include the *Isubu-Bakwiri* of the coast-lands north of the Cameroon delta and the *Barondo-Bakundu* group, which extends from the Rio del Rey to the Sanaga and Nyong rivers. The *Fan* (or *Pangwe*) forms of speech are described as so corrupt they are only just recognizable as Bantu.
*   **Central Africa and Congo:** The *Bangala-Bobangi-Liboko* group comprises commercial languages of the Upper Congo, extending as far north as the limits of the Bantu domain at approximately 3deg 30' N. The *Bateke* (*Batio*) group includes dialects west of the Sanga river and northwest of the Lower Congo.
*   **Gabun and Coast-lands:** The *Mpongwe-Orungu* and *Aduma* languages are found in French Gabun, shading south into the *Kakongo* dialects of the Loango and Sete Kama coast, including *Ba-kama*, *Ba-nyanga*, *Ma-yombe*, *Ba-vili*, *Ba-kamba*, and *Ka-kongo*.

## Semi-Bantu Languages
Certain languages are classified as "semi-Bantu." These include *Mbe*, *Bati*, *Nki*, *Mbudikum*, *Bafut*, and *Bayon*. Located eastwards and north-eastwards of the *Barondo-Bakundu* group, these languages evince affinities with the *Fan* forms of speech and the speech found in the basin of the Central Benue river.

## Non-Bantu Comparisons
Several language groups exist in proximity to the Bantu but are distinct. The *Bondonga* and *Wamanga* languages, located at the northern and north-eastern bend of the Congo between the Rubi river and Stanley Falls, are not Bantu; they are allied with the *Mundu* of the Egyptian Sudan and the *Mbuba-Momfu* of Nepoko and the Ituri.

While some non-Bantu languages, such as those of the lower Niger, Benue, and North Togoland, share affinities in numerals (specifically 2, 3, 4, and sometimes 5) and a few verbal roots, they lack the characteristic Bantu concord. Similarly, while the Timne languages of Sierra Leone use pronominal prefixes and a system of concord similar to the Bantu, they show no resemblance in word-roots.
