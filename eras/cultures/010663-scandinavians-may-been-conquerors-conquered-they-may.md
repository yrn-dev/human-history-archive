# Scandinavians in History and Linguistics

The historical and linguistic presence of Scandinavians is characterized by their influence on the British Isles and their classification within broader linguistic groups. Their legacy is evident in the administrative, legal, and linguistic structures of specific English regions, as well as in the academic debates regarding the relationship between language and ethnicity.

## Linguistic Classification and the "Aryan" Term
In the study of philology, the term "Aryan" has been used by scholars such as Max Muller as a convenient short term for the body of languages known as Indo-Germanic or Indo-European. According to Muller, "Aryas" are defined strictly as those who speak Aryan languages, regardless of their blood, color, hair, or skull characteristics. 

In this linguistic context, the term applies to various groups, including Germans, Romans, Greeks, Hindus, Celts, and Slaves. Muller explicitly avoids ethnological significations, asserting that the blue-eyed and fair-haired Scandinavians may have been conquerors or they may have been conquered. He suggests they might have adopted the language of their subjects or their "darker lords," or vice versa. For Muller, calling a group "Aryan" predicates nothing about them except the grammar of their language.

## Scandinavian Influence in the Danelagh
The impact of Scandinavian settlement is heavily concentrated in the district known as the Danelagh. In this region, the influence is visible across several societal layers:

### Place Names and Language
The place nomenclature of Northern Northamptonshire, Nottinghamshire, Lincolnshire, and Yorkshire is Scandinavian rather than native English. A significant proportion of Danish place-names are found throughout the remaining districts of the Danelagh. It is probable that Scandinavian dialects were the almost sole language spoken in this district until the end of the 10th century. Following an intermediate bilingual state, English eventually triumphed, though large numbers of words from the earlier Scandinavian speech were adopted into the English language.

### Administrative and Social Divisions
The divisions of land in these areas are foreign rather than native. The grouping of shires around a county town, as opposed to old national shires, is likely of Scandinavian origin. This includes the division of Lincolnshire and Yorkshire into "ridings." Furthermore, the use of "wapentakes" instead of "hundreds" in Rutlandshire, Nottinghamshire, Northamptonshire (partially), Lincolnshire, Leicestershire, Derbyshire, and Yorkshire serves as a mark of Danish influence.

Socially, documents such as the Domesday book reveal classes of society with purely Norse names, including *bonde*, *thrall*, *karl*, *karlman*, *dreng*, *hold*, and *lysing*. Additionally, the system of taxation in these districts utilized *carucates* rather than virgates and hides, and employed a duodecimal system of reckoning instead of a decimal one.

## Legal and Lexical Contributions
The Scandinavian legal system was highly developed and left abundant traces in the Danelagh. A primary example is the institution of "lawmen," a judicial body found in cities such as Chester, York, Lincoln, Stamford, and Cambridge. These lawmen are considered the ultimate source of the jury and were of Scandinavian origin. Many other legal terms used in the Danelagh can be definitely traced to Scandinavian sources.

Beyond law and administration, specific English words are attributed to Scandinavian origins. The verb "lug," meaning to pull or drag a heavy object, likely derives from the Swedish *lugg* (forelock) and *lugga* (to pull). This is the root of the word "luggage," referring to items that must be "lugged" about. In Scotland, "lug" is the regular dialectical word for "ear," and in English, it refers to ear-shaped handles on earthenware or metal pots. This root may also have led to the "lug-sail" (a four-sided sail hung obliquely to the mast) and the "lugger" sailing vessel, although the latter may also be connected to the Dutch *logger* (a fishing-boat using drag-nets).
