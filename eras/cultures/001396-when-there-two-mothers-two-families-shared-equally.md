# Inheritance and Family Distribution

The distribution of estates and the rights of family members vary significantly across different historical and legal systems, with specific rules governing the roles of parents, children, and multiple spouses.

## Estate Distribution and Parental Roles
In certain historical legal frameworks, all legitimate children shared equally in the estate of their father upon his death. However, specific reservations were made for dower for daughters, a bride-price for unmarried sons, or property specifically deeded to favorite children by the father. While the eldest son did not possess a birthright, he typically served as the executor, equalizing shares after accounting for what each child had already received; he might even grant excess portions to others from his own share.

In cases where a father had two mothers, the two families initially shared equally in the father's estate, though in later times, the first family received two-thirds. Regarding the property of the mothers, children shared in their own mother's property but held no share in the property of a stepmother.

In other systems, such as those documented in Japanese history, precise rules for inheritance designated that a mother and a step-mother ranked equally with the eldest son, with each receiving two parts. In this system, younger sons received one part, while female children and concubines received one-half of a part.

## Divine Ordinances on Inheritance
The Qur'an provides specific settled portions ordained by Allah regarding the inheritance of children and parents, to be distributed after the payment of debts and legacies. The male child is assigned a portion equal to that of two females. If there are two or more daughters, their share is two-thirds of the inheritance; if there is only one daughter, her share is one-half.

The shares for parents are as follows:
*   **With children:** Each parent receives a sixth share of the inheritance.
*   **Without children:** If the parents are the only heirs, the mother receives a third.
*   **With siblings:** If the deceased left brothers or sisters, the mother's share is a sixth.

Regarding spouses, a husband's share of what his wives leave is one-half if there is no child, and one-fourth if there is a child. Conversely, a wife's share of what her husband leaves is one-fourth if there is no child, and one-eighth if there is a child. In instances where the deceased left neither descendants nor ascendants but left a brother or sister, each receives a sixth; if there are more than two, they share in a third.

## Adoption and Family Status
In some societies, adoption required the consent of the biological parents, typically through a deed that ended the child's claim upon them. Certain individuals, including slaves, palace officials, hierodules, and vestals, had no rights over their children and could not object to adoption. Similarly, illegitimate children and foundlings had no parents to object. An adopted child was considered a full heir and could be assigned the position of eldest son via contract, though they were usually the residuary legatee. Severe penalties existed for adopted children who discovered their true parents and attempted to return to them, including the tearing out of the tongue or eye.

## Family Structures and Social Dynamics
Different cultures maintained distinct patriarchal or marital arrangements. In China, homes were arranged on a patriarchal system where the father and mother ranked equally regarding their juniors; the mother received the same share of ancestral worship after death and deference in life as the father. Marriage typically occurred around age sixteen for girls and eighteen for boys, with the girl joining the paternal home of her husband.

In other historical accounts, such as that of Dionysius the elder, a man might marry two wives simultaneously. Dionysius married a Lokrian named Doris and a Syracusan named Aristomache on the same day. He spent an equal share of his time with each, supping with both and alternating nights between them.
