# Tibet and the North-Eastern Mountain Systems

Tibet, known to its inhabitants as *Bod* or *Bodyul*, is a rugged table-land that occupies the summit of a great mountain mass. This region serves as a complete natural line of division across a large portion of the Asian continent. The table-land is narrow in relation to its length and is characterized by a succession of mountain ranges that generally follow the direction of the land's length. These ranges frequently rise into regions of perpetual snow. Between the flanks of these mountains are narrow, closely hemmed-in valleys with moderate inclinations, which occasionally open into wide plains. These plains are often occupied by rivers or salt lakes that have no outflow.

## Geographical Boundaries and Extent
The eastern termination of Tibet is marked by a line of snowy mountains that flank China on the west, situated between the 27th and 35th parallels of latitude and approximately 103 degrees east. To the west, the table-land extends beyond political limits to about 70 degrees east. In this western direction, the ranges covered in perpetual snow continue as far as Samarkand before diminishing in height and ending in low hills north of Bokhara.

To the south and west, the bounding territories are fixed by Indian survey determinations of Himalayan peaks. In the north, the region is connected via survey to Kashmir and India through Chinese Turkestan explorations. The Kuen-lun mountains, extending from the frontiers of Kashmir (north of Leh) almost due east to the Chinese province of Kansu, serve as the northern limit of the Chang Tang, which are the elevated highland steppes of Tibet.

## The North-Eastern Branch and Tsaidam
From the Kuen-lun mountains, a minor system branches to the north-east from a point at approximately 83 degrees east longitude. This system culminates in the Altyn Tagh (also referred to as the *Astin-tagh*) and extends eastwards as a continuous water-divide to the Nan Shan mountains, located north of the Koko Nor basin. 

The *Astin-tagh* is considered by competent judges—including Przhevalsky, Sven Hedin, Bogdanovich, and Grenard—to be a subsidiary range or branch of the main Kuen-lun range. Rather than a single continuous chain, it consists of two parallel main ranges, increasing to three in the east and up to four parallel main ranges to the north-east of Tsaidam. These are flanked by various offshoots, spurs, and subsidiary chains. This system swells into the *massif* of Anambaruin-ula, which contains at least three minor parallel chains, before contracting again to two main ranges. The southerly of these is the Humboldt Range, which features a pass at 13,200 feet and likely continues as the south Koko-nor Range. The northern branch merges into the Nan-shan, or Southern Mountains.

Between Tibet and the low-lying sands of the Gobi lies the Tsaidam, a system of elevated valleys 8,000 to 9,000 feet above sea level. This area forms an intermediate steppe between the lowest and highest regions east of Lop Nor. This geographical arrangement explains why major trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed far to the north.

## Hydrography and River Systems
Tibet is the source of several major Asian rivers. The two great rivers of China, the Yang-tsze-kiang and the Hwang-ho (Yellow River), rise from the eastern face of Tibet; the Hwang-ho originates from the north-east angle and the Yang-tsze-kiang from the south-east. In Tibet, the main stream of the Yang-tsze-kiang is called the Dichu. Its chief feeder, the Ya-lung-kiang, rises near the Hwang-ho and is regarded as the territorial boundary between Tibet and China.

The great Tsanpo of Tibet and the Brahmaputra are the same river. North of the point where this river effects a great countermarch from east to west, the sources of the Mekong, Salween, Yang-tsze-kiang, and Hwang-ho are found, ordered from west to east. South of this point, the dual sources of the Irrawaddy rise between the Salween and the extreme eastern edge of the Brahmaputra basin. 

The Salween and Mekong flow parallel to each other for approximately 300 miles within narrow, precipitous troughs. These rivers, along with the Yang-tsze-kiang, are separated by a short space of 100 miles between the most eastern affluent of the Brahmaputra and the deep gorges of the upper Yang-tsze-kiang.

## Southern Extensions
From the eastern extremity of the Tibetan mountains (between the 95th and 100th meridians), high ranges extend south from about 35 degrees north. These ranges spread outwards and reach the sea in the Malay peninsula, Cochin-China, and the east flank of Bengal. These mountains are likely permanently snowy up to about 27 degrees north. Between these ranges flow the Irrawaddy, Salween, Menam, and Mekong, the valleys of which form the primary portions of Burma, Siam (including Laos), and Cochin-China (including Cambodia and Tongking).
