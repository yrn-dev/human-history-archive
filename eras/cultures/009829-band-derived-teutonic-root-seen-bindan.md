# Band

The term "band" possesses several distinct meanings and etymological origins, primarily relating to the act of binding, specific articles of clothing, architectural features, and organized groups of people.

## Etymology and General Meaning
In its most basic sense, a "band" is something that fastens or binds one thing to another, such as a tie, rope, or cord. An example of this application is found in book-binding, where straps are used to fasten sheets to the back. This usage is a variant of the word "bond" and is derived from the Teutonic root *bindan*, meaning "to bind." From this same source comes the word "bend," which originally referred to the act of fastening a string to a bow to curve and constrain it.

A separate but related meaning refers to a "strap," defined as a flat strip of material used for fastening. While ultimately sharing the same origin, this specific sense entered the English language directly from the French *bande*.

## Clothing and Accessories
The term "bands" refers to two small strips of white linen worn at the neck. These are survivals of the 17th-century falling collar and are utilized as part of academic, clerical, and legal dress. While usually white, the secular clergy of the Roman Church wear black bands that are edged with white. The "band-box," a light cardboard or chip box currently used for millinery, was originally designed to carry these neck-bands.

## Groups and Companies
When used to describe a troop or company, "band" is likely connected to the Teutonic *bindan* (to bind), though it entered English via French. This meaning appears to have originated in Romanic languages, such as Portuguese, Spanish, and Italian (*banda*), before moving into Teutonic. There are two primary theories regarding the origin of this usage:
* It may be derived from a sash or "band" of a specific color worn by a troop of soldiers as a distinctive mark.
* It may refer to the medieval Latin *bandum* (banner), which was a "band" or strip of cloth attached to a pole.

In this context, the term is frequently applied to companies of musicians, particularly military bands serving in the navies or armies.

## Architecture
In the field of architecture, a "band" is a type of fascia or flat frieze that runs horizontally around parts of a building or a tower. This is particularly common in perpendicular work as base tables, often used alongside the long shafts typical of the 13th century. These bands generally feature bold projecting mouldings above and below. They may be carved with foliages, though they more commonly feature quatrefoils or cusped circles, which frequently contain shields of arms.

## Related Terms and Distinctions
The word "band" must be distinguished from the obsolete "bond," which originally referred to a householder. The word "bond" in that context comes from the Old English *bonda* (derived from the root *bua*, meaning to dwell or have a house) and is cognate with the English "boor" and German *Bauer*. 

Due to an early confusion between "bond" (the householder) and "bond" (from *bindan*, to bind), the meaning of the former shifted toward the idea of slavery and serfdom. This confusion also affected the word "bondage," which properly meant "tenure in villeinage" but is now used as a synonym for slavery. A remnant of the original meaning of "bondager" survives in the south of Scotland and Northumberland, where it refers to a female outworker supplied by a married farm-labourer as a condition of his tenancy to provide field-labour.
