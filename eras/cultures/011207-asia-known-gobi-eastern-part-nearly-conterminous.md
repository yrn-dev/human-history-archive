# Asia: Geography and Natural History

## Continental Form and Boundaries
Viewed as a whole, Asia possesses the form of a great isosceles spherical triangle. Its north-eastern apex is located at East Cape (Vostochnyi) in the Bering Strait. From this point, two equal sides of approximately 6,500 miles extend to the southern point of Arabia in the west and the extremity of the Malay peninsula in the east. The base of this triangle, spanning about 60 degrees of a great circle or 4,500 miles, is deeply indented by the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea on either side of the Indian peninsula.

The north-western boundary is roughly indicated by a great circle drawn from East Cape to the southern point of Arabia, passing over the Ural Mountains, the western Caspian, and the boundary between Asiatic Turkey and Persia. Asia Minor and the north-western half of Arabia lie outside this circle. The eastern boundary is similarly approximated by a great circle from East Cape to the Malay peninsula, passing near the coasts of Cochin-China, China, and Manchuria.

## The Central Asian Desert and Plateaus
The great desert of central Asia, known as the Gobi, is bounded to the north and north-west by the Tian-shan and the Yablonoi Mountains, to the south by the northern face of the Tibetan plateau, and to the east by the Khingan range. Its western portion forms eastern or Chinese Turkestan, while its eastern part is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia. No part of this desert appears to be less than 2,000 feet above sea level. Elevations in the plains near Yarkand and Kashgar range from 4,000 to 6,000 feet, and the route between Kiakhta and the Chinese frontier never drops below 3,000 feet. Some mountain ridges traversing this area exceed 12,000 or 10,000 feet.

A central depression, which may have once been a vast inland sea, extends westwards from the east of the Gobi through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan along the 40th parallel north. This depression is limited to the west by the Pamir's great elevation and the meridional range of Sarikol. While the level between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the Hwang-ho sources likely never exceeds 2,000 feet, research suggests that central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, may actually be below sea level.

To the west of British India (excluding Syria and Arabia) lies another extensive plateau of an area comparable to Tibet, though at a lower altitude. This region encompasses Armenia, Asia Minor, Persia (or Iran), Baluchistan, and Afghanistan. Its northern border follows nearly 36 degrees N to the Black Sea, the Sea of Marmora, and the southern shore of the Caspian. Its eastern flank is marked by hills near the Indus that generally stay between 4,000 and 5,000 feet, though some summits exceed 10,000 feet.

## Hydrography and Continental Divides
The continent is characterized by a great central water-parting or divide. This divide stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and Elburz to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, and finally to the Bering Strait. While some ranges like the Altai, Khangai, Hindu Kush, and Tian-shan reach great heights, much of this divide remains below 5,000 feet. 

South of this divide, the land drops to the central depression of the Gobi, a nearly waterless interior where local drainage is lost to swamps or deserts. Further south lies another mighty hydrographic barrier that separates this depression from the low plains of India, Siam, China, and the Amur.

## Regional Characteristics and Ancient Divisions
The peninsula of Arabia and its north-western continuation, Syria, are among the hottest and driest regions. Except for the northern area conterminous with the Mesopotamian plain (which reaches about 700 feet at its highest point), the land consists of low mountains between 3,000 and 4,000 feet with scanty vegetation. In northern Syria, the mountains of Lebanon rise to approximately 10,000 feet.

In ancient history, the northern and eastern parts of Asia were less known. These included India, the Sinarum Regio (China), and Serica (Cathay). To the north lay Asiatic Scythia and Asiatic Sarmatia (Tartary). Sarmatia was situated between the river Rha (Volga) and the river Tanais, which separates Asia from Europe. Scythia was divided by Mount Imaus, with the Massagetæ and Sacæ being the best-known nations of that region.

## Flora and Fauna
The ornithology of northern Asia is closely linked to that of Europe, with nearly three-fourths of well-known European species extending through Siberia to the Japanese islands. Tropical forms are absent, replaced by cold and temperate groups. The genus *Phasianus* is restricted in its wild state to northern Asia, and eared pheasants (*Crossoptilon*) and gold pheasants (*Thaumalea*) are confined to north eastern Asian mountains.

The seas of southern Asia are home to various fish species. The *Percidae* (perches) are well-represented, particularly the genus *Serranus*. The *Squamipennes* (scaly-finned fishes) are found primarily near coral reefs. Other notable species include the *Mullidae* (red mullets), the *Stromateidae* (pomfrets), and the *Polynemidae*, which includes the mango fish, a delicacy found from Siam to the Bay of Bengal. Additionally, sword fishes (*Xiphidae*), lancet fishes (*Acanthuridae*), and scabbard fishes (*Trichuridae*) are distributed throughout the southern seas.
