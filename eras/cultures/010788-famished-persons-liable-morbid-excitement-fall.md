# Morbid Excitement and Fasting

In the study of human history and behavior, the state of morbid excitement is associated with various physiological, psychological, and spiritual conditions. One significant cause of such excitement is the state of being famished, which has been utilized across diverse cultures to achieve specific supernatural or spiritual goals.

## Fasting and Imaginative Ecstasies
Among various populations, including those described as savages, fasting is a widespread practice used to induce morbid excitement. Persons who are famished are susceptible to imaginative ecstasies, during which they may experience visions, see spectres, and converse with angels and gods. These states are often viewed as a means of receiving supernatural revelations.

Historical and cultural examples of this practice include:
*   **Biblical Accounts:** King Saul is noted to have eaten no bread for a full day and night, a state of weakness and famine that preceded the witch of Endor revealing the ghost of Samuel to him.
*   **North American Indians:** Ecstatic fasting is a regular practice. Individuals record their revelations and visions over a season; if these are verified by events, the elders of the tribe recognize the person as a supernaturally gifted being and reward them with chieftaincy.
*   **The Semitic East:** There is an old belief that fasting in the wilderness for forty days and nights grants power over the Djinns.
*   **India:** The Indian magician fasts for twelve days before performing a cure or producing rain, while the *yogi* fasts until the gods of his Pantheon are seen face to face.
*   **The Bogomils:** This group practiced fasting until they saw the Trinity face to face.

## Religious and Ascetic Applications
Fasting has also been employed within early church traditions for similar purposes of spiritual vision. In the *Shepherd of Hermas*, prayer and frequent fasts are rewarded with a vision of the church. Extra-canonical sources describe James the Less vowing to fast until he was granted a vision of the risen Lord, which occurred after a long and rigorous fast. Some saints are similarly credited with receiving glimpses of the beatific vision through fasting. Dr. Tylor suggests that the consumption of meat and bread might have prevented such "angel's visits," implying that the act of eating could close the "gates of heaven" to the ascetic's gaze.

## Other Forms of Morbid Excitement
Beyond the intentional use of fasting, morbid excitement appears in various other contexts within the archive's records:

**Psychological and Behavioral States**
Morbid excitement can be driven by intense emotional distress or mental instability. For example, a despot's agony of sorrow and brooding over the hatred of the Hellenic world drove him toward madness and a state of morbid excitement. This led to extreme cruelty, resulting in the execution of close friends and partisans based on false accusations or surmises. Additionally, Professor Morselli posits that suicide accomplished through prolonged agony or painful means is, in ninety-nine out of a hundred cases, the act of a mind disordered by madness, fanaticism, or morbid excitement.

**Medical and Physiological Conditions**
In medical contexts, "morbid" refers to diseased or unstable states. Bromide of potassium and bromide of sodium are used as sedatives for all forms of morbid mental excitement, as well as for mania and hyperaesthesia. In respiratory health, the asthmatic state is characterized by a respiratory centre in an unstable and excitable condition, with a morbid connection to part of the nasal apparatus. Furthermore, capillary bronchitis involves the need to expel "morbid secretion" from the fine bronchi. In the study of kidney diseases, "morbid processes" in the kidney can result in actual lesions, changes in urine composition, and broader effects on the body.
