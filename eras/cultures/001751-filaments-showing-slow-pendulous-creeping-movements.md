# Filaments Showing Slow Pendulous and Creeping Movements

In the study of biological structures, filaments exhibiting slow pendulous and creeping movements are found across different classifications of organisms, ranging from microscopic bacteria to botanical species. These movements vary in nature, from the oscillatory motions of certain bacteria to the growth patterns of plants.

## Trichobacterinae and Beggiatoa
Within the order Trichobacterinae, the vegetative body consists of cell-filaments that may be branched or unbranched. The segments of these filaments separate as swarm-cells, known as gonidia. While some families within this order, such as the Trichobacteriaceae, possess rigid, non-motile, and sheathed filaments (including *Crenothrix*, *Thiothrix*, and *Cladothrix*), others exhibit distinct motility.

The genus *Beggiatoa* is characterized by filaments that show slow creeping and pendulous movements. Unlike the rigid filaments of its relatives, *Beggiatoa* possesses no distinct sheath and contains sulphur particles. In its vegetative state, *Beggiatoa* displays peculiar, slow, and oscillatory movements. These motions are compared to those of *Oscillatoria* among the Cyanophyceae. Because no cilia have been observed in *Beggiatoa* and the organism possesses a firm cell-wall, the mechanism behind this movement remains unexplained.

## Botanical Creeping Filaments
Creeping movements are also observed in the development of certain plants. In the moss *Funaria hygrometrica*, a developed protonema includes a creeping filament. This filament is characterized by brown walls, and it serves as the point from which filaments of chlorophyll-containing cells arise.

Additionally, the genus *Asparagus* (of the natural order Liliaceae) features a short, creeping underground stem. From this stem, slender, branched aerial shoots emerge. While the stem creeps underground, the flowers of the *Asparagus* plant are described as small, whitish, and pendulous.

## Growth and Movement in Filamentous Bacteria
The growth of filamentous bacteria, such as *Bacillus ramosus*, involves a process of uniform elongation and division. A rodlet elongates until its length doubles, at which point it is divided by a median septum. If the resulting daughter cells remain connected, they form a filament that repeats this process of elongation and division.

The growth of these filaments is influenced by several environmental factors:
* **Temperature:** There are optimum, minimum, and maximum temperatures for growth. While a slow rise in temperature may accelerate growth, any sudden alteration can cause a check.
* **External Inhibitors:** Growth can be brought to a standstill or checked by exposure to light, the exhaustion or dilution of food-media, and the presence of metabolic products or traces of poisons.
* **Cellular Injury:** The death or injury of a single cell within a filamentous series increases the doubling period because the potential progeny of that cell are removed. Such destruction can be caused by the attacks of other organisms.
