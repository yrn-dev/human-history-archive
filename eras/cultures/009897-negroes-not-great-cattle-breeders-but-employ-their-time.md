# Cattle Breeding and Agricultural Practices among African Peoples

The practices of cattle breeding and agriculture vary significantly among different African populations, with some groups specializing in pastoralism and others focusing on crop cultivation.

## The Bongo
The Bongo are a tribe of Nilotic negroes inhabiting the south-west portion of the Bahr-el-Ghazal province in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. Unlike other Negroes of the upper Nile, the Bongo are not great cattle-breeders. Instead, they employ their time in agriculture. Their primary cultivated crops include durra, sesame, tobacco, and sorghum. 

In addition to these crops, the Bongo consume fungi, tubers, and fruits found in their environment. Their diet is notably inclusive of almost every creature, including reptiles, insects, beasts, and birds, though they do not eat dogs. They are described as despising no flesh, whether it is putrid or fresh, and have been known to drive vultures from carcasses.

## The Baggara
In contrast to the Bongo, the Baggara (meaning "Cowherds") are described as great cattle owners and breeders. They are African "Arabs" of Semitic origin who occupy the country west of the White Nile, principally in Kordofan, between Dar Nuba and the Shilluk territory. As a purely pastoral people, the Baggara move from pasture to pasture based on the availability of food.

The Baggara utilize oxen as pack animals and saddle animals. While they possess firearms, their customary weapons are the sword and lance. They have a reputation for being resolute fighters and were fervent supporters of the mahdi during his 1882 revolt against the Egyptians, serving as his real fighting force. The mahdi's successor, the khalifa Abdullah, was himself a Baggara.

## Other Regional Groups
In the region of Damaraland in south-western Africa (part of German South-West Africa), the population is divided into two distinct groups. The "Cattle Damara" are of distinct Bantu-Negro descent, speak a Bantu language, and breed cattle in the plains. They are distinguished from the "Hill Damara," who are Negroes with much Hottentot blood and have adopted the Hottentot tongue.

In Kordofan, the population is roughly divided between Nubas in the hills and Arabs in the plains. The Nubas are described as a primitive, very black race of small build with distinctive negro features. They are believed to be the aboriginal inhabitants of the country and the original stock of the Nile Valley Nubians. In the plains of Kordofan, the nomad Arabs are divided into two classes: the *Baggara* (cattle owners) in the south and the *Siat El Ilbil* (camel owners) in the dry northern regions.

## Bantu Migrations and Diversity
The peoples who speak Bantu languages do not belong to a single, definite type of negro, but exhibit great diversity in physical characteristics and outward appearance. This group includes the Ova-herero, the Zulu-Kaffirs, the Ba-tonga, the people of the Great Lakes, and the Babangi of the upper Congo. 

It is deduced that approximately 3,000 years ago, a powerful tribe of negroes speaking the Bantu mother-language—physically allied to the negroes of the southern Lake Chad basins and the south-western Nile, though impregnated with the Caucasian Hamite—moved from the heart of Africa into the southern half of the continent. This migration resulted in the assimilation or absorption of the majority of the preceding populations, which had included Forest-negro, Nilotic-negro, Hottentot, and Bushman.
