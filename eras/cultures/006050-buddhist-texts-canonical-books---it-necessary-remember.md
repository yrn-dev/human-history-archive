# Buddhist Texts: The Canonical Books

The canonical books of Buddhism serve as the primary authority for the ethics and philosophy of the earliest Buddhists. These texts originated from the teachings of the Buddha, who, following the literary habits of Indian teachers of his period, taught exclusively through conversation.

## Composition and Language
The Buddha was a highly educated man who spoke to others of similar education. He embodied his doctrines in set phrases known as *sutras*, which served as *memoria technica* or catchwords. These phrases allowed for the preservation and communication of opinions, as the lack of suitable writing materials at the time made the production of lengthy books impossible. The Buddha used these sutras as bases for fuller expositions, which he enlarged upon in various ways on different occasions.

While the Brahmins of the period utilized Sanskrit—which was already a dead language—the Buddha purposely composed his teachings in Pali, the ordinary conversational idiom of the day. Sanskrit was not used for canonical books, nor for any Buddhist works, until long after this period.

## The Nikayas and Early Collections
Following the death of the Buddha, his disciples collected his sayings into the Four Nikayas, or "collections." These texts are not believed to have reached their final form until approximately fifty or sixty years after his death. A supplementary Nikaya was later added, containing verses and sayings largely ascribed to the disciples rather than the Buddha. Additions to this supplementary collection were made as late as the 3rd century B.C., during the time of Asoka.

For one or two generations, these collected books were transmitted via memory, although written memoranda were likely used. From their inception, the teachings were accompanied by running commentaries. Approximately one hundred years after the Buddha's death, a schism occurred within the community. The resulting two schools each maintained their own arrangement of the canon, continuing to use Pali or an allied dialect.

## Major Canonical Works and Supplementary Texts
The Buddhist canon includes several distinct types of literature:

*   **The Vinaya:** This work provides a complete statement of the rules of the order.
*   **The Jataka Book:** A collection of 550 verses consisting of folk-tales current in India. In these stories, the Buddha is identified as the hero in various previous births. While the verses are preserved, the prose exists only in the commentary.
*   **The Sutta Nipata:** A collection of fifty-five poems, most of which are short lyrics. An ancient commentary on these poems is included in the canon as a separate work.
*   **The Abhidhamma:** Seven later works were added to the canon to provide a more elaborate and classified exposition of the Dhamma (doctrine) as presented in the Nikayas. One such manual, the *Dhamma Sangani*, is estimated to date from the 4th century B.C. and focuses on the psychological conceptions underlying Buddhist ethics.

## Core Doctrines in the Texts
The canonical texts emphasize a specific vision of life and a rejection of certain metaphysical theories. A central position in these books is the systematic rejection of theories regarding the "soul." This is evident in the *Dialogues of Gotama*, where the Buddha's first discourse to his first converts is stated to have focused on this subject. Similarly, the *Kathavatthu*—the latest book in the canon—places the question of the "soul" at the head of its discussions. This theme is also prominent in the conversations between the Buddhist teacher Nagasena and the Greek king Menander, in which Nagasena convinces the king that the "soul" in the ordinary sense does not exist.

Furthermore, the canonical texts support the ideal of salvation in this world and in this life, achieved through self-mastery and self-culture. This is the salvation of the Arahat. This differs from later interpretations by European writers who, lacking authentic scriptures, mistakenly interpreted Nirvana as a state reached after death involving the "dying out" of a soul.

## Authorities on the Life of the Buddha
The canonical Pali sources provide episodes from the Buddha's life, including his birth (found in the *Majjhima Nikaya* and *Anguttara Nikaya*), the "Adoration of the babe" (in the *Sutta Nipata*), and his "going forth" (recorded in the *Sutta Nipata* and *Digha Nikaya*). Other long episodes preserved in the *Majjhima Nikaya* and *Vinaya* cover his years of penance, the attainment of Buddhahood, the conversion of his first five converts, and his visit to Kapilavastu. The *Digha Nikaya* (specifically the *Mahaparinibbana Suttanta*) records his last days.
