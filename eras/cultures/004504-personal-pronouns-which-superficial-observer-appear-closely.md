# Personal Pronouns in Various Languages

The study of personal pronouns across different linguistic groups reveals diverse methods of expressing identity, politeness, and grammatical relationship. In several language families, pronouns evolve from original nominative forms to new structures based on oblique bases, or shift in usage to accommodate social honorifics.

## Basque Pronouns and Numerals
In the Basque language, personal pronouns include *ni* ("I"), *hi* ("thou"), *gu* ("we"), and *zu* ("you"). In modern usage, *zu* has transitioned into a polite form of "thou." To express a true plural "you" (referring to more than one person), the pluralizing sign *k--zuek* is suffixed. Third-person pronouns are not distinct but are instead demonstratives: *hura* or *kura* ("that"), *hau* or *kau* ("this"), and *ori* or *kori* ("this" or "that"). These demonstratives also function as articles, as seen in *gazt-en-or* ("this younger one") and *andre-ori* ("this lady at some distance").

Other forms appearing in verbal inflexions include *d*, *it*, and *t* ("I" or "me"), exemplified by *d-akus-t* ("it see I") and *d-arrai-t* ("it follows me"). The reflective "self" is expressed by the word *buru*, meaning "head." While a relative pronoun does not exist, a verbal participle ending in *n* is used instead (e.g., *doana*, "he who is going"), though modern Basque uses the interrogative *zein* or *zoin* as a relative due to the influence of Spanish or French. Common interrogatives include *nor* ("who") and *zer* ("what"), while indefinite pronouns include *bat* ("one"), *batzu* ("several"), and *elkar* ("both").

The Basque numeral system is vicesimal. For example, 34 is expressed as *hogoi ta hamalaur* ("twenty and fourteen"). Numbers one through ten are *bat, bi, hiru, lau, bortz* (or *bost*), *sei, zazpi, zortzi, bederatzi,* and *hamar*. Twenty is *hogoi* (or *hogei*), forty is *berrogoi* (twice twenty), and one hundred is *ehun*. There is no genuine word for a thousand.

## Indo-Aryan and Related Pronouns
In certain languages that closely follow Prakrit originals, there is no dedicated third-person pronoun; this role is filled by demonstratives. Principal forms include *mi* ("I"), *amhi* ("we"), and *tu* ("thou"). The word *apan* means "self" but is also used as an honorific for the second person or to mean "we including you." Demonstratives include *ha* ("this"), *to* ("he" or "that"), and interrogatives such as *kon* ("who?") and *kay* ("what?").

In other related languages, such as those of the Eastern Group (including Maithili, Magahi, and Bhojpuri), singular personal pronouns have largely fallen into disuse. Plurals are used politely for singulars, and new plural forms are derived from these old plurals. While old singulars survive in the speech of villagers and in poetry, the nominatives have disappeared, replaced by new nominatives formed from oblique bases. For instance, in Maithili, Magahi, and Bhojpuri, the singular nominative for "I" is *ham*.

In the case of Bg. pronouns, the old nominative *mui* ("I") has been replaced by the modern *ami*, and *tui* ("thou") by *tumi*. Third-person references use *se* or *tini* ("he"), *e* or *ini* ("this"), and *o* or *uni* ("that"). In O., the words for "I" and "thou" are *ambhe* and *tumbhe*.

## Pronouns in Other Linguistic Contexts
In certain coast Cameroon and north-eastern Congo languages, the word *mbi* or *mba* is used for "I" or "me," which may be borrowed from Sudanian Mundu tongues. The first-person possessive pronoun is derived from forms such as *-ami* and *-angi*.

In English linguistic history, the northern dialect attained the simplicity of Modern English in the declension of pronouns before the end of the 13th century. In contrast, the southern dialect retained a large number of inflections, and the midland dialect retained a considerable number.

## Verbal Integration of Pronouns
Some languages incorporate pronouns directly into the verb. In Basque, the conjugation incorporates the subject pronouns as well as the direct and indirect complements, allowing a single transitive form to have twenty-four variations. For example, *dakigu* means "we are knowing it."

In other systems, tenses are formed by adding personal suffixes to passive participles. In these instances, if there is no third-person pronoun, the third-person verb has no termination and remains an unmodified participle. For a masculine subject, the present "I rise" is *uth^ato* and the past "I rose" is *uth^alo*.
