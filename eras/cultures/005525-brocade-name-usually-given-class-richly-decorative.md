# BROCADE

Brocade is the term generally applied to a class of richly decorative shuttle-woven fabrics. These textiles are frequently produced using coloured silks and may incorporate gold and silver threads. The ornamental features of brocade are created as additions to the main fabric, which can result in the stiffening of the material or, more commonly, the creation of a low relief effect on the face of the cloth. A distinctive appearance is visible on the back of the fabric, where the floating threads or weft of the broached or brocaded sections are either clipped away or hang in loose groups.

## Etymology and Terminology
The term is derived from the Latin word *broccus*, which is related to the French *brocarts* and *brocher*, the Italian *brocato*, and the Spanish *brocar*. These terms imply a form of broaching or stitching; consequently, fabrics woven to look as though they have been stitched or broached are called brocades.

Historical documentation shows distinctions between different types of enriched fabrics. A Spanish document from 1375 distinguishes *brocats d'or é d'argent* (gold and silver brocades) from *los draps d'or é d'argent o de seda* (cloths of gold, silver, or silk). This distinction is evident when comparing Lyons silks that are *brochés* with gold, silk, or other materials against Indian kincobs or cloths of gold. While many Persian silk-weavings and Indian kincobs are often referred to as brocades, they are not technically *broché* or brocaded because they lack floating threads. In French, the word *brocher* is also used for certain silk stuffs that are not considered brocades in the same sense as *brocarts*. Similarly, a 1480 wardrobe account for King Edward IV describes "satyn broched with gold."

## Historical Production and Examples
The early origins of brocading can be traced to oriental weavers, particularly those in Syria, Persia, and regions of northern Africa and southern Europe under Saracen domination. 

Examples of this craftsmanship include:
* **Siculo-Saracenic textiles:** A specimen from the 11th or 12th century features a cream-coloured ground with red and olive green silks and gold thread. It contains a trellis framework with lozenge-shaped compartments housing geese *vis-à-vis* and pairs of seated animals (*addorsed regardant*), separated by conventional trees. The fabric includes Kufic inscriptions, including the repeated word "Arrahm[=a]n" (The Merciful). In this specific piece, gold thread is used to broach only the heads of the birds and animals.
* **The Hotel des Tiraz:** A sumptuous 12th-century Siculo-Saracenic weaving made of gold threads and coloured silks was produced at this famous establishment in Palermo. This fabric was created for an official robe for Henry IV (1165-1197) during his time as emperor of the Holy Roman Empire and is preserved in the cathedral of Regensburg.

## Regional Use and Regulation
In Japan, materials for clothing ranged from coarse homespun to rich Chinese brocade. However, the use of brocade was generally forbidden unless the wearer had received it as a gift from the Court in Kamakura or Kyoto.
