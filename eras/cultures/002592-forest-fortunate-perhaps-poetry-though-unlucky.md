# Forest

The term "forest" appears across historical records in various contexts, ranging from geographic regions and military refuges to metaphorical descriptions of literary movements.

## Forests in Northern Europe and Norway
In the historical accounts of Norway, forests served as critical strategic locations for displaced groups and military forces. During the 12th century, Olaf and his troop spent the winter of A.D. 1168 in the forest districts of the east. 

Later, in the period between A.D. 1174 and 1176, a group known as the Birkebeins—so named because they wound birch-tree bark around their legs due to their worn-out clothing—fled to the forests and deserted hill grounds after being overpowered by bondes and lendermen in Viken. The forest provided a tactical advantage for the Birkebeins; during an expedition at Krokaskog, they avoided an unlucky encounter with a large assembly of men-at-arms and bondes by felling brushwood across the roads and retiring into the forest.

## Forests in Greek Poetry and Resistance
In Greece, the forests are closely associated with the Klephts and Haiduks, groups of men who maintained a struggle against Ottoman tyranny from the mountains and solitudes of nature. The resulting Klephtic ballads, which are anonymous and composed in the language of the people, are described as breathing the aroma of the forests and mountains. These poems lend a voice to the trees and rocks, celebrating the prowess of the Klepht. In these works, the forest is depicted as a sanctuary of freedom; for example, the mountain Olympia is described as having a Klepht beneath every tree of its forests, remaining undesecrated by the footstep of the Turk.

## Literary and Metaphorical Applications
The concept of the forest has been used metaphorically to describe the structure of artistic works. The romantic drama (plays written up to 1642) is characterized not as a "cleared forest" where individual trees grow to shapely perfection, but rather as a "vast stifled jungle" filled with song, wild life, and recesses of poisoned darkness.

## Other Geographic and Cultural References
Historical texts mention forests in other global regions, such as the "furthest forests of India," where the River Buemar is said to be located according to certain versions of Alexander’s Letter to Aristotle. Additionally, in the Land of the Moon (U-nya-Mwezi) in East Africa, local elders maintain a tradition that their patriarchal ancestor became the first Tree after death to provide shade for his descendants. This holy tree is a site of pilgrimage, and it is believed that cutting a twig from it results in sudden and mysterious death.
