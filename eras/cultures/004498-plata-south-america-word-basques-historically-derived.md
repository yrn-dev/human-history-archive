# La Plata and the Basque People

## The Region of La Plata
La Plata refers to several geographical and administrative entities in South America. In Argentina, La Plata is the capital of the maritime province of Buenos Aires. This province is the largest and most populous in the republic, with an official 1903 population estimate of 1,251,000. It is bounded to the north by Entre Rios and Santa Fé, to the east by the Atlantic and the La Plata estuary, and to the south by the Atlantic. Other notable locations in the province include the seaside resort of Mar del Plata and various ports such as Bahia Blanca, Ensenada (La Plata), and San Nicolas.

Historically, the region has been the subject of extensive study. Don Felix de Azara (1746-1811) spent twenty years in South America as a commissioner tasked with delimiting the boundary between Portuguese and Spanish territories. His work, published in 1809, included an account of the discovery and history of Rio de la Plata and Paraguay. Additionally, the region is known for the "bastard chinchilla" (Chinchilla, La Plata), a species of animal with darker underwool and shorter fur than other varieties, which is used in the fur trade.

## The Basque People
The Basques are a people inhabiting Navarre and the three Basque Provinces of Guipuzcoa, Alava, and Biscay in Spain, as well as Mauleon and the arrondissement of Bayonne in France. They refer to themselves as *Eskualdunak*, meaning "those who possess the *Eskuara*," and they call their homeland Eskual-Herria. 

The term "Basques" is historically derived from *Vascones* (also written *Wascones*), a term that also gave rise to the name *Gascons*, though the Gascons are considered a very different race.

## Physical and Cultural Characteristics
The Basque people are characterized by a distinct physical type. While centuries of contact with other peoples have made a "pure" type difficult to find, researchers such as Collignon and Aranzadi have noted that Basques are generally taller than southern races, though not as tall as the British, Teutons, or Scandinavians. They are described as being fairer in face than the Navarrese and fairer than southern races, though less fair than northern Europeans. They are noted for an upright carriage and a fine profile, without a tendency toward prognathism.

Culturally, the Basques are defined by an obstinate conservatism, intense self-respect, and a pride of race. Historically, every Basque freeholder (*vecino*) could prove his nobility, making him eligible for any office under the *fueros* (laws) of Spain. Their social structure is not centered on towns; instead, they live in scattered habitations and small villages. 

The legal position of women in Basque society has been very high. Ancestral property was inherited by the eldest born, regardless of whether they were a boy or a girl. Furthermore, the *fueros* mandated more severe punishments for insults committed against or in the presence of a woman than for similar offenses among men.

## Language and Literature
The Basque language, *Eskuara* (also *uskara* or *euskara*), is an isolated tongue in Europe. It is a polysynthetic, incorporating, and agglutinative system of speech. Grammatically, it recalls Finnic and Magyar languages, occupying an intermediate linguistic position between Ugrian or Ugro-Altaic languages and those of the Americas. The exact meaning of the word *Eskuara* is not fully ascertained, but it likely corresponds to the idea of "clearly speaking."

Basque literature is relatively modern. The first printed book in the language, the *Linguae Vasconum Primitiae* (poems of Bernard d'Echepare), dates to 1545. The standard for the language is considered to be the Protestant translation of the New Testament by Jean de Licarrague, printed in 1571. Other literary forms include *pastorales*, which are open-air dramas derived from French materials.

## Emigration to the Americas
The Basques have a long history of colonization in the Americas, having been early pioneers in the cod-fishery off Newfoundland. Due to the decline of self-sufficient village industries following the invention of machinery, many Basques emigrated to agricultural regions of the Americas. 

Approximately 100,000 Basque emigrants reside in the Americas, with a primary concentration in the region of La Plata in South America. In these distant lands, French and Spanish Basques are identified simply as "Basques," distinct from other races. Their influence is evident in the older colonial families and in the publication of Basque newspapers in Los Angeles, California, and Buenos Aires.
