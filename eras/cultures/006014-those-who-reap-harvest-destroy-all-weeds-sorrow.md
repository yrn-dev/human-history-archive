# Those who reap this harvest destroy all the weeds of sorrow

The phrase "Those who reap this harvest destroy all the weeds of sorrow" originates from a narrative involving the Buddha and a wealthy brahmin farmer. In this account, the Buddha approached a rich farmer during a harvest home, standing by the field with an alms-bowl to beg. When the brahmin questioned why the Buddha begged instead of ploughing and sowing to earn his food, the Buddha responded that he too ploughed and sowed.

## The Spiritual Harvest
The Buddha explained his cultivation through a series of metaphors. In this spiritual farming, faith serves as the seed and self-combat acts as the fertilizing rain. The "weeds" that are destroyed through this process are the cleaving to existence. The Buddha identified wisdom as his plough, with modesty serving as the guiding-shaft. The process is driven by perseverance and guided by the rein of the mind. The field being worked is "in the law," and the resulting harvest is the "never-dying nectar of Nirvana." According to the Buddha, those who reap this specific harvest destroy all the weeds of sorrow.

## Agricultural and Natural Harvests
In physical agriculture, harvests vary by region, crop, and climate. In the land of Kyrene in Libya, there are three seasons of harvest. The process begins at the sea-coasts, moves to the hills in the middle, and concludes in the highest part of the land, resulting in a harvest period that lasts eight months.

In certain provinces, rice is the staple crop, with three annual harvests:
*   **Boro:** Spring rice, sown in October, transplanted a month later, and harvested in March and April.
*   **Aus:** Autumn rice, sown in April or May and harvested in August or September. This crop is grown on high ground and is harrowed when plants reach six inches to clear weeds and thin the crop.
*   **Aman:** Winter rice, the most extensive harvest of the year, grown on low land and harvested in December or January.

Other significant crops include wheat in Behar, oil-seeds such as *sarisha* (mustard), *til* (sesamum), and *lisi* or *masina* (linseed), as well as jute in Bengal.

## Legal and Economic Regulations of Harvests
Historical records indicate strict regulations regarding the reaping of harvests. In some Japanese systems, farmers were required to remain on their assigned land and keep it tilled. Landowners were tasked with investigating the actual amount of the harvest reaped alongside their tenants. Typically, two-thirds of the harvest went to the landowner and one-third remained with the farmer. However, if a natural calamity resulted in a harvest of less than two bushels per acre, the entire yield was left to the farmer.

## Metaphorical and Symbolic Harvests
The concept of the "harvest" is frequently applied to non-agricultural contexts:

*   **Knowledge and Ideas:** Germany is described as having reaped "unfailing harvests" of freely disseminated knowledge, which aided its rise as a manufacturing and trading power. Similarly, periods of revolution are viewed as times when men reap harvests of ideas that grew during interludes.
*   **Destruction:** The term is used to describe devastation. In the town of Longwy, the "angel of destruction" reaped a harvest of ruins, leaving a scene of desolation including wreckage of motor-cars, uniforms, and children's toys.
*   **Religious Growth:** In New England, the Baptist cause is noted for failing to "reap a like harvest" from the War of Independence as it had from the Great Awakening, due to a loss of popularity and failure to secure religious liberty.

## Harvests in Sacred Texts
The Qur'an contains a narrative regarding Joseph and the interpretation of a dream involving seven green ears of corn and seven dry ones. Joseph explains that there will be seven years of diligent sowing. He instructs that the harvests reaped during these years should be left in the ear, except for a small amount for eating. This preparation was necessary because seven "dreadful" or "hard" years would follow, which would devour everything laid by in advance, except for a small amount specifically guarded.

## The Nature of Weeds
While the Buddha viewed "weeds" as the cleaving to existence, in physical farming, weeds are seen as wasteful. For example, the daisy (known as *la marguerite* in French, *gowan* in Scotland, and *bairn wort* in Yorkshire) is considered a weed by farmers because it exhausts the soil and cannot be eaten by stock. Common meadow weeds include the ox-eye daisy (*Chrysanthemum Leucanthemum*).
