# Harrowing by Gift

The phrase "harrowing by gift" refers to a rejected etymological theory regarding the origin of the word "bonfire." This specific derivation suggests that the term is related to the phrase *boen-harow*, which translates to "harrowing by gift."

## Etymological Theories of Bonfire
The term "bonfire" appeared in the 16th century, though the earlier form "bone-fire" remained common until 1760. In Early English, it was written as "bone-fire," and in Scottish as "bane-fire." The earliest known derivation of the word is found in the *Catholicon Anglicum* (A.D. 1483), where it is listed as *ban fyre ignis ossium*, indicating that it was originally a fire of bones. In modern usage, the term describes any large fire lit in the open air for an occasion of rejoicing.

Several other derivations for the word have been proposed but are now rejected. One theory suggested the word was "Baal-fire," evolving through the forms *Bael* and *Baen* to *Bane*. Another theory proposed the "boon-fire" derivation by analogy with *boen-harow* ("harrowing by gift"). The suggestion behind this theory was that bonfires were "contribution" fires, in which every person in the neighborhood contributed a portion of the material. This is compared to the "contributed Ploughing Days" in Northumberland, which are referred to as *Bone-daags*.

## Other Uses of the Term Harrow
The term "harrow" appears in various contexts within the archive's records, ranging from agriculture and warfare to historical punishment.

### Agricultural and Mechanical Use
In agriculture, a harrow is a tool used for preparing fields. While oxen were used for this purpose until a relatively late time, and laws in Wales once forbade the use of horses for ploughing, the Bayeux tapestry contains an exceptional representation of a horse drawing a harrow.

### Military and Figurative Use
The term is used figuratively to describe the devastation of conflict. In the writings of Mary Johnston, the "harrow of war" is described as a force that, once set in motion, brings about an inevitable and fatalistic state of affairs.

### Historical Punishments
In historical accounts regarding the siege of Rabbah and other Ammonite cities, records indicate that inhabitants or garrisons were put "under, or cut them with saws, and under, or with harrows of iron, and under, or with axes of iron," and passed through brick-kilns. There is scholarly debate as to whether this described a method of tormenting people to death or if it meant they were forced into the lowest forms of slavery, such as hewing timber or harrowing fields.
