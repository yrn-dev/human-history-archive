# BORAGE

Borage (*Borago officinalis*) is a herb characterized by its bright blue flowers and its hairy stem and leaves. The name is pronounced like the word "courage" and possibly derives from the Latin word *borra*, meaning rough hair.

## Botanical Classification
Borage belongs to the order Boraginaceae, which is a member of the series Tubiflorae and the sympetalous section of dicotyledons. This order consists of approximately 1200 species across 85 genera. The primary center for these plants is the Mediterranean region, though they extend into Asia and central Europe, becoming less frequent as one moves north. A secondary, smaller center is located on the Pacific side of North America. The order is less developed within the south temperate zone.

Within the Boraginaceae order, borage is distinguished by its rotate corolla. This differs from other members of the order, such as comfrey (*Symphytum*), which has a tubular corolla, hounds-tongue (*Cynoglossum*), which is funnel-shaped, and alkanet (*Anchusa*), which is salver-shaped. In Britain, borage is found as a garden escape in waste ground.

## Physical Characteristics
Plants in the Boraginaceae order are generally rough-haired herbs that can be either annual or perennial; in rarer cases, such as *Ehretia* and *Cordia* (which are sub-tropical or tropical), they may be arborescent or shrubby. The leaves are usually narrow, entire, and generally alternate. 

A defining characteristic of the order is the dorsiventral (one-sided) inflorescence. The cyme begins as a close coil and uncoils as the flowers open. In many species, including *Symphytum*, *Echium*, and *Myosotis*, the flowers undergo a color change, starting as red in the bud and turning blue as they expand.

## Uses and Economic Value
The Boraginaceae order is considered to be of little economic value. However, borage and *Pulmonaria* were formerly utilized in medicine. Specifically, borage has been regarded as having virtue as a febrifuge and a cordial. In culinary and beverage applications, it is used as an ingredient in claret-cup and salads. Other members of the order provide different utilities; for example, the roots of *Alkanna tinctoria* (alkanet) yield brown or purple dyes.
