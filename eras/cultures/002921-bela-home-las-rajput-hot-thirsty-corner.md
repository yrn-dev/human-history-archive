# Bela and Las Bela

Bela, also known as Lus Beyla, is the capital of the small independent state of Las Bela. It is situated at 26 deg. 27' 30" N. lat. and 66 deg. 45' 0" E. long., at an elevation of 350 feet above sea level. The town is positioned on a rocky site above the banks of the Purali river.

## Geography and Environment
Las Bela is located to the south of Kalat in Baluchistan, bordered by Sind to the east and Makran to the west. The state covers an area of 6,357 square miles. The region is characterized by low-lying, alluvial plains that are hot and malarial, occupying approximately 6,000 square miles on the north-east corner of the Arabian Sea. Despite these conditions, the plains are highly fertile and well-irrigated, receiving a plentiful water supply from two rivers flowing from the north: the Kud and the Purali (known anciently as the Arabus).

The landscape is defined by hill ranges to the east—locally known as the Kirthar and the Mor—which separate the plains from Sind. These ranges diminish in height as they approach the sea, creating a route between Bela and Karachi. To the west, the terrain consists of an infinity of minor ridges in parallel formation that eventually form the coast barrier of Makran.

## History and Trade
From time immemorial, Las Bela has served as a vital link in the great trading route between Sind and Persia. This strategic position is the primary reason for the historical importance of Bela. In the medieval period of Arab ascendancy in Sind, the route traversed the barren ridges of the west before entering the Kolwah valley, providing an open road to Persia and Kej.

Ancient history in the region includes traces of Arab occupation, possibly Himyaritic, found in stone ruins at Gondakeha on the Kud river. The Greek name "Arabis" for the Purali suggests a prehistoric connection with Asiatic Ethiopians. On the coast near the village of Sonmiani, the indentation of the former bay of Morontobara can be traced. Additionally, the Turanian town of Rhambakia, once a center for the trade of "bdellium," was situated on the borders of Makran.

In the 7th century A.D., Las Bela was governed by a Buddhist priest during a period when the province of Gandava was Buddhist. Evidence of this era remains in the form of Buddhist caves excavated in conglomerate cliffs at Shahr-i-Rogan (also called Gondrani) near Gondakeha.

With the subsequent influx of Arabs into Makran, Bela rose to prominence under the name Armel (or Armabel). During this time, the delta featured several towns with citadels, bazaars, and caravanserais, including Kambali (possibly the ruins at Khairokot) and Yusli near modern Uthal. These sites were mapped by the medieval Arab geographer Ibn Haukal. The site of Armabel, which was destroyed in 710 by Mahommed Kasim during his march to Sind, is likely represented by Karia Pir, located 1-1/2 miles east of the modern city. Ruins at Karia Pir, Khairokot, and Tijarra Pir contain medieval relics, seals, and Arab pottery.

## Population and Governance
As of 1901, the population of Las Bela was 56,109, of whom 54,040 were Mussulmans. The state is ruled by the Jam (or Cham), who serves as a protected chief under the British Raj.

The dominant tribe in the province is the Lumris, or Lasis, from whom the prefix "Las" in Las Bela originates. The Lumris are comparatively recent arrivals who displaced the earlier occupants of the region, the Brahui and the Tajik. This influx is attributed to a Rajput population. To the south of Kalat, the Mingals also occupy the hills up to the limits of the Rajput province of Las Bela; the Mingals, along with the Brahuis, claim Mongolian descent.

## Landmarks
Beyond the town of Bela, the region contains the tomb of Sir Robert Sandeman, located approximately four miles to the south. The tomb is surrounded by well-kept gardens and is likely to become a "ziarat," or place of pilgrimage.
