# Physical Conditions and Geography of Asia

The physical conditions of a large portion of Asia, as well as the history of its inhabitants, have been profoundly influenced by a massive mountain system. This system includes the Himalaya and all elevated areas physically connected to that range. This mountain mass occupies an area approximately 2,000 miles in length, with a width varying between 100 and 500 miles, situated between 28 and 35 degrees north and 65 and 100 degrees east.

## The Himalayan Barrier and Atmospheric Effects

The mountains of this region contain the highest peaks in the world, with numerous points exceeding 20,000 feet and the entire length of the chain rising far above the line of perpetual snow. Because these altitudes reach levels where vegetable life ceases and animal life is extremely difficult to support, the range acts as an almost impassable barrier to the spread of living creatures. Most passes across the main ridges are located at altitudes of at least 15,000 or 16,000 feet above sea level.

Beyond its role as a biological barrier, the mountain mass creates a complete separation of atmospheric conditions between its opposite flanks. The range penetrates the stratum of the atmosphere that contacts the earth's surface and determines climate. The highest summits obstruct nearly three-fourths of the air mass and nearly all of its moisture. The average height of the chain serves as an almost absolute barrier to one-half of the air and three-fourths of the moisture, while the lower ranges—where one-fourth of the air and one-half of the watery vapour lie below 9,000 feet—also produce significant atmospheric effects.

## Monsoons and Precipitation

The south-west monsoon carries a heated body of air from the Indian Ocean over southern Asia, highly charged with watery vapour. This air releases water as rain when its temperature is notably reduced. Along the Burmo-Siamese peninsula and the coasts of India, this reduction is caused by continuous mountain ranges that interrupt the wind current. These ranges force the air to rise, causing it to be rarefied; this increases its specific capacity for heat, lowers its temperature, and results in the condensation of the suspended vapour.

## Desert Formation in Asia

Deserts are categorized as "cold" or "hot." Cold deserts result from prevailing low temperatures in high latitudes, while hot deserts occur where high temperatures and deficient rainfall lead to evaporation exceeding precipitation. Hot deserts are primarily found in tropical belts of high atmospheric pressure.

In Asia, the zonal arrangement of these deserts is modified by the influence of elevated land. Because the line of elevated land in Asia runs east and west, the desert belt is displaced into higher latitudes. Examples of such deserts in the northern hemisphere include the Gobi, Takla Makan, Turan, Iran, and Arabia. Additionally, local causes can create desert conditions, such as the Indian desert, which is located in a region that the two main branches of the south-west monsoon cannot access.

## General Geographical Principles

The study of mountain ranges is as essential to understanding a continent's physical characteristics as its extent, its position relative to the poles and equator, and its coastlines. While boundaries formed by coastlines (depressions of the earth's crust below ocean level) are convenient for geographical nomenclature and often indicate political and physical differences, variations in elevation above sea level produce equally marked effects. Differences in elevation cause changes in temperature and climate comparable in magnitude to those caused by differences in latitude. Furthermore, the relative position of high and low lands modifies the fall of rain, the direction of winds, and other atmospheric phenomena.
