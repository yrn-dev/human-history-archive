# The Bantu Verb

The Bantu languages are characterized by an agglutinative construction, where syntax is formed by adding prefixes and suffixes to a root. In this system, the verbal root is practically unchanging, with the exception of its terminal vowel. While the first or penultimate vowel or consonant of the root may be modified in pronunciation by a preceding prefix, and the last vowel may be modified by a succeeding suffix, the root itself remains stable.

## Structure and Root Function
The Bantu verb consists of a practically unchangeable root that serves as the second person singular of the imperative. To this root, various particles are attached as prefixes and suffixes. These particles are often reduced prepositions, reduced adverbs, or worn-down verbs that have transitioned into auxiliaries.

The terminal vowel of the common root is almost invariably *a*. Departures from this rule are rare and are generally the result of borrowed foreign words from English, Portuguese, or Arabic, as seen in Swahili.

## Tense and Mood Formation
The building of the verbal root into various moods and tenses likely occurred independently across the principal Bantu language groups, though this process was probably based on a fundamental system common to the original Bantu tongue.

### The Preterite Tense
One exception to independent development may be the method of forming the preterite tense, which is shared by many widely-spread Bantu languages. This is often achieved through the use of a particle such as *-ile* or *-ili*, which is likely connected to the root *li* or *di* (*ni* or *ndi*), meaning "to exist" or "to be." 

The initial *i* in this particle often affects the penultimate or last syllable of the verbal root, causing rare changes in the vocable. For example, in many Bantu dialects, the root *pa* (to give) becomes *pele* in the preterite. In Zulu, the root *tanda* (love) becomes *tandile* (did love or have loved), which is a contraction of *tanda-ile*.

### Subjunctive and Negative Forms
The terminal vowel *a* is frequently modified to indicate mood or sense:
* **Subjunctive:** The terminal *a* changes to *e*. For instance, *tanda* (love) becomes *tande* (may love).
* **Negative:** In certain tenses, the terminal *a* changes to *i* to provide a negative sense.

## Negative Particles
The negative verbal particle in Bantu languages can be traced back to original forms in the Bantu mother-tongue, such as *ka*, *ta*, *sa*, *ki*, *ti*, or *si*. In most modern dialects, the negative particle coalesces with the pronominal particle. 

Historically, these were joined as follows:
* *Ka-ngi* (not I)
* *Ka-ku* or *ka-wu* (not thou)
* *Ka-a* (not he, she)
* *Ka-tu* (not we)
* *Ka-nu* (not ye)
* *Ka-ba* (not they)

Over time, these contracted; for example, *ka-ngi* or *sa-ngi* became *kai* or *sai*, and eventually *ki* or *si*. In some modern languages, the negative particle (such as *si* or *ti*) is used without a pronoun and applies to all persons. In other cases, the negative is expressed through a separate adverb, by stress and accent, or by a suffix traceable to the root *-ko* or *-pe*.

## Modification and Derivation
Bantu verbs have the capacity to modify the sense of the original root through the addition of suffixes. This affixion can modify the preceding consonant and the terminal vowel of the root. Using the root *tanda* (to love) as an example, the following variations occur:
* *tandwa*: to be loved
* *tandeka* or *tandika*: to be lovable
* *tandila* or *tandela*: to love for, with, or by another person
* *tandiza* or *tandisa*: to cause to love
* *tandana*: to love reciprocally
* *tandaka* or *tandanga*: to continue loving (indicating continuance)

Additionally, nouns can be created from verbs by changing the terminal vowel of the verbal root and potentially adding a personal prefix. In Luganda, the root *senyua* (to pardon) can become *ki-senyuo* (a pardon) or *mu-senyui* (a pardoner). Similarly, in Swahili, the root *patan[vs]a* (conciliate) leads to *mpatana[vs]i* (a conciliator) and *upatani[vs]o* (conciliation).
