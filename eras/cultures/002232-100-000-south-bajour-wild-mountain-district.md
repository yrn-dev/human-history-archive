# Bajour

## Geography and Topography
Bajour (also known as Bajaur) is a small district located in the North-West Frontier Province of India. The district measures approximately 45 miles in length and 20 miles in width. It is situated at a high elevation to the east of the Kunar valley, separated from the valley by a continuous line of rugged frontier hills. While these hills form a barrier, they are easily passable at one or two specific points. Historically, the old road from Kabul to India utilized this route before the Khyber Pass became the primary path.

The topography of the district is characterized by a gradual downward slope that begins at the Kunar ridge and terminates at the Panjkora river to the east. The drainage of the region flows eastward, originating from the eastern slopes of the dividing ridge that overlooks the Kunar. A notable feature of the landscape is a mountain spur from the Kunar range that curves eastwards and culminates in the peak of Koh-i-Mor, which is visible from the Peshawar valley.

## Population and Demographics
The district is inhabited almost exclusively by Pathan races of Afghan origin. The primary inhabitants are the Tarkani (Tarkalanri) Pathans, who are further sub-divided into the Ismailzai, Isazai, and Mamunds. Along with a few Utmauzais and Mohmands, the total population of these groups is approximately 100,000.

## Regional Borders and Neighbors
Bajour is surrounded by several distinct districts and states:
*   **South:** The wild mountain district of the Mohmands, another Pathan race.
*   **East:** Beyond the Panjkora river lie the hills of Swat, which are dominated by a different Pathan race.
*   **North:** An intervening watershed separates Bajour from the small state of Dir. This watershed and the valley of Dir serve as the route for the new road from the Punjab and Malakand to Chitral.

## Political History and Administration
Nawagai serves as the chief town of Bajour. The khan of Nawagai is under British protection, a status maintained to safeguard the road to Chitral. 

Jandol, located in one of the northern valleys, has lost its political importance. This decline followed the failure of its chief, Umra Khan, in his attempts to appropriate Dir, Bajour, and a significant portion of the Kunar valley. The conflict between Umra Khan and the amir of Kabul, who claimed sovereignty over these same areas, led to two major events: the demarcation agreement of 1893, which established the Afghan boundary in Kunar, and the 1895 siege of the Chitral fort following an invasion by Umra Khan.

## Historical and Cultural Significance
The region holds various historical associations. At the foot of Koh-i-Mor, Alexander found the Nysaean colony and the ancient city of Nysa, which tradition attributes to the founder Dionysus. Koh-i-Mor has been identified as the three-peaked mountain, Meros, mentioned in the history of Arrian.

Cultural remnants persist in the Kafir community of Kamdesh, who claim the same Greek origin as the Nysaeans and continue to chant hymns to the god who sprang from Gir Nysa (the mountain of Nysa). These people maintain that they migrated from the Swat country to their current home in the lower Bashgol. 

Historically, while Buddhism spread to Swat, Dir, Gilgit, and Chitral—and while the Peshawar valley was a center of Buddhist learning and Ningrahar was filled with temples and monasteries—Buddhism apparently never prevailed in the lower Kunar valley and Bajour, where the Nysaeans or Kafirs maintained their traditions. In the early 16th century, the invader Baber, who wrote extensively about Bajour, fought these people while on his way to establish the Mogul dynasty in India.
