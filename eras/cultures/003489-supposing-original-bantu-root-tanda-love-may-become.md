# The Bantu Verbal Root: Analysis of *Tanda*

In the study of Bantu languages, the verb is characterized by a practically unchangeable root that serves as the second person singular of the imperative. This root is modified through the addition of various prefixes and suffixes—often derived from worn-down verbs, reduced prepositions, or adverbs—to establish tenses and moods. While the building of these forms likely occurred independently across principal Bantu groups, it is believed they were founded on a fundamental system common to the original Bantu tongue.

## The Root *Tanda* and Verbal Modifications
To illustrate the power of Bantu verbs to modify the sense of an original root through suffixes, the root *tanda* (meaning "to love") is frequently used as a model. By applying different terminations, the meaning of the root is altered as follows:

*   **Passive and Qualitative:** *Tandwa* means "to be loved," while *tandeka* or *tandika* means "to be lovable."
*   **Benefactive:** *Tandila* or *tandela* (also appearing as *-ira* or *-era*) means "to love for, with, or by some other person."
*   **Causative:** *Tandiza* (or *-eza*) and *tandisa* (or *-esa*) both mean "to cause to love."
*   **Reciprocal:** *Tandana* means "to love reciprocally."
*   **Continuative:** The addition of the suffix *-aka* or *-anga* provides a sense of continuance; thus, *tandaka* means "to continue loving."

It should be noted that while *tanda* is a common verb in Zulu, it does not possess all these variations in that specific language. In other languages where all such variations might exist, the root itself may be changed to *londa* or *randa*.

## Tense and Mood Alterations
The terminal vowel of Bantu verbal roots is almost invariably *a*. However, specific changes to this vowel signal different grammatical functions:

*   **The Subjunctive:** The terminal *a* is changed to *e*. For example, *tanda* (love) becomes *tande* (may love).
*   **The Negative:** In certain tenses, the terminal *a* is changed to *i* to provide a negative sense.
*   **The Preterite:** The preterite tense is often formed using a method shared by many widely-spread Bantu languages. In Zulu, *tanda* (love) becomes *tandile* (have loved, did love). This is a contraction of *tanda-ile*. The particle *-ile* (or *-ili*) is likely connected to the root *li* or *di* (*ndi* or *ni*), meaning "to be" or "exist." This particle can appear in other forms such as *-idi*, *didi*, *-ire*, or *-ine*.

## Negation and Pronominal Particles
The negative verbal particle in Bantu languages is traced back to original forms in the mother-tongue such as *ka*, *ta*, *sa*, *ki*, *ti*, or *si*. In the vast majority of modern dialects, the negative particle coalesces with the pronominal particle. 

Original constructions included forms such as *Ka-ngi tanda* ("not I love"), where *Ka-ngi* meant "not I." Over time, these contracted; for instance, *ka-ngi* or *sa-ngi* became *kai* or *sai*, and eventually *ki* or *si*. In some modern languages, the negative particle (such as *ti* or *si*) is used without a pronoun, applying indifferently to all persons. In other cases, negation is expressed via stress, an adverb, or a suffix traceable to a root *-pe* or *-ko*.

## Derivation of Nouns from Verbs
Nouns can be created from verbs by changing the terminal vowel of the verbal root and potentially adding a personal prefix. For example:
*   In Luganda, the root *senyua* ("to pardon") becomes *ki-senyuo* ("a pardon") or *mu-senyui* ("a pardoner").
*   In Swahili, the root *patan[vs]a* ("conciliate") becomes *mpatana[vs]i* ("a conciliator") or *upatani[vs]o* ("conciliation").

## Linguistic Context and Origins
The study of these roots is central to reconstructing Bantu history in the absence of historical records. Linguistic research suggests the Bantu people left their primal home in the heart of Africa approximately 2,000 years ago. This date is supported by the widespread use of the root *kuku* (and its variants like *nkuku*, *ngoko*, or *enkoko*) for the domestic fowl, a bird that reached Africa via Egypt between 500 and 400 B.C.

The Bantu language type is defined by the governing of unchangeable roots by prefixes. The term *Bantu* itself is derived from *Ba-ntu*, the most archaic term for "men" or "mankind," where *-ntu* originally meant "object" or "person."
