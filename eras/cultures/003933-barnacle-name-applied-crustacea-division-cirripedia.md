# BARNACLE

Barnacles are organisms belonging to the division *Cirripedia* or *Thyrostraca* within the group Crustacea. They are categorized into two primary forms: stalked barnacles and sessile barnacles.

## Classification and Types
Stalked barnacles, referred to by C. Darwin as *Lepadidae*, are known to attach themselves in large numbers to objects floating in the sea, such as drift-wood. Due to this habit, they are among the primary agents responsible for the fouling of ship bottoms during long voyages. 

Sessile barnacles, referred to by Darwin as *Balanidae* or "acorn-shells," are found in myriads. These organisms encrust rocks on all coasts between the tide-marks.

## Historical Misconceptions and Myths
A persistent myth of medieval natural history, dating back at least to the 12th century, involved a misunderstanding of the origin of the barnack or bernacle goose (*Bernicla branta*). Because the Arctic nesting-places of this winter visitor to Britain were unknown at the time, it was fabled that the bird originated from the shell-like fruit of a tree growing by the seashore. 

Some variations of this story claimed the shell grew like a mushroom on rotting timber in the sea—a description that clearly refers to barnacles of the genus *Lepas*. This fable was an imaginary embroidery based on the fact that barnacles attach to submerged timber and the existence of the bernicle goose. An illustrated account of this belief was printed in an early volume of the Royal Society of London.

In the context of biological theory, this fable is cited as one of the most extreme cases of "heterogenesis." Heterogenesis is the theory that the offspring of an organism may be entirely different from the parent, potentially giving rise to a different species, genus, family, or even a plant. While some modern proponents of heterogenesis have limited their claims to microscopic plants and animals, these observations have generally been explained as cases of parasitism through more minute observation.

## Scientific Development and Zoology
For a significant period after the decline of medieval fables, the true nature of barnacles was not fully appreciated. Due to their external appearance, they were initially classified with the Mollusca. 

The recognition of their true affinities occurred in 1830, when Vaughan Thompson demonstrated that barnacles develop from a typically Crustacean larva that is free-swimming. This discovery led to the understanding that a barnacle is essentially a Crustacean that is fixed by its head and uses its legs to kick food into its mouth.

In the broader history of zoology, the classification of Crustacea has evolved. Aristotle originally placed Crustacea in a class called "Malacostraca" under the "Anaema" (bloodless animals). Later, in 1705, John Ray included Crustacea within his "Insects," a group that also comprised Arachnids, Myriapoda, and Annelida. By the end of the 18th century and the beginning of the 19th, authors such as G. Cuvier, G.P.B. Lamarck, and W.E. Leach definitely separated Crustacea from Insecta as a distinct class.
