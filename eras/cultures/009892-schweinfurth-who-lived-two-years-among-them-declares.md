# G. A. Schweinfurth

G. A. Schweinfurth was an explorer and researcher whose work provided significant ethnographic and geographical data on various regions of Africa and Arabia. His observations are particularly noted for their detail regarding the peoples of the Nile valley and the deserts of the Arabian Peninsula.

## Observations on the Bongo
Schweinfurth lived for two years among the Bongo (also known as the Dor or Deran), a tribe of Nilotic negroes inhabiting the south-west portion of the Bahr-el-Ghazal province in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. He suggested that the Bongo were likely related to the Zandeh tribes of the Welle district. 

According to Schweinfurth, the Bongo population numbered at least 300,000 before the arrival of slave-raiders around 1850. He noted that the Bongo previously resided in numerous independent and peaceful small communities. Physically, he described them as a race of medium height and thick-set build with black hair and a red-brown complexion that resembled the soil of their region. He specifically observed that their heads were nearly round, stating that, to his knowledge, no other African race possessed a higher cephalic index.

Regarding their customs, Schweinfurth noted that Bongo women in later life tended toward steatopygia; he remarked that this deposit of fat, combined with the tail of bast they wore, gave them the appearance of "dancing baboons" while walking. He documented their traditional attire, noting that men formerly wore loin-cloths and dozens of iron rings on their arms to serve as a form of armour, while women wore girdles with attached tufts of grass.

## Observations on the Baggara
Schweinfurth also provided assessments of the Baggara, whom he described as "Cowherds." These African "Arabs" of Semitic origin are nomadic pastoralists located principally in Kordofan, west of the White Nile. 

In contrast to his descriptions of other groups, Schweinfurth declared the Baggara to be the best-looking of the Nile nomads. He characterized the men as types of physical beauty, possessing sinewy limbs, erect athletic bodies, and fine heads. He observed that their skins ranged in color from deep black to dark red-brown, and noted that their features were regular and lacked negro characteristics, concluding that there was little in their appearance that was Semitic.

## Geographical and Regional Research
Beyond his ethnographic studies of the Nile tribes, Schweinfurth conducted research in the south-west of Arabia. Approximately ten years after the 1877-1878 labors of Wilfrid S. Blunt and Charles Doughty in northern Arabia, Schweinfurth and E. Glaser carried out investigations in the south-western region of the peninsula.
