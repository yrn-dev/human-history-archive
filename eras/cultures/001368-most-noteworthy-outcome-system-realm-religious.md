# Astral Theology and Religious Practice in Babylonia and Assyria

The most noteworthy outcome of the religious system in Babylonia and Assyria was the development of an elaborate and complicated method of divining the future through the observation of heavenly phenomena. This system, known as astral theology, served as the theoretical substratum for the religious systems of both regions.

## Principles of Astral Theology
The essential feature of astral theology is the assumption of a close link between occurrences on earth and the movements of the heavens. This belief originated from a popular factor: the natural observation that life, vegetation, and guidance depend upon the two great luminaries, the sun and the moon. Priests expanded this popular belief into a formal theory of close correspondence between earthly events and celestial phenomena.

This system involved the personification of the sun and moon, which was the first step in the process. Subsequently, other deities were assigned seats in the heavens. This culminated in the identification of specific gods and goddesses with planets and stars:
*   **Marduk** was identified with the planet Jupiter.
*   **Ishtar** was identified with Venus.
*   **Nergal** was identified with Mars.
*   **Nebo** was identified with Mercury.
*   **Ninib** was identified with Saturn.

## Divination and Omen Collections
The practice of astral divination was highly formalized. In the royal collection of cuneiform literature deposited in the palace of King Assur-bani-pal of Assyria (668-626 B.C.) at Nineveh, the largest class of texts consists of omen collections connected with this astral theology.

Additionally, there are indications that astral divination was connected with a more popular form of divination involving the livers of sacrificial animals. This latter practice was based on a primitive view that the liver served as the seat of the soul and of life.

## Centralization of the Cult and Incantations
While local cults in the north and south maintained themselves, there was a general tendency toward the centralization of worship. In the south, the cult was centralized around Marduk and his consort, while in the north, it centered on Assur and Ishtar.

This tendency toward centralization is evident in the series of old incantation texts. These series attempted to gather prayers and formulae from various centers and make them conform to the centralized worship of the chief deities. For example:
*   Incantations originally addressed to Nusku (the god of fire) and Ea of Eridu (the god of the watery element) were transferred to Marduk.
*   This transfer was achieved by establishing that Ea conferred his powers upon his son, Marduk, and by positioning Nusku as a messenger between Ea and Marduk.

To increase the effectiveness of these magic formulae, a large number of old local deities were introduced to add their power to the chief gods. The astral system influenced this process through the assignment of attributes to the gods that reflected their seats in the heavens and the introduction of the names of stars. These incantations eventually evolved into prayers and hymns.

## Stability of Religious Rites
Between approximately 3500 and 300 B.C., the general principles of these religious rites remained relatively stable, with only minor variations in details between different centers. The predominance of astral theology as a theoretical foundation acted as an important factor in maintaining this stability across the Babylonian and Assyrian religious systems.
