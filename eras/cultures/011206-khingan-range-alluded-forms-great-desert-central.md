# The Khingan Range and the Central Asian Desert

The Khingan range consists of two mountain ranges located in eastern Asia. These ranges serve as critical orographical dividing lines, separating the high Mongolian plateau from the lower plains of Manchuria and influencing the climatic and floral boundaries of the region.

## The Great Khingan
The Great Khingan is the eastern border ridge of an immense plateau that extends from the Tian-shan Mountains to the Khingan Mountains, and from the Himalaya to the Bering Strait. It is well-documented from 50° N. down to Kalgan (41° N., 115° E.), where the highway from Urga to Peking crosses the range. 

As a border ridge of the Mongolian plateau, the Great Khingan acts as an important climatic boundary and defines the western limits of the flora found in Manchuria. The range's western slope is very gentle, with a base situated at altitudes between 3,000 and 3,500 ft. The crest of the range reaches heights of 4,800 to 6,500 ft., while the eastern slope descends precipitously to the plains of Manchuria, which sit at altitudes of 1,500 to 2,000 ft. The system has a total width of 80 to 100 m., characterized by one or two subordinate ridges parallel to the main range, separated by longitudinal valleys. Geologically, the main range and its south-eastern slopes contain trachytes, basalts, and other volcanic formations; the range experienced volcanic activity between 1720 and 1721.

## The Little Khingan
The term "Little Khingan" is applied to two distinct ranges. The typical range, also known as Dusse-alin or the Bureya mountains, is pierced by the Amur 40 m. below Ekaterino-Nikolsk. This range extends from the Amur to the Sea of Okhotsk and continues in a south-westerly direction toward the Liao-tung peninsula in Manchuria, appearing to be cleft twice by the Sungari. The second range associated with this name is the Ilkhuri-alin mountains (51° N., 122°-126° E.), which run north-west between the Amur and the upper Nonni, west of Blagovyeshchensk.

## The Great Desert of Central Asia
The Khingan range forms the eastern boundary of the great desert of central Asia, known as the Gobi. This central area is further bounded to the north and north-west by the Tian-shan and the Yablonoi Mountains (and their western extension), and to the south by the northern face of the Tibetan plateau. 

The Gobi's western portion forms eastern or Chinese Turkestan, while its eastern part is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia. It is likely that no part of this desert is lower than 2,000 ft. above sea level. Specifically, the plain around Yarkand and Kashgar ranges from 4,000 to 6,000 ft., and the route between Kiakhta and the Chinese frontier never drops below 3,000 ft. While the precise positions of the ridges traversing this area are not fully known, their elevations are considerable, with some points exceeding 12,000 or 10,000 ft.

## Regional Hydrography and Topography
The Khingan range separates the Mongolian plateau from the plains of the Nonni and Sungari. To the east of the range, drainage flows into the Yellow Sea and the Amur; to the west lies an almost rainless region sloping toward the center of the continent.

Asia is divided laterally along the 40 deg. north parallel by a depression that extends from the east of the Gobi through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. This depression, which may have once been a vast inland sea, generally does not exceed 2,000 ft. above sea level, and research suggests that central portions near Lop Nor may actually be below sea level. South of the Great Khingan, the range is continued by the In-shan mountains, which maintain the same terraced escarpment of the Mongolian plateau. Similar escarpments appear in China's Shan-si province along the southern border of the South Mongolian plateau, where they are pierced by the Hwang-ho (Yellow River) between 38° and 39° N.
