# Dietary Abstentions and the Avoidance of Flesh

The avoidance of flesh diets throughout history has been driven by a diverse array of spiritual, superstitious, and practical motivations. These range from the fear of absorbing animal souls to the adherence to strict religious laws and the practical utility of certain animals.

## Spiritual and Superstitious Motivations
A widespread reason for the total avoidance of flesh was the fear of absorbing the irrational soul of an animal, a spirit believed to reside particularly in the blood. This belief led to rules against eating strangled meats, except during sacramental meals where the god inherent in the animal was partaken of. Similar beliefs extended to wine; old Egyptian kings avoided it because they believed wine was the blood of rebels who fought against the gods. Drinking it was seen as imbibing the souls of these rebels, with the resulting intoxication viewed as possession by their spirits. Similar beliefs regarding a "cardiac demon" in wine were held by medieval Israelites and formed the basis for the Mahommedan prohibition of wine-drinking.

Other groups, such as the Pythagoreans, Nazirites, Arab dervishes, and Rechabites, avoided wine and leaven in bread for reasons parallel to the abstention from flesh. Porphyry also noted a belief in the transmigration of human souls into animals; under this view, eating animals was akin to cannibalism. Conversely, some meat-eaters argued that killing animals was a pious duty to release these imprisoned human souls. In some instances, the consumption of specific animal parts was used to gain power; for example, wizards reportedly swallowed the hearts of hawks and ravens to acquire mantic powers of divination.

## Taboos and Kinship
Dietary restrictions were often based on the concept of *taboo*, where certain animals were considered either too holy or too defiling to be eaten. Among Semitic tribes, this often stemmed from a perceived tie of kinship between the people, their gods, and totem animals. 

In Leviticus xi, the sons of Israel were forbidden from eating animals that were not cloven-footed ruminants, specifically excluding the swine, hare, coney, and camel. Other prohibitions included winged creeping things with four feet, most lizards, the mouse, the weasel, and water organisms lacking scales and fins. Certain birds, such as the heron, stork, pelican, hoopoe, and swan, were also forbidden.

Similarly, Egyptians viewed the pig as an abominable animal. Those who touched a pig would immediately dip themselves and their garments in the river. Swineherds were barred from entering temples and were socially isolated, marrying only among themselves. However, Egyptians did sacrifice and eat swine during the full moon for the Moon and Dionysos.

## Cultural and Practical Abstentions
In Japan, dietary choices were influenced by both practical utility and evolving religious views. The military class avoided the flesh of horses and oxen, not due to religious scruples, but because of the animals' usefulness. This is illustrated by the resentment of the Kami Mitoshi when oxen were butchered to provide meat for rice-field workers. 

Later, as Buddhism gained influence in Court circles, decrees were issued to interdict all taking of life for food. Emperor Temmu (673-686) issued the first such decree, which was later renewed by Shomu (724-748). Empress Shotoku (765-770) further forbade the use of cormorants, falcons, or dogs for fishing or hunting during Shinto ceremonials. Despite these decrees, the consumption of fowl, deer, and wild boar continued.

## Asceticism and Purity
Fasting and severe diets have been used in primitive asceticism to achieve spiritual or physical benefits. Daniel and his kinsmen once resolved to abstain from all living creatures and the food of the king's table, requesting instead dates and pulse. This diet resulted in their bodies becoming plumper and fuller than those who ate the king's meats. Furthermore, it was believed that this abstention left their souls more pure and less burdened, making them fitter for hard labor and learning.

## The Qur'an on the Flesh of Kin
The Qur'an addresses the concept of eating flesh metaphorically in the context of social conduct. It commands believers to avoid suspicion, spying, and backbiting. To illustrate the abhorrence of backbiting, the text asks if any person would love to eat the flesh of his dead brother, stating that they would abhor such an act.
