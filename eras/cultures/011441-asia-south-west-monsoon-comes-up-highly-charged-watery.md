# The South-West Monsoon of Asia

The south-west monsoon is a periodical wind system that prevails north of the equator during the hot half of the year. The term "monsoon" is a corruption of the Indian word for season. This meteorological phenomenon is driven by significant changes in atmospheric pressure over Asia and the surrounding oceans.

## Origins and Atmospheric Mechanics
The south-west monsoon arises from a great diminution of atmospheric pressure over Asia, which becomes strongly marked during the rise of temperature in April and May. Simultaneously, there is relatively higher pressure over the equator and the regions south of it. Winds generally blow from areas of greatest pressure to those of least pressure, though this is modified by the earth's west-to-east revolution on its axis.

The diminution of pressure continues as heat increases, reaching a maximum in July shortly after the solstice. As barometric pressure is restored and equalized within the tropics after the October equinox, and as temperatures fall north of the equator, the south-west winds subside. They are then succeeded by the north-east monsoon during the winter months, which is developed by the relatively greater atmospheric pressure then occurring over Asia compared to the equatorial region.

## Moisture and Precipitation
The south-west monsoon carries a heated body of air from the Indian Ocean over southern Asia. This air is highly charged with watery vapour, leaving it in a condition to release large volumes of water as rain whenever its temperature is notably reduced. 

Temperature reduction occurs primarily along the Burmo-Siamese peninsula and the coasts of India through the interruption of wind currents by continuous mountain ranges. These mountains force the mass of air to rise, causing the air to be rarefied. This increases its specific capacity for heat, resulting in a fall in temperature and the condensation of the suspended vapour.

The heaviest rainfall occurs where mountain lines directly face these vapour-bearing winds, such as the west coast of the Malay peninsula and the Western Ghats of India. The Khasi range, located about 100 m. north-east of Calcutta, presents an abrupt front to moist winds from the Bay of Bengal; it has recorded the heaviest rainfall known anywhere in the world, at not less than 600 inches per year. In the Konkan region, monsoon rainfall sometimes approaches 300 inches. In Eastern Bengal, specifically in Backergunje, the strong south-west monsoon coming directly from the Bay of Bengal keeps the atmosphere cool, though the resulting humidity and heavy rain can be sources of disease.

## Regional Variations and Limitations
The direction and strength of the monsoon vary by location due to local influences:
*   **Arabian Sea:** The monsoon begins with west and north-westerly winds, turning south-west as the year advances, and returning to north-west and north in autumn.
*   **Bay of Bengal:** The strength is primarily from the south and south-east, followed by north-east winds after October, which then transition to northerly and north-westerly winds.
*   **China Sea:** This region is fully exposed to both monsoons, with normal directions coinciding with the channel between the eastern islands and the Asian continent.
*   **Malay Archipelago:** The force of the monsoons is much interrupted here. Because of its position on the equator, the southerly summer winds do not extend to the coasts of Java, where south-easterly trade winds develop instead.

The south-west monsoon does not generally maintain its character as a south-west wind once it moves over land. The air current is dissipated, loses force, and is diverted toward areas of least pressure. Winds passing northward over India blow as south winds up the Indus and as easterly or south-easterly winds over the north-eastern Gangetic plain. These winds are hardly felt in the Indus basin or the ranges of the Himalaya and do not reach the table-lands of Afghanistan.

## Influence on Geography and Climate
The monsoonal influence of elevated land modifies the zonal arrangement of hot deserts. In Asia, where elevated land runs east and west, the desert belt is displaced into higher latitudes. Some arid regions, such as the Indian desert, exist because they are inaccessible to both main branches of the south-west monsoon. Similarly, the cessation of rains on the southern border of Baluchistan, west of Karachi, is caused by the projection of the south-east coast of Arabia, which limits the length of the coastline exposed to the current. In Sind and along the Indus, rainfall is small and irregular because there are no obstacles to stop the vapour-bearing winds, which instead carry uncondensed rain to the Punjab and the outer ranges of Afghanistan and the western Himalaya.

## Associated Phenomena and Navigation
In the hot months, the Indian Ocean and China Sea experience revolving storms or cyclones (known as typhoons in the China Sea). In the Bay of Bengal, these storms often originate over the Nicobar and Andaman islands, moving north-west toward the east coast of the Indian peninsula and passing violently across the Ganges delta. These occur from June to October (and rarely in November) and appear to be caused by adverse northern currents meeting the south-west monsoon. In the China Sea, cyclones originate near Formosa and advance from north-east to south-west or east to west.

For maritime activity, the south-west monsoon creates challenging conditions. Along the coast of the Bombay Presidency, only a few harbours—including Karachi-in-Sind, Bombay, Karwar, and Marmagoa—are sufficiently land-locked to protect shipping during the prevalence of these winds.
