# Substantives and Gender Classification

The classification of substantives into genders or classes varies significantly across different linguistic systems, ranging from the use of pronominal prefixes and suffixes to the retention of ancient tripartite gender systems.

## Pronominal Prefixes and Concord
In certain linguistic groups, such as the Bantu languages, substantives are divided into classes or genders that are indicated by a pronominal particle prefixed to the root. These prefixes function in either a singular or plural sense. With the exception of the "abstract" prefix *Bu*, singular prefixes cannot be used as plurals, nor can plural prefixes be used as singulars. There is a degree of correspondence between the two; for example, prefix No. 8 corresponds as a plural to No. 7, and No. 2 almost invariably serves as a plural to No. 3. There are approximately sixteen prefixes common to the entire group.

This system, referred to by Dr. Bleek as "concord," involves attaching the pronominal particle of the noun as a prefix to the roots of verbs, pronouns, prepositions, and adjectives within the sentence that are connected to the governing noun. While these particles may evolve to differ in form from the substantive's prefix over time, they share a common origin. These particles must precede nominal, adjectival, pronominal, and verbal roots, regardless of whether they are in the accusative or nominative case, although they may follow the roots of some prepositions and auxiliary prefix-participles used in verb conjugation.

## Sexual Gender and Markers
In the aforementioned prefix system, sexual gender is not recognized in the concord or the pronouns. Instead, sexual gender is indicated through specific markers:
*   **Male:** Indicated by a male prefix of varying form, which is often identical to a word meaning "father."
*   **Female:** Indicated by a feminine prefix (*na* or *nya*) connected to the root for "mother," or a suffix (*ka* or *kazi*) indicating "female" or "wife."

Additionally, the first and second prefixes are usually restricted to humanity and invariably indicate living beings.

## Indo-Aryan Gender Systems
Among Indo-Aryan languages, Marathi and Gujarati are the only ones to have retained the three genders—masculine, feminine, and neuter—inherited from Prakrit and Sanskrit. While the neuter gender survives sporadically in Rajasthani and rural dialects of Western Hindi, it is otherwise found only in the interrogative pronoun.

In Marathi, the neuter gender is used for inanimate objects and animate beings when the sex is undecided or both sexes are included (e.g., *ghode*, a horse). In the Konkan region, the neuter is also used for females who have not yet reached puberty (e.g., *cedu*, a girl). Conversely, masculine and feminine words can denote inanimate objects. Generally, large and powerful inanimate objects are masculine, while small, delicate things are feminine. 

Gender in Marathi often follows Sanskrit originals, though the loss of terminations makes complete rules difficult to establish. Generally, strong bases in *a* (excluding *tatsamas*) are masculine, while corresponding neuter and feminine words end in *e* and *i* respectively (e.g., *mul^aga* for son, *mul^agi* for daughter, and *mul^age* for a child). In some animals, sex is distinguished by using entirely different words, such as *bokad* (he-goat) and *seli* (nanny-goat).

## Gender in Verbal Forms and Adjectives
In some languages, gender is distinguished primarily through verbal forms. In Basque, for example, the sex of the person being addressed is indicated by a special suffix in the verb. For instance, *eztakit* means "I do not know it," but *eztakinat* is used when addressing a woman and *eztakiat* (or *eztakikat*) when addressing a man. Some dialects also include a childish form (*eztakichut*) and a respectful form (*eztakizut*).

In Marathi, when the subject of a verb is in the nominative, the tense formed by adding personal suffixes to a participle agrees with the subject in number, person, and gender. For example, the masculine second person singular for "thou rose" is *uth^alas*, while the feminine form is *uth^alis*. Similarly, the present tense for "thou risest" is *uth^atos* for masculine and *uth^ates* for feminine.

Strong adjectives and genitives can be declined like substantives and must agree with the qualified noun in case, number, and gender. If a substantive is in an oblique case, the adjective takes a general oblique form without a defining postposition.
