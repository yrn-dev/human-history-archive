# Siam

## Geography and Hydrography
Siam is located in the region of the Indo-Chinese Peninsula. The geography of the region is characterized by its river systems; the great rivers of Siam, Burma, and China emanate from a remarkable region whose hydrography was mapped by the Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky during his travels between 1871 and 1873. 

In terms of cartographic precision, Siam has been a destination for the outward branching of minor extensions of the Indian triangulation system. These extensions have provided a basis for a vast amount of exploratory surveys of a strictly geographical nature, contributing to a higher standard of accuracy in Asiatic geographical surveys than was considered attainable fifty years prior.

## History and Conflict
The history of Siam includes periods of conflict with Burma. In 1771, the Siamese revolted and were never subsequently subdued by the Burmans, although the Burmans maintained control over the sea-coast as far as Mergui. In 1785, Burman forces attacked the island of Junkseylon with an army and a fleet of boats but were driven back. A further invasion of Siam occurred in 1786, when the Burman monarch led an army of 30,000 men, though this attempt also failed.

Peace was eventually concluded between the two powers in 1793. Under the terms of this agreement, the Siamese yielded the seaports of Tavoy and Mergui, as well as the entire possession of the coast of Tenasserim on the Indian Ocean, to the Burmans.

## Architecture and Culture
The Indo-Chinese Peninsula saw the expansion and contraction of various dynasties and kingdoms. Evidence of their advancement in art and wealth is found in magnificent architectural remains dating primarily from the 12th to the 14th centuries. In Siam, these remains are found at Ayuthia. This architecture is characterized by strong individual and generic peculiarities, while being deeply marked by Hindu influence.

## Economy and Trade
The port of Bangkok is formed by a four-mile reach of the Me Nam river immediately below the city. The river is of good depth and ranges from 250 to 400 yards in width, providing convenience for loading and berthing ships. However, a bar at the mouth of the river prevents vessels drawing more than 12 feet from passing, requiring larger ships to use lighters for partial loading and unloading.

The port area is densely lined with wharves, warehouses, and offices of commercial houses, alongside rice-mills, timber yards, the harbour master's office, the custom house, and various consulates and foreign legations. The trade of Bangkok is almost entirely managed by the Chinese and Europeans.

The primary exports of the region are teak and rice, while the primary imports consist of gold-leaf and silk and cotton goods. Between 1900 and 1907, the value of trade more than doubled, reaching £7,100,000 in exports and £5,600,000 in imports by 1907. Approximately 75% of this total trade is conducted with the British empire.

Shipping in the port has seen shifts in nationality. In 1904, of the 750 steamships that cleared the port, three out of seven were German, two were Norwegian, and one was British. By 1905, the introduction of regular services by a Japanese company and a British company altered these proportions. Additionally, while trade with Singapore has shown a tendency to decrease, direct trade with Europe has increased. Local trade with the districts and towns on the west and east coasts of the Gulf of Siam is carried out by a fleet of junks, schooners, and small steamers.

The unit of currency used in Siam is the tical.
