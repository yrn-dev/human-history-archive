# The Interior and Inland Basins

The interior or inland basins of the world comprise a diverse array of hydrographic regions, ranging from vast, waterless depressions and high-altitude plateaus to fertile plains and ancient lacustrine systems. These regions are often defined by their lack of a direct surface outlet to the ocean, with drainage instead being absorbed by soil, lost in swamps, or evaporated.

## Asiatic Inland Basins
In Asia, the interior or inland basins—which include the Gobi depression, the Tibetan plateau, the Syro-Arabian inland basin, the basins of Asia Minor, and the lacustrine regions south of the Arctic watershed—cover an area of approximately 3,141,500 sq. m. This is about half the extent of the northern and southern divisions of the continent's drainage.

A significant feature of the Asian interior is a depression that begins east of the Gobi desert and extends westwards through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. This depression, which is believed to have once been a vast inland sea, is limited to the west by the Pamir elevation and the meridional range of Sarikol. The level of this depression generally does not exceed 2,000 ft. above sea level, and research suggests that in central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, it may actually be below sea level. South of the great continental divide, the Gobi forms a vast, almost waterless interior space where local drainage is lost in swamps or deserts.

## High-Altitude and Lacustrine Basins
Certain inland basins are characterized by extreme elevation. One of the most elevated lacustrine basins in the world is the great central plateau, or *alta-planicie*. While it once drained eastward, it currently possesses no surface outlet. Within this system, Lake Titicaca receives water from several short streams and discharges via the Desaguadero, a sluggish river that flows south for 184 m. to Lake Pampa Aullaguas (also known as Poopo). From Lake Pampa Aullaguas, a small outlet called the Lacahahuira flows west for 60 m. into the Cienegas de Coipasa (salt-swamps). The drainage of this entire district, including the Rio Grande de Lipez in the extreme south, is absorbed by evaporation and the dry desert soil.

In the Central African (or Albertine) rift-valley, Lake Kivu represents a significant lacustrine feature. Situated about 60 m. north of Tanganyika, it is roughly triangular with an area of about 1,100 sq. m. and an elevation of 4,830 ft. above sea level. It is separated from the Nile basin to the north by volcanic peaks. The lake discharges its waters via the Rusizi River, which breaks through huge ridges in a series of steep gorges, descending 2,000 ft. to the lacustrine plain at the head of Lake Tanganyika.

## Regional Basins and Plains
In Europe, the central plain or basin drained by the Peneius is a fertile region in Thessaly. This basin receives streams from Othrys, Pindus, and Olympus. Local inhabitants believed the region was once a lake until the waters found an efflux through the defile of Tempê, which was said to have been cut open by Poseidôn.

In other regions, inland basins are defined by specific watersheds. In one interior region, the Atacora hills form a watershed between the basins of the Volta, the Niger, and the Weme. This area includes the Lama Marsh, a great swamp extending 25 m. east to west and 6 to 9 m. north to south, located approximately 50 m. inland from the coast.

## Geological and Climatic Variations
The nature of inland basins has shifted over geological time. During the Third Interglacial period, the Mediterranean area was likely a great valley below general sea level containing two inland seas that were cut off from the ocean. Similarly, during the Eocene period, the plains of the interior of North America, bordered by mountains to the west, were filled with lacustrine and terrestrial deposits.

Climate in interior regions can be extreme. The interior of Greenland, described as an elevated desert of snow, features a permanent anti-cyclonic region. This results in a continental climate with large temperature ranges; for example, daily ranges can reach 40 degrees. In the interior of the inland ice, winter minimums may sink to -90 deg. F. due to the radiation of heat from the snow-surface in rarefied air.
