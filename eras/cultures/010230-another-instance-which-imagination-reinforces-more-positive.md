# Imagination and Positive Interest

In the study of human history and collecting, imagination often serves as a mechanism that reinforces and enhances the positive interest an individual may have in an object or a pursuit. This phenomenon is particularly evident in the field of bibliophilia, where the value of a book is frequently derived from more than its literal content.

## Imagination in Book Collecting

The imagination of a collector can significantly amplify the appeal of a book through associative interest. The most prominent examples are volumes that can be linked to famous events or notable persons. Books that once belonged to great poets, soldiers, statesmen, or royalty—specifically those who have garnered popular sympathy, such as Mary queen of Scots—possess an interest that defies analysis. This is especially true if the book bears marks indicating it was valued by the owner or is connected to a striking incident in that person's life.

Furthermore, collectors often feel a natural tenderness for their predecessors. Consequently, a copy of a famous work is more highly regarded if its pedigree can be traced through a long succession of book-loving owners. This interest has led to the publication of works documenting such histories, including *English Book-Collectors* by W.Y. Fletcher (1902), *Great Book-Collectors* by Charles and Mary Elton (1893), and Guigard's *Nouvel armorial du bibliophile* (1890).

## The Role of Printing History and Rarity

Imagination also reinforces interest in editions connected to the development, diffusion, or origin of printing. Book-lovers often show a "religious" piety toward the history of the art of printing, which has contributed to their happiness. This manifests in the desire of local antiquaries to acquire the first book printed in their specific town. This drive to measure the quality and quantity of early printers' work has resulted in the preservation of thousands of books that might otherwise have been lost.

The financial value of these books is influenced by several factors:
1. The associative or positive interest found within the book.
2. The scarcity of copies available on the market relative to the number and wealth of potential buyers.
3. The physical condition of the copy (completeness, size, freshness, and absence of stains), except in cases of extreme rarity and interest.

While the desire for first editions is partly based on their superiority for study, it is also driven by associations that are either proven or created by the imagination. This can lead to "fanciful" pricing; for example, high prices paid during the era of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics were difficult to justify, as these were of little value as texts and had no link to the personalities of their authors.

## Imagination in Narrative and Strategy

Beyond collecting, imagination plays a role in the expansion of cultural narratives and the execution of complex military operations. In the development of Homeric themes, the addition of new characters and legends—such as the story of the treachery of Sinon and the Palladium in the *Little Iliad*, or figures like Iphigenia and Palamedes in the *Cypria*—is attributed to the imagination of the people. This growth was further fueled by Greek contact with new countries and races, as well as their own advancement in civilization. For instance, the *Aethiopis* features the Amazons and Memnon's Aethiopians, which are described as creations of "oriental fancy."

In a practical sense, "real imagination" is required to overcome enormous physical and strategic difficulties. During a proposed attack on Zeebrugge, imagination was necessary to plan the seizure of the Mole—a stone breakwater over a mile long equipped with a lighthouse, railway terminus, and heavy guns. The plan required a landing party to overrun the Mole, capture the guns, and destroy the railway viaduct to prevent reinforcements, all while block-ships sank themselves to impede U-boats.
