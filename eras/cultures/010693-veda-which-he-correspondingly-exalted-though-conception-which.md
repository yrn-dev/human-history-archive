# Veda

The Vedas are the ancient scriptures of the Brahman religion. They are composed of several books, including the *Rig Veda* and the *Atharva Veda*. These texts are structured into three distinct components: the Samhita, the Brahmanas, and the Upanishads. Together, these sections represent the spiritual element of the religion and its subsequent nationalistic and magical developments.

## The Atharva Veda
The *Atharva Veda* is the fourth book of the Vedas. While it shares the same tripartite division (Samhita, Brahmanas, and Upanishads) as the other Vedic books, its Samhita differs in content. The sayings, or mantras, within the *Atharva Veda* are composed as spells rather than the hymns or prayers found in other Vedas. This distinction suggests that the *Atharva Veda* represents a stage of religion that is lower than that of the *Rig Veda*.

## Transmission and Early Use
The transmission of the Vedas occurred primarily within households, where they were passed down chiefly by women. In the context of early Sanskrit writings, the *Rig Veda* frequently employs the term *arya* as an epithet for deities. Additionally, the term *aryas* may appear as the genitive of *ari*, which is interpreted as meaning "pious, devoted, active."

## The Veda in Ascetic Practice
In early Indian tradition, the Veda played a specific role in the life of the wandering beggar or hermit. According to the *Apastamba*, a young man who had fulfilled the duties of the student order and chosen to "go forth remaining chaste" was expected to live without a house, fire, protection, or pleasures. Such an ascetic was to remain silent, uttering speech only during the daily recitation of the Veda. Despite this ritual recitation, some traditions held that the ascetic should eventually abandon the Vedas, along with truth, falsehood, this world, and the next, in order to seek the Universal Soul and attain eternal salvation.

## Modern Interpretations: The Arya Samaj
In the 19th century, Dayanand Saraswati, a Guzerati Brahman born around 1825, founded the Arya Samaj, a Hindu religious association with reforming tendencies. Having grown dissatisfied with idol-worship, Dayanand studied Vedic philosophy to find a way to alleviate human misery and achieve final liberation. 

Dayanand's approach to the Vedas was characterized by reading his own beliefs and modern scientific discoveries into the texts. He specifically exalted the *Rig Veda*, although his ultimate conception of God was more heavily influenced by the Christian scriptures, which he also vehemently assailed. 

As part of his reinterpretation, Dayanand redefined the *Yajna* (sacrificial cult). Rather than traditional sacrifice, he explained it as:
* The instruction of the people and the entertainment of the learned based on their worth.
* The application of physics, chemistry, the arts of peace, and the business of manufacture.
* The purification of the air and the nourishment of vegetables through meteorology (referred to in Sanskrit as *Agni-Notri*).

Furthermore, Dayanand denied that the Vedas warranted the caste system. He proposed instead that the four grades be maintained as orders of learning, with admission granted only through examination.
