# The Perennibranchiate State of Axolotls

The axolotl is the Mexican name for larval salamanders belonging to the genus *Amblystoma*. Historically, the classification of these creatures was a subject of scientific debate. In the beginning of the 19th century, Humboldt brought the first specimens of *Gyrinus edulis* (the axolotl) to Cuvier. Cuvier recognized that these Batrachians were not actually related to Perennibranchiates—such as *Proteus* and *Siren*—but instead represented the larval form of an air-breathing salamander. This opinion was largely ignored by systematists of the time. More than half a century later, it was discovered that the axolotl could breed while still in its branchiferous condition. Consequently, the genus was renamed *Siredon* by J. Wagler and was unanimously classified among the permanent gill-breathers.

## Gadow's Theory on Perennibranchiation

According to the opinion of Gadow, the reason why axolotls in certain lakes remain perennibranchiate is obvious and linked to their environment. Gadow posits that the lakes provide a "paradise" of conditions that act as powerful inducements for the creatures to remain in their larval state. These attractions include:

*   A constant abundance of food.
*   A stable amount of water.
*   Innumerable hiding-places located under the banks, in the mud, and among the roots and reeds of floating islands that are scattered throughout the lakes.

Because of these factors, the axolotls retain larval features that are not directly connected to sexual maturity. Gadow notes that while there is nothing to prevent the axolotls from leaving these lakes, there is likewise nothing to induce them to do so. He observes that similar occurrences have been seen occasionally in European larvae, such as those observed by F. de Filippi in the Italian Alps.

## Latent Evolutionary Tendencies

Despite their permanent larval appearance, the axolotl possesses a latent tendency to follow its ancestral course of evolution. While axolotls appear incapable of changing once they have become sexually ripe, this ancestral evolutionary path remains within them. Experiments conducted by Marie von Chauvin have proved that this latent tendency can be revived. If favored by specific circumstances, the ancestral evolutionary traits will reappear in subsequent generations.
