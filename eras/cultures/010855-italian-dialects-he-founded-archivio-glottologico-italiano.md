# Italian Dialects

The study of Italian dialects encompasses the evolution of regional spoken varieties, the emergence of a national literary standard, and the academic efforts to document these linguistic forms.

## Development of the Italian Standard
The process by which a specific dialect gains literary supremacy over a nation is a recognized linguistic phenomenon. In the case of Italy, Tuscan became the type for the Italian language. Once a dialect is adopted as the standard, it typically begins to diverge from its colloquial form. This is evident in modern poetical Italian, which utilizes various older grammatical forms unique to the poetic style.

## Academic Study and Graziadio Isaia Ascoli
Graziadio Isaia Ascoli (1820–1907) was an Italian philologist born in Gorz to a family of Jewish descent. Recognized as the greatest authority on Italian linguistics, Ascoli demonstrated a marked linguistic talent from an early age. While he contributed learned research to the study of Semitic languages, Indo-European languages, and the gipsy language, his primary field of specialization was Italian dialects.

In 1860, Ascoli was appointed as a professor of philology at Milan. In 1873, he founded the *Archivio glottologico italiano*, which served as a major organ for original scholarship on the subject of dialects. Within this publication, he published his *Saggi Ladini*. His expertise was further cemented by his article in the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* (9th edition, revised), which is considered the classic exposition on the subject in the English language.

## Dialects in Historical Manuscripts
Historical texts provide evidence of the use of regional dialects prior to the standardization of the language. For example, it has been presumed that the work of Marco Polo was originally written in an Italian dialect. Evidence for this is found in the Latin version of the work executed by Fra Pipino, who described his task as a translation *de vulgari*. A manuscript copy of Pipino’s Chronicle in the library at Modena specifically refers to this version as being made "*ex vulgari idiomate* Lombardico."

Furthermore, the Magliabecchian Library at Florence holds a manuscript known as *L’Ottima* (due to the purity of its Tuscan) and *Della Crusca* (as it was an authority for that body's Vocabulary). This manuscript is considered the oldest in any Italian dialect. A note on the book states it was written in Florence by Michael Ormanni, who died in 1309, and was later brought into the Del Riccio family.
