# Aryan Stock

The term "Aryan" refers to a group of people and languages belonging to the Eastern branch of the Indo-European (or Indo-Germanic) family. The word is derived from roots found in several ancient languages: *arya-* in Sanskrit, *airya-* in Zend, and *ariya-* in Old Persian. It was used as a national name in Persia, Bactria, and India, and is associated with a sense of dignity among the peoples who used it.

## Linguistic Characteristics and Etymology
The Aryan group of languages is distinguishable from other Indo-European languages by four primary characteristics:
1. The confusion of original long and short *e* and *o* sounds with the original long and short *a* sound.
2. The representation of the short schwa-sound by *i* (for example, the word for father is *pita*).
3. The transformation of original *s* into *s* after certain consonants, *i*, and *u*.
4. A genitive plural suffix *-nam* for stems ending in a vowel, borrowed from stems ending in *-n* (e.g., Sanskrit *asvanam* and Zend/Old Persian *aspanam*).

Within this group, distinctions exist between Sanskrit and Iranian languages. In Iranian, the Aryan voiced aspirates (*gh, dh, bh*) are confused with original *g, d, b*. Additionally, Aryan *s* becomes *h* initially before vowels and in some medial cases, a trait resembling Greek. While Sanskrit and Iranian are remarkably alike—allowing for the transliteration of whole sentences through regular phonetic changes—they differ in their numeral for "one" (*eka-* in Sanskrit versus *aeva-* in Zend and *aiva-* in Old Persian).

Regarding etymology, Max Muller suggested a connection to the Sanskrit word for earth, *ira*, though this is considered uncertain. Spiegel noted that while the word connects to a root *ar-*, there is no certain criterion to decide which specific meaning of that root applies. A proposed connection to *Erin* (Ireland) is considered impossible, though the term may share an origin with the *Ario-* found in Celtic and Germanic names such as *Ariovistus*.

## Distribution and Migration
Scholars use the compound "Indo-Aryan" to describe the branch that settled in India, distinguishing them from the "Iranians" (a term of the same origin) who remained in Persia and Bactria. The term "Aryo-Indian" is sometimes used to distinguish this stock from other inhabitants of the Indian peninsula, such as the Dravidians.

The original home of the primitive Aryan race is not certainly known, but current linguistic tendencies suggest it may have been in Europe. It is now acknowledged by linguists that the theory of Bactria as the cradle of the Indo-European race was wrong. The eastern part of Iran is believed to be the region where the Aryans lived as a single people before separating into Indians and Iranians.

The Hindus are not an indigenous people of India; they likely entered from the other side of the Hindu Kush. By approximately 1000 B.C., the period of their earliest literature, they were settled in the Indus valley, having pushed through the Kabul valley.

## Cultural and Religious Associations
In the context of early Buddhist terminology, the word *ariya* (translated as "noble" in "Noble Path" or "Noble Truth") also means Aryan. In this usage, the ethical meaning of the word became predominant, though it remained associated with tribal meaning.

In the realm of religion, the comparative study of Indo-European speech has not allowed for the reconstruction of a single common religion, as separate Aryan cult-systems likely developed after the population diffused. However, philology reveals that many Aryan peoples used a word derived from a root meaning "bright" to designate a high god, appearing as *Dyaus* in Sanskrit and *Zeus* or *Jupiter* in other branches.

In the Hellenic nation, Aryan tribes from the north blended with pre-Hellenic Mediterranean stocks, resulting in a religion that combined early Aryan or Indo-Germanic beliefs with indigenous Mediterranean practices.

## Zoroastrianism and the Avesta
Zoroaster (Zarathustra), an Aryan, founded the religion of Zoroastrianism. His teachings are preserved in the Zend Avesta. A central feature of this faith is the opposition between Ormuzd, the god of light, truth, and the sun, and Ahriman, the god of darkness, night, and cunning.

The religion is characterized by a sacerdotal system featuring priests, temples, and altars with sacred fires, though it eschews images. It strictly prohibits the burial or burning of the dead. The Parsees of India, the last surviving Zoroastrians, continue the practice of placing the dead in "Towers of Silence" for vultures.

The Avesta is written in a sacred language. While some have called this "old Bactrian" because Zoroaster likely preached in the fertile, mountainous region of Bactria (between the Hindu Kush and the Oxus), scholars have abandoned this term.
