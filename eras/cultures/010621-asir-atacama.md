# ASIR AND ATACAMA

## Asir
Asir is a district located in western Arabia, situated between 17 deg. 30' and 21 deg. N. latitude and 40 deg. 30' and 45 deg. E. longitude. It is bounded by the Red Sea to the west, Hejaz to the north, Nejd to the east, and Yemen to the south. The region's geography consists of a lowland zone known as the Tehama, which is hot, barren, and approximately 20 to 30 miles wide along the coast. The principal locations in the Tehama are the chief port, Kanfuda, as well as the smaller ports of El Itwad and Marsa Hali.

Beyond the lowlands, the terrain rises into a mountainous tract with an average altitude between 6,000 and 7,000 feet. This area is fertile and populous, characterized by a temperate climate and regular rainfall. The mountainous region merges into a highland plateau that slopes gradually northeast toward the Nejd steppes. The district's valleys are well-watered and produce dates and cereals. Notable valleys include the Wadi Besha and the Wadi Taraba; the latter is home to a considerable town of the same name, surrounded by gardens and palm groves, which is noted for its resistance to the forces of Mehemet Ali in 1815. 

Other significant areas include the district of Besha, a vital position between Taif and Sana, and the fertile district of Khamis Mishet, which is the highest in Asir and is traversed by the W. Shahran. To the west of Besha lies the Zahran district, where the large market town of Makhwa exports grain to Mecca. Further south is the Shamran district. The landscape is marked by fortified villages on heights and the presence of juniper forests.

The inhabitants of Asir are described as a brave and warlike race of mountaineers who have maintained their independence through the natural strength of their country. Since the start of the 19th century, they have been bigoted Wahhabis, though they were previously viewed by neighbors as lax Mahommedans. During the occupation of Nejd by Mehemet Ali, the inhabitants of Asir conducted constant raids on Egyptian communications, leading to several punitive expeditions that saw little success. Similarly, the Turks have made repeated attempts to subjugate the region following the reconquest of Yemen, though they have achieved little beyond holding isolated points in the interior, such as Manadir and Ibha, and occupying Kanfuda.

## Atacama Province
Atacama is a province in northern Chile, bounded by the provinces of Coquimbo to the south and Antofagasta to the north. It extends from the Pacific coast in the west to the Argentine boundary line in the east, covering an area of 30,729 square miles. As of 1902, the population was 71,446.

The province is a major mining district. Silver ore was first discovered there in 1832 by a shepherd named Juan Godoi. While many silver and copper mines are worked on a small scale with limited capital, some are among the most productive in the world. The region also possesses extensive deposits of borax and nitrate, and common salt is found in the elevated desert regions of the Andes. Primary exports include borax, guano, nitrate of soda, silver, copper, and various other minerals.

The capital of the province is Copiapo, which in 1902 had an estimated population of 8,991. It is located on the Copiapo River, 37 miles from the coast and 51 miles southeast of Caldera, the district's principal port. Historically, Atacama was considered Bolivian territory until 1842, when guano began to be recognized as an exportable product. Chile decided to explore the desert coast in 1842 and organized the area extending north to the 26th parallel into the province of Atacama in 1843.

## The Atacama Desert
The Atacama Desert is a saline, barren, and arid region in western South America. It is classified as a "hot desert" because high temperatures and deficient rainfall result in evaporation exceeding precipitation. The desert covers the majority of the Chilean provinces of Antofagasta and Atacama, the south-western corner of the Bolivian department of Potosi, and the Argentine territory of Los Andes.

The region is divided by elevation:
*   **Puna de Atacama:** The higher elevations, ranging from 7,000 to 13,500 feet, form a broken, mountainous, and sometimes volcanic region. It features an irregular line of extinct volcanoes and peaks extending northeast into southern Bolivia. The plateaus are dry and cold in winter and subject to rainstorms in summer, featuring occasional coarse grass, dry salt basins, and large saline lagoons.
*   **Western Slope:** The land descends toward the Pacific through mountainous ridges and great basins or terraces, which open into sandy plains known for nitrate deposits. The coast is marked by sharply-sloping bluffs between 800 and 1,500 feet high.

Though considered worthless for three and a half centuries, the region is a mineral treasure-house containing iron, cobalt, nickel, lead, silver, copper, borax, and nitrates.

## Atacamite
Atacamite is a mineral first named by D. de Gallizen in 1801 and originally found in the Atacama Desert. It is a cupric oxychloride ($\text{CuCl}_2 \cdot 3\text{Cu}(\text{OH})_2$) that crystallizes in the orthorhombic system. It is typically dark green, with a specific gravity of 3.7 and a hardness of about 3. 

Atacamite is relatively rare and can form through the action of sea-water on copper-ores or as a volcanic product on Vesuvian lavas. While found in Chile—where it was known as *arsenillo* and referred to as "Remolinite" at the Los Remolinos locality—it also occurs in West Africa (near Ambriz at Bembe) and South Australia (specifically Wallaroo). In a pulverulent state, it was previously used as a pounce under the name "Peruvian green sand."
