# Ashanti and Jaman

## Origins and Early Expansion
The Ashanti first drew the attention of Europeans in the early 18th century, primarily due to their successful military campaigns against kingdoms bordering the maritime territory. The foundation of Ashanti power is attributed to Osai Tutu, who is considered the real founder of the empire. Osai Tutu is credited with either building or significantly extending the city of Kumasi. 

Under his leadership, the empire expanded through conquests to the east and west. In 1719, he subdued the neighboring state of Denkera, as well as Banna and the Mahommedan countries of Gaman (Jaman). Osai Tutu was eventually defeated and slain in 1731, after which his successor, Osai Apoko, continued the empire's growth by making further acquisitions toward the coast.

## Conflict with the Fanti and British Intervention
In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina ascended to the throne. An ambitious leader, he sought to establish communications with white nations. In 1807, a conflict arose when two chiefs of the Assin, whom Osai Tutu Quamina had defeated in battle, sought refuge with the Fanti, the ruling people on the coast. When the Fanti refused to surrender these fugitives, the Ashanti invaded their country, defeating them and driving them toward the sea.

The Ashanti forces reached the coast near Anamabo, where a British fort was located. Despite mediation efforts by the British governor, the townsmen refused to come to terms. This resulted in the destruction of the town and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants. The Ashanti suffered over 2,000 casualties and were unable to storm the English fort, though the garrison was reduced from twenty-four men to eight.

## The Administration of Colonel G. Torrane
A truce was eventually established. The Ashanti king refused to negotiate with anyone other than the governor of Cape Coast, Colonel G. Torrane (who served as governor from 1805 to 1807). Torrane traveled to Anamabo, where he was received with great pomp. He agreed to surrender the fugitive Assin chiefs; while one managed to escape, the other was handed over and executed by the Ashanti.

Torrane concluded an agreement that acknowledged the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland. He delivered half of the fugitives in Anamabo fort to the Ashanti, while most of the remaining fugitives were sold as slaves by Torrane and his council. Additionally, the governor agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for both Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort. Colonel Torrane died on the coast in 1808.

## Diplomatic Efforts and the Battle of Essamako
The British government in London, recognizing the power of the African kingdom, sought to cultivate intercourse with it. In January 1819, Mr. Joseph Dupuis, formerly the consul at Mogador, arrived at Cape Coast to serve as consul to Kumasi. He reached Kumasi on February 28, 1820, after setting out on February 9. Following meetings with the king, Dupuis drew up a treaty that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies.

This treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, who viewed it as a betrayal of the natives under British protection. The government in London supported Hope Smith and assumed direct control of the British settlements in 1821. The first crown-appointed governor, Sir Charles M'Carthy, supported the Fanti. However, on January 21, 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles at Essamako, a location beyond the Prah. The Ashanti force of 10,000 men overwhelmed Sir Charles's 500. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi.

## Decline of Coastal Influence
The defeat of Sir Charles M'Carthy occurred on the same day that Osai Tutu Quamina died, leading to the succession of Osai Okoto. A period of chronic warfare followed until August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a significant defeat at Dodowah. 

Following the battle of Dodowah, Ashanti power over the coastal tribes began to wane. The payment of ground rent for British forts ceased. By 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from George Maclean, the administrator of the Gold Coast. This peace agreement cost the king 600 oz. of gold and required him to send his son to Cape Coast as a hostage.
