# Etymology and Conceptions of Baal and Related Terms

## The Term Baal and Its Variations
The word *Baal* serves as an ordinary title or word for a deity, particularly a local deity. This is evidenced by place names such as Baal Hermon and Baal Hazor, which are likely contractions of longer forms such as Beth Baal Meon (the House or Temple of the Baal of Meon). In certain contexts, *ba'al* is used as a noun of relation; for example, "a ba'al of hair" refers to a hairy man, "a ba'al of wings" refers to a winged creature, and "ba'als of arrows" refers to archers. Additionally, "ba'al of oath" is used to describe conspirators.

## Evolution of Baal Worship
Historically, the conception of Baal evolved from local associations to more universal ones. Local gods associated with springs and streams that fertilized fields were eventually identified with the common source of all streams. This transition allowed numerous baals to be viewed as forms of one absolute deity. Consequently, Baal became identified with supreme powers of nature, such as the sun, the weather, the heavens, or planets. This shift from earthly to heavenly associations is characteristic of a higher type of belief and occurred relatively later.

Specific manifestations of this deity include:
*   **Baal-Shamem:** The Baal of the heavens, known among the Hittites during the time of Rameses II and later as a title for a Phoenician god in the beginning of the 7th century.
*   **Babylonian Baal:** In Babylonia, Baal became a definite individual deity identified with the planet Jupiter.
*   **Egyptian and Greek Influence:** Baal and Astarte were venerated in Memphis and Thebes during the XIXth Dynasty. Through Aramaean influence, who used the spelling *Bel*, Baal eventually became the Greek *Belos*, who was identified with Zeus.

## Baalzebub and Related Deities
In the Old Testament, Ahaziah ben Ahab, king of Israel, inquired of Baalzebub, the god of the Philistine city Ekron. While some suggest *Zebub* refers to a place, it is usually identified with the Hebrew common noun for flies. In this context, Baalzebub is the Baal to whom flies belong or are holy, symbolizing the warmth of the sun. Other interpretations suggest the deity was an "averter of insects" or the Egyptian dung-beetle (*Scarabaeus pillularius*). Some scholars, such as Cheyne, view "Baalzebub" as a contemptuous modification of the name "Baalzebul." Other compounded names include Baalgad, the Baal of Fortune.

## Etymological Theories of "Bonfire"
The word "bonfire" (Early English *bone-fire* and Scottish *bane-fire*) originally referred to a fire of bones, though it now denotes any large open-air fire lit for rejoicing. The derivation *ban fyre ignis ossium* was recorded in the *Catholicon Anglicum* in A.D. 1483. 

Several rejected derivations have been proposed for the term:
*   **Baal-fire:** Some suggested the word derived from *Baal-fire*, evolving through *Bael* and *Baen* to *Bane*.
*   **Boon-fire:** Others suggested it was a "contribution" fire, similar to the "contributed Ploughing Days" (*Bone-daags*) in Northumberland, by analogy with *boen-harow* ("harrowing by gift").

Relatedly, the Old English *bael* referred to a blazing fire or funeral pyre, and is found in the northern English tautology "bale-fire."

## Beltane and Celtic Connections
The term *Beltane* (the 1st of May) was traditionally regarded, following Cormac, as a combination of the Celtic *teine* (fire) and the name of a god such as Bil, Baal, or Bel. This led to theories identifying Beltane fires with the worship of the Semitic Baal. However, scientific philologists now repudiate this etymology. The *New English Dictionary* accepts the view of Dr. Whitley Stokes that the Gaelic form of *beltane* has no connection with *teine* (fire). Aug. Fick further derives the element *bel* from an old Celtic root meaning "shining."
