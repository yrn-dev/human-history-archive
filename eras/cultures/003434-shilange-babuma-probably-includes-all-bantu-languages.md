# Bantu Languages

The Bantu languages constitute a vast and striking family of speech found across the African continent, extending from slightly north of the equator to the southern extremity of Africa. Philologists distinguish this as one of the great language families of the world. The domain of Bantu speech is characterized by a high degree of homogeneity, which stands in contrast to the linguistic confusion found in Africa north of the Bantu borderland.

## Geographical Distribution and Classification
Broadly, the domain of Bantu speech is divided into four primary sections:
*   **The Great Lakes and East Coast:** Extending down to and including the Zambezi basin.
*   **The South-Central group:** Including Bechuana-Zulu.
*   **The South-West:** Stretching from the Angola-Congo coast and Damaraland to the southern part of the Belgian Congo.
*   **The Western group:** Encompassing the languages of Cameroon and the Northern and Central Congo, and potentially group No. 2 of the Semliki river and Albert Nyanza.

Within these broad sections, numerous specific groups and assemblages exist. In the Central Congo, the Bakuba assemblage—which includes dialects such as Shilange, Babuma, and Songe—likely encompasses all Bantu languages between the Upper Kwilu, Kwa-Kasai, and the Lomami river to the east, with the Sankuru river possibly forming its northern boundary. Other notable groups include the Rua-Luba-Lunda-Marungu group (including Lulua, Kanyoka, and Ki-tabwa) in the south central Congo basin, and the Balolo group of the Northern Congo bend.

In the west, the Bateke (Batio) group covers areas east of the Atlantic coast-lands and south of the Ngoko and Upper Ogowe rivers. Further north, the Duala group on the Cameroon river estuary represents a pure Bantu type, while the Fan or Pangwe forms in the interior of French Gabun and German Cameroon are so corrupt they are barely recognizable as Bantu. In the south, the Bechuana group (including Se-suto, Ci-venda, and Se-pedi) extends from the Zambezi in the north to the Orange river in the south.

## Linguistic Characteristics
Bantu languages are identified by distinct phonological and grammatical features. The vowels are of the Italian type, and true Bantu languages lack obscure sounds such as *ue* and *oe*. A fundamental rule is that every word must end in a vowel, and no two consonants may appear together without an intervening vowel, except for sibilants, labials, or nasals. Additionally, consonants are not doubled.

The Bantu verb is structured around a practically unchangeable root, which serves as the second person singular of the imperative. Various particles—often reduced prepositions, adverbs, or worn-down verbs acting as auxiliaries—are prefixed and suffixed to this root to create tenses and moods. While this system was likely based on a common original tongue, the specific building of moods and tenses likely occurred independently across principal groups. One shared exception is the formation of the preterite tense; for example, the Zulu word *tanda* (love) becomes *tandile* (have loved), utilizing a particle (*-ile* or *-ili*) connected to the root for "to be" or "exist."

## Archaic Dialects and Evolution
Certain dialects are noted for preserving archaic forms and original Bantu roots. These include the speech of Ruwenzori, Mount Elgon, Uganda, Unyoro, and the north coast of Tanganyika. Other primitive examples include Kimakonde on the east coast, Ci-bodzo in the Zambezi delta, and the Subiya speech of the western Zambezi. While Zulu-Kaffir was once considered the most archaic, it has been deposed by the languages of the Great Lakes, as it exhibits recent deviations in phonetics and vocabulary. Ki-swahili of Zanzibar is described as primitive in its roots, though not in its prefixes.

## Origins and Human Migration
The study of these languages suggests that approximately 3,000 years ago, a powerful tribe of negroes speaking the Bantu mother-language migrated from the heart of Africa—the region between the western Nile, Congo, and Shari watersheds. This group was physically allied with negroes of the southern Lake Chad and south-western Nile basins, though impregnated with the Caucasian Hamite.

As they pushed into the southern half of the continent, they swept over areas previously inhabited by Forest Pygmies, West Coast negroes, Nilotic-negroes, and Hottentot-Bushmen. The Bantu people assimilated or absorbed the vast majority of these preceding populations. Consequently, while there is some physical resemblance among those who speak clearly-marked Bantu dialects (such as the Ova-herero, Zulu-Kaffirs, and the people of the Great Lakes), there is great diversity in physical characteristics among Bantu speakers today. This includes Forest Pygmies who are dwarfish, individuals with relationships to Hottentots, those resembling West African negroes, and those of Nilotic or Gala (Galla) origin.
