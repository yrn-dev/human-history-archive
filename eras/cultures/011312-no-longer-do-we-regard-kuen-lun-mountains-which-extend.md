# Kuen-Lun Mountains

The Kuen-Lun (or Kwen-Lun) is a major mountain system in Central Asia that generally designates the ranges running along the northern edge of the great Tibetan plateau. In the tectonic structure of Asia, the Kuen-Lun serves as the backbone of the continent and is significantly older than the Tian-shan to the north or the Himalayas to the south.

## Geographical Extent and Definitions
The definition of the Kuen-Lun varies based on the application of the term:
*   **Wide Application:** The system consists of a succession of ranges extending from the Pamirs in the west to 113° E., where they merge with or strike the steep escarpments of the southeastern flank of the Mongolian plateau. In this sense, the mountains stretch in a wavy line for nearly 2,500 miles.
*   **Narrow Application:** The term applies specifically to the ranges separating the Tibetan plateau in the south from the Takla-makan desert in the north, between the Pamirs and the transverse glen of the Kara-muren (approximately 85½° E.).

While geographical usage is often restricted, the system actually extends eastwards to the great depression of Tsaidam (roughly 95° E.). There is scholarly debate regarding its further eastward continuation; F. Grenard, K. Bogdanovich, and Sven Hedin suggest it continues as the parallel ranges of Arka-tagh and Kalta-alaghan, which lie south of the Astin-tagh. The Astin-tagh itself is the westward continuation of the Nan-shan (Southern Mountains), which reach 113° E. into China.

## Physical Characteristics and Structure
The Kuen-Lun is characterized by a "twinning" of mountain ranges and intermont lake-basins, a peculiar feature of the Tibetan plateau. In the west, the ranges are squeezed and folded by lateral compression into a breadth of 150–200 miles, resulting in higher altitudes. In the east, they spread to a breadth of 600 miles and are flatter and lower.

The crests of the component ranges typically reach altitudes of 21,500 to 22,000 feet. Unlike the Himalayas, the Kuen-Lun generally lacks individual towering peaks of commanding elevation, maintaining a relatively uniform altitude slightly above the lofty valleys that separate the ranges.

## Western Kuen-Lun
The western extremity is a rugged region. The Pamir highlands are separated from the East Turkestan lowlands by the double border-ridge of Sarik-kol (comprising the Muztagh or Kashgar range and the Sarik-kol range). The Muztagh-ata peak in this region reaches 25,780 feet. East of the meridian of Yarkand (77° E.), this double border-ridge continues as twin ranges running from the west-northwest to the east-southeast.

The northerly range includes the Keriya, Khotan, and Kilian (or Kiliang) mountains, while the southerly range includes the Ullugh-tagh, Sughet, and Raskem (or Raskan) mountains. These ranges reach elevations of 19,000 feet, with some peaks ascending another 2,000–2,500 feet. The ranges are separated by deep glens, such as the Kara-kash (Khotan-darya) between the Kilian and Sughet mountains, and the Raskem (Yarkand-darya) gorge, which separates the southerly range from the Karakorum Mountains.

## Regional Geography and Trade
The Kuen-Lun forms the northern limit of the Chang Tang, the elevated highland steppes of Tibet. Between Tibet and the Gobi sands lies a system of elevated valleys known as Tsaidam, situated 8,000 to 9,000 feet above sea level. This intermediate steppe, located east of Lop Nor, explains why great trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed far to the north.

To the north of the Kuen-Lun lies the Tarim Basin (Eastern Turkestan), an area of rivers that end in intermittent lakes and swamps rather than the sea. While the mountain barrier to the west of the Tarim Basin is high, it is not forbidding. Travelers from China to Kashgar can move westward via the Tarim valley or along the northern foothills of the Kuen-Lun. Kashgar serves as a convergence point for roads leading over the mountains to Bokhara, Samarkand, and Kokand, acting as a natural historical meeting place for Mongolian and Aryan peoples.

## Hydrography and Environment
The Kuen-Lun is the source of several water systems. The Khotan-darya rises in the Kuen-Lun from two headstreams, the Yurun-kash and the Kara-kash. These unite and flow 180 miles north across the Takla-makan desert—carrying water only in early summer—before emptying into the Tarim.

In the northeast of Tibet, the Kuen-Lun ranges surround the salt lake of Koko-nor (Tsing-hai), which sits at 9,975 feet. This lake is bordered by the southern Koko-nor range to the south and the Nan-shan Mountains to the north.

The environment of the surrounding Tibetan plateau is characterized by extreme cold and drought, resulting in a poor, desert-like landscape with scanty vegetation. Trees are rarely found except in sheltered spots where they are artificially planted.
