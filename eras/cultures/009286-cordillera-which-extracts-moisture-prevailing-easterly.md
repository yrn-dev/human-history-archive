# Cordillera

The term "cordillera" is a Spanish word derived from *cordilla*, which is the diminutive of *cuerda* (meaning a cord or rope), and is used to describe a range or chain of mountains. The term was first applied to the Andes ranges of South America (*Las Cordilleras de los Andes*) and was subsequently used for the extension of that system into Mexico. In North America, the label is used for the parallel ranges of mountains that include the Sierra Nevada and the Rocky Mountains, with the surrounding area termed the "Cordilleran region." While the term has been applied to the eastern mountain system of Australia, it is not used as a generic term for parallel mountain ranges outside of the Americas.

## The Andean Cordilleras of Bolivia

In Bolivia, the Andean cordilleras cross the south-west corner of the republic and project east toward the Brazilian highlands, forming a great obtuse angle. These cordilleras comprise roughly two-fifths of Bolivia's total area. The system is divided into two great parallel chains with spurs and flanking ranges to the east, creating a *massif* consisting of parallel ranges that enclose deep ravines and great elevated plateaus.

North-east of Lake Titicaca, the ridges intersect in a confused mass or knot known as the Nudo de Apolobamba, which contains some of the highest peaks in South America. To the west, the Cordillera Occidental serves as part of the boundary between Bolivia and the northern provinces of Chile, following the coast outline and acting as the western rampart for the *alta-planicie* (the great Bolivian tableland).

## The Bolivian Tableland (Alta-Planicie)

The *alta-planicie* extends from the Serrania de Lipez on the Argentine frontier in the south to the Vilcanota knot in Peru in the north. It measures approximately 500 miles in length and 80 miles in breadth, covering about 40,000 square miles. The plateau has an average elevation of about 12,650 feet near Lake Titicaca, descending by about 1,000 feet toward its southern end. 

The plateau is a great lacustrine basin that once held an inland sea, which exited to the east via the La Paz gorge. The region is characterized by:
* **The Puna:** The northern part of the plateau.
* **The Desert of Lipez:** The southern part, which is barren and arid, resembling the Puna de Atacama.

The plateau is broken by peaks and ridges, the highest of which is the Cerro de Tahua at 17,454 feet. Overlooking this tableland from the west are several snow-clad peaks, including Sajama (21,047 ft.), Huallatiri (21,654 ft.), Parinacota (20,918 ft.), Pomarape (20,505 ft.), Licancaur (19,685 ft.), San Pedro y Pablo (19,423 ft.), Oyahua (19,226 ft.), and Lirima (19,128 ft.).

## Climatic Influence of the Cordillera

Bolivia is located entirely within the torrid zone, meaning temperature variations are driven by prevailing winds, mountain barriers, and elevation. This creates a vertical arrangement of climatic zones, ranging from tropical lowlands to the Arctic cold of snow-capped peaks.

The Cordillera plays a critical role in governing rainfall. To the east of the Cordillera Oriental, rains fall throughout the year, with the rainy season typically occurring from November to March. However, the Cordillera extracts moisture from the prevailing easterly winds. Consequently, the elevated plateaus on the west side of the Cordillera experience limited rainfall in the north, which diminishes toward the south until the land becomes absolutely barren. While electrical wind storms are frequent at these high altitudes, brief and furious rain-storms occur only during a short season and are not frequent on the northern plateau.

## Regional Climatic Zones

The natives of Bolivia categorize the climatic zones based on elevation:
* **Yungas:** Lowlands and mountain valleys up to 5,000 feet. This zone is exceedingly humid with a tropical temperature (mean official estimate of 70° F.) and no winter.
* **Valle (or medio yungas):** Deep valleys from 5,000 to 9,500 feet. This sub-tropical region is sometimes called a region of perpetual summer.
* **Cabezera de valle:** The heads of deep valleys from 9,500 to 11,000 feet. This temperate zone has regular seasons favorable for vegetables and cereals.
* **Puna:** The great central plateau between 11,000 and 12,500 feet. The air is cold and dry with a mean official temperature of 54° F. It has only two seasons (winter and a cold summer or autumn), and the warm season is too short for most crops except barley and potatoes.
* **Puna brava:** Extending from 12,500 feet to the snow limit (approximately 17,500 feet), this bleak territory is inhabited only by miners and shepherds.

Above the *puna brava* lies the region of eternal snow, described as an Arctic zone within the tropics.
