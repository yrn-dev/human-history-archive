# Basketry and Basket Materials

## Overview of Basketry
A basket is a vessel used for carrying, protecting, or holding commodities, constructed by interweaving materials such as rushes, cane, or twigs. The practice of interweaving leaves, rushes, or twigs is found among the world's rudest nations and is considered one of the most ancient and universal industries. It is likely the origin of all textile arts. 

The influence of basketry is evident in other arts; for example, the basket mould used before the invention of the potter's wheel left marks that served as decorative designs in old ceramic ware. Additionally, the art is traceable in the mouldings or capitals of Byzantine architecture and in the willow pattern found on old china. Historically, the importance of this primitive craft tends to be in inverse ratio to the industrial development of a given people. Regarding its etymology, while some have identified the word "basket" with the Latin *bascauda* (which denotes a brazen vessel, tray, or tub), the *New English Dictionary* states there is no evidence for this connection, and the etymology remains unknown.

## Global Materials and Techniques
Different regions utilize various local botanical sources for basket manufacture:

### Asia and the East
In China and Japan, baskets of "unequalled beauty and finish" are manufactured from splits of various bamboo species. The Japanese specifically use bamboo wicker-work to encase delicate egg-shell porcelain, and both the Chinese and Japanese excel in applying this wicker-work to furniture. In India and the East generally, the leaves of various screw pine species and the odorous roots of the khus-khus grass (*Anatherum muricatum*) are used. India also produces "Cajan" baskets from the fronds of the Palmyra palm (*Borassus flabelliformis*), a manufacture that has also been established as an important staple in Germany's Black Forest.

### Africa and the Middle East
In the Seychelles, the fronds of the palm *Lodoicea sechellarum* are used for delicate basket-work. In Spain and Algeria, esparto fibre is employed to create rude fruit baskets. In Africa, tribes in Central Africa and Abyssinians display great adroitness in weaving. The Hottentots and Kaffirs of South Africa utilize plant roots and the Ilala reed. In other African territories, native industries include the making of baskets and mats.

### The Americas and Oceania
In New Zealand, baskets are made from strips of the flax plant *Phormium tenax*. In the West Indies and South America, various species of *Maranta* provide materials; specifically, in Trinidad, a species of *Calathea* known as *Tirite* (a member of the order *Zingiberaceae*) is used. South American Indians also weave useful baskets from other palms, including the Carnahuba. In North America, Indians create strong, water-tight *Wattape* baskets from the roots of a species of *abies*. These are often adorned with patterns made from the dyed quills of the native porcupine (*Erethizon dorsatum*). Some of these specimens are highly valued as heirlooms, with some selling for as much as L300, and it has been estimated that baskets valued at L1,000,000 were drawn from Arizona and California within a two-year period.

## Regional Industries and Modern Developments

### European Industry
In Portugal, the town of Loulé—located in the district of Faro—has basket-making as its principal industry. The town also manufactures products from esparto grass, agave, and palm.

In England, the character of basket-work changed significantly starting around the middle of the 19th century. Cheap continental goods replaced domestic wares like the reticule and old English cradle, while rough casks and cases made from imported timber largely superseded coarse brown osier hampers and packing. However, this was offset by a rise in high-class production, including tea-baskets, luncheon baskets, and furniture such as lounges, tables, and finely made chairs. Other English manufactures include laundry and linen baskets, fruit and vegetable baskets, partition baskets for wine, transit hampers, and protective wicker cases for bottles and glass carboys. Additionally, the English military authorities introduced wicker cases made from cane pith to protect shells. The growth of these developments is reflected in the wages lists of the London Union of Journeymen Basketmakers, which grew from 87 pages in 1865 to 144 pages by 1896.

## General Materials
Beyond specialized plants, baskets are frequently constructed from:
* Straw
* Various sedges (*Cyperus*)
* Splints and shavings of many types of wood
