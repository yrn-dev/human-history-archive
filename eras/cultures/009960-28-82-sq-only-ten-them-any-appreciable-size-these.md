# Bonin Islands

## Geography and Composition
The Bonin Islands, known in Japanese as Ogasawara-jima, consist of a chain of small islands belonging to Japan. The chain stretches nearly due north and south, located a little east of 142 E. and extending from 26 deg. 35' to 27 deg. 45' N. The islands are situated approximately 500 meters from the mainland of Japan. According to Japanese investigations, there are twenty islands in total, possessing a superficies of 28.82 sq. m. and a coast-line of 174.65 m.

Of these twenty islands, only ten are considered to have any appreciable size. Listed from the north, these islands are:
* Muko-shima (Bridegroom Island)
* Nakadachi-shima (Go-between Island)
* Yome-shima (Bride Island)
* Ototo-jima (Younger-brother Island)
* Ani-shima (Elder-brother Island)
* Chichi-jima (Father Island)
* Haha-jima (Mother Island)
* Mei-jima (Niece Island)
* Ani-jima (Elder-sister Island)
* Imoto-jima (Younger-sister Island)

## Nomenclature and European Designations
European geographers have traditionally divided the islands into three distinct groups for naming purposes: the northern group is referred to as the Parry Islands, the central group as the Beechey Islands, and the southern group as the Bailey or Coffin Islands.

In 1827, Captain Beechey applied specific names to several islands in Japanese cartography. He named Chichi-jima—the second largest of all the islands—as Peel Island. He also designated Ototo-jima as Stapleton Island and Ani-jima as Buckland Island. Additionally, Captain Beechey named two bays: Fitton Bay, located on the south of Peel Island, and Walker Bay, located on the south-west of Buckland Island.

## Geological Features and Anchorage
The islands are plainly volcanic in nature. Port Lloyd, which serves as the chief anchorage and is situated on Peel Island, was visited by Commodore Perry in 1853. Perry strongly urged the establishment of a United States coaling station at this location. He observed that Port Lloyd appeared to have formerly been the crater of a volcano, suggesting that the surrounding hills were thrown up from this point and that the entrance to the harbour was once a fissure through which lava poured into the sea.
