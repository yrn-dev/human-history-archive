# Javanese and Kawi Languages

The Javanese and Kawi languages are members of the Malayan class of languages. This group also includes other tongues such as Sundanese and Madurese. While these languages share a common classification, they maintain significant distinctions; for example, the Balinese language—which belongs to the same group—is as distinct from Javanese as French is from Italian.

## Kawi and Old Javanese
The term "Kawi-sprache" is associated with the language of the island of Java, as seen in the work of the scholar William von Humboldt, who published an essay on the subject in Berlin in 1836. The literary language of Bali, specifically, has incorporated many of its elements from Old Javanese. This form of the language was spoken in Java during the 15th century, the period coinciding with the fall of Majapahit.

## Linguistic Relations and Influence
The Javanese language has had some interaction with other colonial tongues. In Dutch East India, "pure Dutch" was largely preserved, although some Javanese and Malay "bastard words" occasionally entered the Dutch vocabulary. This occurred due to the habit of speaking to the local population in either the native idiom or the Malay tongue.

Beyond the native speakers of the region, the language has been a subject of study for foreign scholars. Arthur Coke Burnell (1840–1882), an English Sanskrit scholar and member of the Indian civil service, possessed knowledge of Javanese and Kawi in addition to his expertise in Sanskrit, Tibetan, Arabic, and Coptic.

## Cultural and Literary Expressions
The Javanese language is utilized in various forms of lyrical and dramatic expression. The Javanese distinguish between two specific types of lyrics sung during occasions of popular significance:
*   **Panton**: A short fable or simile.
*   **Tcharita**: A more advanced form of lyric that takes the shape of a dialogue and is recited or sung by actors.

The *tcharita* served as the basis for Javanese drama. In its higher forms, this drama focuses on the stories of kings and gods. Javanese drama shares similarities with Indian drama, particularly in the high importance placed on the manager or director. Additionally, similar to Greek drama, performers in Javanese drama utilize masks constructed from wood. Comic drama in Java is frequently performed by strolling troupes consisting of a woman and two men.
