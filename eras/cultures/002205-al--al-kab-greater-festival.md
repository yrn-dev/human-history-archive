# Al-'[=i]d al-kab[=i]r

Al-'[=i]d al-kab[=i]r, known as the "Greater Festival," is one of the two principal festivals of Islam. In the Turkish language, it is referred to as *Qurb[=a]n Bair[=a]m*, which translates to "Sacrifice Bairam." Arabic speakers also refer to the occasion as *'[=I][d.] al-a[d.][h.][a]*, or the "Festival of Sacrifice."

## Calendar and Timing
The festival is governed by a lunar calendar, which causes its date to shift through the solar year every thirty-two years. Al-'[=i]d al-kab[=i]r occurs during the final month of the lunar year, *Dh[=u]-l-[h.]ijja*. The celebrations begin on the tenth day of this month and continue for the following two or three days.

## Rituals and Commemoration
The central act of the festival is the sacrifice of a legal animal. In the valley of Mina, pilgrims commemorate the ransom of Ishmael with a ram by slaying a cow, a camel, a he-goat, or a ram. This practice is mirrored throughout the wider Muslim world, where all individuals who possess the financial means to do so perform a sacrifice. The resulting flesh is either consumed by the individuals themselves or distributed to the poor.

## Observances and Comparison
The celebration of Al-'[=i]d al-kab[=i]r shares similarities with the "Lesser Festival" (*'[=I]d al-Fitr*), though it is noted to be celebrated with less ardour. Common observances associated with these festivals include:
* The wearing of new clothes.
* The payment of private visits.
* The exchange of congratulations among friends.
* The giving of presents.
* The holding of official receptions.
* The visitation of the graves of relatives.
