# Dualism and Definitional Boundaries in History and Thought

The concept of dualism and the application of sharply defined boundaries appear across various disciplines in the human archive, ranging from metaphysical philosophy and religious systems to historical epochs and biological classification.

## Philosophical and Metaphysical Dualism

In the study of idealism, "common-sense dualism" is defined as the view that experience or knowledge results from an accidental relationship between two independent and separate entities: the mind (including its ideas) and the thing (including its attributes). These two entities serve to limit and condition one another from the outside. This perspective is opposed to idealism, which asserts the complete correlativity of the subject and object in experience.

While modern idealism differs from the "arrested idealism" of Berkeley by agreeing with dualism that knowledge involves a real object different from the self and its passing states, the two theories diverge on the nature of truth. Dualism posits that truth and falsehood are matters of immediate intuition—comparing the truth of propositions to the color of roses—whereas idealism suggests that truth is found in the placement of a fact within a coherent system of mutually related truths. Furthermore, while dualism asserts mere difference to counter the notion of absolute unity, idealism asserts the reality of difference within an external world, distinguishing itself from Spinozism. Spinoza himself sought a refuge from the dualism proposed by Descartes regarding the soul and body.

## Gnostic and Religious Systems

In the religious systems described by the Fathers of the Church, the "sharply-defined dualism" found in other contexts is scarcely present in the system of Basilides. The doctrine attributed to Basilides suggests that 365 heavens and their spirits emanated from a supreme God, known as the *innatus pater*. This numerical value of 365 is also found in the name Abraxas (or Abrasax) and the name of the Persian god Mithras.

The system of Basilides involves the *Pater innatus* being surrounded by Hypostases—ranging from five to seven, including *Nous, Logos, Phronesis, Sophia,* and *Dunamis*, with *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* potentially added. This structure is reminiscent of the *Ameshas-spentas* surrounding Ahura-Mazda. Additionally, the world originates from seven powers, viewed as the lowest emanations of the supreme God. These seven world-creating angels are considered an oriental speculation, likely derived from the planetary deities of later Babylonian religion. The transition of these deities into daemonic or semi-daemonic forces suggests the influence of a stronger Iranian monotheistic religion over the Babylonian system.

## Historical and Social Definitions

The application of sharp definitions is also evident in the categorization of historical periods and social structures. In Japanese history, the Heian epoch is a sharply defined period of 398 years. It began in 794 with the transfer of the Imperial palace from Nara to Kyoto (called "Heian-jo" or Castle of Peace) and ended in 1192 with the establishment of an administrative capital at Kamakura. The first ruler of this epoch was Kwammu, selected by his father, Konin, and Fujiwara Momokawa.

Socially, various civilizations have developed "organizations of restriction" to bar interlopers, such as craft guilds or aristocratic exclusiveness among conquering peoples. Among Aryan peoples, there has been a general tendency to distinguish between common (plebeian) and noble (patrician) families. This distinction was particularly clear in the Middle Ages in Germany, where a methodical conception of the fixity of these classes existed below the princes.

## Scientific and Technical Classifications

In biological classification, specifically regarding certain orders with over a thousand species, researchers like Giesbrecht and Hansen have noted that there are often no "sharply dividing lines" between forms that are free-living, semi-parasitic, or wholly parasitic. Classification efforts have historically relied on mouth-organs (mandibles, maxillae, and maxillipeds), leading to various groupings such as Gnathostoma, Poecilostoma, and Siphonostoma, though some of these were based on erroneous suppositions.

In the field of petrography, marble is described as uniformly crystalline and massive, lacking the bedding or schistosity that would make it fissile. Under polarized light, marble may appear iridescent or show pinkish or greenish white colors. Its microstructure consists of rounded grains of calcite fitting together in a mosaic, often traversed by a lattice-work of sharply defined cleavage cracks corresponding to rhombohedral faces.
