# Ala-tau

The Ala-tau is a mountain range located in Central Asia, serving as a prolongation of the Tian-shan (Celestial Mountains). It is characterized by significant elevation, rising to altitudes of 14,000 or 15,000 feet. Geographically, the range flanks the Syr river to the north and acts as the physical barrier between Lake Issyk-kul and Lake Balkash. The elevation of these lakes is approximately 5,000 feet.

## Regional Geography and Water Systems

The Ala-tau is part of a broader mountainous belt that extends from the 115th meridian at the western end of the Yablonoi range to the extremity of Pamir. This belt is broken into several branches and encloses various isolated drainage areas without outflow, leading to the formation of numerous lakes.

The range is closely associated with the water systems of the region. The Ili, one of the principal rivers of Central Asia, is a chief feeder of Lake Balkash. The head-stream of the Ili, known as the Tekez, rises at an altitude of 11,600 feet on the western slopes of Mount Kash-katur, east of Lake Issyk-kul. After emerging from the mountains and meeting the Kungez, the river becomes the Ili and flows for about 300 meters to Iliysk, positioned between the Boro-khoro and Talki ranges to the north and the Trans-Ili Ala-tau mountains to the south. The Ili eventually falls into Lake Balkash through at least seven mouths.

Lake Balkash itself is the fourth largest lake in Eurasia, with an area of 7,115 square miles and an altitude of 900 feet. It is shaped like a broad crescent, measuring approximately 430 meters in length from west-southwest to east-northeast. While it has no outlet, its waters remain relatively fresh.

## Human Population and Settlement

The Ala-tau range and the surrounding Issyk-kul basin have served as homes for the Kara-Kirghiz nomad highlanders since at least the 13th century. The Kara-Kirghiz are also found in the Chu, Talass, and Tekes river valleys, as well as the Tian-shan range. They are divided into two main sections: the "Right" (On) in the east and the "Left" (Sol) in the west. The On section is situated about Lake Issyk-kul and in the Narin, Tekes, and Chu valleys.

The Kara-Kirghiz are essentially nomads focused on stock breeding. They raise sheep of the fat-tailed species, horses of a hardy but small breed, camels of both species, goats, and oxen, which are used for packing and riding. Their agricultural activities are limited to the cultivation of millet, barley, and wheat; millet is used to distill a coarse brandy or vodka. Trade is primarily conducted via barter, exchanging livestock for manufactured goods with dealers from Russia, Turkestan, and China.

The social structure of the Kara-Kirghiz is governed by tribal rulers known as "manaps," who possess almost unlimited authority over their subjects, including the power to kill or sell them. Since 1864, they have generally recognized Russian sovereignty, though they do not pay taxes.

## Surrounding Ranges and Territories

The Ala-tau is situated among several other significant elevations in the region:
* **Tian-shan:** The Celestial Mountains form the northern boundary of the Gobi desert, lying between 40 and 43 degrees North and 75 and 95 degrees East. Some summits exceed 20,000 feet. The principal cultivated districts and towns of Central Asia are located along the foot of this range.
* **Altai:** Located near the 50th parallel, the Altai rises to 10,000 or 12,000 feet and separates the waters of the lakes of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria from the great rivers of western Siberia.
* **Ural Mountains:** A line of elevation continues west of the Altai to the Ural Mountains, which divide the drainage of south-west Siberia from the plains northeast of the Aral Sea.
