# Religious Worship and Institutions in Bucharest and Ancient History

## Religious Architecture in Bucharest
In Bucharest, the architectural landscape of worship is diverse. The Orthodox Greek churches are typically small structures built of brick in a modified Byzantine style, characterized by very narrow windows. These buildings are generally topped with two or three towers, though the bells are situated approximately twenty paces from the church in wooden porches that resemble lych-gates. 

The metropolitan church, or cathedral, where the metropolitan primate of Rumania officiates, was constructed between 1656 and 1665. This building is shaped like a Greek cross, features four main entrances each with a turret, and is surrounded by a broad cloister. The structure culminates in three brick towers and is situated on high ground, providing a view of the Carpathians and overlooking the city. Other notable churches include the Doamna Balasa (1751), known for its frescoes and rich exterior carved work, and St Spiridion the New (1768), which is the most beautiful and loftiest. Local tradition attributes the Biserica Bucur to a shepherd named Bucur, the legendary founder of the city, though the actual dates and founders of many churches remain unknown because the Greek clergy frequently obliterated Slavonic inscriptions.

Regarding other denominations in Bucharest, the Lipovans, Protestants, and Armenians worship in their own churches. The Jews possess several synagogues. Roman Catholics, who numbered nearly 37,000 in 1899, have only three churches, including the cathedral of St Joseph.

## Worship Practices of the Jews
Historical accounts detail various aspects of the religious life and worship of the Jews. In Jerusalem, the city was viewed as a "common city" by the Idumeans (who were proselytes of justice) and the original native Jews; according to the Rabbins, no single tribe, such as Judah or Benjamin, had exclusive appropriation of the city, as every tribe held an equal right to it when arriving for festivals.

The commitment to Divine worship was often maintained under extreme duress. During Roman assaults, priests continued to burn incense and offer drink-offerings even as enemies attacked with swords, prioritizing their duties to God over their own lives. Similarly, during the siege of the city, Pompey observed that the Jews did not intermit their religious services despite being surrounded by darts. Daily purifications, sacrifices, and every branch of religious worship were performed with utmost exactness. Even as the temple was being taken and people were slain about the altar, they continued the instances of Divine worship appointed by their law.

## Legal and Social Conflicts Regarding Worship
The adherence to specific laws and customs of worship often led to conflict with foreign powers. In one account, Mordecai refused to worship Haman, the son of Amedatha, because he was observant of his own country's laws. Haman, an Amalekite, viewed this refusal with indignation, characterizing the Jews as a "wicked nation" that was unsociable and did not admit the same sort of Divine worship as others.

In other instances, the Jews successfully defended their right to their customs. When the people of Ionia requested that Marcus Agrippa force the Jews to worship the same gods as they did in order to maintain certain citizen privileges, Agrippa ruled that he could not innovate, allowing the Jews to continue using their own customs.

## Comparative Worship and Image Use
There have been various historical perspectives on the use of images in worship. Early Christians were unanimous in condemning heathen image-worship and the adoration of reigning emperors. It was a common accusation—and one that Origen gloried in—that Jews and Christians possessed no temples, no altars, and no known images.

In contrast, other groups integrated images into their practices. Certain Gnostic sects, such as the followers of Marcellina, used painted images and forms made of different materials, including a likeness of Christ they believed was made by Pilate. They crowned these images and placed them alongside images of philosophers like Plato, Aristotle, and Pythagoras.

## Influence on Other Groups
The worship of the Israelites also influenced other populations. The Cutheans, who moved into Samaria from the Persian country of Cuthah, originally brought five different gods from their own nations. After a plague struck them, an oracle revealed they must worship Almighty God. They requested priests of the Israelites from the king of Assyria to teach them the laws and holy worship of God. Once they worshipped Him respectfully, the plague ceased. These people became known as Samaritans in the Greek tongue and Cutlans in Hebrew.
