# Bahr-el-Ghazal

The Bahr-el-Ghazal, also known as the Gazelle river, is the primary western affluent of the Nile river in North-East Africa. It joins the Nile at 9deg 30' N., 30deg 25' E. The river is described as a drainage channel rather than a true river, formed by the junction of numerous rivers.

## Hydrography and Course
The Bahr-el-Ghazal begins at the confluence of the Lol and the Jur; prior to this point, none of the contributing rivers are referred to as the Bahr-el-Ghazal. From this confluence to the junction with the Nile at Lake No, the distance is approximately 200 m. 

The river's course begins by flowing north with lagoon-like expansions that are broad but shallow, reaching a maximum depth of 13 ft. It then turns north-east, where the channel becomes deeper and narrower and is occasionally characterized by reaches of papyrus. Finally, the river turns east and broadens again before reaching Lake No. The banks in this section are typically marked by scrub and anthills; in one valley, the anthills are so densely packed that they resemble a "gigantic graveyard," according to Sir William Garstin. During flood time, the river's rise is barely 3 ft., though this is sufficient to place a vast area of land under water.

## Tributaries and Basin
The basin of the Bahr-el-Ghazal is extensive, reaching south-west to the Congo watershed and north-west to Darfur. The most significant of its tributaries is the Jur. Just above the confluence of the Lol, the Jur expands into Lake Ambadi, which is over a mile broad at low water and 10 m. long, becoming significantly larger during floods. Lake Ambadi is home to many "swimming" variety sudd plants, though ambach and papyrus are absent, and it supports large numbers of *Balaeniceps rex*.

Other notable affluents include:
*   **Bahr-el-Homr:** The main northern feeder, which passes through the country of the Homr Arabs. It is the only important affluent with tributaries originating north of the main stream. Its headwaters are west of 24deg E., where the Shari, Congo, and Nile watersheds meet. It is reinforced by rivers from Dar Fertit and intermittent streams from the hills of Darfur. In 1906, the lower reaches of the Bahr-el-Homr were completely blocked by sudd, stopping the flow of water into the Bahr-el-Ghazal until the Sudan government removed the blocks to restore the waterway.
*   **Lol (Bahr-el-Arab):** The most westerly affluent, which rises in Dar Fertit as the Boro or Telgona. It receives the Bongo, Chel, Sopo, and Raga from the south and south-west. The Lol flows east to about 28deg E. before turning north-east.

## Regional Geography and Environment
The southern affluents of the Bahr-el-Ghazal flow across a plateau of ferruginous laterite in their upper courses, characterized by steep valley banks. North of 7deg 20' N., where rapids occur, the valleys open into tortuous channels often choked by sandbanks. This alluvial region is flooded during the rainy season and transitions around 9deg N. into a sea of swamps that form part of the larger Nile swamp region, making it nearly impossible to trace individual river courses.

The Bahr-el-Ghazal is prone to being choked by sudd, a phenomenon it shares with the Bahr-el-Jebel. During the rule of the Mahdists, the river became almost entirely blocked by these accumulations. British officers removed the sudd from the Jur, the Bahr-el-Ghazal, and other rivers starting in 1901, which established uninterrupted steamboat communication between Wau and Khartum (a distance of some 930 m.) during the flood season.

## Surrounding Territories
The Bahr-el-Ghazal serves as the southern boundary for Darfur, a state of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. The Bahr-el-Homr specifically marks the southern frontier of Darfur for approximately 200 m. In Darfur, the khors (watercourses) in the south and south-east flow toward the Bahr-el-Ghazal.

To the east lies Kordofan. In the south-western region of Kordofan, known as Dar Homr, the Khor Shalango and Wadi el Ghalla drain toward the Homr affluent of the Bahr-el-Ghazal.
