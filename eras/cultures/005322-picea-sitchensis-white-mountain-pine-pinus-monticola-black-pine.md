# Picea sitchensis, Pinus monticola, and Pinus murrayana

The botanical species *Picea sitchensis* (Menzies's spruce), *Pinus monticola* (white mountain pine), and *Pinus murrayana* (black pine) are among the forest growth found in certain provinces of Canada. These species contribute to a region rich in forest growth, where there is a steady demand for lumber in China, Africa, South America, Australia, and other parts of Canada.

## Regional Context and Commercial Timber
In the Canadian province where these trees are found, the forest consists of a variety of important species. Alongside Menzies's spruce, white mountain pine, and black pine, the forests include:
*   **Spruces:** White spruce (*Picea alba*) and Engellmann's spruce (*Picea Engelmanii*).
*   **Pines:** Yellow pine (*Pinus ponderosa*).
*   **Other Conifers:** Western larch (*Larix occidentalis*), Douglas fir (*Pseudotsuga Douglasii*), western hemlock (*Tsuga mertensiana*), giant cedar (*Thuya gigantea*), and yellow cypress or cedar (*Thuya excelsa*).
*   **Broadleaf Trees:** Large leaved maple (*Acer macrophyllum*) and red alder (*Alnus rubra*), as well as western white oak (*Quercus garryana*).

While Menzies's spruce, white mountain pine, and black pine are listed as important trees, the principal timber of commerce in the region is the Douglas fir. This tree can reach 300 feet in height and 8 to 10 feet in diameter; its wood is highly valued for building purposes and ships' spars due to its strength and toughness. Red or giant cedar is also plentiful and used for interior work and shingles. Additionally, western white spruce is frequently employed for various purposes.

## Timber Production and Industry
The province supports approximately eighty sawmills of varying sizes. Timber harvesting is conducted on both provincial and Dominion government lands. In 1904, the total amount of timber cut was 348,031,790 feet, consisting of 22,760,222 feet from Dominion lands and 325,271,568 feet from provincial lands. By 1905, the amount cut on provincial lands increased to 450,385,554 feet, and the cut on Dominion lands also exceeded the 1904 figures.

## Botanical Characteristics of Related Genera
The *Pinus* (pine) and *Picea* (spruce) genera belong to the Coniferae. These gymnosperms are characterized as polycotyledonous, meaning they possess more than two cotyledons arranged in a whorl. In spruce, pine, larch, and fir, observations have recorded six, nine, twelve, or even fifteen cotyledons. These are linear in form and resemble the fasciculated leaves of the larch.

The seed of the pine (*Pinus*) is characterized by a wing, which is a membranous appendage attached to the testa.

## Biological Interactions
Certain species of Uredineae (fungi) exhibit specialized parasitism involving these genera. For example, *Chrysomyxa Rhododendri* develops aecidiospores on *Picea* (spruce) and teleutospores on *Rhododendron*. Similarly, the fungus *Cronartium Ribicola* utilizes *Pinus* (pine) for its teleutospores while developing aecidiospores on *Ribes*.
