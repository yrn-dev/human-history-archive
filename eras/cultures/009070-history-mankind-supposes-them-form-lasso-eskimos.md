# The Eskimo

The Eskimo are indigenous peoples inhabiting the Arctic regions of both the Old and New Worlds. Their society is characterized by specific adaptations to the northern environment, including specialized hunting tools, the breeding of semi-domesticated dogs, and a spiritual worldview centered on the natural elements.

## Population and Distribution
In the region of Danish Greenland, the population has experienced fluctuations. While Hans Egede estimated the population at 30,000 in the middle of the 18th century, this is considered a likely over-estimate. The population decreased during this period, primarily due to infectious diseases, most notably a severe smallpox epidemic. By 1901, the population of the Danish colony was 11,893, including approximately 300 Europeans.

Population trends vary by region. The west coast of Danish Greenland saw a slight increase during the latter half of the 19th century, partly due to native immigration from the east coast. Conversely, the east coast population has generally decreased, with several hundred people living at Angmagssalik. In the north part of the east coast, specifically the Franz Josef Fjord and Scoresby Fjord regions, numerous ruins of settlements exist; although Clavering encountered Eskimo there in 1823, they have since wandered south or died out. Additionally, a small group known as the "Smith Sound Eskimo" or "Arctic Highlanders" lives near Cape York, numbering about 240.

## Spiritual Beliefs
The Eskimo hold a conception of the world where spirits control all affairs of life. These spirits rule over the sky, earth, sea, clouds, and winds, and are themselves subject to a greater spirit. This belief system extends to the geography of the landscape; every island, prominent rock, point, and cove of the seashore is believed to have its own guardian spirit. These spirits are characterized as being of a malignant type. Consequently, individuals wishing to visit the localities where these spirits reside must propitiate them with acceptable offerings.

## Tools and Hunting
The Eskimo utilize a weapon similar to the South American *bolas* for the purpose of killing birds. The *bolas* are generally described as balls of stone attached to ropes of braided hemp or twisted hide. 

Hunting is further supported by the use of the Eskimo dog. These dogs are wolf-like in appearance, possessing rough coats, moderately sharp muzzles, straight bushy tails, and small, upright ears. They are known to howl rather than bark. To maintain the strength and size of these dogs, the Eskimo are stated to cross them with wolves. Fed on meat, game, and fish, these dogs are described as enduring, cunning, and effective hunters. While they are highly valued by Arctic explorers and their owners, they are noted for being rapacious, making it impossible to train them to avoid attacking small domesticated animals such as goats or sheep.

## Government and Trade in Greenland
In Danish Greenland, trade is a monopoly of the Danish crown, a system established in 1774. This is administered via a government board in Copenhagen (*Kongelige Gronlandske Handel*) and through officials within the country. The west coast, up to nearly 74 deg. N., is split into northern and southern inspectorates with seats of government at Godhavn and Godthaab, respectively. 

Each inspectorate is divided into districts. Each district consists of a chief settlement, or *coloni*, and several Eskimo hunting stations and outlying posts. These posts are presided over by an *udligger*, who reports to the *coloni-bestyrer* (district superintendent). There are approximately sixty such trading settlements along the coast over a distance of 1000 m.

The primary article received from fishing and hunting stations is blubber, which is sent to the *coloni* to be boiled into oil before being shipped to Copenhagen between May and November. During the winter months, when navigation is stopped, the seal-killing season occurs. The government's economic principle is to provide natives with low prices for their produce and sell European necessities at prime cost. Other stores, such as bread, are sold at prices that barely cover freight and purchase. On average, merchandise is not charged more than 20% over the cost price in Denmark. Annual price lists for both Danish and Eskimo articles are printed and distributed by the government.
