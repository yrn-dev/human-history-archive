# Vascones

The Vascones were an ancient tribe whose historical presence and nomenclature are linked to the development of the Basque people and the Gascons. In the context of ancient territorial divisions, the boundaries of the Vascones, along with other tribes such as the Varduli, the Caristi, and the Autrigones, likely corresponded more closely to the ancient limits of the dioceses of Calahorra, Bayonne, and Pamplona than to modern civil provinces.

## Etymology and the Basques
The term *Vascones* serves as the historical derivation for the word "Basques." The Basques themselves refer to their own people as *Eskualdunak*, which translates to "those who possess the *Eskuara*," and they name their country *Eskual-Herria*. 

The Basque people inhabit several regions across Spain and France. In Spain, they are found in Navarre and the three Basque Provinces (also known as *Provincias Vascongadas*): Alava, Biscay (or Vizcaya), and Guipuzcoa. In France, they inhabit the arrondissement of Mauleon and the arrondissement of Bayonne. Among the Spanish provinces, Guipuzcoa is the only one that is entirely Basque, while Alava is the least so. 

As of 1900, the Basque Provinces had a population of 603,596 and an area of 2,739 square miles. The total number of "pure Basques" in Europe is estimated at approximately 600,000, with an additional 100,000 emigrants located in the Americas, primarily in the La Plata region of South America.

## The Gascons
The name *Gascons* is derived from *Vascones* when written as *Wascones*. Despite this linguistic link, the Gascons are described as a very different race from the Basques. 

The Gascons appear in historical accounts of military conflicts involving Charlemagne. While the French epic *Chanson de Roland* suggests the defeat of Roncevaux was suffered by Charlemagne's army at the hands of a Saracen army, historical analysis indicates that the Saracens are mythical and the true enemy was the Gascons. Other accounts specify that the victory was the result of a sudden onset by the Gascons, who were possibly assisted by a few Navarrese and the Beaure mountaineers.

## Language and Culture
The language of the Basques, *Eskuara* (also written as *uskara* or *euskara*), is an isolated tongue in Europe. While its exact meaning is not yet fully ascertained, it probably corresponds to the idea of "clearly speaking." Grammatically, it is a polysynthetic, incorporating, and agglutinative system. In the broader organization of linguistic families, it occupies an intermediate position between Ugrian (or Ugro-Altaic) languages and American languages, though it recalls Finnic and Magyar languages from a purely grammatical perspective.

## Related Historical Figures and Terms
The name "Gascon" also appears in other historical contexts:
* **Le Gascon:** A master binder in 16th- and 17th-century France who invented "dotted work." He achieved this by scoring his small gilding tools—arabesques and curves—so that impressions appeared as dotted lines rather than straight lines. His style was imitated by Dutch binders Magnus and Poncyn in 17th-century Amsterdam.
* **Gascon Rolls:** A series of publications continued by the French scholar Charles Bemont, covering years such as 1273–1290 and 1290–1307.
* **Cagots:** A people of the Pyrenees whose origins are debated. Some theories suggest they were descendants of the Visigoths who lived as Christians among "Gascon pagans," though other theories link them to the Saracens or Albigenses.
