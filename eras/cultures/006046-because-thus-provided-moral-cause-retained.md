# Moral Causation and Influence in Historical and Legal Contexts

## Moral Cause in Buddhist Theory
In the development of Buddhist theory, the concept of a moral cause is central to explaining an individual's current position in life. While conflicting views existed in India regarding the precise mode of action of *Karma* before Buddhism, these contradictions are reflected in Buddhist theory. Various sources within the tradition offer different interpretations of what constitutes the *Karma* (doing or action) that determines an individual's position in the next life: some cite an aspiration expressed on a deathbed, others a thought dwelt upon during life, isolated acts, or specific mental dispositions. Despite these conflicting propositions, they share the commonality of providing a moral cause—specifically the individual's own act—for their current state.

Because this provided a moral cause, it was retained in Buddhism. However, since the Buddha did not acknowledge a soul, the connection between successive lives was attributed to the influence that a desire felt in a previous life exercises upon the next. This conclusion regarding the power of cravings over future life was reached independently by both the Buddha and Plato.

## Moral Influence and the Belief in Immortality
The belief in an after-life is described as exercising a potent moral influence. R. W. Emerson asserted that the impulse to seek proof of immortality is the strongest proof of it, suggesting that this desire arises from the truest and worthiest aspects of the human self. According to this view, the desire for immortality is social, religious, moral, and reasonable.

The loss of this belief is said to cast a dark shadow over present life, leading to the rise of pessimism. In such a state, human happiness and grief are viewed as paltry or unworthy of assuaging, and the moral world is reduced to a point where right, wrong, good, and evil become ephemeral and infinitesimal matters. This results in a "moral paralysis" and the fading of affections. Ernest Renan further noted that the disappearance of the belief in an after-life would lead to a "terrific moral and spiritual decadence," arguing that no lever could raise an entire people once faith in the immortality of the soul is lost. In the Christian faith, certainty in this belief is provided by the Gospel and the Risen Lord.

## Moral Conduct and Fidelity in Japanese History
In the history of the Japanese people, the principles of fidelity and morality were often intertwined with political policy. Yoritomo publicly asserted the principle of fidelity, though it is noted that this may have been dictated by selfish policy rather than moral conviction. Examples of this include Yoritomo declaring Yasuhira a traitor for killing his guest, Yoshitsune (despite Yasuhira following Yoritomo's own orders), and executing a disloyal retainer who had taken Yasuhira's head. Conversely, Yoritomo pardoned Yuri Hachiro, an officer of Yasuhira, because Hachiro defended Yasuhira's reputation despite Yoritomo's anger.

Additionally, the ethics of the *bushi* (warriors) emphasized certain obligations. For instance, Minamoto Tametomo viewed filial piety as one of the first obligations of a *bushi*. At age seventeen, Tametomo returned to the capital to attempt to purchase his father's pardon through his own surrender after his father, Tameyoshi, was removed from office as punishment for Tametomo's own contumacy.

However, the *bushido* code had defects. It was characterized by an indifference to intellectual investigation and an excessive reverence for rank that prevented commoners—merchants, artisans, and farmers—from ascending to the samurai class. This led gifted commoners to seek the cloister, turning Buddhism into a stage for secular ambition. Furthermore, philosopher Dr. Inouye Tetsujiro charged that *bushi* ethics focused on private morality while ignoring public morality.

## Legal and Civic Applications of Morality
The term "moral" also appears in legal contexts regarding conduct and character. In the New York penal code, section 289 allows for the prosecution of persons neglecting children if they willfully permit a child under sixteen years of age to have their "morals to become depraved."

In administrative law regarding deportation, 8 U.S.C. § 1251(a)(2XA)(i) specifies that an alien may be deportable if they are convicted of a crime of "moral turpitude" within five years of entry (or ten years for those with lawful permanent resident status) and are sentenced to or confined in prison for one year or longer.

## Morality in Political and Personal Crisis
Historical accounts illustrate the intersection of morality and power. In Bristol, during the American war, Edmund Burke noted that the city's "headlong desires" became its "politics and our morals," leading to a spirit of domination and the silencing of those who retained sentiments of moderation.

In ancient Greece, the noble demeanor and high reputation of the Rhegine general Phyton provided a moral contrast to his suffering. Despite being scourged and insulted by Dionysius, Phyton maintained heroic courage and dignified silence. His refusal to betray his city and his noble behavior eventually excited compassion even among the soldiers of his captor, though this ultimately led Dionysius to order that Phyton and his kindred be drowned to avoid a mutiny.
