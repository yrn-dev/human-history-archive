# Wottawa and the Beraun

## Hydrographic Context of Bohemia
The territory of Bohemia is characterized by a hydrographic structure of striking regularity. While the region belongs to the watershed of the Elbe, the principal river of the country from every point of view is the Moldau (known in Czech as the *Vltava*). The Moldau serves as the main stem of the region's water system, whereas the Elbe and other rivers are considered lateral branches. This is evidenced by the fact that the basin of the Moldau, covering 11,890 square miles, is twice as large as that of the Elbe. Additionally, the Moldau is greater in both width and depth, and its length is 270 miles, which is 47 miles longer than the Upper-Elbe.

The Moldau also functions as the country's primary commercial artery because it is navigable below Budweis, a characteristic not shared by the Upper-Elbe. Despite its importance, the Moldau runs through a deep and narrow valley from above Budweis to approximately 15 miles south of Prague, an area lacking both roads and railways.

## The Wottawa and Beraun Rivers
The Moldau receives several tributaries. On its right side, it is joined by the Sazawa and the Luzniza. On its left side, it receives the Wottawa and the Beraun.

The Beraun is identified as the third most important river in the country. It is formed by the union of several rivers: the Mies, the Radbusa, the Angel, and the Uslawa.

## Regional Geography
Beyond the river systems, the geography of Bohemia includes very few lakes, the majority of which are situated at high altitudes. Regarding the broader watershed, the Elbe receives the Polzen and the Iser on its right, and the Adler on its left. Other tributaries of the Elbe include the Biela, the Moldau, and the Eger, the latter of which has an affluent called the Tepl. The confluence of the Elbe and the Moldau occurs at Melnik; below this point, the Elbe follows the general direction of the Moldau.
