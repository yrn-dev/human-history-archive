# Lake Ambadi

Lake Ambadi is a body of water located in North-East Africa, formed by the broadening of the Jur river. It is situated just above the confluence of the Jur and the Lol.

## Physical Characteristics and Flora
At low water, Lake Ambadi measures 10 miles in length and over a mile in breadth; however, the lake becomes significantly larger during flood time. The lake is noted for being the home of many "swimming" variety sudd plants. Notably, ambach and papyrus are absent from this specific lake.

## Fauna
The lake provides a habitat for the *Balaeniceps rex*. While this species is rare in other locations, it is found in large numbers within Lake Ambadi.

## Regional Geography and the Bahr-el-Ghazal
Lake Ambadi is part of the broader hydrography of the Bahr-el-Ghazal (Gazelle river), which serves as the chief western affluent of the Nile, joining it at 9deg 30' N., 30deg 25' E. The Bahr-el-Ghazal is a deep stream created by the junction of numerous rivers, with the Jur being the most important among them. 

The region surrounding these waters is characterized by diverse terrain. In the upper courses, southern affluents of the Ghazal flow across a plateau of ferruginous laterite with steep valley banks. North of 7deg 20' N., where rapids interrupt the currents, the valleys open into tortuous channels often choked by sandbanks. Around 9deg N., this alluvial region—which floods during the rainy season—transitions into a sea of swamps that form part of the larger swamp region of the Nile. In these swamps, tracing the courses of various rivers is nearly impossible, and the Bahr-el-Ghazal is described more as a drainage channel than a true river.

## The Bahr-el-Ghazal Basin and Tributaries
The basin of the Ghazal is extensive, reaching north-west to Darfur and south-west to the Congo watershed. Key tributaries include:

*   **The Bahr-el-Homr:** The main northern feeder, which passes through the country of the Homr Arabs. It is the only important affluent with tributaries originating north of the main stream. In 1906, its lower reaches were completely blocked by sudd, though the Sudan government later sent engineering parties to remove these blocks and restore the waterway.
*   **The Lol (or Bahr-el-Arab):** The most westerly affluent, which rises in Dar Fertit as the Boro or Telgona. It receives the Bongo, Chel, Sopo, and Raga rivers from the south and south-west. The Lol eventually unites with the Bahr-el-Homr.

From the point where the Lol and Jur meet—the point above which no river is called the Bahr-el-Ghazal—to the junction with the Nile at Lake No, the distance is approximately 200 miles.

## Hydrology and Navigation
The Bahr-el-Ghazal, like the Bahr-el-Jebel, is susceptible to being choked by sudd. During the rule of the Mahdists, the river became almost blocked by these accumulations; Gessi Pasha was imprisoned within the sudd for approximately six weeks. Beginning in 1901, British officers removed sudd blocks from the Jur, the Bahr-el-Ghazal, and other rivers. These efforts established uninterrupted steamboat communication during the flood season between Wau and Khartum, a distance of some 930 miles.

The Ghazal river's flow varies in character. It initially flows north with shallow, lagoon-like expansions (nowhere deeper than 13 feet). As it turns north-east, the channel becomes deeper and narrower, featuring occasional reaches of papyrus. Finally, it turns east and broadens again before reaching Lake No. The banks in this section are typically marked by scrub and anthills; in one valley, the anthills are so densely packed that Sir William Garstin remarked they resemble a "gigantic graveyard." During flood time, the rise of the Ghazal is barely 3 feet, yet this is sufficient to place an enormous area of country under water.
