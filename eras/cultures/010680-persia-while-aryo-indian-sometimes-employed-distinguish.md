# Persia

## Ethnic and Linguistic Origins
The peoples of Persia belong to the Eastern branch of the Indo-Europeans. Scholars use the term *Indo-Aryan* to describe the portion of this Eastern branch that settled in India, while the term *Iranian* (of the same origin as *Iran*) refers to those who remained in Persia and Bactria. The word *arya-* is associated with dignity and was a term of pride for these peoples.

The specific stages of how the Aryan people occupied the Iranian table-land are unknown, as the people possess no tradition of a time before they held these territories. However, there is a close linguistic relationship between the Iranians and the people of the Indus valley. At the period of their earliest literature (roughly 1000 B.C.), the vocabulary remained largely the same; whole sentences can be transliterated between the languages using regular phonetic changes without changing a single word.

## Trade and Communication
Persia has historically been linked to the west and India through various land and water routes. Bagdad serves as a natural line of communication between Persia and the west, acting as a point where the ancient caravan route from Khorasan debouches from the mountains. Bagdad's early importance was derived from its position at the center of communication lines between Persia, India, and the west.

In more recent times, Persian trade with the west has shifted to pass either through the ports of the Persian Gulf or northward via Trebizond. Additionally, Bagdad remains a significant location for Shi'ite pilgrims traveling from Persia to the sacred cities.

Regarding maritime trade, the *Yüan shi* (Yüan History) mentions seaports of the Indian Ocean trading with China. While Hormuz is not explicitly named in that text, a biography of Arsz-lan records that his grandson Hurdutai accompanied *Bu-lo no-yen* on a mission to a country called *Ha-rh-ma-sz*, which may be a reference to Hormuz.

## Material and Cultural Exchange
There has been a long history of material exchange between India and Persia. Indian steel and sword-blades were highly esteemed across the East and continued to be imported into Persia until recent days. This fame dates back to ancient times; Ctesias recorded obtaining two wonderful swords of this material from the king of Persia and the king's mother.

In terms of agricultural products, a spirit distilled from dates is produced in Persia, as well as in Sind, Mekran, and parts of western India. This substance was mentioned by Dioscorides and Strabo and was used as a medicinal stomachic. The wealthy would enhance this spirit with aromatic spices, ambergris, and *Radix Chinae*, while the poor used Persian absinth and liquorice.

Culturally, the north-west frontiers of India featured Perso-Greek states. Much of the Indian art that later spread through Buddhist influence was Greek in origin, derived from these states.

## Geographical Survey and Mapping
Extensive scientific geographical mapping has been conducted along the frontiers of India, including Persia. These efforts have produced maps extending from the Oxus to the Indus and from the Indus to the Euphrates. In Persia, Indian surveyors were assisted by geographers such as Major Percy M. Sykes, Captain H.B. Vaughan, and General Sir A. Houtum Schindler.

Minor extensions of the Indian triangulation have reached the eastern districts of Persia and the coast of Makran. These surveys have contributed to a high standard of accuracy in Asiatic geographical surveys, effectively freeing western Asia (excluding Arabia) from geographical perplexity by the close of the 19th century. Additionally, measurements have been taken between Teheran and Potsdam, covering 3000 m.
