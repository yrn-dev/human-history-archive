## Historical Congregations of the Hebrews
Archive records describe various historical gatherings and congregations associated with the Hebrews. In one account, a king dissolved a congregation after completing oblations for himself and for the Hebrews. This event involved the sacrifice of 120,000 sheep and 22,000 oxen. Following these sacrifices, the temple received the victims first, and all the Hebrews, including their children and wives, feasted there. The king also observed the Feast of Tabernacles magnificently before the temple for twice seven days, feasting with all the people.

In other historical contexts, congregations of the Hebrews are associated with periods of unrest. One account describes a congregation that formed with the intention of stoning Aaron, the high priest, and Moses. This occurred after the people, their wives, and children had lamented and blamed Moses, believing it was impossible to gain possession of the country and claiming that God had promised them fair but would not assist them.

## Hebrew Christians and the Epistle to the Hebrews
The Epistle to the Hebrews is described as an epistolary treatise written on the Pauline model. While its date is uncertain, it is attributed to a writer strongly influenced by St Paul and the Jewish school of Alexandria represented by Philo. Regarding the intended audience, one plausible theory suggests the epistle was addressed to a single congregation of Hebrew Christians located in Rome. Another theory suggests it was addressed to a group of local churches or a local church in Palestine, possibly centered at Caesarea. It is noted as improbable that the mother church at Jerusalem was the intended recipient.

### Benedictine Congregations
The Benedictine order developed a congregational system as a response to public demands for Church reform during the councils of Basel and Constance. 
*   **European Congregations:** In German lands, the Bursfeld Union (1446) became a typical congregation, eventually embracing more than 100 monasteries. In Italy, the congregation of S. Justina at Padua (1421), later known as the Cassinese, established a highly centralized government that destroyed monastery autonomy. This system spread to France, where the congregation of St Maur was formed in the early 17th century, as well as to Spain, Portugal, and Brazil.
*   **Modern Revivals:** Following the suppression of many monasteries during the French Revolution and the rule of Joseph II of Austria, revivals occurred in the 19th century. These included the German congregation of Beuron (1863) and the French congregation inaugurated by Dom Gueranger in 1833. In England, the congregation consists of a cathedral priory (Hereford), a nunnery (Stanbrook Abbey, Worcester), and three large abbeys: Ampleforth, Downside, and Woolhampton. Two vigorous congregations also arose in the United States.
*   **Governance:** Each Benedictine congregation is independent and self-governing, with a president who has limited powers rather than a general superior.

### Other Religious Congregations
*   **Windesheim Congregation:** A Dutch canon regular congregation to which Thomas à Kempis and Hermann Rhyd belonged.
*   **Congregational Churches:** In England, Samuel Eaton taught the first congregational church in the north of England at a chapel in Dukinfield Hall. Later, Alexander Mackennal served as the pastor of the Congregational Church at Bowdon, Cheshire, and acted as chairman of the Congregational Union in 1886.
*   **Dissenting Congregations:** George Benson, an English dissenting minister, served as a pastor for congregations of dissenters at Abingdon in Berkshire and later in Southwark. In 1740, he was appointed by the congregation of Crutched Friars.
