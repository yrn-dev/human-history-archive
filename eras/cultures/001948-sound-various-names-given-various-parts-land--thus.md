## Phonetics and Linguistics
In the study of language, the letter M represents a bilabial nasal sound that is generally voiced. This sound is typically stable, though in certain languages—including Celtic, Germanic, and Greek—it changes into its dental correlative, -n, when it appears as a final sound. In Greek and Latin, M occurs more frequently as an initial sound than in other languages of the same stock; this is due to the disappearance of s before m (as well as before n and l) at the beginning of words.

The sound m is closely related to the sound b. The primary distinction is that during the pronunciation of m, the nasal passage remains open, which allows the sound to be prolonged. In contrast, b is an explosive or instantaneous sound. In some languages, b is inserted between m and a following consonant, such as in the English word "number" or the Greek *mesembria* ("mid-day"). Additionally, in unaccented syllables, the sound m can form a syllable on its own without an audible vowel, as seen in the Anglo-Saxon *fathm*, the origin of the English word "fathom."

## Musical Instruments and Acoustics
The construction of stringed instruments historically relied on the "sound-chest," a feature that remained persistent from the middle ages to the present day. Two primary archetypes of sound-chest construction are identified:

*   **The Lyre:** This instrument featured a sound-chest with a vaulted back designed to imitate a tortoise, with a flat sound-board made of parchment or wood glued directly over it.
*   **The Cithara:** This instrument utilized a shallower sound-chest where the front and back were connected by ribs or sides.

These two methods of constructing sound-chests are typical for every stringed instrument with a neck traceable through early printed books, miniatures, monuments, and other art from the middle ages.

## Architectural and Material Applications

### Building Materials
In Great Britain, various terms are used to categorize the quality of bricks. "Stocks" are described as sound, well-burnt, and hard bricks used for ordinary purposes. "Hard Stocks" are also sound, though they are over-burnt and typically used in positions where appearance is not a priority, such as wall footings. "Shippers" are sound, hard-burnt bricks that possess an imperfect shape and are often used as ship ballast. Additionally, "Grizzles" are sound and of fair shape, but are under-burnt and thus used for inferior work that is not heavily loaded.

### Acoustic Insulation
In flooring, a principal objection to single flooring is that sound passes through from one floor to another, potentially allowing conversation in one room to be understood in another. To prevent this, a remedy known as "pugging" is used. This process involves:
1. Nailing fillets to the sides of joists approximately half-way up their depth.
2. Placing rough boarding on these fillets.
3. Filling the space on top of the boarding with slag wool, usually 3 inches thick.

Further prevention of sound transmission is achieved by using tongued flooring and applying a thick floating coat to the ceiling.
