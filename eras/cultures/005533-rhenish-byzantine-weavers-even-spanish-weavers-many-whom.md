# Weavers and Textile Production

The history of weaving encompasses a wide range of technical specializations, from the production of luxury brocades in Europe and the Middle East to the organized craft guilds of Belgium and the imperial corporations of ancient Japan.

## Brocades and Luxury Textiles
The term "brocade" is derived from the Latin *broccus*, which is related to the French *brocarts* and *brocher*, the Italian *brocato*, and the Spanish *brocar*. The term implies a form of broaching or stitching. In textile production, this refers to fabrics woven to create an appearance of stitching.

Historical distinctions exist between different types of luxury fabrics. A Spanish document from 1375 distinguishes between *brocats d'or é d'argent* and *los draps d'or é d'argent o de seda*. While some fabrics, such as Indian kincobs or Persian gold and coloured silk-weavings, are often called brocades, they may lack the floating threads that characterize true *broché* ornamentation.

Early decorative stuffs were *brochés* with gold threads by oriental weavers, particularly those in Syria, Persia, and regions of northern Africa and southern Europe under Saracen domination. Examples of this craft include:
* **Siculo-Saracenic works:** An 11th or 12th-century specimen features animals and birds where only the heads are broched with gold thread.
* **The Hotel des Tiraz:** Located in Palermo, this facility produced a sumptuous weaving of gold threads and coloured silks for an official robe for Henry IV (emperor of the Holy Roman Empire, 1165-1197), which is preserved in the cathedral of Regensburg.
* **Rhenish-Byzantine manufacture:** A piece from the 12th or 13th century consists of red silk and gold thread, featuring an ogival framing with a Persian (or Sassanian) leaf-shaped fruit device and pairs of parrots.

## Weaving in Ancient Japan
In ancient Japan, weavers were sometimes part of *tamibe*, corporations of naturalized aliens presented to the Japanese Throne by foreign potentates. These individuals were often distinguished by their literary attainments or technical skills and were dependent upon the Court to manufacture articles for the Imperial household.

Some of these naturalized persons were granted the privilege of *uji*. For example, weavers Kure-hatori and Ana-hatori, presented by an Emperor of the Wu dynasty in China, were descendants of the *Kinu-nui-uji* (silk-robe makers). Similarly, the *Hata-uji* (Weavers' uji) was organized to bring together weavers who had scattered across the country. According to records of the Emperor Ojin (A.D. 284), these weavers were settled in villages named Kachibe in honor of their ancestor, Kachi. During the first four centuries of the Christian era, numerous people from Korea and the Wu dynasty were presented to the Yamato Court, resulting in the formation of 710 *uji* based on their craft and art skills.

## European Guilds and Commercial History
In medieval Europe, weaving was a central pillar of urban economy. In the 14th century, Louvain (Belgium) was a prosperous center for the woollen trade in central Belgium. At the time of the *Joyeuse Entrée* in 1356, the town's gild of weavers consisted of 2,400 members. However, following a twenty-year struggle between the citizens (the White Hoods) and the patrician class, Louvain's prosperity declined. This civil strife led many weavers to flee to England and Holland.

Weaving also served as a foundation for commercial dynasties. Johann Fugger, the founder of the famous German banking and merchant family, was a weaver at Graben, near Augsburg. His son, also named Johann, settled in Augsburg around 1367 and combined the business of a weaver with that of a merchant. The family later expanded into the trade of silk, wool, and spices across Europe.

## Occupational Health
The profession of weaving has been linked to specific health risks. Research by Hirt indicates that the inhalation of vegetable dust can cause irritation and the growth of organisms in the bronchial tubes. Consequently, weavers and workers in cotton factories are noted as being particularly liable to develop chronic bronchitis, following millers and grain-shovellers.
