# Bibliographic References of the Late 19th Century

The archival records from the late 19th century contain various references to specialized reports, catalogues, and academic journals used to document scientific, anthropological, and historical research. These documents served as the primary authorities for studies ranging from primitive weaponry and regional geography to the history of musical instruments.

## Anthropological and Military Documentation
In the study of weaponry, specifically the boomerang, several key reports and catalogues are cited as authorities. The "war boomerang" is identified as a deadly weapon when used by an expert, while the "hunting boomerang" is described as lighter and also effective. In contrast, the "return boomerang" is primarily used for killing birds or as a plaything, though it is noted as being potentially as dangerous to the person throwing it as it is to the target. 

Research on these items refers to the *Catalogue of Bethnal Green Collection* (p. 28) and the *British Ass. Report* (1872). Further technical details are found in the *Journ. Royal United Service Inst.* xii. No. 51, as well as works by Pitt-Rivers (Lane Fox) in *Anthropological and Archaeological Fragments* under the section "Primitive Warfare."

## Regional and Geographical Reports
The archive contains extensive lists of authorities regarding the geography and history of British Columbia. These include the *Annual Reports of Minister of Mines* and other departmental reports from the Dominion and Provincial governments, as well as the *Catalogue of Provincial Museum* in Victoria. Other critical sources include:
* *Reports Geological Survey of Canada* (from 1871 onwards).
* *Reports of Canadian Pacific (Government) Surveys* (1872-1880).
* *Reports of Committee of Brit. Assn. Adv. Science on N.W. Tribes* (1884-1895).

Additionally, the *Report Forty-First Meeting British Association, Edinburgh* (August 1871) contains an address by Col. H. Yule.

## Musical Instrument Catalogues
The documentation of musical instruments relies heavily on descriptive catalogues from the late 19th century. Notable among these is the *Catalogue of the Special Exhibition of Ancient Musical Instruments at South Kensington Museum 1872* (London, 1873), specifically page 25, No. 332. Other referenced works include:
* Captain C. R. Day's *Descriptive Catalogue of the Musical Instruments exhibited at the Military Exhibition* (London, 1890), specifically No. 307 (p. 147) and No. 309 (p. 148).
* The *Catalogue des instruments de musique* from the Musee du Conservatoire National de Musique (Paris, 1884), p. 147.

## Bibliographical Science and Archiving
The archive notes the contributions of De Morgan, a bibliophile who treated bibliography as an important science. De Morgan's views on the art of constructing a catalogue corresponded with those implemented by Panizzi in the *British Museum Catalogue*. De Morgan's own work, the *Budget of Paradoxes*, was reprinted by his widow in 1872 with additional material. His professional evidence provided before the Royal Commission on the British Museum in 1850 (specifically Questions 5704*-5815, 6481-6513, and 8966-8967) is cited as essential for those seeking to understand the principles of bibliography.
