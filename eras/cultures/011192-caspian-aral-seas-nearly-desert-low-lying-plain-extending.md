# The Aralo-Caspian Region

The region situated between the northern border of the Persian highlands and the Caspian and Aral Seas is characterized as a low-lying, nearly desert plain. This expanse extends to the foot of the north-western extremity of the great Tibeto-Himalayan mountains and continues eastward through the valleys of the Jaxartes (Syr-Darya) and the Oxus (Amu Darya). To the north, the plain reaches across the country of the Kirghiz to the south-western border of Siberia.

## Geography and Demographics
The region includes Turkestan proper, Khiva, and Bokhara, areas where the Uzbeg Turks are the dominant group. Much of the territory is inhabited by nomadic tribes described as marauders and professional robbers, though those tribes to the north under Russian subjecthood are noted as being more peaceable and disciplined. 

Due to insufficient rainfall for agriculture, farming is only possible through irrigation. Consequently, the fixed population—which includes a non-Turkish element—is relatively small and concentrated within towns and districts located near rivers.

## The Caspian Sea
The Caspian Sea (known historically as *Mare Caspium* or *Mare Hyrcanium*, and in other languages as *Kaspiyskoe More*, *Darya-i-Khyzyr*, *Gurzem*, *Ak-denghiz*, *Sikim*, or *Jurjan*) is an inland sea positioned between Europe and Asia. It extends from 36° 40' to 47° 20' N. latitude and 46° 50' to 55° 10' E. longitude. Its dimensions are 760 miles from north to south and between 100 and 280 miles in breadth, covering an area of 169,330 square miles, including 865 square miles of islands.

The sea occupies the deepest part of the Aralo-Caspian depression. Its surface currently sits 86 feet below the level of the ocean (or 96.7 feet according to Aral-Caspian levelling) and 242.7 feet below the level of the Aral Sea.

## Geological History and Evolution
Evidence suggests the Caspian Sea was once significantly larger and higher. Erosion marks on the rocks of its southern basin's original shoreline exist at heights of 65 to 80 feet above the current level, indicating the sea was formerly at a level similar to the Black Sea. The absence of erosive indicators between that height and the current level suggests a rapid subsidence.

Research by Pallas and other scientists identified salt lakes, dry saline deposits, and marine shells far from current shores, suggesting the sea once covered vast regions. Aralo-Caspian deposits—primarily yellowish-grey clay or compact sandstone—are found between the lower Volga, the Dzungarian Ala-tau, the Aral-Irtysh water-divide, and the outliers of the Hindu-kush and Tian-shan systems. These deposits reach a maximum thickness of 90 feet east of the Caspian.

Typical fossils found in these deposits, such as *Cardium edule* and *Hydrobia stagnalis* in the Kara-kum desert, are species that currently live only in the shallow parts of the Caspian and Aral seas. The nature of the Kara-kum sands, which appear to be purely marine without fluviatile delta deposits from the Oxus, supports the hypothesis that the Caspian extended eastwards into Trans-Caspian territory during the post-Pleiocene age.

## Climate and Environmental Changes
The Aralo-Caspian region experiences extreme temperature variations. The maximum heat can rise to nearly 100° Fahr., while the cold can drop to -20° Fahr. or lower. On the Sea of Aral, the difference between the mean of the hottest and coldest months is 80° Fahr., while at Astrakhan on the Caspian, it is more than 50° Fahr.

Historically, the region has undergone significant transformations:
* **The Pluvial or Lacustrine Age:** During a very wet period as ice receded, a great sea reached the Volga valley and extended an arm westward to join the Black Sea. The Caspian, Aral, and parts of the Turkestan Desert are vestiges of this sea.
* **Forest Period:** Between 4,000 and 3,000 years ago, the climate was more genial, and forests existed in Western Turkestan and south Russia where deserts and steppes now prevail.
* **Recent Dry Period:** Between 1,500 and 2,000 years ago, the region was likely drier, and the Caspian and Aral seas were smaller than they are today.
