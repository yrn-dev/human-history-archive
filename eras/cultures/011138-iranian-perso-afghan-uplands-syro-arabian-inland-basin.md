# Iranian Uplands and the Syro-Arabian Inland Basin

The Iranian (or Perso-Afghan) uplands and the Syro-Arabian inland basin are components of Asia's interior or inland basins. Together with the Tibetan plateau, the Gobi depression, the lacustrine regions south of the Arctic watershed, and the basin of Asia Minor, these regions comprise an area of approximately 3,141,500 square miles, which is about half the extent of Asia's other two major hydrographic divisions.

## The Iranian and Perso-Afghan Uplands

The Iranian uplands encompass a vast space between the line of Mount Zagros to the west—which runs nearly parallel and eastward of the Tigris—and the Indus River to the east. This region was occupied by the Medes and Persians, who are members of the Indo-European family and portions of the Arian race.

### Geography and Hydrography
In the south-west Asian region of Baluchistan, the landscape is characterized by diverse drainage patterns. In the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, water gathers into channels that cut deep gorges through the hills before passing east into the plains of Sind. South of the Jalawan hydrographical area, streams either flow in straight lines to the Arabian Sea north of Karachi or curve westward to disappear into inland swamps.

To the north, the region includes the highlands of Afghanistan. The Kunar valley, known in classics as Khoaspes, forms part of a river system extending from the Hindu Kush to the Kabul river near Jalalabad. This system, which includes the Kashkar, Chitral, and Yarkhun, is more extensive than the Kabul basin itself. The Kunar river meanders through a fertile, cultivated valley flanked by the Bajour uplands to the east and the snow-capped mountains of Kafiristan (Kashmund) to the west, which reach altitudes of 14,000 feet.

In Afghan Turkestan, the great Balkh plain is characterized by a longitudinal ridge or anticlinal formation. This ridge effectively shuts off the northern affluents of the basin from joining the Oxus river.

### Regional Centers and History
Balkh, referred to as the "Mother of Cities," was once a great trade center of Asia with commercial routes extending to China and India. Mythically attributed to the Persian Romulus, Kaiomurs, the city was a central seat of the Zoroastrian religion. By the 7th century, records from Hsuan Tsang indicate the presence of approximately 100 Buddhist convents and 3,000 devotees. Later accounts from the 10th century describe the city as being built of clay with six gates and ramparts, while 12th-century records note its active trade and educational establishments.

The city suffered significant destruction in 1220 by Jenghiz Khan and again in the 14th century by Timur, though Marco Polo later described it as a "noble" and "great" city. Its political control shifted over centuries between the Durani monarchy, the khan of Bokhara, and various Afghan rulers, eventually remaining under Afghan rule after being captured by Mahommed Akram Khan, Barakzai, in 1850.

## The Syro-Arabian Inland Basin and Related Peoples

The Syro-Arabian inland basin is part of the interior drainage system of Asia. The populations associated with this family—including the Syrians, Arabians, Phenicians, and Assyrians—are classified as belonging to the Semitic or Aramæan family. This group also includes the Bani Israel (referred to as Jews in some sources) and, in part, the Abyssinians.

In Asia Minor, the river Halys served as an ethnographical boundary, with the Syro-Arabians located to the east of that river.

## Regional Demographics and Climate

In the broader Iranian and Central Asian sphere, such as the region of Bokhara, the population is diverse. The dominant race consists of the Uzbegs, who are fanatical Moslem Sunnites. The Sarts, who make up the majority of the town populations, are a mixture of Iranians and Turks. The countryside is largely composed of Iranian Tajiks, who are similar to the Sarts. Other groups present in the region include Persians, Afghans, Arabs, Armenians, and a colony of Hindus from Shikarpur. Approximately 20% of the population are nomads and 15% are semi-nomads.

The climate in these regions is extreme. In the lowlands, very hot summers are followed by short, cold winters where temperatures may drop to -20 degrees Fahrenheit and the Oxus river may freeze for two weeks. In the highlands, the hot, dry summer is followed by a four-month winter. In areas above 8,000 feet, the landscape is dominated by glaciers and snowfields, with rainy summers.
