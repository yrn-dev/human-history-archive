# Indigenous Plant Species of South-Eastern Asia

## Estimated Species and Distribution
In the region of south-eastern Asia—which encompasses the Malayan peninsula and islands, as well as India, spanning from approximately the 105th to the 65th meridian—the total number of indigenous plant species was estimated by Sir J.D. Hooker to be between 12,000 and 15,000. 

The flora of this region is characterized as a heterogeneous assemblage of alpine, temperate, and tropical plants. While there is significant variation between arid and humid regions, and between the plains and higher mountain ranges, tropical plants are dominant and define the character of the flora as a whole. The Indian flora is noted for providing a more general and complete illustration of almost all the world's chief natural families than any other country.

## Numerical Importance of Plant Orders
The principal orders of the region, arranged by their numerical importance, are:
* Leguminosae
* Rubiaceae
* Orchidaceae
* Compositae
* Gramineae
* Euphorbiaceae
* Acanthaceae
* Cyperaceae
* Labiatae

Within India, Orchidaceae are present in specially large proportions, and Euphorbiaceae and Scrophulariaceae are universally present. Conversely, Compositae are comparatively rare, and in certain locations, there are deficiencies in Labiatae, Leguminosae, ferns, Gramineae, and Cyperaceae.

## Regional Variations and Altitudinal Zones
The flora of eastern India's wetter and hotter regions is continuous with that of the Malayan islands and peninsula. This tropical flora extends along the lower ranges of the Himalaya, though it becomes less marked and is found at lower elevations as one moves westward toward areas of increased winter cold and diminished rainfall.

The higher ranges of the Himalaya exhibit greater uniformity and a closer general approach to European forms. At the highest elevations, many alpine plants are found that are believed to be identical to species from the north Arctic regions. Upon reaching the Tibetan plateau, the flora takes on features of the Siberian type due to increased dryness, including many Siberian genera and species. Some of these Siberian forms extend into the plains of upper India and the rainy mountain areas. Additionally, Tibet features assemblages of marine plants that grow near salt lakes at elevations of 14,000 to 15,000 feet above the sea.

## Botanical Connections with East Asia
The flora of Hong Kong indicates that nearly all species are either local, Chinese, or common to the Indian region, with the latter accounting for about three-fifths of the species. South China is botanically similar to the great Indian region, into which many Chinese forms penetrate. 

In contrast, the flora of north China is akin to that of Japan and shows a manifest relation to the American continent, with temperate forms extending as far as the Himalaya and almost to Kashmir. It is probable that a complete botanical connection exists between the temperate region of the Himalaya and the interior of northern China.
