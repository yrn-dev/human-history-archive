# The Suffix -aka or -anga

In the study of linguistic structures, the suffixes *-aka* and *-anga* appear in specific language families to modify the meaning of verbal roots or to categorize nouns.

## Bantu Languages
In Bantu languages, the suffixes *-aka* or *-anga* are used to provide a sense of continuance to a verbal root. For example, the root *tanda*, which means "to love," can be modified into *tandaka* to convey the meaning "to continue loving."

Beyond these specific suffixes, Bantu verbs are characterized by their ability to modify the sense of an original root through various suffixes. Using the root *tanda* (to love) as a basis, other variations include:
*   *tandeka* or *tandika*: to be lovable.
*   *tandila* or *tandela*: to love for, with, or by some other person.
*   *tandiza* (or *-eza*) and *tandisa* (or *-esa*): to cause to love.
*   *tandana*: to love reciprocally.

Additionally, within the classification of Bantu nouns, "Aka" (represented as *?Nka-ka-*) is identified as Class 13. This class is usually diminutive in sense, though it is sometimes used as an honorific.

## Indo-Aryan Languages and Prakrit
In the context of Indo-Aryan languages, specifically during the Prakrit stage, a pleonastic suffix *-ka-* was utilized. This suffix contributed to a "superfluity of forms" for nouns, allowing for the existence of short, long, and redundant forms.

In the Prakrit stage, the *k* of this suffix was already elided. This process is evident in the evolution of words from Sanskrit to Marathi. For instance, the Sanskrit *ghota-ka-s* became *ghod-a-u* in Prakrit and subsequently *ghora* (via contraction) or *ghor^a-wa* (via the insertion of a euphonic *w*) in Marathi. The redundant form, such as *ghorauwa*, resulted from the reduplication of the suffix, which was permitted in Prakrit.

These long and redundant forms are primarily used in conversation and are often familiar or contemptuous. In feminine nouns, these forms are frequently used to create diminutives. In some cases, they provide a definite force to the word, such as *ghor^awa* meaning "the horse."

Furthermore, the frequent use of pleonastic suffixes—specifically *-(a)ka-* for masculine and neuter nouns and *-(i)ka* for feminine nouns—is noted. These could be added to any Sanskrit noun regardless of the base termination. Modern forms created with this pleonastic suffix that end in *a*, *i*, or *e* are categorized as "strong forms."

## Other Linguistic and Geographic References
While not functioning as a verbal or nominal suffix in the same manner, the term "Aka" appears in geographic and ethnographic contexts. The Aka Hills are located to the west of the Daphla (or Dafla) Hills on the border of Assam and Eastern Bengal. The Akas are described as a more warlike group than their neighbors, the Daphlas, despite being less numerous.
