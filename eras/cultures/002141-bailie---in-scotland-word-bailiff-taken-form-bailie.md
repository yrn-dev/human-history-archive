# Bailie

The term "bailie" is a Scottish variation of the word "bailiff," derived from the Late Latin *bajulivus* (an adjectival form of *bajulus*, meaning a custodian or governor). In a general sense, a bailiff or bailie is a legal officer entrusted with a degree of jurisdiction, care, or authority.

## Municipal and Judicial Role in Scotland
In Scotland, a bailie serves as a magistrate or superior officer of a municipal corporation. Appointed from within the council, a bailie is invested with administrative and judicial powers within their appointed burgh. Their duties include sitting as magistrates in police courts, where they are typically assisted by a paid legal adviser known as an "assessor." In larger burghs, bailies also function as a licensing court.

While a bailie is often considered analogous to an English alderman due to their superior dignity within the council, there is a key distinction: a bailie continues to represent the ward for which they were elected after their selection. The term of office for a bailie is the same as that of an ordinary councillor, lasting no more than three years.

## Other Uses of the Term
In other contexts, the term appears as follows:
*   **Baili Beg:** According to Pandit Manphul, this was the title used for the native governor of Kashgar under the Chinese Amban (noting that in this instance, *Baili* stands for *beilêh*).
*   **Scientific Collaboration:** J. B. A. Bailie collaborated with the French physicist Marie Alfred Cornu around 1870 to repeat Cavendish's experiment to determine the mean density of the earth.

## Distinction from Similar Terms
The term "bailie" should not be confused with "bailey," which refers to the open space located between the outer and inner lines of a fortification. Some suggest "bailey" is a corruption of *Ballium*, while others derive it from the French *baille* (a corruption of *bataille*), referring to where soldiers drilled in battle array. Examples of baileys include the Upper and Nether Baileys at Colchester, as well as the Old Bailey in York and London.
