# The Aryan Group

The Aryan group is a significant category of Indo-European languages and peoples. Due to its familiarity and accessibility to European science, the Aryan group of languages is more thoroughly understood than any other linguistic group.

## Linguistic Characteristics
The Aryan group is easily distinguishable from other Indo-European languages by four well-marked characteristics:
1. The confusion of original long and short *e* and *o* sounds with the original long and short *a* sound.
2. The representation of the short schwa-sound by *i* (for example, *pita* meaning "father"), a feature found only in this group.
3. The transformation of original *s* into *s* when following *i*, *u*, and certain consonants.
4. The use of a suffix—*nam* for the genitive plural of stems ending in a vowel, which was borrowed by analogy from stems ending in *-n* (examples include the Sanskrit *asvanam*, Zend *aspanam*, and Old Persian *aspanam*).

Additionally, the Aryan, Semitic, and Hamitic language groups share a common feature not found in any other language: grammatical gender.

## Distinctions Between Sanskrit and Iranian
Within the Aryan group, clear distinctions exist between Sanskrit and Iranian languages:
* **Voiced Aspirates:** The Aryan voiced aspirates *gh, dh, bh* survive in Sanskrit but are confused with original *g, d, b* in Iranian.
* **Breathed Aspirates:** Except when combined with certain consonants, the Aryan breathed aspirates *kh, th, ph* become spirants in Iranian.
* **Initial Consonants:** Aryan *s* becomes *h* initially before vowels in Iranian, and occasionally medially. This resembles Greek (e.g., Sanskrit *sapta* compared to Zend *hapta* and Greek *hepta* for "seven").
* **Vowel Changes:** Zend exhibits many vowel changes not shared with Old Persian, some of which result from epenthesis or umlaut (e.g., *airya* compared to Sanskrit *arya*).

The languages are otherwise remarkably similar, with a striking difference appearing in the numeral "one": Sanskrit uses *eka-*, while Zend uses *aeva-* and Old Persian uses *aiva-*. The Iranian stem in this instance is the same as that seen in the Greek *oi(f)o-s* ("alone").

## Prehistoric Aryan Peoples
The original Aryan peoples were characterized as a cattle people rather than a horse people, and a forest people rather than a steppe people, which led them to be wasteful of wood. 

In prehistoric times, a series of ill-defined Aryan tribes ranged north of the Semitic and Mediterranean peoples of the sea coasts and peninsulas, and east and south of the Black Sea, between the Danube and the Persians and Medes. These tribes moved easily and intermixed freely. It is suggested that they broke up and assimilated the Hittite civilization, which was likely pre-Aryan in origin. These tribes were perhaps less advanced along the nomadic line than the Scythians of the great plains.

Around 1500 B.C., the Greeks appeared as one of these wandering, imperfectly nomadic Aryan peoples. They gradually extended their pasturage southward into the Balkan peninsula, where they mixed and conflicted with the preceding Ægean civilization, of which Cnossos was the crown.

## Classification and Related Groups
Ethnologists generally place the Aryans, along with the bulk of Hamitic and Semitic-speaking peoples, within the Caucasian group of races, characterizing them as "white." Among these, the Nordic and Semitic "races" are noted for having a more specialized and distinctive physiognomy than Hamitic-speaking peoples.

In the context of Indo-Aryan languages, the Prakrit stage is divided into two well-defined groups:
* **Inner:** This includes Sauraseni and its connected dialects.
* **Outer:** This includes Magadhi, Ardhamagadhi, and Maharastri with their connected dialects.

These groups differed in their vocabulary, systems of conjugation and declension, phonetic laws, and general character. For instance, Maharastri and Ardhamagadhi frequently use meaningless suffixes (such as *-alla*, *-illa*, and *-ulla*) that can be added to particles, adjectives, or nouns to create playful secondary forms, a feature hardly ever found in Sauraseni.

Modern Indo-Aryan languages include the Eastern Group, which comprises all Aryan languages spoken in India east of the longitude of Benares. This group includes four forms of speech: Bengali, Assamese, Oriya, and Bihari. In 1901, the number of Bihari speakers in British India was 34,579,844, out of a group total of 90,242,167.
