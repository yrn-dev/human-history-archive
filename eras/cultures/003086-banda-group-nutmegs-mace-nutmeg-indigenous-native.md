# Banda Islands

The Banda Islands are a group located in the Dutch East Indies within the Banda Sea, south of Ceram. They belong to the residency of Amboyna and encompass a total land area of approximately 16 square miles. The group consists of three chief islands and several smaller ones.

## Geography and Topography
The islands are of volcanic formation. Lontor (also known as Great Banda) is a sickle-shaped island that, along with Banda Neira and Gunong Api, forms part of a crater circle, a geological arrangement comparable to Santorin in the Aegean Sea. Gunong Api, meaning "Fire Mountain," is an active volcano reaching a height of 2,200 feet. Its earthquakes and eruptions have frequently caused destruction, most notably in 1852, when a huge sea wave caused significant damage.

Other islands in the group include Pisang, located north of Gunong Api, and Suwangi to the north-west. Further west are Wai (or Ai) and Run, which lies to the south-west of Wai. A good harbour exists in the space between Lontor, Neira, and Gunong Api, featuring entrances on either side that allow vessels to enter regardless of the monsoon. A third channel between Neira and Gunong Api is navigable only by small vessels.

## Settlements and Administration
Banda, the chief town, is situated on Banda Neira. It is described as a pleasant settlement and is commanded by two Dutch forts from the early 17th century: Nassau and Belgica. While Lontor is the largest island, it was found to be too unhealthy to serve as the site for the principal settlement. Generally, however, the climate of the islands is not unhealthy, though it is hot.

## Commerce and Agriculture
The primary articles of commerce for the Banda group are mace and nutmeg. Nutmeg is indigenous to the region and is grown under the shade of other trees, most commonly the canari. The annual production of the islands amounts to nearly 1,500,000 lb of nutmegs and 350,000 lb of mace. Botanically, mace is a laciniated scarlet covering, also called an arillode, that forms from above downwards around the seed of the nutmeg.

The history of the plantations, known as *perken*, is marked by significant demographic shifts. After the Dutch cleared off the native population, the plantations were worked by convicts and slaves until emancipation in 1860, after which Chinese and Malay labourers were introduced. Originally, the conquerors of the natives held these plantations while the government maintained a monopoly on the produce at a fixed rate; this monopoly was abolished in 1873. Additionally, jatti (or jalti) wood is cultivated on the small island of Rosingen.

## Colonial History
The Portuguese Antonio D'Abreu discovered and annexed the Banda Islands in 1512, but the Dutch expelled the Portuguese in the early 17th century. The British established a factory on Wai in 1608, which the Dutch demolished. Later, the natives resigned Lontor and Banda Neira to the British, and Run and Wai were added to British dominions in 1620. Despite treaties, the Dutch attacked and expelled the British.

In 1654, Cromwell compelled the Dutch to make satisfaction for the massacre of Amboyna and restore Run. However, due to a lack of adequate support from home, the English settlers lost the island to the Dutch again in 1664. The Dutch held undisturbed possession until 1796, when the British took the islands. They were restored to the Dutch by the treaty of Amiens in 1800, captured once more, and finally restored via the treaty of Paris in 1814.

## Regional Context
Nutmeg is a characteristic tropical form of the Malayan Islands, where the uniformity of humidity and temperature allows it to predominate. Beyond the Banda group, nutmeg is cultivated in other parts of the Dutch East Indies, including the residency of Krawang on the island of Java and the district of Buitenzorg (known to natives as Bogor) in the residency of Batavia.
