# Bushmen

The Bushmen, also known as Bosjesmans, are a people of South Africa. The name is derived from British and Dutch colonists of the Cape. They may refer to themselves as *Saan* (a Hottentot term) or *Khuai*, the latter of which is the title of one group and likely means "small man." Other names for them include *Abatwa* (used by the Kaffirs) and *Masarwa* or *Maseroa* (used by the Bechuana).

## Distribution and History
The Bushmen are considered the aboriginal element of the population of South Africa. While they are currently found sporadically—extending from the inner mountains of Cape Colony through the central Kalahari desert to Lake Ngami, and north-westward to the Ovambo river north of Damaraland—evidence suggests their former range was much larger. Perforated stones used for digging-sticks have been found as far north as the Nyasa and Tanganyika basins, and a belated Hottentot-Bushman group was noted between Kilimanjaro and Victoria Nyanza.

The Bushmen were likely driven south and confined to less fertile tracts by Bantu tribes. Later, European and Kaffir encroachments pushed them into the most barren regions of South Africa, though some remain in the inaccessible parts of the Drakensberg range. In the early years of European occupation, a planned destruction of the Bushmen on the borders of Cape Colony significantly reduced their numbers. In retaliation, the Bushmen conducted raids on colonial cattle.

## Physical Characteristics
The most striking physical feature of the Bushmen is their shortness of stature. Measurements by Gustav Fritsch (1863-1866) showed an average height of 4 ft. 9 in. for men, while Sir John Barrow recorded the tallest man at 4 ft. 9 in. and the tallest woman at 4 ft. 4 in. However, measurements in Bechuanaland during the late 19th century showed heights closer to average, with 5 ft. 4 in. being common and some individuals reaching 6 ft.

Physically, they are described as having a dirty yellow color, a long and low skull, large prominent cheek-bones, and deeply set eyes. Their noses are small and depressed, and their jaws project. Their teeth are regular with a mother-of-pearl appearance. The build is generally slim and lean, with very little subcutaneous fat, resulting in skin that is dry and folds around the joints and stomach. Unlike other negro types, the characteristic fetor of the skin is absent. Women often exhibit considerable steatopygy and a prolongation of the nymphae.

## Social Structure and Culture
The Bushmen are a nomadic race living in widely scattered groups with virtually no tribal organization. While individual families may temporarily appoint a chief, these arrangements are not permanent. 

Family life is characterized by a low social position for women, who serve as beasts of burden, carrying children and property and supplying the encampment with water. Marriage is ratified by a feast following an offer and acceptance; in some tribes, the youth must prove himself an expert hunter. The avoidance of parents-in-law is practiced. Their moral code identifies robbery, rape, adultery, and murder as offenses.

The Bushmen are described as intelligent, quick-witted, and musical, using a rough instrument made from a gourd and strings. They are fond of dancing, particularly during certain stages of the moon. They possess a strong talent for graphic illustration, creating drawings of animals, women, men, and children on cave walls and mountain rocks using four colors (white, black, and red and yellow ochre), or by engraving and chiseling into stone.

## Subsistence and Technology
The Bushmen have no domestic animals (except a few half-wild dogs) and no knowledge of agriculture. They live by hunting, following antelope migrations. Their primary weapon is a bow made of a bent bough strung with sinew, firing reed arrows 2 to 3 ft. long. These arrows are coated with a gummy poison derived from the milky juice of *Euphorbia arborescens* or *Amaryllis toxicaria*, often mixed with snake venom, the venom of a *Mygale* spider, or the entrails of the N'gwa caterpillar.

They also use a 20-inch knobbed club. Women use a *graaf stock* (digging stick)—a sharpened wooden spike with a perforated circular stone secured by a wedge—to dig pitfalls and uproot succulent tuberous roots.

## Beliefs and Customs
The Bushmen do not have a concrete idea of a God but believe in supernatural interference and evil spirits. They use amulets and practice forms of totemism, refusing to eat certain foods, such as goat's flesh or antelopes. 

Death rituals involve anointing the corpse's head, smoke-drying the body, and laying it in a grave covered with earth or stones. The family then deserts the site after raising a pile of stones. Cairns of stone are placed on the graves of chiefs; it is believed that evil spirits hide beneath these mounds and may twist the neck of anyone who fails to throw a stone upon them. As a sign of repentance or mourning, the Bushman may cut off the joints of his fingers.

## Language
The Bushman language exists in several dialects and is characterized by "clicks." While it shares these sounds with Hottentot, the Bushman language is more monosyllabic and does not recognize gender in names, whereas Hottentot is more agglutinative and uses suffixes for plurals.
