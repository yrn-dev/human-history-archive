# Marriage Customs and Rituals

The rituals surrounding the union of a bride and bridegroom vary significantly across different cultures and historical periods, ranging from symbolic feasts and gift exchanges to forced abductions and strategic political alliances.

## Terminology and Symbolic Traditions
The term "bride" is a common Teutonic word (appearing as *bruths* in Gothic, *bryd* in Old English, and *Braut* in Modern German) used for a woman on her wedding day and throughout her first year of wifehood. The "bridegroom" refers to the newly married man. Historically, the "bridal" (derived from *bride-ale*) referred specifically to the wedding feast.

Many traditions involve symbolic foods and objects. The "bride-cake" originated from the Roman *confarreatio*, where the couple ate a cake made of flour, water, and salt, and the bride held three wheat-ears to symbolize plenty. While the cake-eating ceased under Tiberius, the wheat ears remained. In the middle ages, girls would throw wheat grains over the bride outside the church porch. Over time, these grains became thin dry biscuits, which in Scotland were replaced by oatmeal cakes broken over the bride's head. During the reign of Elizabeth, these became small rectangular cakes containing sugar, milk, eggs, spices, and currants. These were thrown at the bride as she crossed the threshold; those landing on her shoulders or head were highly prized. By the time of Charles II, these evolved into a single large cake decorated with ornaments and almond paste. In some rural parishes, such as north Notts, wheat is still thrown over the couple with the cry "Bread for life and pudding for ever" to wish for affluence.

## Customs of Abduction and Seclusion
In certain cultures, the marriage process began with the forcible taking of the woman. Among the Khevsurs, the chief ceremony is a forcible abduction of the girl, with marriages typically arranged by parents while the bride and bridegroom are still in "long clothes." 

In another custom described by Plutarch, the husband would carry off a grown woman who was ripe for marriage by force. Following the abduction, the bridesmaid performed specific duties: she received the bride, cut her hair close to the head, dressed her in shoes and a man's cloak, and placed her alone in a dark chamber on a couch. The bridegroom would enter the room after dining with his mess—remaining sober and avoiding revelry—to loose her virgin zone. After a short time, he would return to spend the night with other young men. For a long period, the couple would visit each other by stealth, fearing discovery by the household. Some couples had children before they ever saw one another by daylight. This practice was intended to exercise self-restraint and ensure that passion and love remained unextinguished by avoiding unchecked intercourse.

## Social and Political Marriage Practices
In early historic Japan, the pastime of *uta-gaki* or *uta-kai* involved youths and maidens singing and dancing in fields, hills, and cities during autumn or springtime. During these gatherings, promises of marriage were exchanged; the man provided gifts as tokens, and the woman, with the approval of her father or elder brother, sent her head-ornament (*oshiki no tamakatsura*) to her lover. On the wedding day, the bride presented the bridegroom with "table-articles" (*tsukue-shiro*) consisting of drink and food.

In other contexts, marriages served as political instruments. In the case of Hideyori and the granddaughter of Ieyasu—who were married at ages eleven and seven, respectively—such unions between children were often motivated by the need for hostages, the cementing of family alliances, the ability to spy on another family, or the intention to sow enmity between houses.

## Legal and Moral Perspectives on Unions
Different societies have imposed various restrictions on marriage to ensure the quality of the union. Some practices sought to suppress dowries, requiring the bride to bring only a few low-value furniture articles and three dresses. This was intended to prevent marriage from becoming a money bargain or a means of gain, promoting instead love, happiness, and the procreation of children.

Conversely, some views emphasized the danger of "disproportioned matches." It was argued that unequal, loveless alliances—such as an old man marrying a young girl or a young man marrying a rich old woman—were not true marriages and should not be permitted by lawgivers or magistrates.
