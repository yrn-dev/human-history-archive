# Borax

Borax, chemically identified as sodium pyroborate or sodium biborate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7$), is a substance that appears in commerce in two primary forms: "common" or prismatic borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and "jewellers'" or octahedral borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O}$). While alchemists historically used the term "borax" in a vague manner, modern definitions specifically identify the substance by its chemical composition.

## Mineralogy and Natural Deposits
Prismatic borax is widely distributed as a natural product. It is found in Tibet, as well as in Transylvania, Canada, and Peru. In California, a large mass of crystallized borax occupies the bed of Borax Lake near Clear Lake; this material is of sufficient purity to be used by the assayer without preliminary purification. Additionally, crystals weighing up to a pound and reaching 7 inches in length have been found in large numbers at Borax Lake in both San Bernardino and Lake counties, California.

In South America, the Atacama Desert—an arid, saline region covering the Argentine territory of Los Andes, the south-western corner of the Bolivian department of Potosi, and the Chilean provinces of Antofagasta and Atacama—is a significant source of borax, among other minerals such as copper, silver, and nitrates. Other major commercial sources of boron compounds include the Atacama Desert and the Mohave desert on the borders of Nevada and California, where extensive borate deposits (primarily calcium borates) are located.

The boron found in salt lake solutions is likely supplied by solfataras and hot springs of volcanic origin, similar to those that charge the lagoons of Tuscany with boric acid. These deposits are typically mixtures of borates, gypsum, and various alkaline salts, including chloride, sulphate, and sodium carbonate.

## Tibetan Deposits
Tibetan mineral deposits have been known since early times. Crude material from these regions was formerly exported to Europe under the name *tincal* to produce other boron salts and pure borax. These deposits vary in purity:
* **Sohaga**: An impure borax found in the lake-plain of Pugha on the Rulangchu (a tributary of the Indus) at an elevation of 15,000 ft. It occurs over an area of approximately 2 sq. m. and is covered by saline efflorescence; rain and snow followed by evaporation produce successive crops.
* **Chu tsale**: Also known as water borax, this purer material is found at the lakes of Rudok, east of the Pugha district, and further east at the great lakes Tengri Nor, north of Lhasa.

## Physical and Optical Properties
Borax crystallizes with ten molecules of water ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 + 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and belongs to the monoclinic system. Its habit and angles closely resemble pyroxene. The mineral ranges in color from white to opaque, and sometimes appears bluish, greenish, or greyish. It has a specific gravity of 1.69–1.72 and a hardness of 2–2 1/2.

The substance exhibits striking crossed dispersion of the optic axes. When viewed through a section cut parallel to the plane of symmetry in convergent polarized light, the optic figure is symmetrical only regarding the central point. The angle between the optic axes is 3 degrees greater for red light than for blue light, and the plane of the optic axes for red light is inclined at 2 degrees to that for blue light.

## Chemical Preparation and Forms
Borax can be prepared through several methods:
* **From Boric Acid**: The primary supply is derived from the boric acid of Tuscany. This is fused in a reverberatory furnace with half its weight of sodium carbonate, and the resulting mass is extracted with warm water after cooling. Alternatively, sodium carbonate is dissolved in steam-heated, lead-lined pans, and boric acid is added gradually until the solution concentrates and borax crystallizes.
* **From Calcium Borate**: Naturally occurring calcium borate is mixed in a finely divided state with soda ash, fused, extracted with water, and concentrated.
* **Hydrate Deposition**: Pentahydrate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is deposited from a supersaturated aqueous solution during evaporation at high temperatures. It can also be prepared by dissolving borax in water to a specific gravity of 1.246 and cooling the solution; the pentahydrate deposits between 56°C and 79°C. Below 56°C, the decahydrate (ordinary borax) is deposited.

## Industrial Applications
Ordinary borax crystals swell significantly when heated, melting into a clear white glass as they lose water of crystallization. Octahedral borax is preferred for fluxing or soldering because it fuses more easily and is less likely to split when heated. 

In soldering, borax is used to remove the superficial film of oxide from metal, as solder only adheres to untarnished surfaces. Fused borax also dissolves many metallic oxides to form complex borates, which often exhibit characteristic colors. Other applications include the glazing of linen, glass-making, and the glazing of pottery.
