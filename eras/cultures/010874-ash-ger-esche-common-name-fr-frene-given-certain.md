# ASH

The term "ash" (German: *Esche*; French: *frene*) is a common name applied to several types of trees. While the term is used broadly, the species vary significantly in their botanical classifications and geographical distributions.

## The Common Ash
The common ash (*Fraxinus excelsior*) is a member of the natural order Oleaceae, which is the olive family. This order also encompasses shrubs and trees such as jasmine, privet, and lilac. The common ash is native to the majority of Europe, Great Britain, and extends into Asia.

The tree is noted for its contour, height, and graceful foliage, typically reaching between 50 and 80 feet. Its biological characteristics include:
*   **Flowers:** Reddish flowers grow in clusters and appear in March and April before the leaves develop. These flowers are generally imperfect, lacking either a pistil or stamens, and are "naked," meaning they lack petals or sepals.
*   **Leaves:** The leaves are pinnately compound and appear late. They feature a slender stalk with four to seven pairs of toothed leaflets that taper gracefully.
*   **Fruit:** The tree produces dry winged fruits known as "keys," which often remain in bunches after the autumn leaf fall.
*   **Winter Appearance:** Although the leaves fall early, the tree remains conspicuous during winter and early spring due to its black buds and greyish twigs.

## Varieties and Related Species of Fraxinus
There are several varieties and species within the *Fraxinus* genus:
*   **Varieties of the Common Ash:** The *var. heterophylla* possesses simple leaves and is found wild in England and Europe. The *pendula* variety is characterized by weeping, pendulous branches; this variety is sometimes grafted onto the tall stem of a common ash for aesthetic purposes and was reportedly first observed in Gamlingay, Cambridgeshire. Other varieties include *crispa*, which has curled leaves, and *verrucosa*, which features warty branches and stems.
*   **Other Species:** *Fraxinus ornus* is the manna ash, a handsome tree native to southern Europe with greenish-white flowers. Southern Europe also hosts the small-leaved ash (*Fraxinus parvifolia*). In the Himalayas, *Fraxinus floribunda* is a large tree featuring white flowers in terminal panicles.
*   **American Species:** North America is home to several species, including the black ash (*F. sambucifolia*), the red ash (*F. pubescens*), and the white ash (*F. americana*).

## Other Trees Called "Ash"
Several trees share the name "ash" despite belonging to entirely different botanical families:
*   **Mountain Ash:** Known as *Pyrus Aucuparia*, this tree belongs to the Rosaceae order (tribe *Pomeae*), making it a relative of pears and apples. In Scotland, it is called the rowan tree. It is recognized by succulent scarlet fruit and clusters of white blossoms. Its resemblance to the true ash—specifically its pinnately compound leaves, graceful ascending branches, and smooth grey bark—is the likely reason for its common name.
*   **Poison Ash:** This is the North American sumach or poison elder (*Rhus venenata*), which belongs to the Anacardiaceae (Cashew family).
*   **Bitter Ash:** Found in the West Indies, *Simaruba excelsa* belongs to the natural order Simarubaceae.
*   **Cape Ash:** *Ekebergia capensis* is a large tree native to the Cape of Good Hope and belongs to the natural order Meliaceae.
*   **Prickly Ash:** *Xanthoxylon Clava-Herculis* is a small tree native to the south-eastern United States. It is characterized by branches armed with sharp, stout brown prickles and a trunk studded with corky tubercles.

## Linguistic and Other Notes
In the book of Isaiah (xliv. 14), the Hebrew word *Oren* is translated as "ash." However, because ash trees are not native to Palestine, this term likely refers to the Aleppo pine (*Pinus halepensis*).

Separately, the word "ash" (or "ashes") is a homonym referring to the residue left after a body or object is burned. This is a common Teutonic word (German: *Asche*) related to the Latin root *ardere*, meaning to burn. Wood ashes are used in the process of lixiviation (or "leaching"), where they are treated with water to obtain a solution of alkaline salts known as lye.
