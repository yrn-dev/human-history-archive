# Basques

The Basques are a sturdy and independent-spirited people currently residing on the north and south slopes of the Pyrenees, numbering approximately 600,000 in Europe. They are characterized by a high degree of intelligence and enterprise. While their current population is concentrated in this region, ancient remains suggest that the Basque people and their speech were once distributed much more widely across Spain.

## Origins and Physical Type
The origin of the Basques is the subject of several primary theories. Some suggest they are descended from the tribes known to the Latins and Greeks as the Iberi. Another theory proposes they belong to fairer Berber tribes ("Eurafrican") and are linked to people depicted on Egyptian monuments via the ancient Libyans. An "Atlantic theory" posits they originate from a lost Atlantic continent, sharing a connection with a fair race on the western coast of Africa and the Guanches of the Canary Islands. A fourth theory suggests they are an indigenous race that has never extended beyond their current quarters.

Physical evidence provides limited clarity. Some skulls from the age of metal found in southeastern Spain resemble Basque skulls from Zaraus. Additionally, figures found at the Cerro de los Santos in Murcia recall the physical type of modern Basques, though these are associated with various other types. Megalithic remains—including stone circles, menhirs, cromlechs, and dolmens—are noted to resemble those of northern Africa more closely than the larger remains found in the British Isles or Brittany.

## Language
The Basque language is fully developed but follows lines absolutely different from the surrounding Aryan languages. For scholars, the structural character of the language was a profound perplexity, leading some to suggest a relationship with Amer-Indian tongues. A. H. Keane has proposed a remote link to the Hamitic languages via the Berber language of North Africa, though this is questioned by other philologists. These critics suggest the language is more akin to stranded vestiges of speech in the Caucasian Mountains, viewing Basque as a specialized, surviving member of a once widely extended group of pre-Hamitic languages that are otherwise extinct.

Basque speech has spread through emigration to the Americas; Basque newspapers have been published in the United States and Argentina. Notably, the earliest "French" settlers in Canada were Basque, and Basque names remain frequent among French Canadians.

## Pre-Christian Religion
Very little is certainly known regarding Basque religion prior to Christianity. Available notices indicate a belief in the immortality of the body if it remained unburied and unburnt. Their worship focused on the elements, specifically the morning star, the moon, and the sun. 

The Romans assimilated local Basque deities into their own pantheon, as evidenced by Latin inscriptions mentioning Herauscorrtsehe and Deo Baicorrixo (Baigorry). The Basques use the terms *Jaincoa* or *Jaungoikoa* to refer to the Deity, which may translate to "master" or "lord," or "Lord of the high." In the Roncal dialect, *Goikoa* means "the moon," which would render *Jaungoikokoa* as "Lord of the moon." The term *Jaun* (lord or master) is also used in a secular context, such as *Etcheko Jauna*, referring to the master of the house.

## Christian Practice and Church-State Relations
The Basques are described as a highly religious and conservative people. Their influence on European religion is marked by figures such as Francis Xavier of Navarre and Ignatius de Loyola of Guipuzcoa. They have preserved primitive religious forms forgotten elsewhere in the West, including the primitive order of deaconesses and the earliest form of Christian marriage. In Basque almanacs, the feast of Corpus Christi (instituted in 1262 by Pope Urban IV) is referred to as *Phesta-berria*, or the New Feast.

Despite their conservatism and liberality toward the clergy, the Basques have resisted being "priest-ridden" and have opposed the authority of Spanish bishops. For example, in 1477, Ferdinand the Catholic was forced to send back the bishop of Pamplona when he came to Biscay to swear to the *fueros*. In some locations, a deputy who spoke to a priest before a session lost his vote for that day, and no strange priest was permitted to enter the town while the junta was sitting. 

In Guipuzcoa, ecclesiastical patronage was held by the municipalities, the nobles, the king, or determined by election or competitive examination, rather than by bishops. The Basques also prevented priests from interfering with dances or games. In 1757, when Spanish bishops forbade drama throughout Spain, the cortes of Navarre compelled the king to withdraw the order.
