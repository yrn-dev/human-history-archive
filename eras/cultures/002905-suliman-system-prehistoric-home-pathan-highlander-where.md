# Suliman System

The Suliman system consists of a series of rough and picturesque highlands located in northern Baluchistan. These ridges are situated between Afghanistan and the plains of the Indus, serving as the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander.

## Geography and Climate
The northern Pathan district of the Suliman system has an average breadth of 150 miles, though it expands to more than 200 miles along the railway line and narrows to less than 100 miles on the line of the Gomal. The region is characterized by a clear and bracing climate that does not experience great extremes of temperature, and the vegetation is frequently alpine.

The drainage of the region follows two primary patterns: some water runs northwards to the Gomal through uplands west of the Suliman Range, while other water gathers in narrow lateral valleys behind the mountains before bursting eastwards through the limestone axis of the hills to reach the Indus via the shortest transverse route.

## Demographics and Tribal Divisions
The northern section of Baluchistan is generally occupied by Pathans. Among the most prominent tribal divisions in this area are the Mando Khel of Zhob and the Kakars and Dumars of Pishin. Some of the independent Pathan tribes inhabiting the Suliman range, along with those in the Salt Range, the Indus valley, and south of the Hindu Kush, are referred to as Bar Durani. These territories were first conceded to them by Ahmad Shah, the founder of Afghan national independence.

In contrast, the "curved wrinkles" and twisted flexures that mark the end of the Suliman system are occupied by true Baluchis rather than Pathans. Specifically, the Marri and Bugti sections of the Rind confederation—tribes of Arabic origin—occupy the most southern buttresses of the Suliman Mountains. To the north of these groups are the Bozdars, another Rind clan.

## Political Status and Administration
The northern section of Baluchistan acts as a buffer system of independent tribes positioned between Afghanistan and the British frontier. The eastern boundary of this region is defined by the "red line" at the foot of the frontier hills, which marks the border of British India.

While the Indian government generally interferes as little with the internal jurisdiction of the tribal chiefs in the Suliman Range as it does with northern chiefs, the British maintain a line of posts on the Zhob river. Because the Zhob river flanks the range almost from end to end on the west, this occupation gives the British command of the hills and control over communications with Afghanistan. This strategic presence has tended to maintain peace and order on the southern frontier to a greater degree than is found in the north.

## Military History and Security
The Pathan tribes of the Suliman hills are described as "ever-restless." To address this and stop border raiding on the Indus plains south of the Gomal, a series of politico-military expeditions were conducted in 1890 and 1891. These operations resulted in the occupation of the Zhob valley, the establishment of the central cantonment of Fort Sandeman, and the creation of a line of outposts extending from Quetta to Wana, north of the Gomal. These measures effectively reduced the independent Pathan tribes of the Suliman mountains to order.
