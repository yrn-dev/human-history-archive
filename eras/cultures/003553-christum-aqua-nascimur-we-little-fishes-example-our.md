# Christum in aqua nascimur

The phrase *Christum in aqua nascimur* relates to an early Christian conceptualization of baptism and the nature of Jesus Christ, specifically the idea that believers are "born in the water" following the example of Christ. This concept is closely linked to the symbolism of the fish and the theological school of adoptionism.

## The Symbolism of the Fish
The representation of Christ as a fish appears in the literature of the 2nd century and in the art found within the catacombs. This symbolism is rooted in the idea of spiritual rebirth through water. The early writer Tertullian utilized this imagery to preface his work on baptism, stating, "*Nos pisciculi secundum* [Greek: ICHTHUN] *nostrum Jesum Christum in aqua nascimur*," which translates to: "We little fishes, after the example of our Fish Jesus Christ, are born in the water." In another account, Tertullian expands on this, referring to Jesus as the "great fish" ([Greek: ichthyn]) and asserting that believers are not in a state of salvation except by abiding in the water.

Similarly, the Gaulish poet Orientius wrote around the year 440 that Christ is the "originator of baptism," describing him as a "fish born of the waters" (*Piscis natus aquis*).

## Rebirth and Adoptionism
The concept of being "born in the water" is tied to the interpretation of Luke iii. 22. Ancient texts, including the *Gospel of the Hebrews*, render the passage as: "Thou art my beloved Son, this day have I begotten Thee." This reading suggests that Jesus was re-born of the Spirit at this moment, becoming the Messiah and an adoptive Son of God. This perspective forms the basis of the adoptionist school of Christology.

Hilary further developed this idea, writing that Jesus "was born again through baptism" and subsequently became the Son of God. Hilary explains that while Jesus was the son of man, he was constituted the son of God through baptism, describing the event as the "begetting of a man who is being reborn" to become a "perfect son."

## Liturgical and Historical Influence
The belief in the spiritual rebirth of Jesus in the Jordan River influenced the Epiphany feast, which acquired the name *natalis dies* (birthday) to commemorate this event. This occurred before the *natalis in carne* (birthday in the flesh), as termed by Jerome and others, became associated with the feast.

Although this idea was condemned as Ebionite during the 3rd century, it continued to influence Christian writers. Evidence of this belief is found in the Epiphany hymns of the orthodox Greek church, as well as in the homilies and hymns of the Armenians.
