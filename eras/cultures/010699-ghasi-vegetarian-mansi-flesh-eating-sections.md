# Ghasi and Mansi Sections of the Arya Samaj

The Ghasi and Mansi represent two diverging sections of the Arya Samaj, a movement that sought to present a view of India's original faith that harmonized with European civilization's ethics and the discoveries of modern science. The movement appealed to educated natives whose faith in current Hinduism had been undermined but who remained predisposed against foreign religious influences. By 1900, the membership of the Arya Samaj had grown to over 92,000, having previously reached 40,000 by 1890.

## The Ghasi and Mansi Division
Despite its growth, the Arya Samaj experienced internal divisions, most notably a cleavage between the Ghasi and the Mansi sections. This split was centered on the consumption of animal products. The Ghasi section is characterized as vegetarian, adhering to the tenet of the Samaj that forbids the consumption of animal food and the destruction of animal life.

The Mansi section, described as flesh-eating, consists of those who declined to adhere to the prohibition against animal food. This section includes Rajputs who, while generally remaining in sympathy with the broader Arya Samaj movement, would not accept the ban on the destruction of animal life.

## Membership and Requirements
Admission to the Arya Samaj is permitted starting at the age of eighteen. Members of the organization are expected to contribute to its funds, with a requirement to provide at least 1% of their income.

### Satnamis and Kabirpanthis
In certain districts, other religious movements have emphasized abstinence from meat. The Satnamis, followers of Ghasi Das (who died in 1850), were established between 1820 and 1830 to seek freedom from the trammels of caste for the Chamar caste. Ghasi Das preached the equality of men and the unity of God, prohibiting idol adoration. Similarly, the Pankas are followers of the 15th-century reformer Kabir. Both the Satnamis and the Kabirpanthis abstain from liquor and meat.

### Historical and Global Perspectives on Flesh-Eating
The archive notes various historical attitudes toward meat:
*   **Ancient India:** Sanscrit epics describe a fair, beef-eating people who migrated from Persia into North India and eventually became vegetarians.
*   **Egypt and Libya:** Egyptians and certain Libyans abstained from the flesh of cows. The women of the Kyrenians also avoided cow's flesh due to the Egyptian Isis.
*   **Israelites:** The Israelites expressed deep regret for the loss of the "flesh-pots" of Egypt—including fish and succulent flesh—while wandering in the wilderness.
*   **Other Traditions:** The Orphic mysteries involved a fast from meat, broken only by an annual banquet of raw bovine flesh (originally perhaps human). Some early beliefs held that fish could be safely eaten because they were born of water without sexual connection, unlike animals *quae copulatione generantur*.

## Sacred Texts on Dietary Laws
The archive's sacred texts provide specific commands regarding what is permissible to eat:

### The Qur'an
The Qur'an instructs believers to eat of the good things provided by Allah and to be grateful. It specifically forbids the consumption of:
*   Dead meat (carrion) or that which has died of itself.
*   Blood poured forth.
*   The flesh of swine, which is described as an abomination, foul, or unclean.
*   Meat on which a name other than Allah's has been invoked (impious or immolated to others).

The text notes that if a person is forced by necessity, without willful disobedience, craving, or transgressing due limits, then the Lord is Oft-Forgiving and Merciful, and it is no sin.

### Other Scriptural References
In Leviticus xi, the sons of Israel are forbidden from eating animals that are not cloven-footed ruminants, specifically excluding the swine, hare, coney, and camel. They are also forbidden from eating most lizards, the mouse, the weasel, and water organisms lacking fins and scales.
