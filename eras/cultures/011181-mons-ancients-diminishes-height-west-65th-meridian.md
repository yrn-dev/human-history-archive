# Paropamisus Mons and the Asian High Region

## The Asian Plateau
A vast plateau exists in Asia west of British India, excluding the regions of Syria and Arabia. This high region covers an area comparable in size to that of Tibet, although it is situated at a much lower altitude. The plateau is nearly conterminous with several provinces and countries, including Iran (or Persia), Afghanistan, Armenia, Baluchistan, and the provinces of Asia Minor.

The boundaries of this plateau are defined by several geographical markers. Its southern border is formed by the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Sea, and the Tigris, extending westward to the north-east angle of the Levant. To the north, the high land follows nearly the 36th parallel North, reaching the Black Sea, the Sea of Marmora, and the southern shore of the Caspian.

## Mountain Ranges and Elevations
The eastern flank of this table-land is marked by a line of hills located a short distance from the Indus, between the Himalaya and the mouth of the river, approximately on the 72nd meridian. While most of these hills do not exceed elevations of 4,000 or 5,000 feet, some summits reach 10,000 feet or more.

The southern and south-western faces of the plateau follow the coast of the Persian Gulf from the mouth of the Indus. Further west, this face is formed by a mountain scarp that flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris, rising in many points to 10,000 feet. This scarp extends through Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian, where it turns along the north-eastern angle of the Mediterranean and the Taurus range.

## The Afghan Table-land and Paropamisus Mons
The north-eastern portion of the Afghan table-land forms a continuous mass of mountain with Tibet and the Himalaya between the 34th and 36th parallels North and the 71st and 72nd meridians. From the intersection of the 36th parallel and the 71st meridian, an unbroken mountain range extends in two directions: one side stretches north-east toward the crest of the northern slope of the Tibetan plateau, and the other extends nearly due west toward the Caspian.

The north-eastern section of this range is characterized by great altitude. It serves as a divide between the headwaters of the Indus and its tributary, the Kabul—which unite below Peshawar and flow south into the Arabian Sea—and the headwaters of the Oxus, which flow toward the Aral Sea. The western portion of this specific range was known to the ancients as Paropamisus Mons; this section diminishes in height west of the 65th meridian.

## Relation to Tibet
The great mass of mountain described above serves as a natural line of division across a large part of the continent. The summit of this mass is occupied by Tibet, known to its inhabitants as *Bodyul* or *Bod*. Tibet is a rugged table-land that is narrow relative to its length and is broken by mountain ranges that often rise into regions of perpetual snow. These ranges are separated by narrow, moderately inclined valleys that occasionally open into wide plains. These areas are often occupied by rivers or salt lakes with no outflow.

The Tibetan table-land terminates in the west at approximately 70 degrees east. The ranges covered in perpetual snow extend as far west as Samarkand, after which they rapidly diminish in height and end in low hills located north of Bokhara. To the east, Tibet terminates in a line of snowy mountains that flank China on the west, situated around 103 degrees east and between the 27th and 35th parallels of latitude.
