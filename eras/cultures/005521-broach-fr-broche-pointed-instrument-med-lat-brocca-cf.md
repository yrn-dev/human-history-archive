# BROACH

The term "broach" (derived from the French *broche*, meaning a pointed instrument, and the Medieval Latin *brocca*) refers to a variety of pointed tools and specific applications in architecture and textiles. It is related to the Latin adjective *brochus* or *broccus*, which means "projecting" and was used in reference to teeth.

## Pointed Instruments and Tools
In a general sense, a broach is a name for many forms of pointed instruments. Examples include a bodkin, a spit used for roasting meat, and a wooden needle employed in the making of tapestry. In a technical capacity, a broach is also referred to as a "rimer," a tool used in conjunction with a wrench to smooth or enlarge holes. 

The use of similar pointed instruments to tap casks has led to the verbal use of the term "to broach" or "tap" a cask. A related term, "brooch," serves as a doublet with a specialized meaning, denoting a fastener or clasp for dress that features a pin, a catch or loop at one end, and a spring or hinge at the other.

## Architectural Application
In English architecture, the term "broach-spire" describes a specific form of spire. This designation is derived from the stone roof of the spire's lower portion. Because the tower is square on plan while the stone spire is octagonal, four angles remain that require covering. These angles are covered by a stone roof with a slight pitch compared to the spire itself. The "broach" is specifically the intersection where this roof meets the octagonal faces of the spire.

## Textiles and Brocades
The Latin word *broccus* is linked to the French *brocher* and *brocarts*, as well as the Spanish *brocar* and Italian *brocato*. These terms imply a form of broaching or stitching. Consequently, textile fabrics that are woven to appear as if they have been stitched or broached are called "brocades."

Historical distinctions exist between different types of luxury fabrics. A Spanish document from 1375 distinguishes *brocats d'or é d'argent* (gold and silver brocades) from *los draps d'or é d'argent o de seda* (cloths of gold, silver, or silk). While some Persian and Indian kincobs are often called brocades, they may lack the floating threads that characterize true *broché* ornamentation. 

The origins of brocading can be traced to oriental weavers, particularly those in Syria, Persia, and parts of northern Africa and southern Europe under Saracen domination. Examples of this work include:
* An 11th or 12th-century Siculo-Saracenic specimen where gold thread is used to broach only the heads of birds and animals.
* A sumptuous Siculo-Saracenic weaving produced at the Hotel des Tiraz in Palermo for the official robe of Emperor Henry IV (1165-1197), which utilized gold threads and coloured silks.
* A 1480 wardrobe account of King Edward IV which lists "satyn broched with gold."

## Etymological Connection to Brokerage
The word "broker" is etymologically linked to the Latin *brocca* (spike or spit) and *broccare* (to broach). This connection is found in the Old French phrase *vendre à broche*, meaning to retail wine from the tap. 

In a primary mercantile sense, a broker is a general agent whose role is to bring buyers and sellers together to facilitate a contract in exchange for a commission or brokerage fee. While originally limited to the purchase and sale of commodities (such as produce, wool, or cotton), the term now encompasses various agents, including bill-brokers, ship-brokers, insurance-brokers, and stockbrokers. Pawnbrokers are distinguished from this group because they act as principals rather than agents.
