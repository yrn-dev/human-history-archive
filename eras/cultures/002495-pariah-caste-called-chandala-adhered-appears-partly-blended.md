# Pariah Caste (Chandala)

The Pariah caste, also referred to as the *Chandala*, represents a social group positioned outside or at the lowest extremity of the traditional Hindu caste system. This social stratification is observed in various forms across India and in certain Southeast Asian islands.

## Social Position and the Caste System
In the traditional Indian social structure, the primary divisions consist of the Brahmins (priests and teachers), the Kshatriyas (warriors), the Vaisyas (land-owners, money-lenders, merchants, and herdsmen), and the Sudras. The Pariahs are situated outside these four primary castes. 

While the first three castes are sometimes referred to as the "twice born," some theories suggest these divisions were established by Vedic Aryan conquerors to prevent racial mixing with the Pariahs and Sudras. Under this view, the Sudras are seen as a subsequent wave of northern conquerors, while the Pariahs are identified as the original Dravidian inhabitants of India. However, these speculations are not universally accepted; an alternative perspective suggests that the uniform conditions of the Ganges valley over many centuries stereotyped these class differences.

## The Chandala and the Laws of Manu
The *Chandala* are specifically associated with the lowest strata of society. Some accounts suggest that the Chandalas are descended from Brahmans, noting that Brahmans in southern India were as black as the lowest castes. 

According to the teachings of Manu, the Chandala is subject to severe restrictions and vituperative conditions:
* They are forbidden from dwelling within a town.
* Their sole permitted wealth consists of asses and dogs.
* Their clothing must be composed of the mantles of people who have deceased.
* Their dishes must be broken pots.

## Regional Variations and Blending
The caste system persists in different forms outside of mainland India. In the islands of Bali and Lombok, Hinduism remains in vogue, and the system of four castes is adhered to, along with a fifth, the Pariah caste (called *Chandala*). In these regions, the system appears to be partly blended with Buddhism and is further overgrown with a belief in *Kalas*, or evil spirits. To appease these spirits, the Balinese make offerings through *Devas* (agrarian or domestic deities) or perform the *Menyepi*, or Great Sacrifice, which involves driving away evil spirits with torches, fire-brands, and the striking of houses with heavy implements like rice-crushers.

## Interaction with Other Systems
The influence of the caste system has extended to other religious and social structures. In the island of Ceylon, the priests of Buddha—despite a life that originally protested against caste—re-established the system, reducing the *radias* or *radis* to a state similar to that of the Pariahs.

In more recent history, the hold of the caste system on the Hindu mind is evident in the efforts of Christian missions. To convert the south of India, Jesuit missionaries such as Fra dei Nobili and Xavier adopted Brahman customs, including wearing yellow or cavy-colored dress, performing frequent ablutions, and using sandalwood paste on their foreheads. Furthermore, the Danish mission of Tranquebar and the German mission led by Schwarz permitted their followers to retain their caste. Consequently, Christianity in India has primarily touched the out-castes rather than the higher classes.
