# Residential Architecture of the Richer Classes

Across various historical and geographical contexts, the residences of the wealthy have frequently been characterized by specific structural layouts, most notably the use of interior courts and specialized rooms designed for climate control and social reception.

## Interior Courts and Layouts
In certain architectural traditions, the houses of the richer classes are regularly constructed around an interior court. In these dwellings, the ground floor is primarily dedicated to utility and service areas, including stables, servants' quarters, store-rooms, and kitchens, with the exception of the *serdab*. 

The principal rooms of the house are located on the first floor. These rooms open directly onto a covered veranda, which is accessed from the interior court via an open staircase. This upper level serves as the reception area and the winter residence for the family.

In other traditions, such as those described in Greek contexts, nearly all houses were built with an inner court or "patio." This space featured a roof supported by columns, and the other rooms of the house opened into this central area. This interior peristyle or court surrounded with columns is also referred to as an *atrium* or *cavædium*.

## Climate Adaptation and Roof Usage
The design of these homes often incorporates features to manage temperature and privacy. Roofs are typically flat and enclosed by parapets. These parapets serve two primary functions: they separate the residents from their neighbors and protect the inhabitants from being observed by those living opposite. During the summer, the roofs are used for dining and sleeping, effectively functioning as a third storey of the home.

For daytime activities during the summer, family life often centers in the *serdab*. This is a cellar located somewhat below the level of the courtyard. To maintain a cool environment, the *serdab* is kept damp through frequent wettings. Its half-windows are covered with hurdles thatched with camel thorn and are kept dripping with water. In some instances, *serdabs* are equipped with punkahs.

## Regional Variations in Wealthy Housing
Residential patterns for the affluent vary by region and culture:

*   **Ashanti (British West Africa):** In Kumasi, native houses are constructed from red clay and are often richly ornamented. The houses of the better class are enclosed within compounds that contain several separate buildings. Additionally, the principal Ashanti chiefs own large houses built in the European style, which are leased to strangers.
*   **Ancient Religious Structures:** While not a private residence, the "holy house" featured gates of immense scale and luxury. Nine of these gates were covered in silver and gold—plates poured by Alexander, the father of Tiberius. One specific gate of Corinthian brass excelled the others. These structures included rooms built like towers, exceeding forty cubits in height, supported by pillars twelve cubits in circumference.
*   **Norway:** In old Norway, the king's high-seat was traditionally placed in the middle of a long bench. However, King Olaf introduced changes, placing his high-seat on a high bench across the room and introducing chimney-places in the rooms.
