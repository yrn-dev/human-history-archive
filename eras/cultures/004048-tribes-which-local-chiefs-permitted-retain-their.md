# Tribes and Local Chiefdoms

The organization of tribal societies varies significantly across different regions and eras, ranging from centralized systems of paramount authority to fragmented political aggregates. In many instances, the relationship between a central authority and local chiefs is defined by the payment of tribute, the acknowledgment of sovereignty, or the strategic distribution of territory.

## Administrative Structures in Barotseland
In Barotseland, the authority of the paramount Barotse chief extends over the lands of the Barotse proper and the territory of fifteen subject tribes that are contiguous to them. This territory is bounded by the Congo-Zambezi watershed to the north, the Zambezi and Linyante (or Kwando) rivers to the south, the Kwito river to the west, and the Kafue river to the east. 

The administration of this region is divided into three distinct groups:
* **Central Provinces:** These are administered directly by the paramount chief from the capital, Lialui, as well as by the Mokwai from Nalolo and two chiefs of the blood from Sesheke.
* **Outlying Provinces:** In areas lacking a central local government system, Barotse chiefs administer districts under the direction of the paramount chief.
* **Subject Tribes:** Certain tribes are permitted to retain their local chiefs. However, this permission is contingent upon the payment of annual tribute and the requirement that the chiefs perform homage in person at Lialui when summoned.

## Tribal Governance and Social Life
In early Greek society, social life was centered on the households of leading men. The hall of the chief served as a comprehensive hub for the tribe, where people gathered for games, exercises, feasts, and to hear bards. Primitive craftsmen also gathered there. While unimportant people and retainers slept in various locations, the tribe maintained an air of patriarchal communism; the chief, as the head of the tribe, owned the rivers, forests, and grazing lands.

In Arab tribal structures, particular families have been exalted above their equals through fortune, gratitude, or superstition. This has led to the hereditary descent of the dignities of emir and sheick within chosen races. Despite this, the order of succession remains precarious. The office of guiding valor and composing disputes through advice is typically preferred for the most aged or worthy noble kinsmen. In some instances, women of spirit and sense have been permitted to command. While a momentary junction of tribes creates an army, a more lasting union forms a nation led by a supreme chief known as the emir of emirs.

## Territorial Conflict and Displacement
Tribal existence is often marked by displacement and conflict over land. The Ansibarii, a German tribe, were expelled from their native possessions by other tribes and settled on desert lands on the Lower Rhine reserved by the Roman government for soldiers. The chief of the Ansibarii, Boiocalus, argued before the Roman legate Avitus that vacant lands should be public, asserting that the gods were given the sky and mortals were given the earth. Despite Boiocalus's fifty years of fidelity to Rome, Avitus refused the tribe's request to stay. Boiocalus spurned a private offer of land for himself separate from his tribe. Eventually, the Ansibarii were expelled by Roman arms; after wandering, the tribe was annihilated, with the warriors slain and the women and children sold as slaves.

In Japan, tradition tells of a tribe from the north commanded by a chief named Yachimata no Orochi (meaning "eight-headed serpent"). This tribe invaded the province of Izumo, taking possession of the valleys and hills in the upper reaches of the river Hi. The tribe demanded a yearly tribute of females from the local prefect.

## Tribute and Political Integration
The use of tribute as a means of coexistence is seen in the history of the Israelites. The Benjamites, who held Jerusalem, permitted its inhabitants to pay tribute. This arrangement allowed the inhabitants to cultivate the ground rather than face death or danger. Other tribes followed the example of Benjamin, permitting the Canaanites to live in peace in exchange for tribute. However, the tribe of Ephraim took a different approach during the siege of Bethel; after a citizen betrayed the city in exchange for the preservation of his family, the Israelites slew all the inhabitants and kept the city for themselves.

In Athens, the statesman Kleisthenês implemented a system to prevent local factions and the predominance of the city over the country. He assigned demes to tribes in a way that they were not adjacent, ensuring that no tribe corresponded to a continuous portion of territory. Consequently, the tribe became a political, religious, and military aggregate rather than a local interest group. Each tribe maintained a common fund and a chapel in honor of an eponymous hero.
