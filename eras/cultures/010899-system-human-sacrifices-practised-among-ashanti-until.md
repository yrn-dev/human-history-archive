# Human Sacrifice among the Ashanti

## Overview of the Ashanti Nation
The Ashanti are a powerful and conquering nation of the pure negro type, believed to be of the same race and language as the Fanti. Tradition suggests they originated from bands of fugitives driven by Moslem tribes migrating south from the Niger and Senegal countries during the 16th or 17th century. After settling in a region of impenetrable forest, they developed a national character defined by valour. 

The society is divided into numerous tribes—including the Kumasi, Bekwai, Adansi, Juabin, Kokofu, Mampon, Nsuta, Nkwanta, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Ofinsu, and Adjisu—each with its own king. Since the beginning of the 18th century, the king of Kumasi has been recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti, succeeding to the "golden stool," which serves as the symbol of authority and sovereignty.

## Political Structure and Governance
Historically, the Ashanti government was a combination of military aristocracy and monarchy. The state's social fabric was based on six great divisions or clans organized for war. The King's Council was composed of the chiefs of these clans and several sub-chiefs with hereditary rights. In many instances, the council exercised more power than the king himself, unless the monarch possessed an exceptionally strong character.

Land within the Ashanti system is held in common by the tribes. Any land that remains unallotted is referred to as "stool lands" and is attached to the office of the king or head chief. Socially, the practice of polygamy is observed by all who have the financial means to afford it.

## Human Sacrifice under the Rule of Mensa
The archive's records specifically link the practice of human sacrifice to the reign of King Mensa. Mensa ascended to the golden stool after the Ashanti deposed Kofi Karikari following the war of 1873-74. 

Mensa's tenure as monarch is described as tyrannous. His rule was characterized by repeated human sacrifices, a practice that stained his administration. This period of tyranny eventually led to a revolution in 1883, which displaced Mensa and brought Kwaka Dua II to power.

## Conflict and Treatment of Captives
The Ashanti history is marked by significant military conflict with coastal tribes and European powers. In 1824, during a battle at Essamako, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy and a force of 500 Europeans. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed in the engagement; subsequently, the skull of the governor was taken to Kumasi and utilized as a royal drinking-cup.

The Ashanti also engaged in the capture of Europeans. In June 1869, an Ashanti force invaded Krepi, capturing Mr. J. Kuhne and Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer along with his wife and infant son. Additionally, a French trader named Monsieur M.J. Bonnat was captured. These individuals were taken as prisoners to Kumasi.

## British Annexation and Legal Transition
Following various conflicts and the deposition of King Prempeh in 1896, Ashanti was formally annexed to the British dominions via an order in council on September 26, 1901. Under British administration, the country was governed by the governor of the Gold Coast, represented by a chief commissioner in Kumasi.

The British administration established civil and criminal courts, which the natives showed a marked preference for, indicating a faith in impartial treatment. The British constitution for Ashanti stipulated that the governor should respect native laws regulating the civil relations of tribes and chiefs, provided those laws were not clearly injurious to the welfare of the natives or incompatible with British sovereignty.
