# Festivals and Rituals in World History

The archive's records detail a diverse array of festivals, religious rites, and ceremonial gatherings across various civilizations, ranging from the mythological age of Japan to the religious practices of British India and the Islamic world.

## Religious Festivals of India and the Islamic World

In British India, the village of Badrinath in the Garhwal district of the United Provinces serves as a site of high sanctity. It contains a temple dedicated to an incarnation of Vishnu, featuring a three-foot-high idol of black stone. The temple is a popular destination for pilgrims from across India. While the number of visitors typically ranges between 7,000 and 10,000 in ordinary years, this number is said to increase to 50,000 every twelfth year during the celebration of the Kumbh-mela festival.

The term "Bairam" is a Perso-Turkish word for "festival" used to describe the two primary festivals of Islam. The first is the "Lesser Festival," known as *Kutshuk Bairam* or *Sheker Bairam* by Turks, and *'Id al-Fitr* ("Festival of Fast-breaking") or *Al-'id al-saghir* by Arabic speakers. This festival occurs during the first three days of the tenth month, Shawwal, immediately following the fasting-month of Ramadan. It is characterized by official receptions, private visits, the giving of presents, the wearing of new clothes, and visits to the graves of relatives.

The "Greater Festival" is known as *Qurban Bairam* ("Sacrifice Bairam") by Turks and *Al-'id al-kabir* or *'Id al-adha* ("Festival of Sacrifice") by Arabic speakers. It takes place on the tenth and the following two or three days of the final month, *Dhu-l-hijja*. During this time, pilgrims in the valley of Mina slay a cow, camel, he-goat, or ram to commemorate the ransom of Ishmael with a ram. Throughout the Moslem world, those who can afford it sacrifice a legal animal, either consuming the meat or providing it to the poor. Both Islamic festivals follow a lunar calendar and move through the solar year every thirty-two years.

## Ancient and Mythological Rites

In the mythological age of Japan, the "harvest festival" (*nii-name*, meaning "tasting the new rice") was established after Amaterasu obtained seeds of the "five cereals" and offered a portion to the Kami. When Ninigi set out to conquer Japan, rice seed was given to the ancestors of the Imibe and Nakatomi families, who then conducted the autumn festival. This rite involved the sovereign and a prayer recited by the head of the Nakatomi for the longevity of the Emperor and the eternity of the Imperial line. Other Japanese rites included the *Odono matsuri* (consecration of the palace), the "fire-subduing fete," the "spirit-tranquillizing fete," and the *Oharai* ("great purification"), which was performed twice annually on the last days of the sixth and twelfth months.

In ancient Egypt, the city of Bubastis hosted a yearly festival. Participants traveled by boat, with men playing flutes and women using rattles, singing, and clapping. As they passed other cities along the river-bank, some women would dance, pull up their garments, or jeer at the women in those cities. Upon arriving at Bubastis, they performed great sacrifices and consumed more grape wine than during the rest of the year. According to native accounts, the number of men and women attending this event reached seventy myriads, excluding children.

## Ceremonial and Institutional Observances

The Great Kaan maintained elaborate birthday customs involving 12,000 Barons and Knights. On this day, these individuals wore robes of silk and gold in the same color as the Great Kaan, though less costly, and wore gold girdles provided by the Sovereign. Some of these suits were adorned with precious stones and pearls, with single suits valued at 10,000 golden bezants. The Great Kaan presented such raiment to his Barons and Knights thirteen times a year, with a different color assigned to each festival.

In the context of religious societies, a centenary festival was celebrated in 1904 by foreign missions and the Reformed Churches. This event drew messages of congratulation from the rulers of every Protestant nation in Christendom and resulted in a centenary thanksgiving fund of 250,000 guineas to extend the society's work.
