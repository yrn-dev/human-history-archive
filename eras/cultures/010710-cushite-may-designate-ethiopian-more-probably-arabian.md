# Cushite

The term "Cushite" is used in historical and genealogical contexts to designate different groups of people. While it may refer to an Ethiopian, it is considered more probable that it designates an Arabian, as seen in the identification of Cush as the "father" of the Sabaeans.

## Regional Identifications and Conflicts
In historical narratives concerning the kingdom of Judah, the identity of certain figures is debated. For instance, the figure of Zerah is sometimes interpreted as an Ethiopian or a Sabaean prince. Some scholars argue that since no known king Zerah of Ethiopia existed during the period of Asa, the Sabaean identification removes the primary difficulty of the narrative. During this era, Judah was frequently troubled by inroads from desert hordes. If traditions locating a battle at Mareshah are correct, it is probable that these invaders were allied with Philistine towns, a situation that recurred during the reigns of Jehoram and Ahaz.

## The Ethiopians
Historical accounts describe the Ethiopians as being divided into two distinct bodies. Those from the direction of the sunrising (Asia) differed from the Ethiopians of Libya primarily in their language and hair; the Eastern Ethiopians possessed straight hair, while those of Libya had hair that was thicker and woollier than any other men.

### Military Equipment and Appearance
The Ethiopians of Asia were armed similarly to the Indians. They wore the flayed skin of a horse's forehead—including the ears and mane—upon their heads, with the mane serving as a crest. They utilized crane-skin defenses instead of shields. In contrast, the Libyans used leather equipment and javelins with burnt points.

Other descriptions of Ethiopians note the use of leopard and lion skins tied to the body. Their weaponry included long bows made of palm-wood (at least four cubits in length) with small reed arrows tipped with sharpened stone used for engraving seals. They also carried clubs with knobs and spears tipped with the sharpened horn of a gazelle. In battle, they smeared half of their bodies white and the other half red.

### Geography and Culture
The Ethiopian land extending furthest toward the sunset is described as producing abundance in gold, ebony, and huge elephants, as well as trees of all kinds. The people there are characterized as the tallest, most beautiful, and most long-lived of all men. In the part of Libya by the Southern Sea, the "Long-lived" Ethiopians are said to possess a "table of the Sun," a meadow where the earth continually produces boiled flesh-meat of four-footed creatures for the citizens to feast upon.

## The Arabians
The Arabians are described as wearing loose mantles and carrying long bows that bent backward at their right side. They are noted for the production of gum-mastich (which they call ladanon), a sweet-scented resin found in the beards of he-goats and used extensively as incense. 

Arabian livestock includes two unique kinds of sheep. One variety has a tail at least three cubits long; to prevent sores from the tail dragging on the ground, shepherds build small cars to carry the tails. The second variety has a tail that is a cubit in breadth.

## Persian Administration and Tribute
Under Persian rule, both Arabians and Ethiopians were integrated into the empire's military and administrative structures. Arsames, the son of Dareios and Artystone, served as the commander for the Arabians and the Ethiopians who dwelt above Egypt. 

Regarding tribute and gifts to the Persian king:
* **Arabians:** Brought one thousand talents of frankincense annually.
* **Ethiopians (bordering Egypt):** Brought gifts every other year, consisting of two quart measures of unmelted gold, two hundred blocks of ebony, five Ethiopian boys, and twenty large elephant tusks.

During the expedition of Xerxes against Greece, the land-force was composed of forty-six different nations, which included both the Arabians and two distinct Ethiopian groups: those in Asia and those south of Egypt.

## Interactions with Egypt and Assyria
The relationship between Egypt and Ethiopia involved significant migration and military aid. During the reign of Psammetichos, two hundred and forty thousand Egyptian warriors revolted from their posts at Elephantine and migrated to Ethiopia. The king of the Ethiopians allowed them to settle in the land by driving out Ethiopians who were at variance with him. Consequently, the Ethiopians adopted milder manners through the customs of the Egyptians.

In military history, the king of Assyria, Sennacherib, besieged Pelusium. He broke off the siege and returned without success upon hearing that Tirhaka, the king of the Ethiopians, was marching through the desert with great forces to aid the Egyptians. While some accounts, such as those by Herodotus, mistakenly identify the king in this conflict as an Arabian king, other records clarify he was the king of the Assyrians.
