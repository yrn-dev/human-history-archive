# Playing to the Score and Game Scoring

In various card and table games, the strategic approach to scoring varies based on the current state of the game, the value of specific points, and the specific rules governing the victory conditions.

## Bridge Scoring and Strategy
In bridge, the strategy for choosing a suit is influenced by the current score. At the beginning of a hand, when the score is "love-all," the advantage of reaching a specific score does not significantly affect the choice of suit. However, as play progresses, players should consider the advantage of reaching specific points, most notably 6, 18, and to a lesser extent, 22. This is because the scores of 8, 12, and 24 are common for the dealer to make and can take the dealer out from those respective points.

The value of points changes depending on the score's progression. Moving the score from 4 to 6 is worth an average of 4 or 5 points, whereas moving from 6 to 8 is worth 1 point. When approaching the end of a game, it is advantageous to make a declaration that might take the player out, or one that does not exactly take the adversaries out.

The relative value of suits and honours also shifts:
* **Suit Advantage:** When the score is 24 to 22 against the dealer, clubs and hearts are half a trick better relative to diamonds than they are at love-all.
* **Honours vs. Tricks:** In the first and second games of a rubber, a point scored for honours is generally worth about half a point scored for tricks. This value decreases in a close game and increases in a one-sided game. In the deciding game of a rubber, the importance of winning the game reduces the value of honours to one-third of a point scored for tricks.

Regarding declarations, the dealer should make club declarations more frequently when their score exceeds twenty. Spades should be declared with five at a score of twenty-eight and with six at twenty-six. When a player is significantly behind in the score, they may declare a "risky no-trumper," such as one with an established suit of seven or eight cards lacking a card of entry.

## Dominoes Scoring
Different variations of dominoes employ distinct scoring systems based on pips and matching.

**All Fives (Muggins)**
Players score pips if the leader poses a double-five, six-four, five-blank, or three-two. Additionally, if a player makes the sum of the end pips equal 5, 10, 15, or 20, they score that number. For example, playing a double-four to a two-four scores 10. At the point of "domino," the winner scores a multiple of five nearest to the number of pips in the adversary's hand (e.g., 27 pips results in a score of 25).

**Matador**
In Matador, the goal is to pose a number that, when added to the end, equals seven. Scoring occurs when the game is definitely blocked; the player with the lower aggregate of pips scores the number of the combined hands, though sometimes only the excess in the opponent's hand is counted. The game is usually played to 100.

**Threes-and-Fives**
Similar to Muggins, but only one point is scored for each three or five made at the two ends. These can be scored in combination; for instance, a player may score 5 for five threes and 3 for three fives.

## Billiards and Bagatelle Scoring
**Billiards Proper**
This game is typically played for 100 or more points. Scoring is as follows:
* **Red hazard:** 3 points
* **White hazard:** 2 points
* **Cannon:** 2 points

Opponents score points through forfeitures: a "coup" (running a ball off the table or into a pocket without hitting another ball) scores 3, and ordinary misses score 1.

**Bagatelle Varieties**
In "Bagatelle Proper," the black ball counts double, and the game is decided by the aggregate score over an agreed number of rounds. In the French "Sans Egal" version, the game is decided by rounds or by points, usually 21 or 31.

In the "Cannon Game," a cannon counts for 2 points. Missing the white object-ball scores 1 for the adversary, and missing the black scores 5 for the adversary. If pockets are used, holing the white object-ball scores 2 and the black scores 3, provided a cannon was made by the same stroke; otherwise, the score goes to the adversary. In the "Irish Cannon Game," all pocketed balls count for the adversary.
