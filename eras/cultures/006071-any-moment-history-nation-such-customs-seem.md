# The Nature and Evolution of National Customs

## The Fluidity of Social Customs
To a superficial observer, the customs of a nation may appear to be fixed and immutable at any given moment in history. However, these practices are never entirely the same across successive generations or centuries. A primary example of this is the concept of caste; the numerous and complicated details categorized under this single name depend solely on the sanction of public opinion. While this opinion may seem stable, it constantly varies regarding the complexity of the groups in which details are observed and the degree of importance attached to specific particulars.

## Customs as a Key to National Understanding
A thorough knowledge of a people—including their folklore, legends, mythology, and history—is necessary to truly understand them. Habits and customs that appear odd, strange, or even subversive of ordinary morality to a foreign observer can be explained and seen as reasonable when traced back to their origins. For example, certain national manners may seem hard-hearted to outsiders, such as the Japanese discipline of fortitude and politeness, which encourages endurance without groaning and the restraint of sorrow to avoid marring the serenity of others. This creates a national trait of apparent stoicism, though the effort to restrain natural promptings may actually make the individuals more sensitive.

Studying the manners, laws, and customs of different nations, as well as the character of their governors, allows for an understanding of the factors that contribute to the grandeur or decay of states. Such study provides instruction in the principles of government, the rules of policy, the conduct of life, and the arts of war and empire.

## Marriage and Lineage Customs
Various nations have historically practiced different forms of marriage and succession. Among the Mongols, plurality of wives is not prohibited, with the first wife managing domestic concerns and receiving the most respect. While polygamy is not as general now as it was when Asia lay at their feet, it remains prevalent among the Yakuts (whose lineage appears to be Eastern Turk) and the Buraets, who retain old Mongol customs and take as many wives as they choose.

Historically, a custom existed where a son, upon succeeding his father, could take any of his deceased father's wives. This practice is illustrated in Mongol histories and was ascribed by Herodotus to the Scyths. While a modern Mongol writer states that taking a deceased brother's wives is now obsolete, the memory of the practice is preserved in a proverb. This specific custom is also observed in some Mahomedan nations, notably the Afghans, and has been cited as supposed proof of Hebrew lineage.

## Ritual and Religious Practices
Customs related to burial and sacrifice provide insight into ancient beliefs. In early Japan, the sacrifice of personal attendants occurred during burials, such as that of Prince Yamato in A.D. 2. Following shocking incidents, this custom was abandoned in favor of substituting clay images for human beings. Terracotta figures (haniwa) often encircled the principal misasagi, representing a "hedge of men," while clay effigies within dolmens served as substitutes for high-ranking retainers. Although compulsory sacrifices appear to have ceased around the first century of the Christian era, voluntary sacrifices continued for many subsequent ages. This rite of animistic religion was not unique to one race but was practiced from time immemorial by the Manchu Tatars, the Chinese, and various other nations of northeastern Asia.

## Professional and Legal Customs
Customs also extend to professional conduct and legal obligations. In England, bankers follow a custom of not paying part of a cheque's amount if the available funds are insufficient to meet the total, a practice based on section 53 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882. This differs from Scotland, where banks are bound to pay whatever funds are available toward the satisfaction of the cheque. Additionally, bankers are bound to maintain secrecy regarding a customer's account unless there is good cause for disclosure, an obligation that continues even if the account is overdrawn.

## Customs and National Identity
Departures from regular customs can be viewed as signs of discontent or instability. In Sparta, the regent Pausanias faced suspicion because he departed from regular customs and imitated barbarians, which was viewed as contempt for the laws. 

In the study of ancient migrations, some students of Chinese history have suggested that coincidences in manners, customs, and language—though often of a shadowy character—point to Babylonia as the region from which the Chinese migrated. However, authentic Chinese records of the early past contain no mention or hint of such a migration.
