# BOOT

## Etymology and Definitions
The term "boot" has two distinct origins and meanings within the English language. The first derivation comes from the Old English *bot*, a term common to Teutonic languages (such as the Goth *bota*), meaning "advantage" or "good." This root is related to the word "better" and the Old High German *Buoza* (Modern German *Busse*), meaning "fine" or "penance." In this sense, "boot" refers to profit or advantage. This usage survives in the word "bootless," meaning unavailing or useless, and in archaistic phrases such as "what boots it?" 

Additionally, the old form "bote" is found in several compound legal terms referring to specific rights of "estover" (the Norman French equivalent of the Saxon *bote*), including "hedge-bote," "fire-bote," and "house-bote." The form also appears in "thief-bote," which refers to the customary compensation paid for injuries under Old English law.

## Footwear and Apparel
The second meaning of "boot" refers to a covering for the foot. This word entered the English language via the Old French *bote* (modern *botte*) and Medieval Latin *botta* or *bota*. While common usage applies the term to footwear that reaches only above the ankle—distinguishing it from a "shoe"—properly, a boot covers the entire lower part of the leg, sometimes extending to or above the knee.

## Application to Coaches
The term "boot" is also applied to parts of a coach, sharing the same derivation as the footwear. Originally, the word referred to the French *botte*, which was the fixed outside step of the vehicle. It later referred to the uncovered spaces beside or on the step where attendants sat facing sideways. Both of these original meanings are now obsolete. In modern usage, the "boot" of a coach refers to the covered receptacles located under the seats of the coachman and the guard.
