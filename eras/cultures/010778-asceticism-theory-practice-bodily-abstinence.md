# ASCETICISM

Asceticism is the theory and practice of self-mortification and bodily abstinence, typically associated with religious motivations. The term is derived from the Greek verb *askeo* ("I practise"), leading to the noun *askaesis* and the adjective *askaetikos*. This terminology originates from a metaphor based on the ancient *palaestra*, or wrestling-place, where victory was granted to those who had most effectively trained their bodies. While early Greek philosophic asceticism—specifically among the Neo-pythagoreans and Cynics—continued to use metaphors from athletic contests, true asceticism is defined by a moral purpose. It is based on the eternal contrast between the proposition "This is right" and the proposition "That is pleasant."

## Primitive and Non-Moral Asceticism
The ascetic instinct is likely as old as humanity, though primitive forms often lack lofty spiritual meaning. In early practices, many things were considered holy that may appear trifling or obscene from a modern perspective. Primitive asceticism often involves self-torture and abstentions that seem valueless for the discipline of feelings or the training of character.

Such non-moral restraints, or *taboos*, may have contributed to the human faculty of preferring a larger good over immediate impulse. While Aristotle suggested in his *Ethics* that the "barbarian's ideal of life" was "the living as one likes," the archive's sources indicate that savages are actually tied down by superstitious restrictions and scruples more than civilized men. In these cases, the ascetic instinct is over-developed but ill-informed, often working in ways that are morally harmful or unessential.

## Practices of Abstinence and Mortification
Ascetic practices vary widely, ranging from conscious methods of ethical perfection to those based on demonological beliefs or superstitions.

### Dietary Restrictions and Fasting
Fasting is a common element of primitive asceticism. Some animals and vegetables are designated as *taboo* because they are considered too holy or too defiling to eat. For example, in Leviticus xi, the sons of Israel (Bani Israel) are forbidden from eating animals that are not cloven-footed ruminants, including the swine, hare, coney, and camel. They are also forbidden from eating water organisms without fins and scales, as well as various birds such as the heron, stork, pelican, swan, and hoopoe. Other forbidden animals include the mouse, weasel, most lizards, and any winged creeping things with four feet. These restrictions often stemmed from totemism, where a kinship tie between the people, their gods, and the animal prevented its consumption. Similarly, Porphyry noted that Phoenicians and Egyptians avoided the flesh of the cow, and hundreds of millions of Hindus have followed similar abstentions.

### Physical Hardships and Self-Torture
Other forms of asceticism include the denial of sexual instincts and the subjection of the body to physical discomfort. These may include:
*   **Environmental and Personal Hardships:** Sleeping on the bare ground, living in caves, wearing hair shirts, avoiding baths, avoiding hair-cutting, and wearing clean raiment.
*   **Physical Alterations:** Tattooing and the deformation of the feet, teeth, or skull.
*   **Severe Mortification:** Scourging, branding, fire-walking, burial alive, cutting with knives, and hanging oneself by hooks plunged into the skin to suspend weights from the tenderer parts of the body.
*   **Vows:** Life-long vows of silence or silence observed during pilgrimages.

Some of these acts had specific perceived goals; for instance, the self-mutilation practiced by the priests of Cybele (the Galli) was likely a magical ceremony intended to stimulate crops and fertilize the soil.

## Historical and Regional Examples
Asceticism has appeared across various cultures and eras as a means of seeking spiritual power, salvation, or peace.

### Eastern and Asian Traditions
In the East, before the time of Buddha, men and women repudiated property and marriage, seeking solitude and abstinence to find spiritual powers and escape worldly stresses. Although Buddha opposed ascetic extravagances, many of his disciples maintained severe monkish lives. In Japan, the genius Kume reportedly obtained supernatural power through asceticism. After being taunted by officials during forced labour, he fasted and prayed for seven days and nights, resulting in a thunder-storm and the miraculous movement of heavy timber.

In other regions, devotees known as Sensin practice extraordinary abstinence. They wear black and blue hempen dresses, sleep on mats, and refuse to take wives. Their lifelong diet consists solely of bran mixed with hot water. They worship fire and feminine idols with women's names.

### Mediterranean and Western Traditions
In the first century B.C., the Essenes in Alexandria and Judea abandoned the world for austerities and mystical contemplation. During the first and second centuries A.D., a "world-wide resort" to renunciation occurred as people sought salvation from slavery, cruelty, and mental insecurity. This led penitents to the Serapeum and converts to Mithraic caves.

In the history of the papacy, Pope Gregory's reign established the duty of sexual abstinence for priests in Western Catholic Christianity.

## Philosophical and Theoretical Perspectives
Some philosophical systems view asceticism as a logical result of their ethics. For Malebranche, the passions and senses have no relation to the higher life of the soul. He viewed the union of the rational and sensuous nature as a confusion to be cleared up by the spirit withdrawing from that which defiles it. Consequently, his theory of reason leads to mysticism and, in practice, to asceticism.

Great religious reformers—including Moses, Buddha, Paul, Mani, Mahomet, St Francis, and Luther—have sought to direct the ascetic instinct toward higher aims, replacing ritual purity with true personal holiness.
