# Tumbuka, Henga, and A-tonga Peoples

## Geographic Distribution and Classification
The Tumbuka section is located in the western part of Nyasaland, situated south of the North Nyasa (or Nkonde) group. This section occupies the territory between the Upper Luangwa river and the western shores of Lake Nyasa. The group is defined by the languages of the Tumbuka, Henga, and A-tonga peoples.

## Linguistic Context
The languages of the Tumbuka, Henga, and A-tonga are categorized within the broader framework of Bantu languages. In the region of Lake Nyasa, they are positioned south of the North Nyasa group, which includes dialects such as *Iki-nyikiusa* and *Ici-wandia* on the north-west and north coasts, as well as *Ishi-nyi[chi]a* on the Nyasa-Tanganyika plateau. To the east of the North Nyasa group is the *Kinga* speech of the Livingstone mountains, while further east, between the Ruaha river and the Livingstone mountains, lies the *Sango-Bena-Hehe-Sutu* group.

## The Bantu Migration and Physical Characteristics
The Tumbuka, Henga, and A-tonga are part of the larger population of Bantu Africa. According to archive records, a powerful tribe of negroes speaking the Bantu mother-language migrated from the heart of Africa—the region between the western Nile, Congo, and Shari watersheds—into the southern half of the continent approximately 3,000 years ago. This migrating group is described as being physically allied to the negroes of the southern Lake Chad and south-western Nile basins, though impregnated with the Caucasian Hamite.

As these Bantu speakers swept across the continent, they assimilated or absorbed the majority of the preceding populations. In the area from Lake Nyasa and Lake Tanganyika south-westwards to the Cape of Good Hope, the prior inhabitants consisted of Nilotic-negro, Forest-negro, Bushman, and Hottentot populations.

While there is a certain physical resemblance among various tribes speaking clearly-marked Bantu dialects—including the Ba-tonga, the people of the Great Lakes, the Zulu-Kaffirs, the Ova-herero, the Awemba, and the Babangi of the upper Congo—there remains a great diversity in physical characteristics. This diversity includes variations in the shape of the head and outward appearance. Some Bantu-speaking tribes are dwarfish and belong to the Forest Pygmies, while others show relationships to the Hottentots or resemble the black West African negro. Others, particularly in the north, are of Nilotic or Gala (Galla) origin.

## Related Linguistic Groups
While the Tumbuka section is distinct, other groups in the broader region share Bantu linguistic roots. To the north-west, the *Ki-bemba* (or *Emba*) genus occupies the ground between Lake Mweru, the south coasts of Tanganyika, and the Upper Chambezi river, including the *Ki-mambwe* and *Ki-lungu* dialects. Additionally, a separate group styled as *Subiya-Tonga-Ila* exists in Zambezia. This group includes the *Ba-ila* (*Mashukulumbwe*), the *Ba-tetela*, and the dialects of Central Zambezia. The *Ci-subiya* language is dominant in South-West Zambezia and the lands between the Chobe-Linyante river and the Zambezi. Both *Subiya* and *Tonga* in this region are noted for having only a slight relationship with the Zulu group and no strong affinity to *Oci-herero*.
