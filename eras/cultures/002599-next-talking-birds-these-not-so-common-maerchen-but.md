# Talking-Birds in Popular Poetry

Talking-birds are a recurring element in the tradition of popular poetry and ballads. While they are not as common as they are in *Maerchen* (the German term for nursery tales), they remain very general across various cultures and typically cause no surprise to the human characters who listen to them.

## Characteristics and Roles
In the context of genuine popular song, talking-birds often serve specific narrative functions. M. Ampere, in his instructions to a committee searching for the remains of ballads in France between 1852 and 1853, noted that French ballads would likely employ "bird choruses" and utilize talking-birds as messengers.

These birds appear in various forms across different regional traditions:
* **Border Minstrelsy:** Features an "omniscient popinjay" who "up and spoke."
* **Romaic Ballads:** These include the *tria poulakia* (three golden birds) which serve to introduce the story or sing the prelude to the songs.
* **French Folk-Song:** Includes a "wicked dove" that whispers temptation.
* **The *Water o' Wearies Well*:** Features a bird that "came out of a bush, on water for to dine."
* **Zulu Tradition:** Wise birds whose speech is still understood by those who are exceptionally gifted.
* **Servian Song:** According to Talvj, talking-birds pervade this tradition.

## Association with Primitive Luxury
The appearance of talking-birds is often linked to a broader characteristic of primitive poetry: the "lavish and reckless" use of precious metals. This tendency to represent common everyday objects as being made of gold or silver is a common note in folk-songs. 

Examples of this "primitive luxury" include:
* **Aristophanes:** Mentions talking birds that have gold on their wings.
* **The *Kalewala*:** A Finnish epic formed from popular songs where almost everything is gold.
* **Mount Rhodope:** Ballads collected by M. Verkovitch display similar uses of gold.

This motif extends beyond birds to other elements of ballads, such as horses with silver or gold shoes (found in Romaic ballads and the song *Willie's Lady*), knives of fine gold, and gold chairs and tables.

## Context within Ballad Literature
The presence of talking-birds and other recurring motifs—such as the use of assonance instead of rhyme, textual repetition, and the constant use of numbers like three and seven—helps scholars identify the nature of popular poetry. 

These elements suggest that many romantic ballads share a "universal sameness of tone, of incident, of legend, and of primitive poetical formulae." This commonality is found across the ballads of Italy, Denmark, Portugal, Provence, France, and Greece, as well as Scotland. This suggests that, like *Maerchen*, these ballads are an immemorial inheritance of European peoples, possessing stock situations and ideas of "dateless age and world-wide diffusion," even if their current linguistic forms are relatively recent due to centuries of oral recitation.
