# Baluchistan

Baluchistan is a sparsely populated region of south-western Asia, covering a larger area than any Indian province with the exceptions of Bengal, Madras, and Burma. Geographically, it is part of a rainless region whose flora is continuous with that of northern and eastern Africa, extending from the coast of Senegal to the meridian of 75 deg. E.

## Geography and Topography
The region is divided into two distinct parts by a railway line, its associated plains, passes, and the dominant hills surrounding Quetta. 

North of the railway line lies a region of rough, picturesque highlands between the plains of the Indus and Afghanistan. This area includes the central ranges of the Suliman system, which served as the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander. This northern district has an average breadth of 150 miles, narrowing to less than 100 miles at the Gomal and expanding to over 200 miles along the railway. The climate here is clear and bracing with alpine vegetation and no great temperature extremes. Drainage in this sector either flows north toward the Gomal or east through limestone hills toward the Indus.

South of the railway is a square block of territory measuring approximately 300 miles by 300 miles. This area is primarily the home of the Baluch and the Brahui. It features the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, where drainage cuts deep gorges to flow east into the plains of Sind. Other waters in this southern block either run in straight lines to the Arabian Sea north of Karachi or curve west to disappear into inland swamps.

## Ethnography and Races
Baluchistan is inhabited by a diverse array of races. The underlying population consists of Tajiks (Persians), who are found throughout the country. They are sometimes represented by dominant tribes, but more frequently serve as the general community's bondsmen and agricultural slaves, such as the Nakibs, Dehkans, or Dehwars. The Durzadas (identified as the *Derusiaei* of Herodotus) also extend throughout Makran.

The region's ethnography is strongly influenced by Arab immigration. All Rind tribes claim Koraish extraction and Arab origin. Arab occupation began early in southern Baluchistan and Seistan, later spreading through the Sind valley until the 12th century. In the central desert—known in medieval Arabic geography as the Kir, Kej, Katz, or Kash Kaian and part of the ancient Kaiani kingdom—scattered nomad tribes called Rekis (desert people) reside. The Mohamadani are the most numerous of these nomads and are likely of Arab origin, occupying the central mountain region.

In the Mashkel and Kharan deserts, the Naushirwanis reside. Described as a fine, manly race of purely Persian origin, they entered Baluchistan within historic times and are considered identical to the Tahukani or Tahuki found in Perso-Baluchistan. Between the Naushirwanis and the fish-eating coastal populations are numerous tribes located near the sources of the Hingol or within the narrow valleys of the Kej and Rakshan tributaries.

## Political Status and Administration
Baluchistan is not regarded as a distinct entity among Asiatic nations in the same manner as Afghanistan. Its independence is qualified by British control. 

"British Baluchistan" officially comprises the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. Although these districts were nominally handed over to Kalat forty years prior to 1879, they were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879. These districts now form an official province within the Baluchistan Agency, which extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. While some districts within the agency maintain a high degree of independence, the province's overall political status is similar to that of the native states of the Indian peninsula, with supreme control exercised by the agent to the governor-general of India and his political assistants.

## Economy of Kalat
The revenues of the khan of Kalat are derived from agricultural revenue and subsidies, totaling approximately Rs.500,000 per annum. Since 1882, the khan has received Rs.25,000 as government rent for the Quetta district and Rs.30,000 in lieu of transit duties in the Boian (a sum recently increased by Rs.9,000). In 1899, the total value of Kalat's imports was Rs.700,000, while exports were valued at Rs.505,000.
