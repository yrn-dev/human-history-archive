# BALUSTRADE

A balustrade is a low screen or parapet consisting of a rail or coping that is supported by balusters. While often serving as a functional barrier, it is sometimes employed purely as a decorative element, such as beneath the sill of a window that does not extend to the ground.

## Composition and Variations
The primary supporting elements of a balustrade are balusters. A baluster is a small moulded shaft, which may be circular or square in shape. These shafts are typically constructed from wood or stone, though metal is also used. The term "baluster" is derived from the Italian *balaustro*, which is believed to be named for its resemblance to the *balaustion*, or wild pomegranate flower; in English, this term has been corrupted into "banister."

While many balustrades utilize these shafts, other variations exist:
* **Foliage:** In some instances, flowing foliage is used in place of a standard parapet.
* **Pierced Stone:** Certain balustrades are formed from vertical slabs of stone that have been pierced. Examples of this style can be found on the balconies of the minarets at Cairo and at the Ca' d'oro in Venice.
* **Open-work:** Delicate, open-work balustrades are found in specific architectural contexts, such as on the first story of the Sainte-Croix Church in Caix.

## Historical Development
The earliest known examples of balustrades appear in bas-reliefs depicting Assyrian palaces, where they were used for windows and featured Ionic capitals. They do not appear to have been used by the Romans or the Greeks. Early examples later emerged in the palaces of Verona and Venice.

The feature gained significant importance through Italian revivalists, who frequently employed balustrades for roof parapets and window balconies. In Saxon architecture, the term "baluster shaft" refers to the shaft that divides a window. Some of these shafts are located in the south transept of the abbey at St Albans in England; these are thought to have originated from an old Saxon church, though they have since been fitted with plain cylindrical Norman shafts and Norman capitals and bases.

## Technical Specifications and Maintenance
Parapets and balustrades must be constructed to withstand environmental pressures. In exposed locations, such as sea fronts or areas lacking protection from buildings and trees, it is necessary to increase the thickness of parapets and walls and to provide heavier flashings and copings. In England, the greatest wind pressure in the most exposed positions may reach 56 lb per sq. ft., while 40 lb is sufficient for most cases, and 28 lb is adequate for protected areas.

Regarding urban construction and safety, the London County Council by-laws of 1891 (made under the Metropolis Management and Buildings Acts Amendment Act 1878) specify requirements for the finishing of parapet-walls. The top of such walls must be finished using one of the following:
* A course of hard, well-burnt bricks set on edge in cement.
* A coping made of any other fire-resisting and waterproof material that is properly secured.

## Related Structures
The term "parapet" is used in various contexts beyond decorative balustrades. In hydraulic engineering, parapet walls or stone ramparts may be constructed in rivers above aqueducts. These subaqueous walls, originally four to eight feet high, serve to raise water levels during low seasons to provide a more abundant supply and greater impetus for water wheels. These structures typically include a central passage to allow boats to pass. In military or maritime contexts, parapet walls may also be found in fortifications or on structures such as a Mole.
