# Fresh Waters of Southern Asia

The fresh waters of southern Asia are characterized by a specific distribution of fish species, showing a notable deficiency in the typical forms of the *Acanthopterygii*. Instead, these waters are primarily inhabited by siluroids, carp, simple or spined eels, and fishes capable of walking and climbing.

## Fish Populations and Distribution

The *Cyprinidae*, or carp, are largely represented in southern Asia. In this region, they reach sizes not found in Europe; for example, a *Barbus* captured in the Tigris weighed 300 lb. The diversity and size of this family are most prominent in mountain regions with temperate climates, while smaller species inhabit low-lying rivers and hotter regions.

The *Siluridae* reach their chief development in tropical regions. While only one *Silurus* is found in Europe, this same species extends into Africa and southern Asia. In contrast, the *Salmonidae* are entirely absent from southern Asian waters. While trout are found in streams flowing into the Caspian beyond the Indus watershed, they are unknown in Indian rivers.

Other notable fish include:
*   **Clupeidae (Herrings):** Numerous forms occur in Asiatic waters and can ascend rivers for many hundred miles. The hilsa is one of the best-known Indian fishes of this family.
*   **Sturgeons:** These are found in Asiatic Russia and in an allied form that extends to southern China.
*   **Walking and Climbing Fishes:** Peculiar to Africa and south-eastern Asia, these fishes are organized to breathe outside of water. This adaptation allows them to survive in regions of rain and periodical drought that would be fatal to other fish species.

## Asian Lakes and Water Bodies

Asia contains innumerable lakes that vary significantly in scale and origin. These range from highland lochs and the extended swamps of Persia to inland seas such as Lake Balkash and Lake Baikal. Lake Baikal, located in East Siberia, is the largest fresh-water basin in Eurasia and the sixth largest lake in the world. It has an area of 13,200 sq. m. and a length of 386 m.

Many Asian lakes are situated at high elevations and are considered vestiges of an ancient period of glaciation, typically showing signs of a gradual decrease in size. Other lakes are relics of earlier geological periods where land recently upheaved from the sea was spread at low levels with alternating fresh and salt water inundations; Lop Nor and the Helmund *hamuns* are typical examples of this.

In south-west Baluchistan and Persia, the plateau *hamuns* change their extent and form from season to season. Many of these are impregnated with saline deposits from underlying strata. In the Persian desert, the *kavirs* (salt depressions) are more often widespread deposits of salt and mud than areas covered by water.
