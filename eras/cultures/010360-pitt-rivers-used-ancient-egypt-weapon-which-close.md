# The Boomerang and the Research of General Pitt-Rivers

## Overview of the Boomerang
The boomerang is a missile weapon utilized by other peoples and the Australian aborigines. The term originates from a native name used by a single tribe in New South Wales, which Captain King referred to in 1827 as "the Port Jackson term." Contrary to some beliefs, the weapon is not onomatopoeic, as it whistles in the air rather than "booming." Additionally, it has been erroneously linked to the *womera*, or spear-thrower.

## Types and Characteristics
There are two primary categories of boomerangs: the return boomerang and the non-return, or war, boomerang. Both types are found throughout most parts of Australia. In general, both are sickle-shaped and constructed from wood, though in India they may be made of steel or ivory. Their dimensions typically follow a ratio where the thickness is approximately 1/6th of the breadth, and the breadth is 1/12th of the length. Lengths vary from 6 inches to 3 or 4 feet.

The return boomerang generally weighs about 8 oz. and measures 2 to 3 feet in length. While it may have two straight arms at an angle between 70 and 120 degrees, in Australia it is always curved at an angle of 90 degrees or more. Its cross-section is asymmetrical, featuring a convex upper side and a flat or nearly flat lower side. The arms are twisted 2 or 3 degrees from the center plane, and the ends are raised above the plane of the weapon at the center. This specific design requires the weapon to be thrown with the right hand. The non-return boomerang is similar but possesses a skew in the opposite direction.

## Utility and Global Distribution
The war boomerang is described as a deadly weapon when used by an expert, while the lighter hunting boomerang is also effective. The return boomerang is primarily used for killing birds or as a plaything; however, it can be as dangerous to the person throwing it as it is to the target.

Beyond Australia, various forms of the weapon exist:
*   **North-East Africa:** A weapon closely resembling the boomerang survives here, from which metal throwing knives evolved in allied forms.
*   **Ancient Egypt:** According to General Pitt-Rivers, the return form of the boomerang was used in ancient Egypt.
*   **South India:** A boomerang-shaped instrument capable of returning is found among the Dravidians.
*   **Arizona:** The Hopis (Moquis) utilize a non-return form.

Despite these similarities, it remains uncertain if the instruments found in India and Egypt possess a real resemblance to the Australian return boomerang.

## Contributions of General Pitt-Rivers
Major-General H. L. F. Pitt-Rivers (also referred to as Lane Fox) has contributed research on primitive warfare and archaeology. His work on the boomerang is cited in *Anthropological and Archaeological Fragments* under "Primitive Warfare," as well as in the *Catalogue of Bethnal Green Collection* and the *Journ. Royal United Service Inst.*

Beyond weaponry, Pitt-Rivers conducted archaeological studies on early dwellings. In 1877, he described remains of British dwellings found near Lewes in *Archaeologia*, noting they were constructed of osier work (wicker-work) plastered with clay. By comparing these excavated remains with a modern hamper, Pitt-Rivers determined that the construction method was identical.
