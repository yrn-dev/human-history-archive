# AZIMUTH

Azimuth is an astronomical term derived from Arabic. In the field of astronomy, it is defined as the angular distance measured from the north or south point of the horizon to the foot of the vertical circle that passes through a heavenly body. When applied to a horizontal line, the azimuth represents the line's deviation from the north or south direction.

## Role in Coordinate Systems
Within the apparent system of astronomical coordinates, azimuth serves as the longitudinal coordinate. It is paired with the latitudinal coordinate, which is known as the altitude or zenith distance. This differs from other systems, such as the equatorial system (which uses declination and right ascension) or the ecliptic system (which uses latitude and longitude).

## Instrumentation and Observation
The measurement of azimuth is facilitated by various astronomical instruments. The azimuth circle, along with the altitude circle and the modern equatorial, are considered astrolabes in the strictest and oldest sense of the term. Historically, these instruments were evolved from earlier forms such as "armillae" and spherical astrolabes, with Hipparchus playing a significant role in their development.

In the history of observatory equipment, azimuth instruments were utilized by various astronomers. Nasir ud-din (1201-1274), who directed an observatory established by Hulagu Khan at Maraga in Persia, equipped his facility with altitude and azimuth instruments in addition to a mural quadrant with a 12 ft. radius.

## Application in Cartography and Projections
Azimuth is also utilized in the mathematical projection of spherical surfaces, such as the earth, onto a plane. In these applications, any point on a sphere can be selected as the center. Spherical trigonometry is then used to calculate the distance and azimuth of the points where meridians and parallels intersect relative to that center. In the resulting representation, the azimuth is represented unaltered.

A specific example of this is the Zenithal Equal-area Projection applied to Africa, with the central point located on the equator. In this system, if $\phi$ and $\alpha$ represent the latitude and longitude of a point relative to the center, the azimuth ($A$) is determined by the formula: $\tan A = \sin \alpha \cot \phi$. On a physical map, a line is drawn from the center at this azimuth $A$. In such systems, any exaggeration of angle, area, or linear scale is a function of the distance from the center only and remains the same regardless of the azimuth.

## Related Astronomical Concepts
The determination of azimuth relies on the concept of the horizon. In astronomy, the horizon is the great circle of the sphere whose plane is at right angles to the direction of the plumb line. While a distinction is sometimes made between the apparent horizon (the circle where sky and earth seem to meet) and the rational horizon (determined by a plane through the earth's center parallel to the observer's station), these two planes are coincident on the celestial sphere.
