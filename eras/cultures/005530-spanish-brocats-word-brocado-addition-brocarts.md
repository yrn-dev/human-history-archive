# Brocades and the Terminology of Brocading

## Etymology and Definition
The term "brocade" is derived from the Latin word *broccus*, which implies a form of broaching or stitching. This Latin root is equally related to the French terms *brocarts* and *brocher*, the Italian *brocato*, and the Spanish *brocar*. Consequently, textile fabrics that are woven to create an appearance of broaching or stitching are termed brocades.

## Spanish and French Terminology
In Spanish, the word *brocado* is used contemporaneously with the term *brocats*. Historical records from Spain illustrate a specific distinction between different types of luxury fabrics; for example, a Spanish document dated 1375 distinguishes between *brocats d'or é d'argent* (brocades of gold and silver) and *los draps d'or é d'argent o de seda* (cloths of gold, silver, or silk). 

Similarly, in French, the word *brocarts* is used, though the French also employ the term *brocher* in relation to certain silk stuffs. However, these *brocher* silks are not considered brocades in the same sense as *brocarts*.

## Technical Distinctions in Weaving
The distinction between a brocade and other enriched fabrics can be seen by comparing Lyons silks, which are *brochés* with materials such as silk, gold threads, or other substances, against Indian kincobs or cloths of gold. While many Persian silk-weavings and Indian kincobs are often referred to as brocades, they are frequently so named even when the ornamentation is not actually *broché* or brocaded and lacks floating threads.

The application of these techniques is also found in other luxury materials. For instance, a wardrobe account from 1480 for King Edward IV mentions "satyn broched with gold," describing an enriched satin.

## Historical Origins and Examples
The early origins of brocading can be traced to oriental weavers, particularly those in Syria, Persia, and regions of northern Africa and southern Europe under Saracen domination. These weavers produced decorative stuffs partly *brochés* with gold threads several centuries before the late 15th century.

Notable historical examples of this craft include:
* **Siculo-Saracenic Weavings:** A specimen from the 11th or 12th century features pairs of birds and animals where only the heads are broched with gold thread.
* **The Hotel des Tiraz:** In Palermo, this famous center produced a sumptuous weaving using gold threads and coloured silks for an official robe for Henry IV (emperor of the Holy Roman Empire from 1165-1197), which is currently preserved in the cathedral of Regensburg.
* **Other Varieties:** Further textiles classified as *brocat* date from the 12th or 13th century.
