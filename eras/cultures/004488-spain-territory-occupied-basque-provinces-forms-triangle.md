# Basque Provinces

The Basque Provinces (_Provincias Vascongadas_) are a division of north-eastern Spain consisting of three provinces: Alava, Guipuzcoa, and Biscay (also known as Vizcaya). As of 1900, the region had a population of 603,596 and an area of 2,739 square miles, making it the third most densely populated area in Spain.

## Geography and Territory
The territory of the Basque Provinces is shaped like a triangle. Its boundaries are defined by the provinces of Logrono to the east, and Santander and Burgos to the west and south. To the north, the region is bounded by France and the Bay of Biscay. 

Of the three provinces, Guipuzcoa is the only one that is entirely Basque, while Alava is the least so. The capital of Alava, Vitoria, is said to have been founded in 581 by the Gothic king Leovigild. In the province of Biscay, the landscape is predominantly mountainous, traversed by the Cantabrian chain and characterized by narrow valleys and small plains. The coastline of Biscay, stretching from Ondarroa to just east of Castro Urdiales, is rugged and deeply indented. The primary river in Biscay is the Nervion (also called Ansa or Ibaizabal), upon which the city of Bilbao is located.

## Governance and the Fueros
Historically, the Basque Provinces maintained a strong habit of self-government, practicing administrative, judicial, and legislative functions. This resulted in the _fueros_ (or _fors_), a code of written or unwritten laws for each province, as well as _cartas-pueblos_ for the towns. While these charters often appeared as grants from a sovereign, they were actually codifications of ancient customary laws.

The Basque municipal institutions utilized the old Roman term _respublica_ for the _civitas_ and its territory. Municipal officers were elective, employing various methods ranging from nomination by a person chosen by lot to universal suffrage. Municipalities sent deputies to provincial juntas or parliaments. These assemblies originally met in the open air; notable examples include the Bilzaar in the French Pays Basque and the oak of Guernica in Biscay.

The provinces operated as a "land apart" (_una tierra apartada_) and a self-governing republic (_una verdadera autonomia_) while remaining loyal to the absolute monarchy. The kings of Spain, Castile, and Navarre were required to swear to observe the _fueros_ upon their accession. Royal orders or decrees of the Cortes held no force in the Provinces unless they were countersigned by the juntas. To symbolize their unity, the provinces used a seal engraved with the word _Iruracbat_, meaning "The Three One." This autonomy lasted until 1833, following the death of Ferdinand VII.

## History and Integration
The provinces were historically dependent allies of either Castile or Navarre. Guipuzcoa was definitively united to Castile in 1200. Alava was united to the crown of Castile in 1332 via the confederation of Arriaga. In Biscay, the counts of Haro served as lords from 1093 to 1350, and the province achieved definitive union with Castile in 1370.

Between 1425 and 1498, the region was desolated by party wars among lesser nobles (_parientes mayores_). These conflicts ended when Ferdinand the Catholic and Henry IV forbade the construction of fortified houses and strengthened the power of the towns. Despite their union with the Spanish crown, the provinces maintained a degree of international independence, signing their own treaties with France and England. For example, in 1482, Guipuzcoa signed a ten-year treaty of friendship and free trade in Westminster. The Basques are also mentioned separately from Spain in the 1713 treaty of Utrecht.

## Economy and Industry
The primary industries of the Basque Provinces are iron mining and sea fisheries. Some mines near Bilbao have been operational since prehistoric times. In 1905, the Basque Provinces produced 5,302,344 tons of iron—the vast majority of which came from Biscay—out of Spain's total production of 9,395,314 tons. More than half of this iron (5,845,895 tons) was exported to England.

The region is also known for specialized manufacturing. Eibar has long served as a small-arms factory, and the swords of Mondragon in Guipuzcoa were renowned even before those of Toledo. In the 19th century, Senor Zuloaga revived the art of inlaying gold and silver into iron and steel in Eibar.

In agriculture, the region was once a major producer of cider, though wine-growing gradually replaced this during the 19th century. In Biscay, the mountainous slopes are dotted with traditional farm-houses called _caserios_, where peasants work under the _metayer_ system, sharing soil profits with absentee landlords. Due to the decline of village industries caused by machinery, many Basques emigrated to agricultural regions in the Americas, particularly the La Plata region of South America.

## Ethnology and Language
The Basques call themselves _Eskualdunak_ ("those who possess the _Eskuara_") and their country Eskual-Herria. Their language, Eskuara, is an agglutinative and polysynthetic system that remains isolated from other European tongues, though it bears grammatical similarities to Finnic and Magyar languages.

The origin of the people is linked to the _Vascones_. Ancient boundaries of the dioceses of Calahorra, Bayonne, and Pamplona likely corresponded to the territories of ancient tribes: the Vascones, Varduli, Caristi, and Autrigones. William von Humboldt proposed that the Basque language is a remnant of the Iberian language, which may have once spread across the south of France, Italy, and the islands of Sardinia, Sicily, and Corsica.
