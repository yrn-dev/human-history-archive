# Brocades and Related Textiles

## Etymology and Definition
The term "brocade" is derived from the Latin word *broccus*. This root is equally related to the Italian *brocato*, the Spanish *brocar*, and the French *brocarts* and *brocher*. The term implies a form of broaching or stitching. Consequently, textile fabrics that are woven to create an appearance of broaching or stitching have come to be known as brocades.

## Distinctions in Textile Types
Historical documentation reveals specific distinctions between different types of enriched fabrics. A Spanish document from 1375 differentiates between *brocats d'or é d'argent* (gold and silver brocats) and *los draps d'or é d'argent o de seda* (cloths of gold, silver, or silk). This distinction is evident when comparing Lyons silks, which are *brochés* with materials such as silk, gold threads, or other substances, against Indian kincobs or cloths of gold.

In Spain, the word *brocado* was used contemporaneously with *brocats*. In France, the term *brocher* is utilized in connection with certain silk stuffs; however, these are not considered brocades in the same sense as *brocarts*. An example of this distinction appears in a 1480 wardrobe account of King Edward IV, which lists "satyn broched with gold."

It is noted that some Persian silk-weavings and Indian kincobs are frequently referred to as brocades despite the fact that they lack floating threads and the ornamentation is not actually brocaded or *broché*.

## Historical Origins and Production
The early origins of brocading can be traced to oriental weavers, particularly those in Syria, Persia, and regions of northern Africa and southern Europe under Saracen domination. These weavers produced decorative stuffs partly *brochés* with gold threads several centuries prior to the 15th century.

Examples of this craftsmanship include:
* **Siculo-Saracenic works:** A specimen from the 11th or 12th century features pairs of birds and animals where only the heads are broched with gold thread.
* **The Hotel des Tiraz:** Located in Palermo, this famous center produced a sumptuous weaving of gold threads and coloured silks for an official robe for Henry IV (emperor of the Holy Roman Empire between 1165 and 1197), which is preserved in the cathedral of Regensburg.
* **Other varieties:** Further textiles classed as *brocat* date from the 12th or 13th century.
