# Transitions in Belief, Language, and Nature

The concept of transition appears across various disciplines of human history, manifesting as the evolution of religious thought, the gradual shift in linguistic structures, the movement of ecclesiastical authority, and the physical changes in the natural world.

## Evolution of the Baal Deities
In the development of early beliefs, a transition occurred regarding the nature of the "Baal." Initially, local gods were associated with earthly objects, specifically the springs and streams that fertilized fields. Over time, these local deities became identified with the common source of all streams. This progression allowed numerous baals to eventually be viewed as different forms of a single absolute deity.

Consequently, the Baal became identified with supreme powers of nature, such as the sun, the heavens, a planet, or the weather. While the specific path of this development varied by location, the shift from earthly associations to heavenly or astral beings is considered characteristic of a higher type of belief and occurred relatively later. This theory of higher development explains why ruder conceptions of nature were often retained in popular or unofficial cults, whereas the opposing theory—that Baal was originally a sky-god—fails to explain the local character of the many baals.

## Linguistic Transitions in English
The chronology of the English language is marked by gradual linguistic changes that occurred at different rates across the country. The north generally advanced in these changes before the midland, while the south was the slowest to change. Because these transitions were gradual, drawing precise lines between stages is often a matter of opinion.

Based on the midland dialect, from which literary English primarily descends, the approximate stages of transition are as follows:
*   **Old English (Anglo-Saxon):** Up to 1100.
*   **Transition Old English ("Semi-Saxon"):** 1100 to 1150.
*   **Early Middle English:** 1150 to 1250.
*   **Normal Middle English:** 1250 to 1400.
*   **Late and Transition Middle English:** 1400 to 1485.
*   **Early Modern or Tudor English:** 1485 to 1611.
*   **Seventeenth Century Transition:** 1611 to 1688.
*   **Modern English:** 1689 onward.

## Ecclesiastical and Philosophical Transitions
In the early Christian era, a transition occurred regarding church leadership. Following the apostolic age and the movement that began on the Day of Pentecost, the "charismatic" ministry of prophets and apostles began to subside. The *Didache* marks the moment of transition where these roles gave way to permanent local officials, specifically deacons, presbyters, and bishops.

In philosophy, Spinoza defined joy and pain through the lens of transition. He described joy as the "transition from less to greater perfection" and pain as the "transition from greater to less perfection." Spinoza rejected asceticism, viewing it as a *torva el tristis superstitio*, and argued that progress toward perfection and participation in the divine nature is marked by greater joy.

## Natural and Biological Transitions
Transitions are also observed in the physical environment and biological forms. In Bulgaria, the climate is characterized by rapid transitions of temperature, with extreme heat in the summer and intense cold in the winter.

In botany, specifically within the *Jungermanniaceae Anacrogynae* group found in Britain, the species *Blasia pusilla*—which commonly grows by streams and ditches—serves as a transition to foliose types. This is evidenced by its thin marginal lobes of limited growth, which are comparable to the more definite leaves found in other anacrogynous forms.

## Localized Divine Associations
In Greek retrospective faith, the transition from nature to divinity was often expressed through genealogy. The Greeks sought an unbroken line of filiation between themselves and the gods. This was achieved by embodying local names—such as springs, mountains, lakes, and rivers—as persons. These eponymous personages were sometimes viewed as indigenous men sprung from the divinized earth or as the sons of local gods.

Similarly, in the myths of Tegyra, the birth of Apollo is associated with local geography. While some legends suggest the goddess was delivered between two trees, others associate the birth with two springs known as "The Olive" and "The Palm."
