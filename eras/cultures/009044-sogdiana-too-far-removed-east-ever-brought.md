# Sogdiana

Sogdiana was an ancient region of Central Asia, known to the ancients as the area surrounding Bokhara. Due to its extreme eastern location, it was too far removed to ever be brought under the dominion of Rome. The region is characterized by its involvement in the various bloody revolutions of Asia and its location relative to the Oxus and Jaxartes rivers.

## Ancient History and Early Conquests
The foundation of the capital of Sogdiana is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana became part of the empire of the Seleucidae, sharing a similar fate to the neighboring region of Bactria. While Alexander conquered Bactria with relative ease, he encountered strong resistance in Sogdiana, which lay further north, beyond the Oxus.

Later, the nomad Yue-chi migrated from the east into the valley of the Oxus, eventually establishing themselves as a settled territorial power in both Sogdiana and Bactria. Around A.D. 45, the dominions of the Yue-chi king, Kadphises I, extended from Bokhara to the Indus. This territory was subsequently reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty.

## Graeco-Bactrian Influence and Early Middle Ages
During the Seleucid period, Diodotus, the satrap of Bactria, took advantage of internal difficulties and attacks by Ptolemy II to declare independence around 255 B.C. Diodotus subsequently conquered Sogdiana, founding the Graeco-Bactrian kingdom. This Greek influence persisted for a time, though the empire was eventually undermined by internal dissensions, usurpations, and wars between rival kings.

By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand. Following the Arab conquest of Persia between 640 and 642, Islam was introduced to the region and rapidly became the dominant faith.

## The Era of Sughd and Transoxiana
In the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the district—then called Sughd—was one of the most celebrated and flourishing regions of Central Asia. It was also known during the early middle ages as Ma wara 'l Nahr (or Ma-vera-un-nahr), a term translated as Transoxiana. 

The region contained two primary cities: Samarkand and Bokhara. Samarkand generally served as the seat of government, while Bokhara earned a high reputation as a center of learning and religion. At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah, the third of the Persian Seljuk dynasty, crossed the Oxus and subdued the entire country watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus.

## Mongol and Timurid Rule
In 1216, Mahommed Shah Khwarizm subdued Bokhara, but this conquest was seized by Jenghiz Khan in 1220. Although the region was wasted by the fury of Jenghiz Khan, it recovered some prosperity under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent and humane.

The House of Chaghatai eventually settled in the valley of the Jaxartes and upon the pastures of the Ili, ruling the wealthy cities of Sogdiana. During the reign of Kúblái, Kaidu—the grandson of Okkodai—refused to acknowledge the transfer of supreme authority to the House of Tuli. Kaidu exercised immediate authority over Southern Central Siberia and parts of Eastern Turkestan, and he brought the Khans of Chaghatai under his influence, with whom he generally acted in concert.

The Mongol descendants maintained possession of the region until 1369, when Timur (also known as Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand.

## External Relations
Sogdiana maintained connections with distant powers. Fergana, a province of Sogdiana, engaged in political transactions with Vou-ti, the first emperor of the seventh dynasty of China.
