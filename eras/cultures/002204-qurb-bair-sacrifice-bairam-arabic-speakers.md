# Qurban Bairam

Qurban Bairam is one of the two principal festivals of Islam. The term "Bairam" is a Perso-Turkish word meaning "festival." While Turkish speakers refer to this specific occasion as Qurban Bairam, which translates to "Sacrifice Bairam," Arabic speakers refer to it as *Al-'id al-kabir* ("Greater Festival") or *'id al-adha* ("Festival of Sacrifice").

## Calendar and Timing
Qurban Bairam is the "Greater Festival" of the Islamic lunar calendar. It begins on the tenth day of the final month of the year, *Dhul-hijja*, and continues for two or three following days. Because it follows a lunar calendar, the date of the festival moves through the solar year every thirty-two years.

## Observances and Rituals
The festival is characterized by the act of sacrifice. In the valley of Mina, pilgrims slay a camel, a cow, a he-goat, or a ram to commemorate the ransom of Ishmael with a ram. This practice is mirrored throughout the wider Muslim world; those who have the financial means to do so sacrifice a legal animal. The resulting flesh is either consumed by the practitioners themselves or given to the poor.

Aside from the central act of sacrifice, the festival is celebrated in a manner similar to the "Lesser Festival" (*Kutshuk Bairam* or *'id al-Fitr*), though it is noted to be observed with less ardour. General celebrations include:
* The wearing of new clothes.
* Paying private visits.
* Holding official receptions.
* Giving presents.
* Congratulating friends.
* Visiting the graves of relatives.

## Comparison with the Lesser Festival
Qurban Bairam is distinguished from the "Lesser Festival" (*Kutshuk Bairam*), which is also known as *Sheker Bairam* ("Sugar Bairam") or *'id al-Fitr* ("Festival of Fast-breaking"). The Lesser Festival occurs on the first three days of the tenth month, *Shawwal*, immediately following the fasting-month of *Ramadan*. While the Lesser Festival exhibits more outward signs of rejoicing, Qurban Bairam is technically designated as the "Greater Festival."
