# Forms and Laws in Human History

The concepts of "forms" and "laws" appear across various historical, philosophical, and political contexts, ranging from the definition of physical nature and the organization of government to the linguistic classification of peoples.

## Philosophical Definitions of Form
In certain philosophical contexts, "forms" are defined not as abstract entities, but as the laws and determinations of absolute actuality. These forms govern and constitute simple natures—such as weight, light, and heat—within any matter or subject susceptible to them. In this framework, the form of light or heat is identical to the law of light or heat.

While individual bodies in nature exist and perform pure individual acts according to fixed laws, the investigation and explanation of these laws serve as the foundation for both operation and knowledge. Consequently, "forms" are described as laws of pure act or "true differences of things." Some perspectives suggest that forms are merely figments of the human mind unless they are understood specifically as laws of action. Therefore, the primary object of attention should be matter, its configurations, changes of configuration, and the laws of motion or action.

## Legal and Political Structures
Laws have historically been used to organize societies, manage resources, and define the limits of power.

### Governance and Social Order
In the history of the Bani Israel, Moses set down the form of their government and the laws they were to obey in writing. These laws, which were suggested to Moses by God, were intended to ensure that the people lived in a way that pleased God and prevented quarrels among one another.

In Japan, laws governed the appointment to office and promotion. While the law stated that these should depend on knowledge and capacity rather than rank, the system was not liberal in practice. Admission to the university—which categorized graduates into those of eminent talent (*shusai*), those with extensive knowledge of Chinese classics (*meikei*), and those advanced in knowledge (*shinshi*)—was restricted to nobles or the descendants of literati.

### Political Conflict and Reform
In Rome, laws were often the center of political struggle. Julius Caesar introduced a law to divide nearly all of Campania among the needy. This measure was opposed by Cato, who continued to speak against the law even as he was being dragged to prison. Later, through grants of land and laws, the people were cajoled into voting Caesar the government of all Gaul and Illyricum with four legions for five years.

In Greece, the role of *Nomothetæ* was suggested as a means to reform the state. The proposal was not to create new laws, but to repeal existing "mischievous" laws, specifically those regarding military service and the Theôric fund. The Theôric fund was criticized for distributing money to those staying at home rather than to soldiers on service.

## Social and Behavioral Norms
Laws and norms also extended to personal conduct and civic virtue. In Sparta, some argued that men of few words required few laws. Other Spartan figures emphasized that courage served as a better defense for a town than brick walls, and that the best man in Sparta was he who was least like those who asked unreasonable queries.

In the Roman Republic, the influence of Greek philosophy caused concern for some, such as Cato, who feared that young men would prioritize fluent speaking and distinction over warlike exploits. He urged the Senate to ensure that Roman youth returned their attention to the magistrates and the laws.

## Linguistic Classification
The term "Aryan" has been used by philologists, notably Max Muller, as a short term for the body of languages known as Indo-Germanic or Indo-European. In this context, "Aryas" refers strictly to those who speak an Aryan language, regardless of their blood, color, hair, or skull characteristics. This definition applies to Greeks, Romans, Germans, Celts, Slaves, and Hindus. Muller explicitly rejected ethnological significations, asserting that calling someone an Arya predicates nothing about them except the grammar of their language.

## Sacred Guidance on Marriage and Divorce
The Qur'an provides guidance on the laws regarding marriage and divorce. It states there is no blame on those who speak indirectly when asking for women in marriage or keep the proposal concealed. However, it commands that promises not be given in secret unless spoken in a lawful manner, and that the marriage tie not be confirmed until the writing is fulfilled.

Regarding divorce, the text teaches that there is no sin or blame in divorcing women before consummation or the fixation of their dower. In such cases, it is a bounden duty for those who do good to provide for the women; the wealthy should provide according to their means, and the poor or straitened in circumstances should do the same, ensuring a fair provision according to usage.
