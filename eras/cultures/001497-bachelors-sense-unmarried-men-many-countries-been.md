# Bachelors

The term "bachelor" is used in a general sense to describe a young man, though it has historically possessed several distinct applications and meanings. Etymologically, the word is derived from the Medieval Latin *baccalarius* (with the rare variant *baccalaris*), passing through the Old French *bacheler*. The term *baccalarius* originally referred to the tenant of a *baccalaria*—presumably a grazing farm—which was similar to a *vaselleria*, or the fief of a sub-vassal. By the 8th century, the term was applied to *rustici* (men or women) who worked for the tenant of a *mansus*. Throughout its various evolutions, the word has maintained a core idea of subordination.

## Legal and Penal Restrictions
In various countries and historical periods, unmarried men have been subjected to penal laws and financial burdens.

### Ancient Greece and Rome
In Sparta, citizens who remained unmarried after a specific age faced several penalties. They were forbidden from witnessing the gymnastic exercises of maidens and were forced during the winter to march naked around the market-place. During these marches, they sang songs composed against themselves that expressed the justice of their punishment. Furthermore, bachelors were not afforded the usual respect that the young typically paid to the old. While Athens lacked definite legislation on the matter, certain minor laws reflected a spirit similar to the Spartan doctrine.

In Rome, the first clearly known law regarding this matter was the Lex Julia, passed around 18 B.C. This law, which was later incorporated with the Lex Papia et Poppaea in A.D. 9, imposed heavy penalties on unmarried persons and restricted marriages between different social classes. Conversely, the law provided privileges to citizens with several children and lighter penalties for married persons who were childless. Additionally, the Roman censor Camillus once used arguments and the threat of fines to persuade unmarried citizens to marry widows, whose numbers had increased due to war.

### Middle Ages and Britain
Isolated penalties for bachelors occurred during the middle ages. For example, a 1223 charter of liberties granted by Matilda I, countess of Nevers, to Auxerre imposed an annual tax of five *solidi* on any man who was a bachelor and did not have a wife. 

In Britain, there was no direct legislation targeting bachelors, but taxes were occasionally structured to weigh more heavily on them. Examples include the act passed in 1695 (6 and 7 Will. III.), the tax on servants in 1785, and the income tax of 1798.

## Professional and Social Classifications
Beyond marital status, the term "bachelor" has been applied to specific subordinate roles in religious, military, and academic contexts.

### Ecclesiastical and Academic Use
In the church, the term was used for ecclesiastics of an inferior grade, such as recently appointed canons or young monks. In academia, the term *baccalarius* or *baccalaureus* appeared at the university of Paris in the 13th century under the system of degrees established by Pope Gregory IX. It referred to scholars *in statu pupillari* who held a preliminary degree, which allowed them to proceed toward the degree of master (*magister*), the only rank entitled to teach.

### Military and Knighthood
In the context of knighthood, "knights bachelors" represented the lowest stage of knighthood. This category included knights who were too young to handle the dignity and responsibility of knights bannerets, or poor vassals who lacked the means to take the field under their own banner. 

There is a historical connection between knights bachelors and the Knights of the Bath. While the "most honourable" Order of the Bath was established by George I in 1725 (claiming to revive an order from Henry IV in 1399), Knights of the Bath were essentially knights bachelors who were created with more elaborate ceremonies on great occasions. Although they were allowed precedence over knights bachelors, they remained knights bachelors in essence.

## Cultural Variations
Different cultures have maintained distinct customs regarding unmarried individuals. Among the Bari, a Nilotic tribe living on the upper Nile, unmarried girls and men go practically naked. This contrasts with married women, who wear goatskins dyed red.
