# The Suliman Range

The central range of the Suliman hills is the dominant geographical feature of northern Baluchistan. Geologically, the range is composed of one or more great folds of the Cretaceous series, with its central axis situated slightly east of the 70° E meridian. The system concludes in a mass of twisted flexures and curved wrinkles.

## The Takht-i-Suliman Massif
Towards the northern extremity of the range lies an oblong block or massif known as "Kaisargarh" by the Sherani clansmen who occupy it, and generally as "Takht-i-Suliman" (Solomon's throne) due to a celebrated shrine located near its southern abutment. This massif is a high tableland approximately 8,000 feet above sea level, formed by a cap of coral limestone estimated to be between 4,000 and 5,000 feet thick.

The tableland is bounded by steep, rugged parallel ridges. The western ridge reaches its peak at Kaisargarh, which stands at 11,300 feet. The eastern ridge culminates in a detached headland to the south, where the "zirat" or shrine is located at an elevation of 11,070 feet. The plateau is characterized by a sprinkling of juniper on higher slopes and a fairly thick growth of edible pine, known as chilghosa. This area was first surveyed and ascended in 1883.

The tableland is rent at both ends by gorges that descend to the channels of the Dhana to the south and the Draband (or "Gat") to the north. These channels carry mountain streams from the western slopes across the mountain axis and through minor ridges to the plains of the Indus.

## Regional Geography and Drainage
The Suliman system forms part of the rough highlands that hedge northern Baluchistan between Afghanistan and the Indus plains. In this northern district, the average breadth is 150 miles, expanding to over 200 miles near the railway and narrowing to less than 100 miles along the Gomal. The climate here is clear and bracing, with alpine vegetation and no great extremes of temperature.

Drainage in this region follows two primary patterns:
1. Water runs northwards to the Gomal, passing through uplands west of the Suliman Range.
2. Water gathers in narrow lateral valleys behind the mountains and bursts eastwards through the limestone axis via the shortest transverse route to the Indus.

To the north-west, the curved recession of the ranges creates a space of flat alluvial desert. This inlet receives the drainage of the Nari and Bolan basins, which flow south-eastwards from the central Quetta-Pishin plateau. These water-courses are prone to terrific floods due to rainfall on the flat-backed limestone ridges. They eventually disappear into the Kach Gandava, an alluvial bay extending 130 miles from the Indus to Sibi.

## Geology and Mineral Wealth
The range's geological structure includes folds of the Cretaceous series overlaid by shales and coarse sandstones. To the east, facing the Indus plains, is a higher, more rugged ridge of hills marking the Siwaliks. These hills contain evidence of a geological past where gigantic mammals, similar to those of the Himalaya forests, once roamed.

Mineral wealth in the region is considered inconsiderable. However, petroleum of excellent quality has been found in the Sherani hills and likely exists in other parts of the Suliman Range. Coal has been extracted from Tertiary beds along the Harnai route to Quetta, though the quality is poor and seams are thin.

## Human Geography and Administration
The central ranges of the Suliman system are the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander. Specifically, the Mando Khel of Zhob, as well as the Dumars and Kakars of Pishin, are prominent tribal divisions among the Pathans. The term "Bar Durani" is sometimes used to describe the independent Pathan tribes inhabiting the Suliman range and other hill districts south of the Hindu Kush.

In contrast, the twisted flexures at the end of the Suliman system are occupied by true Baluchis, specifically the Bugti and Marri sections of the Rind confederation, who claim Arabic origin. The Bozdars, another Rind clan, also reside north of them.

Strategically, the British government has maintained order among the "ever-restless" Pathan tribes of the Suliman hills through the occupation of the Zhob valley. A line of posts along the Zhob river flanks the range almost from end to end on the west, providing command of the hills and controlling communication with Afghanistan.
