# Persian Musical Instruments

The musical traditions of Persia have utilized a diverse array of string, wind, and percussion instruments, many of which have influenced nomenclature and instrument design across different cultures and eras.

## Stringed Instruments
Persian string instruments include various forms of lutes and harps. The *barbat* was a variety of *rebab*, serving as a bass instrument. It differed from the *rebab* primarily in its size and the number of strings it possessed. In the nomenclature of Persians and Arabs, slight deviations in an instrument's construction often necessitated a new name. The term *barbud*, applied to the barbiton, is said to be derived from a famous musician who lived during the time of Chosroes II (A.D. 590-628) and excelled in playing the instrument.

Historical references translated into German describe guests at a feast holding several instruments: the *Barbiton* (*barbut*), *Leyer* (*chang*), *Laute* (*rubab*), and *Floete* (*nei*). The *barbut* and *rubab* remained distinct instruments in Persia as late as the 19th century. Before the bow was applied to the pear-shaped *rebab*, there were few differences between it and the lute; both featured gut strings plucked by the fingers, vaulted backs, and a construction where the body and neck were one.

## Percussion Instruments
Persian percussion has had a notable influence on the terminology of other languages. The Persian word *tambal* is the source of the Spanish *attambal* (or *attabal*) and the modern French *timbales*. 

Additionally, the German word for kettledrum, *Pauke* (Middle High German *Bûke* or *Pûke*), is mentioned alongside other Eastern-derived terms, though it is noted as probably being derived from *byk*, the Assyrian name for the instrument.

## Influence and Comparisons
The development of musical instruments in the East often shares characteristics with those found in other regions. For example, the *santir* is compared to the German *hackbrett*. Similarly, the *barbat* is linked to the *rebab* and the lute. In the broader context of Eastern instruments, the *pisantir* (translated as "psaltery") appears in bas-reliefs from Kuyunjik in the British Museum, depicting ten-stringed dulcimers played with long sticks curved at the ends while the strings were damped by hand.
