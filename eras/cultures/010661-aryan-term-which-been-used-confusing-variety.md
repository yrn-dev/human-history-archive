# ARYAN

The term "Aryan" has been utilized by various philologists in a variety of confusing significations. In a broad sense, it has been employed as a convenient short term for the entire body of languages more commonly known as Indo-Germanic or Indo-European. In a narrower sense, the term is sometimes restricted specifically to Indo-Iranian speech.

## Linguistic Definitions and Characteristics
Max Muller used "Aryan" to describe the language group and "Aryas" to refer to the speakers of those languages. Muller explicitly avoided ethnological definitions, asserting that "Aryas" are those who speak Aryan languages regardless of their blood, color, hair, or skull. According to Muller, the term predicates nothing about the speaker other than the grammar of their language; thus, even the "blackest Hindus" may represent an earlier stage of Aryan speech and thought than the "fairest Scandinavians."

The Aryan group is distinguishable from other Indo-European languages by four specific characteristics:
1. The confusion of original long and short *e* and *o* with the original long and short *a* sound.
2. The representation of the short schwa-sound by *i* (e.g., *pita* for "father").
3. The transformation of original *s* into *s* after *i*, *u*, and certain consonants.
4. A genitive plural suffix *-nam* for stems ending in a vowel, borrowed by analogy from stems ending in *-n*.

Further distinctions exist between Sanskrit and Iranian. The Aryan voiced aspirates (*gh, dh, bh*) found in Sanskrit are confused with original *g, d, b* in Iranian. Additionally, Aryan *s* becomes *h* initially before vowels in Iranian, similar to Greek.

## Origins and Migrations
It is suggested that 6,000 years ago, during the Neolithic Age, a single original speech existed from which all Aryan languages differentiated. These tribes, sometimes called "Aryan Russians" by Sir H. H. Johnston, likely wandered between western Asia and central Europe. While they were mostly of the Caucasian group—specifically the blond, northern "Nordic" race—it is noted that a common language indicates common intercourse and intermixture rather than a strictly common biological origin.

These peoples were originally forest and cattle people rather than steppe or horse people. However, as Aryan tribes spread north and east of the Black and Caspian Seas, they encountered Mongolian peoples of the Ural-Altaic linguistic group. From these Mongolian races, the Aryans appear to have acquired the use of the horse for warfare and riding.

The movement of these nomadic peoples was characterized by annual swings—moving north in summer and south in winter—covering hundreds of miles. This migration pattern included tribes such as the Cimmerians, Sarmatians, and Scythians, the latter of whom, along with the Medes and Persians, contacted the Assyrian Empire by 1000 B.C. or earlier.

## Diversification of Peoples and Languages
As the primitive Aryan speech spread, it broke into various daughter languages:
* **Westward:** Nordic Aryan peoples pressed into the Italian and Greek peninsulas, developing the Latin and Greek groups. Others drifted toward the Baltic and Scandinavia, leading to ancient Norse (the parent of Icelandic, Norwegian, Danish, and Swedish), Gothic, and High and Low German. The Kelts developed the Keltic language, which further differentiated into Irish Gaelic, Scotch, Welsh, Breton, and the language of Gaul.
* **Eastward:** North of the Black Sea and Carpathians, the Slavonian dialect emerged, leading to Polish, Russian, Bulgarian, and Serbian. In Asia Minor and Persia, the Indo-Iranian branch developed, eventually splitting into Persian and Sanscrit.

Between 3000 and 1000 B.C., fair-complexioned Aryan speakers using Sanscrit pushed into India, conquering the dark Dravidian peoples. Other Aryan tribes between the Danube and the Medes and Persians may have assimilated the pre-Aryan Hittite civilization.

## Early Social and Cultural Life
Early Aryans, such as the Greeks described in the *Iliad*, were sturdy but undisciplined fighters. They utilized the horse to pull rude fighting chariots into battle, though oxen were still used for ordinary draught purposes. 

Their social structure was based on families led by chiefs who performed sacrifices. Their religion lacked deep sacramental feeling or mystery, with priests serving primarily as keepers of sacred places. Political organization was loose; elders and chiefs met in council to appoint a king with loosely defined powers. These societies operated on customs rather than formal laws or exact standards of conduct.
