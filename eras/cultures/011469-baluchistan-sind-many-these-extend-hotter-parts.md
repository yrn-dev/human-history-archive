# Baluchistan and Sind

Baluchistan and Sind are regions located within the borders of British India, characterized by extreme heat and aridity. Together with southern Persia and Arabia, they form an almost unbroken desert tract. This region is noted for a considerable uniformity in its types of life, which closely resemble those found in the neighboring hot and dry regions of Africa.

## Geography and Administration of Baluchistan
Baluchistan extends from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea, and from the borders of Afghanistan and Persia to those of Sind and the Punjab. It is a sparsely populated territory, larger in area than any Indian province except for Bengal, Madras, and Burma. The region is divided into two primary administrative divisions:

*   **British Baluchistan:** A portion of British India covering 9,403 sq. m. Much of this land was ceded by Afghanistan under the treaty of Gandamak in 1879 and formally incorporated into British India in 1887.
*   **Foreign Territories:** These areas are under the superintendence of the chief commissioner acting as agent to the governor-general. This category includes tribal areas (such as those held by the Bugti and Marri tribes, covering 7,129 sq. m.), native states (including Las Bela and Kalat, which encompasses Kharan and Makran), and directly administered districts. The directly administered areas, totaling 36,401 sq. m., include the Bolan Pass and the Zhob and Chagai political agencies.

The landscape is divided by a railway line running from the Indus to Quetta. North of this line lie the rough highlands of the Suliman system, the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander, where the climate is bracing and vegetation is often alpine. South of the railway is a block of territory roughly 300 m. by 300 m., primarily home to the Baluch and Brahui. This area includes the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, where drainage flows east into the plains of Sind, and coastal districts that discharge into the Arabian Sea.

## Climate and Natural History
The climate of the region is defined by extreme dryness and heat. In Upper Sind, the environment resembles the sultry deserts of Africa; temperatures in the shade have been recorded as high as 130 deg. F., and the waters of the Indus can reach "blood heat." In Lower Sind, the mean maximum temperature at Hyderabad during the six hottest months is 98 deg. F. in the shade. Unlike much of India, Sind is largely unaffected by the monsoon rains.

Botanically, the vegetation consists of plants diffused across Africa, Sind, and Baluchistan, with many extending into the hotter parts of India. In the mountainous regions of south-western Arabia (Arabia Felix), the flora is similar to that of Baluchistan, Afghanistan, and southern Persia, featuring aromatic shrubs and trees such as *Celtis*, *Pistacia*, *Dodonaea*, poplars, and the date palm. Prickly forms of *Astragalus* and *Statice* cover the dry hills.

Zoologically, the extreme south-west of the continent—comprising southern Persia, Palestine, Arabia, Sind, and Baluchistan—is classified by Dr. Sclater as part of the Ethiopian region, which also extends over Africa south of the Atlas.

## Ethnography and Population
Baluchistan is inhabited by a variety of races. The underlying population is Persian (Tajik), often appearing as agricultural bondsmen or slaves, such as the Nakibs (including the Durzadas and Dehwars). 

Other notable groups include:
*   **The Rekis:** Nomadic "desert people" found in the Baluchistan half of the desert, most notably the Mohamadani, who are likely of Arab origin.
*   **The Naushirwanis:** A purely Persian race inhabiting the Kharan and Mashkel deserts. They are described as a fine, manly race, identical to the Tahukani of Perso-Baluchistan.
*   **The Rind tribes:** These groups claim extraction from the Koraish and are of Arab origin.

Arab influence is strongly impressed upon the ethnography of the region, as Arabs occupied southern Baluchistan and Seistan early on and later spread through the Sind valley until the 12th century.

## Las Bela and Trade
Las Bela is a small independent state south of Kalat, ruled by a protected chief known as the Jam. Its capital, Bela, is situated on a rocky site above the Purali river. The state consists of fertile, highly irrigated, but malarial alluvial plains. 

Historically, Las Bela served as a vital link in the great trading route between Sind and Persia. This route traverses barren ridges and the Kolwah valley, providing an open road to Kej and Persia. This path was one of the most significant trade routes during the medieval period of Arab ascendancy in Sind.
