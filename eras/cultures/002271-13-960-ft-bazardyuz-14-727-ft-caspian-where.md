# Baku and the Caspian Region

## Geography and Administration
Baku is a government of Russian Transcaucasia situated along the west coast of the Caspian Sea, spanning from 38deg 30' to 41deg 50' N. lat. Its borders are defined by the province of Daghestan and the government of Elisavetpol to the west, and Persia to the south. The government covers an area of 15,172 square miles.

The region's landscape is diverse, featuring the Kuba plain on the north-east slope of the Caucasus. The eastern extremity of the Caucasus range extends from the Bazardyuz (14,727 ft.) and the Shad-dagh (13,960 ft.) to the Caspian Sea, where the range terminates at the Apsheron peninsula. Other geographical features include the steppes of the lower Aras and Kura on the south of the Caucasus, as well as a narrow coast-belt between the Caspian and the Anti-Caucasus. This coastal region partially surrounds the south-opening Kizil-agach Bay.

## Agriculture and Natural Resources
The slopes of the Caucasus are characterized as very fertile and well irrigated, containing fields of cereals, rice, and fine forests, as well as flourishing gardens. While the Kura steppes are also fertile, they require artificial irrigation, particularly for the cultivation of cotton. The region also sees extensive growth of mulberry and vine, alongside cattle-breeding.

In contrast, the Apsheron peninsula is bare of vegetation and dry. However, it contains famous petroleum wells. These wells reach depths between 700 and 1700 feet and produce crude naphtha. From this naphtha, kerosene or petroleum is distilled. The heavier residue, known as *mazut*, is utilized as fuel—such as for the locomotives of the Transcaspian railway—and as lubricating oil.

## The Petroleum Industry
The oil-fields are located around the town of Baku. The largest field is Balakhany-Sabunchi-Romany, covering 6 square miles and located 8-1/2 miles north of the town. Other fields include Bibi-Eybat, situated 3-1/2 miles to the south, and the "black town" (Nobel's), located 2 miles south-east. Beyond these lies the "white town" (Rothschild's).

The industry saw significant growth in output, rising from 5,500 tons of crude naphtha in 1863 to 9,833,600 tons by 1904, although a serious fire in 1905 caused substantial injury to business. Lighter oils are transported via pipes to Batum on the Black Sea for export. Heavier oils are transported in tank railway-cars to the ports of Poti, Novorossiysk, and Batum.

## History and Culture of the Caspian Basin
The Caspian Sea has a history of varied navigation and visitation. In the beginning of the 9th century, Mas’udi reports that a fleet of 500 Russian vessels emerged from the Volga to ravage the western and southern populous shores of the Caspian, an event that shocked a population previously accustomed only to fishermen or peaceful traders. By 1375, Genoese navigation of the Caspian had become familiar, as evidenced by the number of names on maps from that period and accounts of enterprises by Genoese buccaneers.

The Caspian is also noted for its enormous quantity of fish, which is attributed to the mouth of the Volga and the mass of vegetable food in the shallower northern waters. These fisheries have an annual value estimated between three and four millions sterling.

Additionally, the region has been linked to global health events; the Black Death is described as the human form of a disease endemic to small rodents, such as jerboas, in the districts surrounding the head of the Caspian Sea.

In terms of religious sites, a now disused temple of the Parsee fire-worshippers is located at Surakhani, 13 miles east of the town of Baku.
