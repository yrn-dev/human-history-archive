# Festivals of the Christian Church

The Christian Church observes several oecumenical festivals of high solemnity, most notably Christmas, Easter, Pentecost, and the Feast of the Ascension. These celebrations are characterized by specific liturgical practices, historical traditions, and, in some instances, the integration of earlier cultural customs.

## The Feast of the Ascension
The Feast of the Ascension is an oecumenical festival that ranks in solemnity with Christmas, Easter, and Pentecost. It celebrates the ascension of Christ into heaven, which occurred forty days after the resurrection. Consequently, the feast is held forty days after Easter, or ten days before Whitsunday, and always falls on a Thursday, known as Ascension Day or Holy Thursday.

While there is no discoverable trace of the festival before the middle of the 4th century, subsequent records suggest it was established early. St Augustine noted that it had been kept from time immemorial and was likely instituted by the apostles. Historical records from the year 390 indicate that the people of Constantinople celebrated the feast in a suburb of the city as an "old custom." Similarly, Chrysostom mentioned celebrations in the church of Romanesia outside Antioch.

Historically, the festival was associated with professional pilgrimages to commemorate the passing of Christ and his apostles to the Mount of Olives. In the 7th century, such a procession took place at Jerusalem in the church on Mount Olivet. Around A.D. 385, the *Peregrinatio* of Etheria (Silvia) recorded that the festival was held in the Church of the Nativity at Bethlehem. In the West during the Middle Ages, processions involving banners and candles outside the church served as symbols of Christ's triumphant entry into heaven.

In the Anglican Church, Ascension Day and its octave remain a great festival, utilizing a special preface to the consecration prayer in the communion service, similar to the practices for Trinity Sunday, Whitsunday, Easter, and Christmas. The Lutheran churches also retained the celebration, citing Holy Scripture as warrant.

## The Christmas Festival
The Christmas festival is noted for its association with gaiety, as it synchronized with and superseded Teutonic and Latin feasts of the winter solstice. In the Roman tradition, the festival was fixed to December 25th, the *Brumalia* or winter solstice, the date on which Pagans celebrated the birth of the sun. However, the churches of Asia, Egypt, and perhaps Gaul celebrated both the baptism and nativity of the Savior on January 6th, a day Christians refer to as Epiphany.

A distinct tradition associated with Christmas is the "carol" (from the Old French *carole*). Originally, carols were hymns of praise sung in the open air, often modeled on songs accompanying choric dances. In churches, the "crib" of the Saviour served as the center for these dances, and some famous Latin Christmas hymns were written to dance tunes. While the clergy sought to limit these heathen survivals, the practice of carolling—combining song and dance—persisted.

## Other Religious Observances and Customs
Beyond the major festivals, the Christian Church adopted various charitable and liturgical customs:

*   **Doles:** The practice of "doles"—the distribution of food and money in charity—was common. St Chrysostom noted that doles were used at funerals to seek a propitious judge for the soul of the deceased. Some doles were specifically tied to Christmas, such as a 1708 bequest to provide twelve loaves to poor widows at a grave every Christmas, or the tradition at St Peter's, Walworth, where a Christmas dinner is cooked in the church for parish poor aged sixty and over.
*   **Fasting:** Following a rule of Esdras, Christians observed the practice of not fasting on festival days, a rule quoted in the Apostolical Constitutions.
*   **Veneration of Martyrs:** Over time, the respect for martyrs of the faith evolved into religious adoration. The tombs of spiritual heroes, such as St. Peter and St. Paul, became trophies and objects of veneration, with their bones eventually deposited under the altars of Christ.
