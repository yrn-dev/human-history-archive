# Emigration and Solitude in Human History

The experience of solitude and the act of emigration appear across various cultures and historical contexts, ranging from strategic colonial expansions and forced displacements to the personal characteristics of specific ethnic groups.

## The Basque People
The Basque character is defined by an intense self-respect, an obstinate conservatism, and a pride of race. Historically, every Basque freeholder, known as a *vecino*, could prove his nobility, which rendered him eligible for any office under the *fueros* and Spanish law. 

The Basques are not characterized as a town race; their villages typically consist of a few houses, with the population residing in scattered habitations. Because they do not fear solitude, they have proven to be excellent missionaries and emigrants. This trait contributed to their success as splendid seamen and whale fishermen in the Bay of Biscay, as well as their role as the first to establish cod-fishery off the coast of Newfoundland. They participated fully in the colonization of America, leaving a legacy of Basque names among older colonial families and establishing Basque-language newspapers in Los Angeles, California, and Buenos Aires.

## Greek Colonialism and the Founding of Taras
In ancient Greece, emigration was sometimes the result of social disgrace and political instability. The city of Taras (Tarentum) was founded as a Spartan colony around 707 B.C. following the first Messenian war. The emigration was led by an Herakleid named Phalanthus and consisted primarily of the *Partheniæ* (youth who were denied citizenship rights) and the *Epeunaktæ* (citizens degraded to the rank of Helot servitude). 

The *Epeunaktæ* had remained at home while other soldiers were away for many years fighting in Messenia. Upon the return of the soldiers, the children born from intercourse between the returning men's wives and daughters and the *Epeunaktæ* were held in dishonor. This social friction led to a conspiracy led by Phalanthus, who intended to attack authorities during the festival of the Hyacinthia. However, Phalanthus betrayed the plot beforehand to secure the safety of those involved, leading to the eventual emigration to Taras.

## The Migration of the Hungarians
The Hungarians, known nationally as Magiar and identified by the Greeks as Turks, were driven toward the frontiers of the Roman empire by the tide of war and emigration. They were pushed westward by the weight of more distant tribes who were simultaneously fugitives and conquerors. 

Centuries after their initial migration, missionaries from the king of Hungary visited their ancient country near the banks of the Volga. There, they found a people of Pagans and Savages who still bore the name of Hungarians and spoke their native tongue. One of the Hungarian princes attempted to replenish the solitude of Pannonia by establishing a domestic colony from the heart of Tartary, though this design proved fruitless.

## Religious Perspectives on Fear and Conflict
The Qur'an presents various teachings regarding fear, conflict, and the resolution of disputes. In the context of marital breach, the text advises appointing two arbiters—one from the husband's family and one from the wife's—to effect harmony and reconciliation if the parties desire peace. Regarding the roles of spouses, it states that men are the maintainers of women because Allah made some excel others and because men spend out of their property; it describes good women as obedient and guarding the unseen.

The text also describes the response of believers to fear. When told that a great army was gathering against them, some found that the threat only increased their faith, declaring that Allah is sufficient for them and the best Protector. Additionally, the text recounts the story of Firon (Pharaoh), who oppressed the people of the land, slaughtering their sons while letting their women live. In response, Allah desired to show favor to the oppressed, making them heirs and leaders to show Firon, Haman, and their hosts the very things they had feared. In the narrative of Moses, the mother of Moses was inspired to cast her child into the river when she had fears for him, with the assurance that he would be restored to her and made a messenger.

## Solitude as a State of Misery
While some cultures view solitude as a strength, other historical accounts describe it as a symptom of societal collapse. In the kingdom of Herod, a period of madness and mutual distrust saw intimate friends become "wild beasts to one another." This environment of random destruction and the lack of defense or refutation resulted in a "melancholy solitude" that deformed the kingdom, reversing its previously happy state. Herod himself lived in constant fear, unable to trust anyone and imagining his son attacking him with a sword.
