# Broccus and the Origin of Brocades

The Latin word *broccus* (or the adjective *brochus*) is a linguistic root associated with projecting elements, such as teeth, and is related to the Medieval Latin *brocca*. This root has evolved into various terms across several European languages, most notably influencing the terminology of textiles and tools.

## Relation to Textiles and Brocades
The term *broccus* is related equally to the Italian *brocato*, the Spanish *brocar*, and the French *brocher* and *brocarts*. In the context of textiles, these terms imply a form of broaching or stitching. Consequently, fabrics that are woven to create the appearance of stitching or broaching are termed "brocades."

Historical documentation illustrates the specific distinctions made between these materials. A Spanish document from 1375 differentiates between *brocats d'or é d'argent* and *los draps d'or é d'argent o de seda*. This distinction is evident when comparing Lyons silks that are *brochés* (using threads of silk, gold, or other materials) with Indian kincobs or cloths of gold. While many Persian silk-weavings and Indian kincobs are frequently called brocades, they often lack floating threads, meaning the ornamentation is not technically *broché* or brocaded.

In French, the word *brocher* is used for certain silk stuffs, though these are not considered brocades in the same sense as *brocarts*. Similarly, a 1480 wardrobe account for King Edward IV describes "satyn broched with gold."

## Historical Production and Influence
The origins of brocading can be traced back to oriental weavers, particularly those in Syria, Persia, and regions of northern Africa and southern Europe under Saracen domination. Examples of this craftsmanship include:
* **Siculo-Saracenic textiles:** An 11th or 12th-century specimen features pairs of birds and animals where only the heads are broched with gold thread.
* **The Hotel des Tiraz:** Located in Palermo, this center produced a sumptuous weaving of gold threads and coloured silks for an official robe for Henry IV (emperor of the Holy Roman Empire, 1165-1197), which is preserved in the cathedral of Regensburg.

## Relation to Tools and Architecture
Beyond textiles, the root is found in the word "broach" (from the French *broche* and Medieval Latin *brocca*). In this context, it refers to various pointed instruments, including:
* A bodkin.
* A wooden needle used for making tapestry.
* A spit used for roasting meat.
* A "rimer," which is a tool used with a wrench to smooth or enlarge holes.

The act of using such an instrument to tap a cask led to the phrase "to broach" or "tap" a cask.

In English architecture, the term "broach-spire" describes a specific form of spire. This occurs when an octagonal stone spire is placed atop a square tower. To cover the four resulting angles, a stone roof of slight pitch is used; the intersection of this roof with the octagonal faces of the spire is termed the "broach."

## Linguistic Context
The word "brooch" is considered a doublet of "broach," though it has developed a special meaning. These terms exist within a broader framework of European languages. English, French, German, Spanish, Italian, Greek, Russian, Armenian, and Persian belong to the Indo-European (or Aryan) family, sharing fundamental roots and grammatical ideas, often varying according to laws such as Grimm's Law.
