# Borax

Borax, also known as sodium biborate or sodium pyroborate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7$), is a chemical substance that appears in commercial trade in two distinct forms: "common" or prismatic borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and "jewellers'" or octahedral borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$). It should be noted that alchemists used the term "borax" in a vague manner, which does not necessarily correspond to the specific substance known by that name today.

## Mineralogy and Physical Properties
Prismatic borax crystallizes with ten molecules of water, resulting in a composition of $\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 + 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$. These crystals belong to the monoclinic system and closely resemble pyroxene (a silicate of iron, magnesium, and calcium) in their angles and habit. The mineral exhibits a perfect cleavage parallel to the orthopinacoid, with less perfect cleavages parallel to the prism faces. 

Physically, borax is transparent to opaque and can appear white, greenish, bluish, or greyish. It has a specific gravity of 1.69–1.72 and a hardness of 2–2½. Its optical characteristics are notable for a striking crossed dispersion of the optic axes. When viewed through a section cut parallel to the plane of symmetry in convergent polarized light, the optic figure is symmetrical only regarding the central point. Additionally, the plane of the optic axes for blue light is inclined at 2 degrees to that for red light, and the angle between the optic axes is 3 degrees greater for red light than for blue light.

## Natural Deposits and Occurrence
Prismatic borax is widely distributed as a natural product. Historically, Tibetan mineral deposits were known in early times; crude material was exported to Europe as *tincal* to prepare boron salts and pure borax. In Tibet, impure borax (*sohaga*) is found in the lake-plain of Pugha on the Rulangchu (a tributary of the Indus) at an elevation of 15,000 ft., covering approximately 2 square meters and covered by saline efflorescence. Purer material, known as *chu tsale* or water borax, is found at the lakes of Rudok and the great lakes Tengri Nor, north of Lhasa.

Other natural occurrences include:
* **North America:** Canada and California. In California, Borax Lake near Clear Lake contains a large mass of crystallized borax suitable for use by assayers without preliminary purification. Large isolated crystals, some weighing a pound and reaching 7 inches in length, have been found at Borax Lake in both Lake and San Bernardino counties.
* **South America:** Peru and the Atacama desert.
* **Europe:** Transylvania.

Commercial sources of boron compounds have more recently shifted toward extensive borate deposits (primarily calcium borates) in the Atacama desert and the Mohave desert on the borders of Nevada and California. These deposits, often mixtures of gypsum, alkaline salts (chloride, sulphate, sodium carbonate), and borates, likely originated from volcanic solfataras and hot springs, similar to those that charge the lagoons of Tuscany with boric acid.

## Production and Chemical Behavior
Borax is mainly derived from the boric acid of Tuscany. This is processed by fusing the acid in a reverberatory furnace with half its weight of sodium carbonate, after which the mass is extracted with warm water. Alternatively, sodium carbonate is dissolved in steam-heated, lead-lined pans, and boric acid is added gradually until the solution is concentrated enough for the borax to crystallize. It can also be prepared by fusing naturally occurring calcium borate with a requisite quantity of soda ash in a finely divided state, followed by water extraction and concentration.

The deposition of different hydrates depends on temperature:
* **Pentahydrate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$):** Deposited from supersaturated aqueous solutions during evaporation at high temperatures, or when a solution with a specific gravity of 1.246 is cooled to between 79°C and 56°C.
* **Decahydrate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$):** Deposited when the solution temperature falls below 56°C.

Upon heating, ordinary borax crystals swell significantly, lose their water of crystallization, and melt into a clear white glass. Octahedral borax crystals fuse more easily and are less prone to splitting when heated than the prismatic form.

## Applications
Because they are less likely to split and fuse more easily, octahedral borax crystals are preferred for fluxing or soldering. Fused borax is capable of dissolving many metallic oxides to form complex borates, which often exhibit characteristic colors. In soldering, borax is used to remove the superficial film of oxide from metal surfaces, as solder only adheres to untarnished metal. Other applications include the glazing of linen, glass-making, and the glazing of pottery.
