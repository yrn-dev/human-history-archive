# Improvements Across Human History

The record of human history is marked by various advancements in technology, social organization, art, and science. These improvements have manifested in diverse fields, ranging from the cultivation of nature and the science of navigation to the methods of warfare and the administration of finance.

## Agricultural and Social Advancements
The expansion of the Roman Empire facilitated the diffusion of social life improvements. In early antiquity, the world was divided between a West inhabited by rude, warlike barbarians—who either disdained or were unknown to agriculture—and an East possessing luxury and arts. Under an established government, the industry of civilized nations and the productions of happier climates were introduced to Western Europe.

This process included the importation of numerous vegetable and animal species from Egypt and Asia. Many European garden fruits, herbs, and flowers are of foreign extraction. For instance, the apple originated in Italy. When Romans encountered the orange, citron, pomegranate, peach, and apricot, they initially used the common term "apple" for all these new fruits, distinguishing them by their country of origin. Additionally, while the vine grew wild in Sicily and the adjacent continent during the time of Homer, it was not improved by the skill of the savage inhabitants. A millennium later, Italy produced more than two-thirds of the eighty most celebrated and generous wines.

## Navigation and Cartography
In the field of cartography, Portolano charts provided remarkable accuracy for Mediterranean seamen during a general period of stagnation. These charts, first mentioned in connection with the 1270 Crusade of Louis XI, originated much earlier. In the eastern Mediterranean, they utilized materials available before the time of Ptolemy, while the delineation of the west is attributed to a later date and possibly to Catalan seamen.

These charts relied on detailed surveys of coastlines and estimated distances and bearings between capes and ports. Initially, bearings were determined by observing the heavens. While the compass was used on ships as early as 1205 according to Guiot de Provins, it only became fully serviceable after the needle was attached to the compass card, an improvement likely introduced by Flavio Gioja of Amalfi at the start of the 14th century.

## Military Technology and Warfare
The evolution of military architecture and weaponry has shifted the balance between attack and defense. During the 13th and 14th centuries, siege-engines improved; however, the concentric system of castle design, combined with natural inaccessibility, allowed defenses to remain superior to attacks during the latter middle ages. This superiority ended with the introduction of gunpowder as a propellant. This transition began in the 14th century, developed fully in the 15th, and rendered the feudal fastness an anachronism by the 16th century.

In more modern warfare, the improvement of firearms has increased the distance between fighting forces. This shift has rendered the traditional "battle painter" less useful, as soldiers are concealed in trenches and by the ground. Modern commanders no longer "see" the battle directly but direct operations via telephone based on sifted reports. Furthermore, the use of field-grey uniforms provides a tactical advantage by allowing soldiers to merge into the ground's coloring, unlike the visible brilliant uniforms of the French.

## Arts, Literature, and Administration
Improvements in the arts and administration vary by discipline. In music, the speed of composition can impact quality; for example, one composer wrote the instrumentation of an entire opera in thirty hours. While some argue that more elaboration would not have improved the work—citing the superior last act of the *Favorita* as a product of a single night—the sheer volume of his sixty-four operas made comprehensive revision impossible.

In the realm of accounting, recent years have seen numerous improvements in methods, following the general principles of book-keeping. In literature, the reputation of Dante experienced various shifts. After being admired in Italian universities following his death, he was obscured during the Renaissance, held inferior to Petrarch in the 16th century, and largely neglected through the first half of the 18th century. His fame has since been vindicated, leading to a vast number of editions, commentaries, and studies.
