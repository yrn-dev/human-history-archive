# Zulu-Kaffir and Related Bantu Groups

The Zulu-Kaffir people are a group of dark-skinned negroids of Bantu stock residing primarily in South Africa. Their territory encompasses parts of the eastern portion of the Transvaal, Natal, Swaziland, Rhodesia, and the eastern half of the Cape Colony. Within the broader classification of Bantu-speaking peoples, they are grouped alongside other tribes such as the Bechuana and Damara.

## Physical Characteristics and Origins
The Zulu-Kaffirs, along with the Awemba, some East Coast tribes, the Ba-tonga, the Ova-herero, the people of the Great Lakes, and the Babangi of the upper Congo, share a certain physical resemblance. However, there is significant diversity in outward appearance, head shape, and other physical characteristics among the various peoples inhabiting Bantu Africa.

General deductions from the study of Bantu languages suggest that a powerful tribe of negroes speaking the Bantu mother-language—physically allied to the negroes of the southern Lake Chad and south-western Nile basins, though impregnated with the Caucasian Hamite—migrated from the heart of Africa approximately 3,000 years ago. This group pushed into the southern half of the continent, which was then sparsely populated. In the regions they entered, they assimilated or absorbed the majority of the preceding populations, which included Forest-negroes, Nilotic-negroes, Hottentots, and Bushmen.

## Language and Linguistics
The Zulu-Kaffir language is a member of the Bantu family. While it preserves a few characteristics of the hypothetical Bantu mother-tongue, it exhibits marked deviations in phonetics and vocabulary, likely of recent date. For a period, Zulu-Kaffir was considered the most archaic type of Bantu speech; however, it has since been deposed from this position by the languages of the Great Lakes. It is noted for being unusually divergent from typical Bantu in certain features.

The Zulu language is divided into at most three dialects. Related dialects, specifically Ki-ngoni (or Ci-nongi) and Tebele, are spoken in Western Nyasaland, South-West Rhodesia, and on the plateaus north-east of Lake Nyasa. These dialects were carried to those regions by Zulu raiders during the early 19th century.

## Culture and Society
The Zulu-Bantu vocabulary includes the word *indaba*, which refers to an important conference. These meetings are held by the *indunas*, or principal men, of the Kaffir (Zulu-Xosa) tribes. An *indaba* may be limited to the *indunas* of a single tribe or may include representatives from other peoples or tribes.

In terms of weaponry, the Zulu and other Kaffir tribes of South Africa utilize the *assegai* (or *assagai*). This is a light spear or javelin made of wood and pointed with iron, designed for hurling or throwing. There are two primary versions of this weapon: a long-handled variety and a shorter version intended for use at close quarters.

## Migration and Movement
Evidence suggests that the Zulu-Kaffir race may have migrated directly from countries located to the north-east of Tanganyika, crossing the Zambezi to reach their current home. This is supported by a slight resemblance between Zulu-Kaffir and the Bantu languages of North Eastern Rhodesia, northern Nyasaland, and the Kafukwe basin. 

Interestingly, several hundred years after this initial southward migration, internal conflicts and intestine wars led to a return migration of Zulus toward the north-east, moving from Matabeleland.
