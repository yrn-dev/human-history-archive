# The Gobi and the Lop Nor Depression

The Gobi is a vast desert in central Asia, bounded to the north and northwest by the Yablonoi Mountains and their extension in the Tian-shan, to the south by the northern face of the Tibetan plateau, and to the east by the Khingan range. Geographically, its eastern portion is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia, while its western portion forms eastern or Chinese Turkestan.

## Topography and Elevation
The Gobi is characterized by a complex system of elevations and depressions. While some sources suggest that no part of the great central Asiatic desert is less than 2,000 ft. above sea level—with the plains of Yarkand and Kashgar ranging from 4,000 to 6,000 ft.—other research focuses on a significant lateral depression. This depression begins on the east of the Gobi and extends westwards through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan, limited to the west by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol.

Between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the sources of the Hwang-ho, the level of this depression, which was once a vast inland sea, likely never exceeds 2,000 ft. above sea level. Modern research indicates that in the central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, the land may actually be below sea level. This area forms a vast, almost waterless interior space where local drainage is lost to swamps or deserts.

## Lop Nor and the Central Depression
The great central depression, stretching from the foot of the Pamir plateau through the Tarim desert to Lop Nor and the Gobi, has been the subject of extensive exploration. Sven Hedin revealed the phenomenon of the periodic shifting of the Lop Nor system. Additionally, Hedin and Dr. M.A. Stein demonstrated that the now sand-strewn wastes of the Turkestan desert once housed highly civilized centers of industry and Buddhist art.

Lop Nor was historically a significant meeting point for trade roads originating from Khotan, L’Hasa, Shachau, Urumtsi, and Kashgar. Its relative importance was derived from this position despite being situated in a poor country. Some historical accounts identify Lop as the *Napopo* (or *Navapa*) of Hiuen Tsang, also known as the country of Leulan.

## Regional Geography and Boundaries
The geography surrounding the Gobi is defined by several key mountain ranges and plateaus:
*   **The Altyn Tagh:** A range discovered by Nicolai Prjevalsky in 1876. It forms part of a continuous water-divide extending eastwards to the Nan Shan mountains.
*   **Tsaidam:** Located between Tibet and the low-lying sands of the Gobi, Tsaidam consists of a system of elevated valleys 8,000 to 9,000 ft. above sea level, serving as an intermediate steppe.
*   **The Kuen-lun Mountains:** Once thought to be the southern limit of the Gobi or Turkestan depression, this longitudinal chain is now recognized as the northern limit of the Chang Tang, the elevated highland steppes of Tibet.

The presence of these elevated systems, such as Tsaidam, helps explain why ancient trade routes from Peking to the west were pushed far to the north.

## Explorations of the Region
The Russian explorer Nicolai Prjevalsky conducted several significant journeys through this region between 1871 and 1885. His contributions include:
*   Defining the geography of Tsaidam and mapping the hydrography of the region from which the great rivers of Burma, Siam, and China emanate.
*   Visiting the sources of the Salween and the Hwang-ho (Yellow River).
*   Crossing and naming the Dzungarian extension of the Gobi desert.
*   Traversing the Gobi from Hami to Sachu and crossing the Altyn Tagh to reach Lop Nor.

Prjevalsky's work demonstrated the extraordinary physical changes that have occurred in the heart of the Asiatic continent since the era of Jenghiz Khan.
