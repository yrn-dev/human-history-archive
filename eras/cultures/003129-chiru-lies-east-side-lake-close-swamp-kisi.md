# Chiru and Kisi

The archive contains references to two distinct geographical locations identified as Chiru and Kisi. One set of locations consists of islands within Lake Bangweulu in British Central Africa, while the other refers to a historic island and city in the Persian Gulf.

## Islands of Lake Bangweulu

Lake Bangweulu is a shallow lake located in British Central Africa, situated between 10° 38' and 11° 31' S. and cut by 30° E. The lake sits in the north-west portion of a central basin on an extensive plateau, approximately 3,700 feet above sea level. Much of the surrounding land slopes gently into a depression, where numerous streams drain into a morass of papyrus and reeds. While the term Bangweulu is sometimes used for the entire depression, it properly refers to the area of clear water.

Within this lake, there are several islands that diminish the surface of the water. The largest of these is Kirui, also known as Chiru, which is located on the east side of the lake, positioned close to the swamp. Another island, Kisi (also referred to as Chishi), is a small island that occupies a central position just south of 11° S. Further south of Kisi lies Mbawali, an island measuring 20 miles in length and 3 miles in breadth.

The lake's geography is further characterized by long, narrow sandbanks that nearly separate the main body of water from the western pan, known as Chifunawuli. To the south of the lake, the swamp extends to 12° 10' S. The Luapula river leaves Lake Bangweulu at its southernmost point; it is roughly a mile wide at the outflow before narrowing to about 300 yards. The Chambezi, the most remote head stream of the Congo, flows into the east side of this swamp, mingling its waters with the Luapula without ever entering the lake itself.

## Kisi of the Persian Gulf

Separate from the African lake, the archive records a location known as Kisi (also referred to as Kish or Kais). This is an island and city located in the Persian Gulf, approximately 200 miles from the mouth of the Gulf. Historically, it served as one of the primary ports for trade with the East and India.

The island is noted for being singular among the islands of the Gulf because it is wooded and possesses a good supply of fresh water. Ruins of a city exist on the north side of the island. According to the account of Wassáf, the island was named after a man named Kais, the son of a poor widow from Síráf (a major port of Indian trade on the northern shore of the Gulf). In the 10th century, Kais reportedly made a fortune during a voyage to India, and the proceeds were invested in an establishment on the island.
