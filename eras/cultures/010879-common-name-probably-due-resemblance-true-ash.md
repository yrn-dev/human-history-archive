# Ash (Trees)

The term "ash" (German: *Esche*; French: *frene*) is a common name applied to several types of trees. The most prominent is the common ash, though the name is also used for various other species across different botanical families.

## The Common Ash
The common ash (*Fraxinus excelsior*) is a member of the natural order Oleaceae, also known as the olive family, which includes jasmine, privet, and lilac. It is native to the majority of Europe, Great Britain, and extends into Asia. 

The tree is characterized by its contour and height, typically reaching between 50 and 80 feet. It produces reddish flowers in clusters during March and April, prior to the development of its leaves. These flowers are naked, meaning they lack petals or sepals, and are generally imperfect as they lack either a pistil or stamens. The leaves appear late and are pinnately compound, featuring four to seven pairs of toothed leaflets that taper gracefully on a slender stalk. A defining characteristic of the common ash is its dry winged fruits, known as "keys," which frequently remain in bunches after the autumn leaf fall. While the leaves fall early, the tree remains conspicuous in early spring and winter due to its black buds and greyish twigs.

## Varieties and Related Species
There are several varieties and related species of ash:
*   **Varieties of *Fraxinus excelsior*:** The *heterophylla* variety possesses simple leaves and is found wild in England and Europe. The *pendula* variety features weeping, pendulous branches; this version is sometimes grafted onto the tall stem of a common ash for aesthetic purposes and was reportedly first observed at Gamlingay in Cambridgeshire. Other varieties include *crispa*, which has curled leaves, and *verrucosa*, which is characterized by warty branches and stems.
*   **Other *Fraxinus* species:** *F. Ornus* is the manna ash, a handsome tree native to southern Europe with greenish-white flowers. Southern Europe also hosts the small-leaved ash (*Fraxinus parvifolia*). In the Himalayas, *F. floribunda* is a large tree featuring terminal panicles of white flowers.
*   **American species:** North America is home to the red ash (*F. pubescens*), the white ash (*F. americana*), and the black ash (*F. sambucifolia*).

## Other Trees Named "Ash"
Several trees share the name "ash" despite belonging to different botanical families:
*   **Mountain Ash:** Known in Scotland as the rowan tree, *Pyrus Aucuparia* belongs to the order Rosaceae and the tribe Pomeae (which includes pears and apples). Its resemblance to the true ash—specifically its graceful ascending branches, smooth grey bark, and pinnately compound leaves (though smaller than the true ash)—is likely the reason for its common name. It is recognized by its succulent scarlet fruit and clusters of white blossoms.
*   **Poison Ash:** This name refers to *Rhus venenata*, also known as the North American sumach or poison elder, which belongs to the Anacardiaceae (Cashew family).
*   **Bitter Ash:** *Simaruba excelsa* is the bitter ash of the West Indies, belonging to the natural order Simarubaceae.
*   **Cape Ash:** *Ekebergia capensis* is a large tree native to the Cape of Good Hope, belonging to the natural order Meliaceae.
*   **Prickly Ash:** *Xanthoxylon Clava-Herculis* is a small tree native to the south-eastern United States (natural order Xanthoxyleae). It is distinguished by a trunk studded with corky tubercles and branches armed with sharp, brown, stout prickles.

## Linguistic and Other Notes
The Hebrew word *Oren*, translated as "ash" in Isaiah xliv. 14, does not refer to an ash tree because the species is not native to Palestine; it likely refers instead to the Aleppo pine (*Pinus halepensis*). 

Separately, the word "ash" or "ashes" (referring to the residue left after burning) is a common Teutonic word (*Asche* in German) related to the Latin root *ardere*, meaning to burn.
