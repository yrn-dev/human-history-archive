# God of the Jews

## Nature and Characteristics
The God of the Jews is described as the one God of the whole world, an invisible and remote deity who exists in the heavens, high above sacrifices and priests. Unlike the national gods of other peoples, who were embodied in images and resided in temples, the God of the Jews is an unseen God of all the Earth who dwells in a temple not made with hands. This deity is characterized as a Lord of Righteousness and a jealous God who is intolerant of idolatry.

Because of this nature, the Jews maintained a strict monotheism, refusing to acknowledge any other gods. This belief manifested in a refusal to worship god-Cæsars or salute Roman standards, as such acts were viewed as idolatry. Consequently, no graven images were permitted to enter Jerusalem.

## Relationship with the Jewish People
The Jews believed that the God of Abraham had chosen them to be his peculiar people. This divine selection was tied to a common destiny: to restore Jerusalem and establish it as the capital of Righteousness in the World. Some perspectives within the Jewish tradition viewed God as a "trading god" who had entered into a bargain with their father Abraham, which would eventually bring the Jewish people to predominance in the earth.

The archive records that this relationship was governed by covenants and laws of worship. The Jews were persuaded that their God acted providently in their affairs. Examples of this providence include the preservation of religious Jews during expeditions and the miraculous preservation of leaders such as Simon, Judas, and Jonathan during the defense of their religion. Conversely, those who fought for honor in a vain-glorious way, without a commission from God or the family He raised to deliver them, were defeated.

## Divergent Interpretations and Perspectives
Within Jewish society, different groups held varying views on the application of God's laws and the scope of His promises:

*   **The Pharisees:** Described as "high and narrow," this orthodox group was punctilious regarding the minutest details of the law. Their devotion was so intense that they refused to defend Jerusalem on the Sabbath day—a day when work is forbidden—which contributed to the city falling to the Seleucid monarch Antiochus IV and later to Pompey the Great.
*   **The Hellenizers (including Sadducees):** These "broad Jews" were more disposed to assimilate with Greeks. They were open to accepting proselytes, believing they could share God and His promise with all mankind. However, this group was also characterized as worldlings, and the Sadducees specifically did not believe in immortality.

## Teachings of Jesus
The archive notes a significant shift in the conception of God introduced by Jesus, which caused dismay and anger among those who relied on the idea of being a chosen people. Jesus taught that God was not a bargainer and that there were no favorites or chosen people in the Kingdom of Heaven. 

According to these teachings, God is the loving father of all life, as incapable of showing favor as the universal sun. In this vision, all men are brothers—both sinners and beloved sons—of a divine father. Through parables such as that of the Good Samaritan and the labourers, Jesus rejected the claim that the Jews had a special claim or "first mortgage" upon God. He taught that God serves all whom He takes into the kingdom alike, with a bounty that has no measure. Furthermore, as evidenced by the incident of the widow’s mite and the parable of the buried talent, God demands the utmost from everyone, offering no rebates, privileges, or excuses in the Kingdom of Heaven.

## Interactions with External Powers
The Jewish faith in their God often brought them into conflict with Roman authorities. Caius Caesar, who viewed himself as a god, attempted to place his statues in the temple in Jerusalem. However, the archive notes that God "concerned himself" with these commands. 

In one instance, the Roman official Petronius observed the power of the Jewish God. After Petronius spoke to the Jews, great showers of rain fell unexpectedly during a year of severe drought. This event was interpreted as a sign that God took care of the Jews and acted as their Governor. Petronius subsequently reported to Caius that the power of the Jewish God was evident and that the Jews would not suffer their laws of worship to be set aside even under threat of war.
