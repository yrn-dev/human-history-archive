# BASQUE PROVINCES (Provincias Vascongadas)

The Basque Provinces, known in Spanish as *Provincias Vascongadas*, are a division of north-eastern Spain. The region comprises three provinces: Alava, Biscay (or Vizcaya), and Guipuzcoa. As of 1900, the population of these provinces was 603,596, covering an area of 2,739 square miles. This makes the region the third most densely populated area in Spain.

## Geography and Boundaries
The territory of the Basque Provinces is shaped like a triangle. Its boundaries are defined by the provinces of Santander, Burgos, and Logrono to the west and south, and by Navarre to the east. To the north, the region is bordered by France and the Bay of Biscay. While the Basque Provinces are located in Spain, the French Pays Basque is also noted as forming part of the arrondissements of Mauleon and Bayonne.

## Regional Characteristics and History
Among the three provinces, Guipuzcoa is the only one that is entirely Basque, while Alava is the least so. The capital of Alava, Vitoria, is said to have been founded in 581 by the Gothic king Leovigild. 

The current civil provincial divisions are of uncertain date and are considered less reflective of ancient tribal boundaries than the older limits of the dioceses of Calahorra, Bayonne, and Pamplona. These ancient diocesan boundaries likely corresponded more closely to the territories of the Vascones, the Varduli, the Caristi, and the Autrigones, who possessed differing dialects.

## Economy and Industry
The primary industries of the Basque Provinces are iron mining and sea fisheries. Iron mining is particularly prominent in Biscay; some mines around Bilbao have been operational since prehistoric times. In 1905, the Basque Provinces produced 5,302,344 tons of iron, with over five million tons originating from Biscay. This accounted for a significant portion of Spain's total iron production of 9,395,314 tons. More than half of this total (5,845,895 tons) was exported to England.

Other notable industrial contributions include the small-arms factory at Eibar in Guipuzcoa and the renowned swords of Mondragon, which were famous before those of Toledo. In the 19th century, Senor Zuloaga revived the artistic inlaying of iron and steel with silver and gold in Eibar.

In terms of agriculture, the Basque Provinces and the Pays Basque were historically great cider-producing regions, though wine-growing gradually replaced cider during the 19th century. While the agriculture may appear poor to those accustomed to larger farms, the region's old scattered homesteads reflect a sense of security. The Basques have demonstrated adaptability, particularly as self-sufficient village industries declined due to the invention of machinery. This led to emigration to agricultural regions of the Americas, contributing to a period of high prosperity.

## Major Settlements and Landmarks
Bilbao serves as the principal port and capital, with a population of 83,306. Other significant towns include Baracaldo (15,013) and Sestao (10,833). Bermeo (9,061) is the primary fishing station. 

Durango (4,319), located on the river of the same name, was founded in the 10th century by the early kings of Navarre and became a countship in 1153; it is home to San Pedro de Tavira, one of the oldest churches in the region. Guernica (3,250), a village on the river Mondaca, served as the meeting place for the provincial parliament until 1876. The deputies there met under an old oak tree, which was celebrated in a song by the poet Jose Maria Iparraguirre that is regarded by Spanish Basques almost as a national anthem.

In the province of Biscay, the population increased rapidly in the late 19th century due to a high birth rate, low death rate, and minimal emigration. By 1900, its population density reached 372.4 per square mile, the highest of any Spanish province.
