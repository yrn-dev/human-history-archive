# Jews

The history of the Jews is characterized by the establishment of diverse communities across the globe, the maintenance of a specific religious canon, and the construction of synagogues as centers of worship and community life.

## Religious Canon and Scholarship
The Jewish canon for the Old Testament is divided into three primary sections: the Torah (or "Law"), which consists of five books; the "Prophets," comprising eight books; and the "Writings" (or "Hagiographa"), which contain eleven books. The "Prophets" are further divided into the "Former Prophets" (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings) and the "Latter Prophets" (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Minor Prophets, the latter of whom are counted as a single book called "the Twelve"). The "Writings" include poetical books (Psalms, Proverbs, and Job), the five *Megilloth* or "Rolls" (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, and Esther), and the remaining books of Daniel, Ezra and Nehemiah (forming one book), and Chronicles. The *Megilloth* were grouped together due to the custom of reading them in synagogues during five sacred seasons.

In the 19th century, Samuel David Luzzatto (1800–1865), a distinguished Italian Jewish scholar, contributed to the history of synagogue liturgy and the revival of interest in secular verse and medieval Hebrew hymnology. Luzzatto was the first Jew to suggest emendations to the text of the Hebrew Bible, editing Isaiah and writing a commentary on the Pentateuch.

## Communities in China
A colony of Jews existed at Kaï-fung fu in the Ho-nan province of China. According to oral tradition, they arrived from *Si Yĭh* (likely Persia) via Samarkand and Khorasan during the first century of the era under Emperor Ming-ti of the Han Dynasty. However, lapidary inscriptions provide conflicting dates: one suggests seventy families arrived during the Sung Dynasty (A.D. 960–1278), another cites the Han Dynasty (B.C. 206–A.D. 221), and a third claims the religion was preached during the Chau Dynasty (B.C. 1122–255).

In Chinese records, such as the *Yuen shi*, Jews are mentioned in 1329 regarding tax collection laws and in 1354, when rich Jews and Mahommetans were invited to the capital to join the army; in these texts, they are referred to as *Chu hu* (Djuhud). Their religion was commonly known as *Tiao kin kiao* ("Extract Sinew Religion").

The community at Kaï-fung fu maintained a synagogue, which inscriptions state was built in 1163 under the Sung Emperor Hiao. It was rebuilt in 1279 by a rabbi (possibly on the site of a ruined mosque), again in 1421 during the reign of Yung-lo, and once more in 1653 after being destroyed by a flood of the Hwang-ho in 1642. European knowledge of this colony grew in the 17th century through Jesuit missionaries, including Matteo Ricci. In 1760, Jewish merchants from London sent a Hebrew letter to the Kaï-fung community, and in 1867, J. L. Liebermann, a Jewish merchant from Vienna, visited the colony. The community was eventually dispersed in 1857 during the T’aï-P’ing rising.

## Regional Settlements and Synagogues
Jewish presence and the establishment of synagogues are noted in several other regions:
*   **Brest-Litovsk:** In this fortified Russian town, more than half the population was Jewish as of 1901. The town contained a Jewish synagogue that was regarded as the first in Europe during the 16th century.
*   **Egypt:** Historical accounts mention a famous settlement of Jews and numerous synagogues in Alexandria. Additionally, a great synagogue existed in Cairo.
*   **Cesarea:** Jews in Cesarea maintained a synagogue. A conflict arose during the twelfth year of Nero's reign when a Greek owner of the land surrounding the synagogue built workshops that obstructed the passage to the house of worship. Despite offers of eight talents to stop the work, the situation contributed to the onset of war.

## Customs and Social Observations
Historical observations describe the Jews as maintaining "lofty and exclusive privileges" and adhering with "inflexible rigor" to laws regarding specific days and meats. These distinctions, along with the rite of circumcision, were noted as factors that separated them from other nations and could repel potential proselytes from the synagogue. In the context of the Holy of Holies, it was observed that the Jews did not keep an image of God within it, leading to the saying that they adore nothing but the clouds and the divinity of the heavens.
