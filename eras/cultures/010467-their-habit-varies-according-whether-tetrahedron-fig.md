# Crystal Habits and Mineral Characteristics

In mineralogy, the term "habit" refers to the external form and characteristic appearance of crystals. This habit can vary significantly depending on which geometric forms predominate during the crystal's development.

## Boracite
Boracite is a mineral noted for its optical anomalies. It is found in considerable numbers embedded in anhydrite and gypsum within the salt deposits of Luneburg in Hanover, where it was first observed in 1787. The crystals are cubic with inclined hemihedrism, sharing the same symmetry as tetrahedrite and blende. 

The habit of boracite varies based on whether the cube, the rhombic dodecahedron, or the tetrahedron predominates. In some instances, penetration twins are observed with a tetrahedron face serving as the twin-plane. These crystals possess a vitreous lustre and range from transparent to translucent; while they are white or colourless, they are frequently tinged with green, yellow, or grey. Boracite has a specific gravity of 3.0 and a hardness of 7 on the Mohs scale.

As observed by R.J. Hauy in 1791, boracite crystals are markedly pyroelectric. When a cube of the mineral is heated, four of its corners become negatively electrified while the four opposite corners become positively electrified. In crystals where the rhombic dodecahedron predominates, the larger and bright tetrahedral faces are located at the antilogous poles, while the smaller, dull tetrahedral faces are at the analogous poles; the latter become positively electrified upon heating.

## Calcite
Calcite crystals exhibit extremely varied forms. Generally, these are categorized into four distinct habits: tabular, scalenohedral, prismatic, and rhombohedral. 

*   **Rhombohedral:** The primitive rhombohedron is relatively rare unless combined with other forms. Other variations include a more acute rhombohedron and a flatter rhombohedron.
*   **Prismatic:** This habit can occur as a combination of the rhombohedron and the prism.
*   **Scalenohedral:** This habit is often associated with the primitive rhombohedron.
*   **Tabular:** This form is a combination of the prism, the basal pinacoid, and the rhombohedron.

Because of these habits, various trivial names are used to describe calcite. Crystals of scalenohedral habit, common in limestone caverns and the lead mines of Derbyshire, are called "dog-tooth-spar." Those of tabular habit, which can be as thin as paper, are known as "slate-spar" (or *Schieferspath* in German). "Cannon-spar" refers to crystals of prismatic habit terminated by the basal pinacoid, and "nail-head-spar" refers to crystals terminated by the obtuse rhombohedron, common in the lead mines of Alston Moor in Cumberland.

Calcite is also known for its twinned crystals. There are four well-defined twin-laws:
1.  **Twin-plane c (111):** Involves reflection across the plane or a 180° rotation about the principal trigonal axis.
2.  **Twin-plane e (110):** The principal axes of the two portions are inclined at 52° 30½'. This repeated twinning creates twin-lamellae often found in the grains of crystalline limestones subjected to pressure.
3.  **Twin-plane r (100):** The principal axes are nearly at right angles (89° 14'). This law produces "heart-shaped twins" or "butterfly twins" (scalenohedral habit) found in Cleator Moor in Cumberland and Eyam in Derbyshire, as well as prismatic habit crystals formerly found at Wheal Wrey in Cornwall.
4.  **Twin-plane f (11-1):** The rarest twin-law, with principal axes inclined at 53° 46'.

## Corundum
Corundum crystallizes within the hexagonal system. Its habits vary by variety:
*   **Ruby:** A form where the prism is combined with a rhombohedron, a hexagonal pyramid, and the basal pinacoid.
*   **Sapphire:** A typical crystal where the prism is associated with a rhombohedron and acute pyramids.
*   **Tabular Habit:** Usually consisting of a rhombohedron and the basal pinacoid, this habit is said to characterize corundum that has consolidated from a fused magma.

Corundum is the hardest known mineral after diamond, generally rated at 9 on the hardness scale, though sapphire is harder than ruby, and ruby is harder than common corundum. It lacks true cleavage but possesses parting planes. The mineral's colours are varied, with chromium and iron acting as possible agents for the tints. Transparent varieties are referred to as "Oriental" stones. Due to its properties, corundum is used for bearings in electrical apparatus and watch-jewels.
