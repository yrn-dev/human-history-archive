# Bohemia and Moravia

Bohemia is a kingdom and crownland of Austria, serving as the principal province of the Austrian empire. It is situated in the geographical center of the European continent, bounded to the northeast by Prussian Silesia, to the southeast by Lower Austria and Moravia, to the south by Upper Austria, to the southwest by Bavaria, and to the northwest by Saxony. Enclosed by high mountains, it remains accessible via the valley of the Elbe to the German plain and through Moravia from the Danubian plain. The region functions as a borderland between the Slavonic and German worlds.

## Early History and Christianization
The introduction of Christianity to Bohemia first arrived from Germany, though these attempts saw little success due to an innate distrust of the Germans. The faith was more successfully adopted when it approached from Moravia, where the Greek monks Cyrillus and Methodius from Thessalonica had taught its doctrine. Around the year 873, Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius, leading to a rapid adoption of Christianity. 

Among early rulers, Wenceslas (surnamed the Holy) was prominent; he was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized. Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, expanded the frontiers of Bohemia to include Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and the area now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. However, this empire did not endure. Boleslav III lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland, and subsequent internal civil wars between Boleslav III and his brothers, Jaromir and Ulrick, led the Bohemians to dethrone him in favor of the Polish prince Vladivoj.

## Medieval and Early Modern Conflict
Bohemia's history includes periods of significant religious and political strife. George of Podebrad, the only Hussite king of Bohemia, faced opposition from Romanist nobles and the papal see. After Pope Paul II excommunicated George and released Bohemians from their allegiance, King Matthias of Hungary supported the Romanist league. Matthias invaded Moravia and took possession of Brunn, and on May 3, 1469, he was proclaimed king of Bohemia at a meeting in Olmutz.

Later, conflict erupted between Rudolph and his brother Matthias. The rupture was formally caused by Rudolph's refusal to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded. While the estates of Moravia renounced allegiance to Rudolph, the Bohemians declined Matthias's overtures in 1608. Eventually, an agreement was reached on June 25, 1608, where Rudolph ceded the government of Moravia, Hungary, and Upper and Lower Austria to Matthias, while Rudolph retained his rights as king of Bohemia.

## National Aspirations and the 19th Century
Following 1815, Bohemian national aspirations revived, initially through literature. By 1845, the estates of Bohemia opposed the government in Vienna, asserting their right to vote on taxes based on the constitution of 1627. In March 1848, a public meeting in Prague petitioned for the Bohemian language to have equal rights with German in government offices and for a general diet elected on extensive suffrage. Emperor Ferdinand responded on April 8, stating that equality of rights for both nationalities would be secured and that the reunion of Moravia and Silesia to Bohemia would be decided by a general meeting of Austrian representatives.

In the late 19th century, a great party led by Rieger and Palacky demanded the full restoration of the Bohemian monarchy, including Silesia and Moravia. They insisted the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia in Prague and that the region receive a position similar to Hungary's. This movement was supported by many Czechs and several German-descent nobles, such as Count Leo Thun and Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg. Conversely, Germans in Bohemia, fearing Czech control, advocated for centralization.

## Linguistic and Political Struggles
The contest between Czech and German influences was most vehement in Bohemia and Moravia, where the two populations were nearly equal. Over twenty years, the Czech language encroached upon the German. An 1880 ordinance required government business to be handled in the language in which it was introduced across Bohemia and Moravia. In 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Bruenn and Prague. Furthermore, Czech elementary and middle schools were founded, and in 1882, Czech classes were introduced at the university of Prague.

Politically, the "declaration" of 1868 became the official statement of Czech claims, demanding the restoration of the Bohemian kingdom and asserting that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia. This led to serious riots and the decree of exceptional laws in Bohemia and Moravia.

In 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the empire, and the sovereign recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. However, this failed by 1872. Later, under Count Eduard Taaffe, representatives of Bohemia returned to the parliament in Vienna, though they maintained that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state. Taaffe's government removed electoral anomalies that had favored the German minority and supported the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.

Despite these efforts, political instability continued. In one instance, the parliament consisted only of Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia, a situation deemed impossible to govern. Subsequent attempts by Potocki to persuade the Czechs to accept the constitution failed, as the Czechs and Feudals adhered to the declaration of 1868. Following the dissolution of the diets, the Czechs gained a large majority in Bohemia but refused to recognize the diet as the legal representative of the Bohemian kingdom, viewing it instead as an informal assembly.
