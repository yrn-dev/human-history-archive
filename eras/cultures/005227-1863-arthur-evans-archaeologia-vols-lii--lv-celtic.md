# Celtic Studies and Related Archaeological Works

The study of Celtic history, art, and linguistics involves a diverse range of archaeological and philological research. Scholars have focused on the material culture of ancient Britain, the evolution of Celtic languages, and the mythological traditions associated with the Celtic area of Europe.

## Celtic Art and Archaeology
Research into late Celtic art is highlighted in the work *Horae Ferales* (1863), authored by A.W. Franks and J.M. Kemble. Further contributions to the field were made by Arthur J. Evans, whose work appears in volumes lii. through lv. of *Archaeologia*. 

Broader studies of ancient Britain and the invasions of Caesar are summarized in T.R. Holmes' *Ancient Britain* (1907). Other significant archaeological references include the works of Sir John Evans, specifically *Stone Implements*, *Bronze Implements*, and *Ancient British Coins*. Additionally, Boyd Dawkins published *Early Man in Britain* in 1880, and J. Rhys authored *Celtic Britain* (3rd edition, 1904). For the study of ancient earthworks, A. Hadrian Allcroft's *Earthwork of England* (1909) is cited.

## Celtic Philology and Linguistics
The study of Celtic ethnology and philology is described as being in an "age of discussion." A primary example of Celtic linguistic influence is found in the word "Avon," which is Celtic for water. This root appears in several forms across the Celtic regions of Europe:
* **British Islands:** Appearing as *afon* in Welsh, *aon* in Manx, and *abhuinn* (pronounced *avain*) in Gaelic. Other variations include *Evan*, *Aune*, *Anne*, *Ive*, and *Inney*.
* **France and Brittany:** Appearing as *Aff*, *Aven*, *Avon*, and *Aune*.
* **Other Regions:** *Avenza* and *Avens* in Italy, *Avia* in Portugal, and *Avono* in Spain.

The root is considered radically identical to the Latin *aqua* and *amnis*, as well as the Sanskrit *ap*. This linguistic root is also evident in names such as *Punjab* and *Doab*. Furthermore, the terminal syllable of various Latinized French river names—such as the *Garumna*, *Matrona*, and *Sequana*—is believed to have originally been this same Celtic word.

## Mythology and Tradition
In Welsh mythology, Avalon (also written as Avellion, Avollon, Avilion, or Avelion) is identified as the kingdom of the dead. It later evolved into an earthly paradise located in the western seas and, in Arthurian romances, became the abode of heroes where King Arthur was taken after his final battle. 

The Welsh name *Ynys yr Afallon* is generally interpreted as the "Isle of Apples," derived from the Welsh word *afal* (apple). This is thought to symbolize the enjoyments and feasting of elysium, as apples were the only large fruit well known to the races of northern Europe. Alternatively, the name may be connected to a Celtic tradition regarding a king over the dead named Avalloc (Afallach in Welsh). Other names for this location include *Ynysgutrin* and *Ynysvitrin* ("Isle of Glass"), which are viewed as identical to the Teutonic kingdom of the dead, Glasberg.

## Related Scholarly Contributions
Sir Arthur Evans has contributed to various fields beyond Celtic art. In the study of prehistory, he posits that the neolithic age in Crete began more than 14,000 years ago. In the realm of anthropology, Evans suggests in *Anthropology and the Classics* that sign-language preceded speech in America, noting that while languages differ among North American Indians, sign-language is common to all.

Other authorities on ancient British history and archaeology include W. F. Skene, who wrote *Celtic Scotland* (1877), and various researchers of burial mounds and barrows, such as Canon W. Greenwell and Dr. J. Thurnam.
