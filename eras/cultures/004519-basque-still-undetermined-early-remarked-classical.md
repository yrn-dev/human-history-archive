# Basques

The Basques are a sturdy and independent-spirited people residing on the north and south slopes of the Pyrenees. In Europe, their population is estimated at approximately 600,000. Their primary territory in north-eastern Spain is divided into the Basque Provinces (*Provincias Vascongadas*), which include Guipuzcoa, Alava, and Biscay (Vizcaya). Guipuzcoa is the only province that is entirely Basque, while Alava is the least so. The region is bounded by France and the Bay of Biscay to the north, Navarre to the east, and the provinces of Logrono, Burgos, and Santander to the south and west. The French Pays Basque is situated within the arrondissements of Mauleon and Bayonne.

## Language and Origins
The Basque language is a fully developed system of speech that differs absolutely from the surrounding Aryan languages. For scholars, the language has been a source of profound perplexity. Some theories suggest a remote link to the Berber language of North Africa and the wider Hamitic group, while others propose a connection to "stranded vestiges of speech" in the Caucasian Mountains, viewing Basque as a surviving member of an extinct, widely extended pre-Hamitic group. Other hypotheses include a relationship to Amer-Indian tongues or a "Basque-Caucasian-Dravidian-Sumerian-proto-Mongolian" ancestral system.

Ancient remains and toponymy suggest the Basque language was once more widely distributed across Spain. Classical scholars noted that ancient Spanish place-names transcribed by Greek and Latin writers—such as *Iliberis* (Iriberry) and *Iluro* (Oloron)—strongly resemble actual Basque names. While the majority of students accept that a people speaking a language akin to Basque once extended throughout Spain and possibly into Sardinia and Sicily, some Basque scholars deny this. The exact connection between the Basques and the ancient Iberians or Celtiberians remains conclusively unproven.

## Physical Characteristics
Due to centuries of interpenetration with other peoples, a "pure" physical type is not found among present Basques, though they remain distinguishable from their neighbors. Early accounts by Einhard (778) highlight their "wonderful agility," and the 12th-century Codex Calixtinus describes them as fairer in face than the Navarrese. Anthropological research by Collignon (1899) and Aranzadi (1889, 1905) indicates that Basques are fairer than southern races but less fair than northern Europeans. They are taller than southern races but shorter than British, Teutons, or Scandinavians. They are characterized by an upright carriage, a frequently fine profile, and a lack of the prognathism found in some Celts.

## Character and Society
The Basque character is defined by obstinate conservatism, intense self-respect, and pride of race. Historically, under the *fueros* (laws) of Spain, every Basque freeholder (*vecino*) could prove himself noble, making him eligible for any office. They are not a town-dwelling race; instead, they live in scattered habitations and small villages. This comfort with solitude has made them successful missionaries and emigrants.

Basques are renowned as splendid seamen, having been early leaders in the Bay of Biscay whale fisheries and the first to establish cod-fisheries off Newfoundland. They participated fully in the colonization of America, and Basque names are frequent among French Canadians. In military matters, they are noted as powerful marchers with great endurance—traits the Romans also observed in the Celtiberians—and are considered more effective in defense than in attack.

Socially, the legal position of women has been very high. Ancestral property was inherited by the eldest born regardless of gender, a practice common among both the peasantry and higher classes. Under the *fueros*, insults committed against or in the presence of a woman were punished more severely than those among men. Basque women are described as robust, particularly those who worked as rowers on the Bidassoa or loaded ships in Bilbao.

## Religion and Governance
The Basques are a highly religious and conservative people. They have contributed significantly to European religion through figures such as Francis Xavier of Navarre and Ignatius de Loyola of Guipuzcoa. They maintained primitive orders, such as deaconesses and the earliest form of Christian marriage, long after they vanished elsewhere in the West.

Despite their piety, the Basques resisted being "priest-ridden" and opposed the authority of Spanish bishops. In Guipuzcoa, ecclesiastical patronage was held by the king, nobles, or municipalities, and priests were sometimes elected by the people or chosen via competitive examination. The Basques also protected their cultural traditions from clerical interference; for example, the cortes of Navarre forced the king to withdraw a 1757 order from Spanish bishops that had forbidden drama across Spain.

## Geography and Urban Centers
The principal port and capital is Bilbao (population 83,306), followed by the industrial town of Baracaldo (15,013) and Sestao (10,833). Bermeo serves as the chief fishing station. Other notable locations include Durango, founded in the 10th century by the kings of Navarre, and Guernica, which served as the meeting place for the provincial parliament until 1876. In Guernica, deputies assembled under an old oak tree, a site celebrated in a song by poet Jose Maria Iparraguirre.
