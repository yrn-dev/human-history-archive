# Bombay and the Diverse Populations of the Eastern World

## The City of Bombay
Bombay serves as the great port and meeting-place of the Eastern world. Due to its central position between the East and the West, as well as the diversity of races found within India, it possesses a variety of types greater than any other city in the world. 

The dominant element of the population, following the European rulers, is the Mahratta race. However, the city is home to many other influential and diverse groups, including:
* Parsee merchants
* Arab traders from the Gulf
* Afghans and Sikhs from northern India
* Bengalis and Rajputs
* Chinese, Japanese, Malays, negroes, Tibetans, Sinhalese, and Siamese

The population is divided into several large sections, including Hindu, Mahommedan, Parsee, Jain, and Christian. Among these, the Parsees are one of the smallest groups, numbering only some 46,000 in total, yet they are among the most influential. This influence is evidenced by the fact that Parsee millionaires own most of the great business houses and have founded most of the large charities.

## The Siamese and Indo-China
Siam (or Thai) is part of a region described as Indo-China, which includes Burma, Cambodia, and Annam. These areas lie between India and China in both position and civilization. The Siamese represent a late invasion from southern China, having descended from there around the 13th century. They speak a monosyllabic language of the Chinese type, though it is written using an Indian alphabet.

In Siam, the city of Korat serves as the capital of the provincial division of Nakawn Racha Sema. Its population of approximately 7,000 is mixed Cambodian and Siamese. Korat is a center for silk-growing, and in 1904, the government's sericultural department was established there with the help of Japanese experts to improve the quality of Siamese silk. Historically, Korat has been part of or tributary to Siam since the founding of Ayuthia in the 14th century, though it was likely part of Cambodia prior to that.

## Tibetans and the Mongolian Type
Tibetans, along with the Burmese and Siamese, occupy countries between India and the Mongolian regions. These populations are chiefly of the Mongolian and Chinese type, utilizing fundamentally monosyllabic languages and adopting Buddhism from the Indians. 

The Burmese are linguistically allied to the Tibetans and are believed to have entered Burma from the north-west. Modern philologists suggest that the Burmese and Tibetans were part of a vast Indo-Chinese immigration swarm from western China; some settled in Assam and Tibet, while others moved south into the plains of Burma. Additionally, there is a connection between the Tibetan race and certain central Indian tribes, specifically the Kols and Bhils. It is probable that these tribes are remnants of a Mongolian race that displaced an earlier Negroid population before being displaced themselves by a Caucasian irruption.

## Malays and the Sinhalese
The Malay civilization is noted in the history of Cochin China, where the kingdom of Champa once existed before being absorbed by Annam. In the Eastern dependencies, Malays are found among the populations of the Straits Settlements. 

The Sinhalese are a major racial group, numbering 2,000,000, and make up a large part of the population of Ceylon. The influence of the Sinhalese is also seen in the religious landscape of Indo-China, where a later, purely ecclesiastical introduction of Sinhalese Buddhism brought its own specific art and literature to the region.

## Regional Influence and Geography
Indian influence, characterized by Buddhism (which was not always clearly distinguishable from Hinduism), extends across Indo-China, Tibet, Mongolia, and much of the Malay Archipelago, as well as Japan and China. This influence brought Indian art, literature, and alphabets to regions including Korea, Java, Cambodia, and Tibet.

Geographically, the region is documented through various surveys. In Siam, a regular survey was organized between 1881 and 1883 by Mr. J. McCarthy, which assisted in determining the Franco-Siamese frontier in 1906. In British India, surveys have extended beyond the proper boundaries of India since 1862, with an Indian Atlas including the Malay Peninsula and Ceylon.
