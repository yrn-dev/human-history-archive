# Bantu Language Prefixes: The 5th and 7th Classes

In the study of Bantu languages, substantives are categorized into classes or genders, which are identified by a pronominal particle prefixed to the root. These prefixes function in either a singular or plural sense. A system of "concord" exists where the pronominal particle of a noun is attached as a prefix to the roots of verbs, pronouns, prepositions, and adjectives within a sentence to maintain connection with the governing noun. While there are approximately sixteen prefixes common to the whole group, these forms have varied significantly as different Bantu dialects became more corrupt over time.

## The 5th Prefix and its Variations

The 5th prefix is characterized by several forms depending on the dialect and the era of the language. In the present day, the most common form of the 5th prefix is *Li-*, although *Di-* is considered the older and more correct version. 

This prefix is subject to frequent linguistic shifts: it is often dropped entirely—though the concord remains—or it transforms into the forms *Ri-*, *I-*, *Ji-*, and *Ni-*. The concord associated with the 5th prefix is the same as the prefix itself.

## The 7th Prefix and its Development

The 7th prefix, *Ki-*, follows a parallel course of evolution across many non-related dialects. In these instances, the prefix transitions from *Ki-* through *Ci-* and eventually into *Si-* (which can be represented as *Shi*). The concord for this class resembles the prefix.

The 7th prefix is linked to the 8th prefix through a singular-plural relationship; the 8th prefix serves invariably as the plural of the 7th. The 8th prefix is noted for being even more variable than the 7th, appearing in its oldest form as *Ibi-* or *Mbibi-*. In various Bantu speeches, this plural form can manifest as *Vi-*, *Pi-*, *Fi-*, *Fy-*, *P[vs]i-*, *[vS]i-*, *I-*, *By-*, *Bzi-*, *Psi-*, *Zwi-*, *Zi-*, and *Ri-*.

## General Prefix Mechanics and Concord

The Bantu prefix system generally prohibits a singular prefix from being used as a plural, or vice versa, with the exception of the "abstract" prefix *Bu* (No. 14). The original prefixes are believed to have consisted of two distinct particles. For example, in the 6th plural prefix *Nga-ma-*, the first syllable likely functioned as a demonstrative pronoun, while the second acted as an adjective or numeral. Specifically, *Mu-* is thought to have meant "one," and *Ma-* likely served as a collective numeral for indefinite numbers, used for herds of beasts, tribes of men, liquids (particularly water), or anything in a mass.

The persistence of concord is a key feature of these languages. Even when a prefix falls out of use as a definite article for a noun, the corresponding pronoun or concord often persists. An example of this is found in the Swahili of Zanzibar regarding the 10th prefix; while the plural prefix *zinombe* has ceased to be used for the word *Nombe* (cows), the *Zi-* particle reappears in the concord forms *hizi* and *zangu* in the phrase *Nombe hizi zangu* ("These cows are mine").
