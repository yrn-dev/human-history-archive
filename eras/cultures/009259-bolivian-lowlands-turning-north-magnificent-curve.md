# Bolivian Lowlands

The lowlands of Bolivia are defined by their river systems, which are categorized into three distinct regions: the Central Plateau, La Plata, and the Amazon. The Amazon system is the largest, forming a drainage basin that covers more than one-half of the republic.

## The Amazon River System
The Amazon system consists of rivers flowing directly or indirectly into the Madeira, a major tributary of the Amazon, as well as small tributaries of the Purus and Acre in the north. The two primary rivers in this system are the Beni and the Mamore. These two rivers unite at latitude 10 deg. 20' S. to form the Madeira.

The Mamore river, known as the Chimore in its upper section, originates on the north-east slopes of the Sierra de Cochabamba, slightly south of the 17th parallel. It follows a serpentine northerly course, primarily between the 65th and 66th meridians, for approximately 600 m. From Chimore (located 925 ft. above sea level) to the rapids near its mouth, three-fourths of the river passes across a level plain and is navigable.

The Guapay (also known as the Grande) is the principal Bolivian tributary of the Mamore. Rising in the Cordillera Oriental east of Lake Pampa Aullaguas, it flows east to the north extremity of the Sierra de Misiones, where it enters the Bolivian lowlands. From there, it turns north in a magnificent curve, passing around the south-east extremity of the Sierra de Cochabamba and skirting the Llanos de Chiquitos. It then turns north-west to unite with the Mamore at Junta de los Rios (15 deg. 20' S. lat. and 64 deg. 40' W. long.). The Guapay has a tortuous, non-navigable course exceeding 700 m. Its main tributaries include the Yapacani—which rises on the east slopes of the Cordillera Real and flows east by Cochabamba before bending north—as well as the Piray (or Sara) and the Mizque.

Other significant Bolivian tributaries of the Mamore that rise on the north-east flanks of the Andes include the Secure, Chapare, Manique (or Apere), and the Yacuma. The Yacuma drains a region of swamps and lakes north of the Sierra Chamaya.

## The La Plata Drainage Basin
The south-east drainage basin discharges into the Paraguay and is smaller and less economically significant than the Madeira basin. This region extends from the Cordillera Oriental in the east to the Paraguay river, and from the Sierras de Chiquitos in the north to the Argentine frontier.

The Pilcomayo is the only large Bolivian river in this system. It rises on the east slopes of the Cordillera Oriental opposite the south end of Lake Pampa Aullaguas, flowing east and south-east through the sierra region into the Bolivian Chaco. The river flows through nearly level country with a current so sluggish that its channels are heavily obstructed. In the sierra region, its largest tributaries are the Mataca, Cachimayo, and the Pilaya (or Camblaya), the latter of which is formed by the San Juan and Cotagaita.

Other streams in this region include the Parapiti, which rises on the eastern slopes of the Sierra de Misiones and flows north-east through a low plain for about 150 m. before being lost in the marshy districts of the Bolivian plain. The Otuquis, formed by the Tucabaca and San Rafael which drain the Cerro Cochii range, is lost in great marshes 50 m. from the Paraguay. Additionally, the Tarija (or Rio Grande) drains a small district south-east of the Santa Victoria sierra and serves as a large tributary to the Bermejo, an Argentine river.

## Trade and Communications
Due to the loss of its maritime department, Bolivia relies on the ports of the Paraguay and Madeira rivers, their affluents, and Lake Titicaca (specifically Guaqui). Because these ports require transhipment in foreign territory, transport costs are increased. Access to the Amazon was previously available via Puerto Acre on the Acre river; however, Brazil closed the Purus and Acre rivers to foreign vessels and acquired the port and territory under a 1903 treaty.

Currently, Bolivia's Amazon outlet is restricted to the Madeira river, where navigation is interrupted by falls before reaching Bolivian territory. The port of entry, Villa Bella, is located above the falls of the Madeira at the confluence of the Mamore and Beni. It is separated from navigable rivers above by the falls of the Mamore and Beni, and is reached from the lower river via a costly and long portage.

In the south, Bolivian trade is connected to the upper Paraguay via Puerto Suarez (on Lake Caceres), Puerto Pacheco (on Bahia Negra), and the projected port of Puerto Quijarro (on Lake Gaiba). Direct communication with Montevideo and Buenos Aires would be possible if a navigable channel were opened in the Pilcomayo.

To improve these conditions, treaties with Chile (1904) and Brazil (1903) provided for railway construction. Brazil agreed to build a 180 m. railway around the falls of the Madeira to provide north-eastern Bolivia with Amazon access and provided L2,000,000 for internal Bolivian railway construction.
