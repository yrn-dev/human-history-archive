# Asia Minor

Asia Minor is the general geographical name for the peninsula located on the extreme west of the continent of Asia. It forms part of the empire of Turkey and is bounded by the Black Sea to the north, the Aegean Sea to the west, and the Mediterranean Sea to the south. At its northwestern extremity, it is separated from Europe only by the narrow straits of the Dardanelles and the Bosporus.

## Geographical Boundaries and Dimensions
The peninsula's greatest length is 720 miles along its northern edge and approximately 650 miles along its southern edge. Its maximum breadth, measured from Cape Anamur to Cape Kerembe, is 420 miles. At its narrowest point—the "waist" of the peninsula between the southernmost bight of the Black Sea at Ordu and the head of the Gulf of Alexandretta—the width is slightly less than 300 miles.

While no natural boundary separates Asia Minor from the Armenian plateau to the east, a descriptive boundary is often drawn from the southern extremity of the Giaour Dagh (east of the Gulf of Alexandretta) along the crest of that chain, then along the eastern Taurus to the Euphrates near Malatia. From there, the line follows the western arm of the river to Erzingan and bends north to the Black Sea along the course of the Churuk Su, which flows out west of Batum. This makes the Euphrates the primary eastern limit.

## Topography and Plateaus
The greater portion of Asia Minor consists of a plateau that rises gradually from west to east, ranging from 2,500 to 4,500 feet. East of the Kizil Irmak (Halys), the terrain rises more sharply toward the highlands of Armenia. This region is part of an extensive high plateau that includes Iran (Persia), Afghanistan, Baluchistan, and Armenia, covering an area as large as Tibet but at a lower altitude.

The central, or Lycaonian, plateau is characterized by deposits of freshwater limestones and marls of Neogene or late Tertiary age, consisting of nearly horizontal strata. To the south, the plateau is buttressed by the high chains of the Anti-Taurus and the Taurus, which form a crescent facing south. The Taurus range extends in a broken, irregular line from the Persian frontier to the Aegean. Within the main Taurus range, extensive upland basin-shaped valleys known as *vailas* are found.

## Northern Mountains and Coastal Features
The plateau is supported to the north by a range of varying altitude that follows the southern coast of the Black Sea; this range has no distinctive name. This system is divided into the West Pontian and East Pontian arcs. In the east, a well-defined mountain system runs from Sinope to Batum, nearly parallel to the coast, forming a gentle curve with its convexity facing south. These mountains are largely composed of serpentine and Cretaceous limestones.

The northern coast lacks considerable plains and good harbors, with the exceptions of Vona and Sinope, and is characterized by the deltas of the Yeshil and Kizil Irmaks. In contrast, the western edge of the plateau is broken by broad valleys and a deeply indented coastline with long rocky promontories and a fringe of protecting islands. The western coast features deep gulfs and narrow plains of limited extent. To the south, the coastline includes the broad bay of Adalia, the deep-seated Gulf of Alexandretta, and the isolated plains of Cilicia and Pamphylia.

## Geology and Volcanism
The surrounding mountain chains of Asia Minor form a complex system of intensely folded beds. Along the south-eastern margin, in front of the Taurus, is a line of now-extinct volcanoes stretching from Argaeus to Kara-Dagh, which were likely active until the end of the Tertiary period. 

Various geological deposits are found across the region:
* **Carboniferous rocks:** Present at Eregli (Heraclea Pontica), where they are worked for coal.
* **Devonian fossils:** Located near the Bosporus.
* **Carboniferous fossils:** Found at Balia Maden in Mysia.
* **Mesozoic band:** Triassic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous beds form a band south of the Sea of Marmora and along the Black Sea coast.

## Nomenclature and Administration
The term "Asia Minor" was not used by classical geographers, appearing first in the 5th century A.D. in the work of Orosius. It likely emerged from a popular distinction between the continent and the Roman province of "Asia," which once included most of the peninsula west of the central salt desert (*Axylon*). 

The term *Anatolia* (or *Anadol*) is used by natives to refer to the western part of the peninsula (*cis Halym*), excluding Pontus and ancient Cappadocia. This term first appears in the 10th century in the work of Constantine Porphyrogenitus. Historically, Anatolia was the official title of the principal *eyalet* of Asia Minor, under the military jurisdiction of the beylerbey of Anadoli, who was based at Kutaiah.
