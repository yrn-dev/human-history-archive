# Grand Morin and Petit Morin

The Grand Morin and Petit Morin are rivers in northern France that serve as chief tributaries to the Marne.

## Geographic Context and the Brie Region
The Grand Morin and Petit Morin are primary rivers within the Brie, an agricultural district located to the east of Paris. The Brie is bounded to the north by the Marne and to the south and west by the Seine. This region comprises the majority of the department of Seine-et-Marne, as well as portions of the departments of Aube, Marne, Aisne, Seine-et-Oise, and Seine.

The Brie is characterized as a plateau with few eminences, with altitudes ranging from 300 to 500 feet in the western portion (known as the *Brie française*) and between 500 and 650 feet in the eastern portion (the *Brie champenoise*). The region's surface soil consists of clay containing fragments of siliceous sandstone, which are utilized for construction and millstones, while the subsoil is composed of limestone. Despite the presence of the Grand Morin, Petit Morin, and the Yères (a tributary of the Seine), the region is not abundantly watered, with rainfall measuring only between 20 and 24 inches.

## The Marne River System
The Grand Morin and Petit Morin flow into the Marne, a river that rises on the Plateau of Langres, 3 miles south-by-east of Langres. The Marne flows northward passing Chaumont before turning west into the department of Marne, where it waters the wide plain of Champagne-Pouilleuse and the Perthois. After receiving the Blaise and the Saulx, it passes through Châlons and Epernay.

As the Marne continues its course through the departments of Aisne (passing through Chateau-Thierry) and Seine-et-Marne (where it drives the mills of Meaux), it receives its chief tributaries in those departments: the Grand-Morin, the Petit-Morin, and the Ourcq. The Marne eventually unites with the Seine at Charenton, an eastern suburb of Paris. The total length of the Marne is 328 miles, with a basin area of 4,894 square miles.

## Topography of the Marne Valley
Upon emerging from the plains of Champagne at Epernay, the Marne flows through the plateaus of the Ile de France toward Paris. In this section, the river has carved a path through the solid mass of the plateau, creating a valley between 500 and 2,000 meters in width. The valley walls, which average approximately 150 meters in height, are intersected by the ravines and valleys of numerous tributary streams, resulting in a landscape of deep hollows, bastions, and capes. This topographical variety provides significant military advantages and creates a charming landscape of terraces and winding curves.
