# Balkan Peninsula

## Geography and Boundaries
The Balkan Peninsula is the most easterly of the three large peninsulas forming the southern extremities of the European continent. It possesses an area of 184,779 sq. m., which is more than twice the size of the Italian Peninsula and approximately 35,000 sq. m. less than the Iberian Peninsula. In its general contour, the peninsula resembles a triangle or an inverted pyramid, ending at its apex in the subsidiary peninsula known as the Morea or Peloponnesus.

The boundaries of the peninsula are defined by several bodies of water and river courses. To the east, it is bounded by the Aegean, the Sea of Marmora, and the Black Sea; to the south by the Mediterranean; and to the west by the Adriatic and Ionian Seas. Its northern boundary extends from the Adriatic Sea near Fiume to the Kilia mouth of the Danube and is generally regarded as being marked by the courses of the Kulpa, Save, and Danube rivers. Except for the Albanian littoral and the Black Sea coast, the shores are flanked by groups of islands and are considerably indented.

## Topography and Hydrography
The surface of the Balkan Peninsula is almost entirely mountainous. The only extensive plains are the basin of Thessaly, which is drained by the Salambria (anciently known as the Peneus), and the plains formed by the valleys of the Maritza and the Danube. The Danubian plain is enclosed to the south by the Balkans—from which the peninsula takes its name—and to the north by the Carpathians; however, this plain lies for the most part outside the peninsula.

The mountain systems of the region are complex. The Dinaric Alps and the parallel ranges of Bosnia generally run from north-west to south-east. In the center of the peninsula, the great chain of Rhodope traverses the land, extending spurs toward the Aegean and the Black Sea. Further west are the mountains of Albania and Montenegro, as well as the lofty Shar Dagh, which are continued by the heights of Aetolia and Acarnania and the Pindus range.

The principal summits include:
* Olympus: 9,794 ft. (overlooking the Gulf of Salonica)
* Musalla: 9,631 ft. (Rhodope system)
* Liubotrn: 8,989 ft. (Shar Dagh)
* Popova Shapka: 8,855 ft. (Rhodope system)
* Elin: 8,794 ft. (Perin Planina)
* Belmeken: 8,562 ft. (chain of Dospat in southern Bulgaria)

## Geology of the Region
The stratified formation of the area presents a remarkable variety. The Archean system, consisting of crystalline schists and gneiss traversed by eruptive veins, extends over the Rhodope, the Rilska Planina, and the greater part of the Eastern Rumelian plain. North of the Balkans, this system appears only near Berkovitza.

The western Balkans contain Carboniferous beds of sandstone and argillite where anthracitiferous coal is found. The Permian system, represented by conglomerate and red sandstone, appears particularly around the basin of Sofia. Mesozoic deposits, ranging from the upper Jurassic to the Trias, occur in the central part of the range and the western Balkans. The Cretaceous system is found throughout Northern Bulgaria, from the Danube to the summits of the Balkans.

Other geological features include:
* **Flysch:** Widely distributed, representing the Eocene and Cretaceous systems.
* **Tertiary Deposits:** The Eocene appears with nummulitic formations on both sides of the eastern Balkans. The Neogene (younger Tertiary) appears near Pleven in Tegel and Leithakalk forms, and between Burgas and Varna with beds of spaniodons. The Sarmatian stage is found in the districts of Varna, Silistria, and the plain of the Danube.
* **Quaternary Era:** Represented by typical loess covering most of the Danubian plain, as well as alluvial deposits in riparian districts containing remains of *Equus* and *Ursus*.

## Historical Context
The region has been the site of significant migrations and conflicts. The Bulgarians, described by some as a branch of the Ougres and by others as having more resemblance to the Turks, were of the Finnish race. After being driven from possessions beyond the Caspian and occupying part of Armenia north of the Araxes, part of the nation moved westward to modern Bulgaria. By approximately the year 500, they reached the Danube and subdued the Slavo-Servians established on the Lower Danube. Although conquered by the Avars, they freed themselves in 635. The resulting Danubian Bulgaria remained a formidable entity to the Byzantine empire for a long period.

In the middle ages, the vilayet formed part of the Servian Empire. A significant event in Balkan history occurred in 1389 at the plain of Kossovo (Kossovopolje), a long valley watered by the Sibnitza (a tributary of the Servian Ibar). The battle of Kossovo resulted in the destruction of the power of Servia by the Turks.
