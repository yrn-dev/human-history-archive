# The Great Central Depression of Asia

The great central depression is a vast lateral division of the Asian continent, situated along the parallel of 40 degrees north. It extends westwards from the east of the Gobi desert through Mongolia and into Chinese Turkestan. This region is characterized by its low altitude and unique hydrographic properties, serving as a central section of the continent where drainage is typically lost in swamps, deserts, or *hamuns*.

## Geography and Boundaries
The depression is bounded to the west by the great elevation of the Pamir and the meridional range of Sarikol, with the Sarikol serving as the eastern face of the Pamir. To the north, the depression is separated from the northern section of the continent by a great central water-parting or divide. This divide stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush, the Elburz, and the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, eventually reaching the Bering Strait in the extreme north-east. 

To the south, the depression is separated from the low plains of India, Siam, China, and the Amur by another mighty hydrographic barrier. This southern barrier is described as one of the world's most significant upheavals. In the narrower geographical context of the Kuen-lun mountain ranges, the depression of Tsaidam (approximately 95° E) marks an eastern extent of the mountain system.

## Physical Characteristics and Elevation
The average level of the central depression probably does not exceed 2,000 feet above sea level. In the central portions of the Gobi, specifically around Lop Nor, modern research suggests the land may actually be below sea level. The region between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the sources of the Hwang-ho also generally remains below 2,000 feet.

The depression forms a vast, almost waterless interior space. In east Persia, central depressions with a mean elevation of barely more than 500 feet above the Caspian are found in the Great Kavir. These areas are characterized by saline streams that create dangerous, slimy quagmires covered in brine during winter and a treacherous salt crust during summer.

## Geological and Historical Evolution
Evidence suggests that the central depression was once a vast inland sea. In the region reaching from the foot of the Pamir plateau through the Tarim desert to Lop Nor and the Gobi, researchers have uncovered various secrets of the land's history. Sven Hedin revealed the periodic shifting of the Lop Nor system, while both Hedin and Dr. M.A. Stein demonstrated that the sand-strewn wastes of the Turkestan desert once housed highly civilized centers of industry and Buddhist art.

Regarding the depressions westward of the Aral and Caspian basins, Russian scientists have hypothesized that the Caspian Sea extended eastwards into Trans-Caspian territory during the post-Pleiocene age. This theory is supported by the discovery of shells—currently living in the Caspian—found 100 meters inland at altitudes between 140 and 280 feet above the current Caspian level. Additionally, the Kara-kum sands appear to be a purely marine formation, lacking the fluviatile deposits that would indicate they were delta deposits of the Oxus.

## Exploration and Mapping
Knowledge of the regions of the great central depression and the Pamir highlands was limited until the late 19th century. Early European pioneers included Robert Barkley Shaw and George Hayward, who arrived at Yarkand in 1868. In 1870, Sir Douglas Forsyth led a mission to Yarkand, during which Henry Trotter produced the first maps of Chinese Turkestan.

Further geographical data was acquired via the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission (1884–1886), where Sir T.H. Holdich, Sir Adelbert Talbot, and Colonel St George Gore mapped the Oxus regions and Afghan Turkestan. During this period, Ney Elias crossed from China through Badakshan and the Pamirs, identifying the "Dragon Lake" (Rangkul). Other notable explorations include:
* **Captain (Sir William) Lockhart:** Crossed the Hindu Kush into Wakhan.
* **Colonel Woodthorpe:** Verified the explorations of W.W. M'Nair regarding the conformation of the Hindu Kush.
* **Arthur Douglas Carey and Andrew Dalgleish (1885):** Contributed new data to the map of Asia by following the tracks of Prjevalsky.
* **Captain (Sir Francis) Younghusband (1886):** Crossed the Muztagh, the mountain barrier between Kashmir and China.
* **Dr. Sven Hedin:** Explored the central depressions of the Great Kavir in 1906.

## Regional Connectivity
When dividing the continent into three sections—the northern section (average altitude under 5,000 ft), the central depression (average level under 2,000 ft), and the southern section (comprising high table-lands and low peninsulas)—there is only one direct connection between the north and south that avoids formidable mountain barriers. This connection is the narrow valley of the Hari Rud, located to the west of Herat. This route allows passage from the Caspian to Karachi without encountering an orographic obstacle greater than the divide separating the Hari Rud valley from the Helmund *hamun* basin, which stands at approximately 4,000 feet above sea level.
