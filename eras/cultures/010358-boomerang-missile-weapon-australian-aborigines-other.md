# BOOMERANG

The boomerang is a missile weapon utilized by the Australian aborigines and other peoples. The term originates from a native name used by a single tribe in New South Wales; it was identified by Captain King in 1827 as "the Port Jackson term." Contrary to some beliefs, the word is not onomatopoeic, as the weapon whistles in the air rather than making a "boom" sound. Additionally, it has been incorrectly associated with the *womera*, or spear-thrower.

## Types and Distribution
There are two primary types of boomerangs: the return boomerang and the non-return (or war) boomerang. Both varieties are found across most regions of Australia. Beyond Australia, similar weapons have been identified in other parts of the world:
* **Africa:** General Pitt-Rivers noted the use of the return form in ancient Egypt. A closely resembling weapon persists in North-East Africa, which has evolved into allied metal forms known as throwing knives.
* **India:** Among the Dravidians of South India, there is a boomerang-shaped instrument capable of returning, though it may be constructed from ivory or steel.
* **North America:** The Hopis (Moquis) of Arizona utilize a non-return form.

It remains uncertain if the instruments found in India and Egypt possess a genuine resemblance to the Australian return boomerang.

## Physical Characteristics
The general form of both the return and non-return boomerangs is sickle-shaped and typically made of wood. Their dimensions generally follow a specific ratio where the thickness is approximately 1/6th of the breadth, and the breadth is 1/12th of the length. Lengths vary from 6 inches to 3 or 4 feet.

The return boomerang typically measures 2 to 3 feet in length and weighs around 8 ounces. While it may feature two straight arms at an angle between 70 and 120 degrees, in Australia it is always curved at an angle of 90 degrees or more. A critical feature is the "skew," where the arms are twisted 2 or 3 degrees from the center plane. The cross-section is asymmetrical, featuring a convex upper side and a flat or nearly flat lower side. This specific design requires the weapon to be thrown with the right hand. The non-return boomerang is similar in construction but possesses a skew in the opposite direction.

## Flight and Mechanics
The flight of the boomerang is primarily dependent on its skew. To throw a return boomerang, the user holds it vertically with the concave side forward, throwing it in a plane parallel to the ground while imparting as much rotation as possible. 

The flight path typically follows these stages:
1. The weapon travels straight for 30 yards or more with nearly vertical rotation.
2. It then inclines to the left, rising in the air and lying over on its flat side.
3. After describing a circle with a diameter of 50 yards or more, it returns to the thrower.

Australian natives are capable of throwing the weapon so that it travels 100 yards or more before the leftward curve begins, reaching heights of 150 feet and circling five times before returning. While some observers claim it returns after striking an object, it is confirmed that the weapon can strike the ground without affecting its return.

The non-return type is normally thrown like the return type to achieve immense distances. However, it can be made to return in a nearly straight line if thrown at an angle of 45 degrees. While accurate measurements for Australian throws are unavailable, one recorded English throw reached 180 yards, significantly further than the same thrower's 70-yard distance with a cricket ball.

## Usage and Application
The different types of boomerangs serve distinct purposes:
* **War Boomerang:** In the hands of an expert, the war boomerang is a deadly weapon.
* **Hunting Boomerang:** The lighter version used for hunting is also effective.
* **Return Boomerang:** This type is primarily used for killing birds or as a plaything. Because of its flight path, it is often as dangerous to the person throwing it as it is to the intended target.
