# Russian History and Geography

## Geography of Siberia
Siberia comprises the portion of Asia located between the Arctic Ocean and the mountainous belt that bounds Turkestan, Mongolia, and Manchuria to the north. The region is characterized by an immense, broken high plateau that extends from the south-west to the north-east, decreasing in both altitude and width as it advances. This plateau is bordered by high ridges that transition into high plains (1,500 to 2,000 ft. high) in the north-west, eventually descending into lowlands a few hundred feet above sea level.

The environment is marked by extreme heat and cold. While rainfall is not heavy, it is sufficient to support vegetation compatible with the temperature, often resulting in peaty or swampy surfaces. Pine forests commonly cover the mountain-sides, while shrubs or grasses cover the plains. The region is traversed by some of the world's largest rivers, specifically the Lena, Yenisei, and Ob.

## Population and Indigenous Races
The population of Siberia is very scanty, and cultivated tracts are restricted to more settled districts. While the towns are entirely Russian, the indigenous races are nomadic Mongols. These populations are described as being of a peaceful character, though in a very backward state of civilization.

In the broader context of northern Asia, Mongolians are almost entirely hunters, shepherds, or herdsmen. Those occupying Siberia are considered the least advanced but the most peaceful of these groups. Further south, the Turks, Moguls, Mongols proper, and Manchus—all known as Tatars and referred to by ancients as Scythians—occupy the zone between the 40th and 50th circles of north latitude.

## The Rise of the Russian Empire
Historically, in the empire of Kipchak and Turkestan, nomadic populations roamed wide areas while settled populations in towns such as Moscow and Kieff sustained a life of pre-Mongol Christianity. These towns were governed by Tartar governors or Russian dukes who collected tribute for the Khan of the Golden Horde.

The Grand Duke of Moscow eventually gained the Khan's confidence and established ascendancy over other tributaries. In 1480, under Ivan III (Ivan the Great, r. 1462–1505), Moscow refused to pay tribute and threw off its Mongol allegiance. Ivan III claimed to be the heir to Byzantium due to his 1472 marriage to Zoe Palæologus of the imperial line, adopting the Byzantine double-headed eagle for his arms. The foundations of the modern Russian Empire were further laid when Moscow subjugated the Northman trading republic of Novgorod, establishing a link to Baltic mercantile life.

Ivan III's grandson, Ivan IV (Ivan the Terrible, r. 1533–1584), assumed the imperial title of Tsar (Cæsar). Despite this title, the tradition of the ruler of Moscow remained autocratic after an unlimited Asiatic pattern, reflecting Tartar rather than European influence. The state practiced the "orthodox" Eastern form of Christianity, which had been introduced to Russia via Bulgarian missionaries from Constantinople prior to the Mongol conquest.

## Mining and Trade
The Ural Mountains, with an average altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 ft. and highest summits not exceeding 6,000 ft., contain Russia's chief mining districts in their southern half. 

Russian trade also extends into neighboring regions. For example, Russia imports cattle, wool, and furs from the district of Kobdo in north-west Mongolia. In exchange, Russia exports iron, copper goods, leather, cotton, velveteen, and boxes.

## Ethnic Distinctions and Terminology
A distinction exists between the "Kazak" and the "Cossack." The term Kazak, interpreted as "riders," was used by steppe nomads and first referenced by the historian Firdousi in 1020 to describe dreaded mounted marauders. This term eventually spread to South Russia as "Cossack" (spelt Kazak or Kozak in Russian). While the two words share an origin, "Kazak" now designates a Mongolo-Tatar nomad race, whereas "Cossack" refers to various members of the Slav family. Since the 18th century, Russians have used the term "Kirghiz-Kazak" to distinguish the nomads from their own Cossacks who were overrunning Siberia.
