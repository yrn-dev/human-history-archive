# Bonfires in Cornwall and European Tradition

## The Nature and Use of Bonfires
The term "bonfire" has historically encompassed several distinct meanings. These include fires used for corpses or funeral piles, fires of bones, and fires used for immolation, such as those employed to burn proscribed books and heretics. Additionally, the term refers to large fires lit in the open air to serve as signals of alarm—such as those used to warn England of the approaching Armada—or as celebrations of national rejoicing.

## European Customs and Origins
Throughout Europe, peasants have practiced the lighting of bonfires on specific days of the year since time immemorial, often accompanying the fires with dancing or leaping over the flames. These customs are traceable to the Middle Ages, and their similarity to certain ancient usages suggests that the bonfire originated in the early days of heathen Europe.

Evidence of these ceremonies is found in the efforts of Christian synods during the 7th and 8th centuries to suppress them as pagan rites. For example, the 65th canon of the third council of Constantinople (A.D. 680) commanded an end to the "ridiculously and foolishly" performed custom of leaping over fires kindled before houses and shops on new moons. Similarly, in A.D. 742, the Synodus Francica under Pope Zachary forbade "sacrilegious fires" referred to as *Nedfri* (or bonefires), along with other pagan observations.

Ancient parallels are also noted in Ovid's *Fasti*, which describes the Palilia (the feast of the shepherds' goddess Pales), where shepherds would light heaps of straw and jump over them as they burned.

## Bonfires in Cornwall
In Cornwall, the practice of lighting bonfires is observed on the eve of St Peter's day and the eve of St John the Baptist. Because of these traditions, midsummer is referred to in the Cornish language as *Goluan*, a term that signifies both "festivity" and "light."

The lighting of bonfires during Christian festivals is viewed as a compromise made by the early Church with heathen traditions. In some instances, these fires involve the burning of effigies or a pretence of burning a living person. There are further grounds for belief that, in ancient times, actual human sacrifices were conducted within these bonfires. These events typically occur during midsummer and spring.
