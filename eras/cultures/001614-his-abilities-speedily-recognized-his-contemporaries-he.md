# Recognition of Ability and Talent in History

Across various eras and disciplines, the recognition of intellectual, military, and administrative ability by contemporaries has served as a primary marker of historical significance. This recognition often manifests through professional advancement, public esteem, or the acknowledgment of rivals.

## Intellectual and Academic Recognition

In the academic spheres of Europe, early recognition of talent often led to prestigious associations. Roger Bacon, an English philosopher and man of science born near Ilchester in Somerset, saw his abilities speedily recognized by his contemporaries. This recognition allowed him to enjoy the friendship of eminent figures such as Robert Grosseteste, the bishop of Lincoln, and Adam de Marisco. Bacon completed his studies at Oxford before traveling to France to study at the university of Paris, which was the intellectual center of Europe at the time.

Similarly, Herbert Henry Asquith demonstrated distinguished intellectual ability during his time as a scholar at Balliol, Oxford. As president of the Union, he impressed his contemporaries, including Dr. Jowett, who predicted Asquith's signal success in any chosen career. After leaving Oxford, Asquith was recognized as a lawyer of unusual clarity and mental distinction, though his forensic success was less conspicuous than that of some of his peers. His vigorous ability as a speaker and sound political judgment eventually attracted Mr. Gladstone, leading to his appointment as home secretary in the new Liberal ministry in August 1892.

## Political and Administrative Ability

In the history of Greece and Rome, ability was often weighed against personal integrity and policy. Aristeidês was recognized by his contemporaries, including the poet Timokreon and the allies of Athens, for his incomparable integrity and public and private probity. While he was considered inferior to Themistoklês in flexibility, resource, and power in coping with difficulties, his reputation for being "the Just" earned him a firmer hold on public esteem than eminent talents alone could provide.

In Rome, Julius Cæsar was recognized for a rare combination of administrative talent, intellectual power, literary acquirements, and bodily activity. Even his enemies, such as Cicero, did not refuse to testify to his generous character and great abilities. Cicero specifically ranked Cæsar in the first class of orators, and his writings on the Civil War and the Gallic Campaign are noted as literary monuments that distinguish him from other commanders.

## Military Leadership and Conquest

Military ability has frequently been recognized through the scale of conquest and the organization of forces. Philip of Macedon possessed a rare political and military talent, characterized by indefatigable personal activity, adventurous courage, and restless ambition. He transformed the Macedonian kingdom from a narrow territory around Pella into a supremacy that extended from the Ionian Sea to the coasts of the Propontis. His ability to organize an efficient army, utilizing the best processes of scientific warfare from Iphikrates and Epaminondas, created a military engine later bequeathed to Alexander.

In the Americas, the successes of Cortes were attributed to his valour, genius, and unscrupulous policy. The ability he displayed in his conquests excited the admiration of his countrymen in Spain and effaced previous censures regarding the irregularity of his operations. This recognition led Charles V to appoint him captain-general and governor of Mexico.

## Specialized Skills and Technical Progress

Recognition of ability also extends to specialized cultural pastimes and technical innovations. In the Muromachi epoch of Japan, the pastime of *ko-awase* (comparing incense) tested a player's knowledge of ancient literature and their ability to recognize different varieties of incense by odour. By the sixteenth century, Shino Soshin converted this pastime into a philosophy, leading to the recognition and naming of sixty-six distinct kinds of incense based on literary allusions.

In the realm of technology, the introduction of steel springs to wheel carriages around 1670 was speedily recognized for its practical advantages. This innovation reduced vibration, allowed for lighter carriage construction, and lessened the draught required from horses.
