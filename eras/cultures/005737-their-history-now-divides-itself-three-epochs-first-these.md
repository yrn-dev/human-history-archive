# Historical Epochs and Periodizations

The division of history into epochs is a recurring method used by historians and scholars to categorize the development of nations, arts, and natural history. These divisions often mark significant shifts in political power, cultural expression, or scientific understanding.

## National and Political Epochs

Different civilizations have defined their histories through distinct temporal divisions. In Japanese history, one of the most sharply defined periods is the Heian epoch. This era spanned 398 years, beginning in 794 with the transfer of the Imperial palace from Nara to Kyoto and ending in 1192 with the establishment of an administrative capital at Kamakura. The term "Heian-jo," meaning "Castle of Peace," was given to Kyoto shortly after it became the residence of the Mikado. The first ruler of this epoch was Kwammu, a prince selected by his father, Konin, at the suggestion of Fujiwara Momokawa.

In Persian history, a specific period of 117 years is recorded, covering the reigns of six kings: Darius (son of Hystaspes), Xerxes I, Artaxerxes Longimanus, Xerxes II, Sogdianus, and Darius Nothus. This history begins in the year of the world 3483 and ends in 3600. This period is divided into two parts; the first consists of 90 years, extending from the start of Darius I's reign to the 42nd year of Artaxerxes, coinciding with the start of the Peloponnesian war. This first part focuses on Persian expeditions against Greece, including battles such as Marathon, Salamis, and Thermopylæ.

Other historical narratives divide their subject into three distinct epochs based on behavioral and political shifts. One such history describes a first epoch extending from a period of rise to the 1671 capture of Panama by Morgan, characterized by a lack of government aid and restriction. The second epoch lasted from 1671 to 1685, the height of their power, when operations expanded from the Caribbean to the Pacific range from Chile to California. The third and final period is described as a time of disintegration and disunion, where previous "rude honour" devolved into brutality and vice.

## Epochs of Art and Literature

The evolution of artistic and literary forms is also categorized by epochs. Greek tragedy, which remained primarily Attic, is divided into three periods. The first (535–499) preceded Aeschylus and included authors such as Thespis, Choerilus, Pratinas, and Phrynichus; the latter is noted for introducing female characters and writing the *Capture of Miletus*. The second is the Classical Period of Attic Tragedy (499–405), represented by the masters Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides.

In the history of art, the Roman epoch is noted for two currents: one originating in Rome and another in Hellenic Asia. Following the fall of Rome, the emergence of Christian civilization led to the Byzantine style of architecture, which was closely identified with the outward expression of the Christian church.

Intellectual history also recognizes epochs of poetic development. The transition from short narrative songs to the combined and continuous poems of the Homeric epoch represents a significant mental advancement. This process involved an organizing poetical mind recasting isolated materials into a larger aggregate.

## Theoretical and Natural Epochs

Some scholars have proposed broader, more theoretical frameworks for historical development. Philippe Joseph Benjamin Buchez, a French author and politician, developed a theory dividing historical development into four great epochs, each originated by a universal revelation. He further divided each epoch into three periods—desire, reasoning, and performance—and each of those into theoretical and practical ages.

In the realm of natural history, the French naturalist Buffon wrote *Epochs of Nature* in 1778. Buffon extended the estimated age of the world to 70,000 or 75,000 years. He divided his account into six epochs to align with the six days of the Creation story, arguing that these days were actually figurative ages. This approach allowed the emerging science of geology to maintain a peace with orthodox religious teaching until the mid-nineteenth century.
