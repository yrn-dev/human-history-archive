# Asia

## Geographic Form and Dimensions
Due to the vast extent of Asia, ordinary maps often provide misleading conceptions of its outline because of the distortions inherent in projecting large spherical areas onto flat surfaces. When viewed on a globe, Asia takes the form of a great isosceles spherical triangle. Its north-eastern apex is located at East Cape (Vostochnyi) in the Bering Strait. From this apex, two equal sides—each approximately 6,500 miles in length, or a quadrant of the sphere—extend to the southern point of Arabia in the west and the extremity of the Malay peninsula in the east. 

The base of this triangle, connecting the southern point of Arabia and the Malay peninsula, occupies about 60 degrees of a great circle, or 4,500 miles. This base is deeply indented by the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea on either side of the Indian peninsula.

## Boundaries and Extremities
The boundaries of Asia are defined by various natural landmarks and coordinates:

*   **Northern Boundary:** Formed by the Arctic Ocean, with the coastline falling between 70 and 75 degrees N. The extreme northern point is Cape Sivero-Vostochnyi (also known as Chelyuskin) at 78 degrees N.
*   **Southern Boundary:** The coastline is irregular, with the China Sea, the Bay of Bengal, and the Arabian Sea reaching approximately to the northern tropic at the mouths of the Canton, Ganges, and Indus rivers. The Malay peninsula extends to within 1.5 degrees of the equator, while the peninsulas of Cambodia, Hindostan, and Arabia descend to about 10 degrees N.
*   **Western Boundary:** The extreme western point is Cape Baba on the Mediterranean shore at 26 degrees E, near the Dardanelles. From there, the boundary moves in two directions: one through the Mediterranean and down the Red Sea to the southern point of Arabia (at the strait of Bab-el-Mandeb, 45 degrees E), and the other through the Black Sea and along the Caucasus range (approximately 40 degrees N) to the Caspian. From the Caspian, it turns north near the 60th meridian along the Ural Mountains to meet the Arctic Ocean opposite the island of Novaya Zemlya.
*   **Eastern Boundary:** The most easterly point is East Cape (Vostochnyi or Dezhnev) at 190 degrees E at the entrance of the Bering Strait. The boundary from this point to the Malay Peninsula follows the China Sea and the Northern Pacific coast, characterized by the recession of the Yellow Sea, the Gulfs of Siam and Tongking, and the Sea of Okhotsk, as well as the projections of the Korea and Kamchatka peninsulas.

## Regional Features and Islands
In the Bay of Bengal, the Andaman and Nicobar groups of islands are situated on a line connecting Cape Negrais (the south-western extremity of Burma) and the north end of Sumatra; these may owe their existence to volcanic agencies extending across the Indian Ocean from Sumatra. Additionally, the Maldives and Laccadives are groups of small coral islands located along the 73rd meridian near the Indian peninsula.

## Historical Cartography and Trade
Historically, cosmographers up to the middle of the 15th century often relied on traditions, classical legends, and the writings of Church Fathers such as Isidore or Orosius rather than new data from travellers. One common representation of the inhabited earth was a "circled T," where a large "O" was divided by a "T" into three parts: Asia occupied the greater half-circle, while Africa and Europe occupied the two quarter-circles.

Trade routes to Asia evolved significantly between the 15th and 17th centuries. Originally, East Indian products reached Europe via old trade routes through Asia Minor and Persia. However, the opening of the route around the Cape of Good Hope in 1498 shifted the dyewood trade to Portugal and Spain. 

The Dutch East India Company (*Oostindische Vereenigde Maatschappij*), founded by a charter from the Netherlands states-general on March 20, 1602, sought to regulate trade in the Indian Ocean and support the war of independence against Portugal and Spain. After unsuccessful attempts to find a route to the East via the north of Asia and Europe, the Dutch utilized the Cape of Good Hope route to reach the Spice Islands of the Malay Archipelago. An early expedition led by Cornelius Houtman sailed on April 2, 1595, using sailing instructions by Jan Huyghen van Linschoten, who had visited Goa. This voyage resulted in a treaty with the sultan of Bantam in Java.
