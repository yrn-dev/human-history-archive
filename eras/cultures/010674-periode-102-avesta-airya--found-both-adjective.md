# The Aryan Language Group

The Aryan group is a branch of the Indo-European languages that is easily distinguishable from other groups by four specific characteristics. First, there is a confusion of original long and short *e* and *o* sounds with the original long and short *a* sound. Second, the short schwa-sound is represented by *i* (for example, *pita* meaning "father"). Third, the original *s* becomes *s* after *u*, *i*, and certain consonants. Finally, the genitive plural of stems ending in a vowel utilizes a suffix *-nam*, which was borrowed by analogy from stems ending in *-n* (such as the Sanskrit *asvanam*, Zend *aspanam*, and Old Persian *aspanam*).

## Linguistic Divisions and Distinctions

Within the Aryan group, there are clear distinctions between the Iranian and Sanskrit languages. The voiced aspirates *gh*, *dh*, and *bh* are preserved in Sanskrit but are confused with original *g*, *d*, and *b* in Iranian. Additionally, the breathed aspirates *kh*, *th*, and *ph* generally become spirants in Iranian, unless they are combined with specific consonants. 

Another primary difference is that the Aryan *s* becomes *h* in Iranian when it appears initially before vowels or in certain medial cases, a trait that resembles Greek (e.g., the Sanskrit *sapta* compared to the Zend *hapta* and Greek *hepta*). Furthermore, Zend exhibits many vowel changes not shared with Old Persian, some of which result from epenthesis or umlaut, as seen in the word *airya-* compared to the Sanskrit *arya*.

Despite these differences, the languages remain remarkably alike. One of the few striking differences is found in the numeral for "one": Sanskrit uses *eka-*, while Zend uses *aeva-* and Old Persian uses *aiva-*. The Iranian stem in this instance is the same as that found in the Greek *oi(f)o-s*, meaning "alone."

## Etymology and Usage of "Aryan"

The term "Aryan" (Sanskrit *arya-*, Zend *airya-*, and Old Persian *ariya-*) was used as a national name in Persia, Bactria, and India. In Sanskrit, the archive distinguishes between two probable words: *arya*, which is the origin of "Aryan" and the source of the usual term *arya*, and *arya*, which appears in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet for deities. In some instances, *aryas* may be the genitive of *ari*, meaning "pious, devoted, active."

The exact etymology of the word is subject to debate. While Max Muller suggested a connection to the Sanskrit word for earth (*ira*), this is considered far from certain. Spiegel noted that while the word can be connected to a root *ar-*, there are several such roots with different meanings, and no certain criterion exists to decide which one is related. 

Other proposed connections have been dismissed or remain uncertain:
*   A connection to *Erin* (Ireland) is considered impossible.
*   The word may share an origin with the *Ario-* found in Celtic and Germanic names, such as *Ariovistus*.
*   The origin of the name Armenia (Old Persian *Armina-*) remains uncertain.

## Related Scholarship and Texts

The *Zend Avesta* is a primary source for the study of these languages. James Darmesteter, a French author and antiquarian, published a thesis on the mythology of the *Zend Avesta* in 1875 and later produced a complete translation of the text with philological and historical commentary between 1892 and 1893. Darmesteter also edited the *Zend Avesta* for Max Muller's *Sacred Books of the East*. Regarding the chronology of the texts, Darmesteter argued that the extant texts were more recent than commonly believed, placing the bulk of them in the 3rd century A.D. and the earliest in the 1st century B.C.
