# Brocher and Brocaded Textiles

The term *brocher* is used in the French language in connection with certain silk stuffs. While related to the term *brocarts*, these specific silk stuffs are not considered brocades in the same sense as *brocarts*.

## Etymology and Definition
The French word *brocher* is derived from the Latin *broccus*, which is also the root for the Italian *brocato* and the Spanish *brocar*. The term implies a form of broaching or stitching. Consequently, textile fabrics that are woven to create the appearance of broaching or stitching are termed "brocades."

## Historical Applications and Distinctions
Historical records illustrate a distinction between different types of luxury fabrics. A Spanish document from 1375 distinguishes between *brocats d'or é d'argent* and *los draps d'or é d'argent o de seda*. This difference is evident when comparing cloths of gold or Indian kincobs with Lyons silks that are *brochés* using materials such as silk, gold threads, or other substances. 

It is noted that some Persian gold and coloured silk-weavings, as well as many Indian kincobs, are frequently called brocades despite the fact that the ornamentation is not actually brocaded or *broché*, and they lack floating threads.

## Examples in Historical Records
The use of the term to describe enriched fabrics appears in various historical contexts:
* **English Wardrobe Accounts:** An entry from 1480 in the wardrobe account of King Edward IV describes "satyn broched with gold."
* **Siculo-Saracenic Weavings:** Between the 11th and 12th centuries, oriental weavers—particularly those in southern Europe, northern Africa, Syria, and Persia under Saracen domination—produced decorative stuffs that were partly *brochés* with gold threads. One specimen from the 11th or 12th century features pairs of birds and animals where only the heads are broched with gold thread.
* **Imperial Robes:** A sumptuous weaving produced at the Hotel des Tiraz in Palermo for an official robe of Henry IV (who served as emperor of the Holy Roman Empire from 1165-1197) utilized gold threads and coloured silks.

## Related Silk and Gold Stuffs
Beyond the specific French terminology of *brocher*, other rich silk and gold brocades have been recorded in historical trade:
* **Baldachini (Baudekins):** Originating from Baghdad (also known as Baudas or Baldac), these were rich silk and gold brocades. Originally made entirely of silk, they later incorporated cotton or thread (*sericum mixtum*). These fabrics were used for umbrellas and state canopies of Italian dignitaries, leading the word *Baldacchino* to eventually refer to architectural canopies. These stuffs were manufactured in Cyprus, Damas, and the province of Ahwaz, and were exported as far as England and France.
* **Asian Varieties:** In the 14th century, the *Yüan ch’ao pi shi* mentioned *Nakhut* (a kind of gold brocade) and *Nachidut* (silk interwoven with gold) sent from Baghdad to Okkodai Khan. The *Yüan shi* also refers to *na-shi-shi*, explained as *kin kin* (gold brocade). Similar stuffs, known as *Nasich* and *Naques*, were woven in provinces near Cathay. These materials appear in 14th-century French and English inventories under names such as *Nac*, *Nacques*, *Nachiz*, *Nacíz*, and *Nasís*.
