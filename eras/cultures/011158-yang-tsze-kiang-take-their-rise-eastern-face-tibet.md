# Yang-tsze-kiang

The Yang-tsze-kiang is one of the two great rivers of China. It is characterized by a vast hydrographic basin, which covers an area of 685,000 square miles.

## Origins and Upper Course
The Yang-tsze-kiang takes its rise from the south-east of the eastern face of Tibet. Within Tibet, the main stream of the river is known as the Dichu. Its primary feeder is the Ya-lung-kiang, which originates near the source of the Hwang-ho (Yellow River) and is regarded as the territorial boundary between Tibet and China.

The river's source is located in a mountain wilderness that separates China from Tibet and Burma. Specifically, the source of the Yang-tsze-kiang is found north of the point where the Tsanpo (Brahmaputra) effects a great countermarch from east to west. In the sequence of rivers originating in this region from west to east, the Yang-tsze-kiang follows the Salween and the Mekong, and precedes the Hwang-ho.

In its upper reaches, the river runs from north to south through deep gorges. The most westerly branch of the upper Yang-tsze-kiang is separated from the most eastern affluent of the Brahmaputra by a short distance of 100 miles. The river continues its course until it turns eastwards, not far north of the 25th parallel.

## Geography and Tributaries
The Yang-tsze-kiang is separated from the upper tributaries of the Hwang-ho by a border range that is likely homologous to the Great Khingan. 

In the province of Kiang-si, the river is associated with the Po-yang Lake. The Kan Kiang, the largest river in Kiang-si, flows north-east into the Po-yang Lake, which in turn joins the Yang-tsze-kiang.

## Commercial and Urban Centers
The river serves as a vital artery for trade and communication. A notable city situated on the south bank of the Yang-tsze-kiang is Kiu-kiang Fu, located in the province of Kiang-si at 29° 42´ N. and 116° 8´ E. Kiu-kiang is positioned 15 miles above the point where the Kan Kiang flows into the Yang-tsze-kiang from the Po-yang lake.

Due to its position on the Yang-tsze-kiang and its proximity to internal communication channels leading to the green-tea-producing districts of Ngan-hui and Kiang-si, Lord Elgin designated Kiu-kiang as a treaty port in 1861. However, the city's commercial success has been hindered by the fact that it stands above, rather than below, the outlet of the Po-yang lake. The foreign settlement in Kiu-kiang extends westward along the bank of the Yang-tsze-kiang and is bounded to the west by the P'un river, which flows into the Yang-tsze-kiang.
