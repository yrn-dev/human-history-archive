# THUNDER AND THE THUNDER-GOD

## The Thunder-God in Germanic and Saxon Traditions
In the religious traditions of the heathen Angli and the ancient Saxons, Thunor (also referred to as Thor) was recognized as the thunder-god. The influence of this deity is preserved in the English language, as the name for Thursday is derived from the Saxon "Thor's day." In the traditions of the ancient Saxons, the names of their own divinities were substituted for those of Greek gods when adopting the week from an Eastern nation.

In Norway, Thor was regarded as the most considered among the gods. Historical accounts describe an image of Thor adorned with gold and silver, seated within a temple. Another description of the deity's likeness notes that he was of great size, though hollow within, stood upon a high stand, and carried a hammer in his hand. This image was further adorned with gold and silver and was offered four cakes of bread and meat daily.

## Rituals and Worship
The worship of the thunder-god involved the use of temples and holy shrines. During the rule of Hakon the earl in Norway, ancient temples of Thor that had been ruined across the land were raised again. These sites served as locations where hardy warriors and shield-bearers offered the "sacrifice of gore."

In other Germanic traditions, such as those of the Angli, worship occurred at shrines located in sacred enclosures where sacrifices were offered. These societies maintained a priesthood of persons specially set apart, who were prohibited from riding except on mares and were not allowed to bear arms. While the gods were worshipped, notices of sacred stones, springs, groves, and trees are more frequent in the records than references to the gods themselves.

## Conflict and Transition to Christianity
The transition from the worship of the thunder-god to Christianity is marked by instances of iconoclasm. King Olaf, upon entering a temple, used a gold-inlaid axe to strike the image of Thor, causing it to roll from its seat. His men subsequently threw down all the other gods. Following this act, the local bondes were given the choice to either fight or accept Christianity; they chose the latter and were baptized.

## Thunder in Other Historical and Sacred Contexts
The phenomenon of thunder appears in various other historical and religious records:

**The Qur'an**
The Qur'an presents thunder as a feature of rain-laden clouds from the sky, accompanied by darkness and lightning. It describes people who, in terror of death, press their fingers into their ears to block out the stunning thunder-clap. Additionally, in the account of Musa (Moses) and the sons of Israel (Bani Israel), the text records that when the people demanded to see Allah manifestly, they were dazed with thunder and lightning as they looked on.

**Roman History**
In Roman military history, a victory by the Romans over the Quadi, Marcomanni, Vandals, Iazyges, and Sarmatians was attributed to a sudden storm. Hail and thunder confounded the enemies, leading to a triumph known as "The Miracle of the Thundering Legion." While pagan writers attributed this to the magic of an Egyptian named Arnuphis, Christians attributed it to prayer. Some sources suggest the legion was called "Thundering" or "Lightning" because their shields featured a figure of lightning.

**Other Associations**
In the Prologue of the prose Edda, there is an identification of the figure Tros with Thor. In the context of Hellenistic kings, the popular nickname *Ceraunus* translated to "Thunder."
