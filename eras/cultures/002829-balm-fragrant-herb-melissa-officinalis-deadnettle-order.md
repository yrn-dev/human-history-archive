# BALM

The term "balm" refers to several distinct botanical and chemical substances, ranging from fragrant garden herbs to oleo-resins used in perfumery and ancient incense.

## Melissa officinalis
Balm, known scientifically as *Melissa officinalis*, is a fragrant herb belonging to the Deadnettle order (*Labiatae*). It is characterized by opposite, ovate, and crenulated leaves that are wrinkled on the upper side, and it produces small flowers that are either white or spotted with rose. The plant's name is derived from the Greek word *melissa*, as it is frequently visited by bees.

Native to southern and central Europe, *Melissa officinalis* is often cultivated in gardens. It has become naturalized in the south of England and is found growing wild as a garden escape in North America. An allied species known as Bastard Balm (*Melittis Melissophyllum*) is also found in the south and south-west of England and is native to southern Europe.

## Balsams and Oleo-resins
In a strict chemical sense, a balsam is a resin or oleo-resin containing cinnamic acid, benzoic acid, or both. These substances form a distinct class based on their properties and composition. Several varieties are found in commerce:

*   **Balsam of Peru:** A thick, viscid, deep brown or black oleo-resin with a fragrant balsamic odour. It is produced by the *Myroxylon Pereirae*, a lofty leguminous tree native to a limited area in San Salvador, Central America, and introduced to Ceylon. While used in perfumery, it possesses no special medicinal virtues.
*   **Balsam of Tolu:** Produced from *Myroxylon toluiferum* in equatorial America, this brown substance is thicker than the Peru variety and becomes more solid over time. It is utilized in perfumery and as an ingredient in lozenges and cough syrups.
*   **Liquid Storax:** Also known as *styrax preparatus*, this is a soft resinous substance with a pleasing balsamic odour produced by *Liquidambar orientalis*, native to Asia Minor. It is used internally as an expectorant and externally for some parasitic skin diseases.
*   **Liquidambar Balsam:** Derived from *Liquidambar styraciflua* in Mexico and the United States, this substance contains cinnamic acid but lacks benzoic acid.

## Historical and Scriptural Uses
The Hebrew Scriptures mention several substances used for perfume or incense, including balm (Hebrew: *tsori*). This is identified as the oleo-resin of *Balsamodendron gileadense* and *Balsamodendron opobalsamum*. 

Historical accounts regarding the origin of the balsam tree vary. Some sources suggest it may have been brought into Judea from Ethiopia, Egypt, or Arabia by the queen of Sheba. While some have claimed the precious balsam was peculiar to Judea, others note that Sabea, Arabia, and Egypt also possessed it. It should be noted that certain references to "balm" or "balsam" in texts such as Genesis 37:25 and 43:11, or the "balm of Gilead" in Jeremiah 8:22, may actually refer to turpentine (the juice of the turpentine tree) rather than the precious balsam resin.
