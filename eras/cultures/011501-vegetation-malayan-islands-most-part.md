# Vegetation of the Malayan Islands

The vegetation of the Malayan Islands is characterized primarily by the flora found in the hotter and wetter regions of India. This tropical flora is continuous with that of the Malayan peninsula and islands, extending along the lower ranges of the Himalaya. However, the Malayan Islands exhibit a greater uniformity of humidity and temperature, which leads to the predominance of specific tropical forms that are not as conspicuous in India.

## Regional Characteristics and Influences
The flora of the Malayan province—which includes the Malay islands, the Malay peninsula, and the Philippines—is noted for possessing an extraordinary number of interesting and peculiar genera. While tropical plants are dominant and define the character of the flora as a whole, the region contains a heterogeneous assemblage of tropical, temperate, and alpine plants.

A significant influence on the vegetation of the islands comes from the proximity of the Australian continent. This has allowed for the partial diffusion of Australian types not seen in India. Examples of this influence include the casuarina, which is abundant on the mountains of the eastern part of Java, and various temperate Australian forms found on the higher mountains of Borneo. Australian genera further extend to the Philippines and southern China.

## Specific Flora and Distribution
Certain trees and plants are particularly characteristic of the region:
*   **Java:** The liquidambar is one of the most conspicuous trees on the island. Notably, the mountains of Java lack truly alpine flora, and *Saxifraga* is not found there.
*   **Borneo and Sumatra:** Rhododendrons occur in these regions, descending even to the level of the sea.
*   **General Tropical Forms:** Nutmeg is noted as one of the predominant tropical forms in the region.
*   **Banka:** The surface of this island is largely covered by forests where the logwood tree especially abounds.
*   **Billiton:** The forests of Billiton provide various kinds of wood for boat-building and produce sago, cocoa-nuts, and gum.

## Botanical Classification and Diversity
The region of south-eastern Asia, encompassing the Malayan peninsula, the islands, and India (from approximately the 65th to the 105th meridian), was estimated by Sir J.D. Hooker to contain between 12,000 and 15,000 indigenous plant species. 

The principal botanical orders in this region, arranged by numerical importance, are:
1. Leguminosae
2. Rubiaceae
3. Orchidaceae
4. Compositae
5. Gramineae
6. Euphorbiaceae
7. Acanthaceae
8. Cyperaceae
9. Labiatae

Within this broader region, there is significant variation between the vegetation of arid and humid areas, as well as a total difference between the flora of the plains and that of the higher mountain ranges. While Orchidaceae are universally present in large proportions, and Euphorbiaceae and Scrophulariaceae are also universally present, other groups such as Compositae, Gramineae, and Cyperaceae are comparatively rare or deficient in certain places.

## Economic and Vegetable Products
The vegetation of the islands supports various trades and industries. In the kingdom of Boni, the Bugis people carry on a considerable trade in vegetable products, specifically camphor and nutmegs. On the island of Billiton, the forests are utilized for boat-building materials and the export of sago and cocoa-nuts.
