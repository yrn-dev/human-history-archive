# Districts of Persian Baluchistan and Related Terms

## Administrative Divisions of Persian Baluchistan
Persian Baluchistan is a province of Persia located along the northern shore of the Arabian Sea. It encompasses an area of approximately 60,000 square miles and is bordered to the west by Kerman, to the north by the central Persian desert and Seistan, and to the east by independent Baluchistan and British Baluchistan. The geography of the region is characterized by barren hills, mountains, and arid, waterless plains consisting of stony deserts and moving sands. Water is scarce, and only a small portion of the land is cultivated. The primary rivers are the Bampur and the Mashkid, which flow away from the sea and disappear into depressions known as *hamuns*; other rivers that flow into the sea are generally unimportant and remain dry for most of the year.

Administratively, Persian Baluchistan is a division of the province of Kerman. It is subdivided into twenty districts. These districts include:

*   Makran (described as the country of the Ichthyophagi, which further contains the subdistricts of Bahu, Dasht, Bint, Geh, and Kasrkand)

The total population of Baluchistan is under 200,000. While the province was practically independent for a time, Persian troops occupied Bampur in 1849, and Persian supremacy was not recognized over some of the extreme eastern districts until 1872.

## Historical Figures Named Magas
Separate from the administrative district in Baluchistan, historical records identify a Magas who was the brother of Ptolemæus Philopator. This Magas possessed significant influence with the army due to his mother. Because of this influence, the king feared Magas and attempted to use Kleomenes—whom the king had made a member of his secret council—to kill his brother. Despite pressure from the court, Kleomenes refused the request, stating that his duty was to raise more brothers for the king to strengthen the throne.

## Cultural and Linguistic Uses of "Sib"
The term "sib" appears in different contexts within the archive's records:

**Tartar Customs**
Among the Tartars, there is a custom regarding children who die before marriage. If a man's daughter and another man's son both die unmarried, the parents may arrange a wedding between the deceased. They create a formal contract and burn the papers so the parties in the other world are aware of the marriage. Following this ritual, the parents consider themselves "sib" to one another, as if their children had lived and married. Dowries agreed upon for these unions are painted on paper and burned so the dead person receives the articles in the other world.

**Old English Context**
In a specimen of English from approximately 1140 A.D., the term "sib" is used in a political context. Following a conflict between the king and the earl Henri (who had married the queen of France and acquired Peitou), the archbishop and wise men mediated a settlement. It was agreed that the king would remain lord and king during his life, and after his death, Henri would become king. The text notes that the king held Henri as a son, and Henri held the king as a father, and that "sib" and search should be between them throughout all England.
