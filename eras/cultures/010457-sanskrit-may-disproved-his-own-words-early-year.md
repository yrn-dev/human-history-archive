# Sanskrit

Sanskrit is an ancient language that serves as a primary subject of comparative philology and the foundation for various Indo-Aryan languages. It has been extensively studied by scholars to deduce original grammatical structures, trace phonetic laws, and investigate the origin of grammatical forms.

## Comparative Philology and Scholarship
The study of Sanskrit has been central to the development of comparative grammar. Franz Bopp, appointed to the chair of Sanskrit and comparative grammar at Berlin in 1821, produced several significant works, including the *Ausfuhrliches Lehrgebaude der Sanskrita-Sprache* (1827) and a Sanskrit and Latin glossary (1830). Bopp's most extensive effort was his *Comparative Grammar* (published in six parts between 1833 and 1852), which compared Sanskrit with Zend, Greek, Latin, Lithuanian, Old-Slavonic, Gothic, and German. This work was translated into English by E.B. Eastwick in 1845 and into French by Professor Michel Breal in 1866.

Other notable scholars include Arthur Coke Burnell (1840–1882), an English Sanskrit scholar who collected 350 manuscripts for the India library and published the *Classified Index to the Sanskrit MSS. in the Palace at Tanjore* in 1880. Max Muller also contributed significantly to the field, illustrating Sanskrit usage in his works on comparative philology and the science of language.

## Linguistic Influence and Derivatives
Sanskrit is closely linked to several other languages and dialects:

*   **Prakrits:** The vocabulary of Sauraseni Prakrit was the same as that of Sanskrit, allowing for phonetic development. Maharastri, a form of Prakrit spoken in the great Aryan kingdom extending from the Vindhya range to the Kistna, was used for lyric poetry, formal epics (*kavya*), dramatic works, and non-canonical Jaina scriptures.
*   **Marathi:** The word "Marathi" is the modern form of the Sanskrit *Maharastri*. Marathi incorporates *tatsamas* (words borrowed from Sanskrit) to add dignity to sentences, though it also contains a large proportion of *desyas* (words strange to Sanskrit).
*   **Bengali:** In literary Bengali, 80 to 90% of the vocabulary is pure Sanskrit.
*   **Bihari:** The Bihari vocabulary contains *tatsamas* borrowed from Sanskrit in modern times, though these are few in number compared to home-born *tadbhavas*.

## Vocabulary and Etymology
Sanskrit provides the root for various terms and concepts:
*   **Banyan:** The term "Banyan" is an Arab corruption borrowed by the Portuguese from the Sanskrit *vanij*, meaning "merchant."
*   **Aryan:** The word *arya* is used as a national name in India, Bactria, and Persia. Within Sanskrit, a distinction is made between *arya* (the origin of "Aryan") and *arya*, which appears in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet of deities. Some interpret *aryas* as the genitive of *ari*, meaning "active, devoted, pious."
*   **Religious Terms:** Philology has revealed that many Aryan peoples used a word for a high god derived from a root meaning "bright," appearing in Sanskrit as *Dyaus*.

## Grammatical Features
Sanskrit grammar includes specific suffixes and stylistic variations. For example, the suffix *tyakas* (Pr. *cao*) can be added to either the unmodified base of a word or to the locative (e.g., *ghotaka-tyakas* or *ghotake-tyakas*). 

Historically, Sanskrit writers recognized different regional styles, contrasting the "flowery complexity" of eastern India with the "simple elegance" of the Deccan, also known as the *Vaidarbhi* style.
