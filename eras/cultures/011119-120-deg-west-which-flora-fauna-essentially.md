# Wallace's Line and Regional Flora and Fauna

## Wallace's Line
In the region between the Asiatic continent and the islands of the Malay archipelago, which extends from Sumatra to New Guinea, old geographers did not establish a distinctly marked boundary. However, modern science has identified a line of physical separation located along the Straits of Macassar, the channel between Celebes and Borneo. This boundary follows approximately 120 deg. E. 

To the west of this line, the flora and fauna are essentially Asiatic in type. To the south and east of this line, the Australian element becomes distinctly marked and eventually predominant. This boundary is known as Wallace's line, named after A.R. Wallace, the eminent naturalist who first indicated its existence.

## Flora and Fauna of Asia
The distribution of life forms in Asia is closely dependent on climatic conditions. In northern Asia, the flora is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, though they differ in species rather than genera. Notable characteristics include the absence of all heaths and the oak east of the Ural. The primary trees on the mountains are birch, larch, and pines, while poplars, alders, and willow are found on lower ground. In Siberia, the northern limit of the pine is approximately 70 deg. N.

A flora that is essentially European in character extends along the warm temperate zone from the Himalaya to the Mediterranean. While many European species reach the central Himalaya, few are known in the eastern parts. Conversely, Japanese and Chinese forms are diffused along this zone, appearing most numerous in the eastern Himalaya and less frequent in the west.

Additionally, the rainless region of south-western Asia possesses a flora continuous with that of northern and eastern Africa. This region extends from the coast of Senegal to the meridian of 75 deg. E., encompassing the peninsula of Arabia, south Persia, Afghanistan, Baluchistan, and the shores of the Persian Gulf. Its western limit reaches the Cape Verde Islands, and it is partially represented in Abyssinia.

## Other Global Biological Boundaries and Distributions
Various other geographical features serve as boundaries for the distribution of species:

*   **Africa:** The Zambezi river forms a definite boundary line for many species of flora and fauna. Because of this, Barotseland is considered to essentially belong to Central Africa rather than South Africa. The river has also prevented the spread of lung sickness and tick fever, which are disastrous cattle diseases in South Africa, into Barotseland.
*   **Greenland:** While it was once believed that the flora and fauna of Greenland were essentially European, evidence suggests otherwise. Land mammals such as the white polar wolf, the banded lemming (*Cuniculus torquatus*), the musk-ox, the dog, the Eskimo, and probably the reindeer came from America. Other mammals, including the polar fox, the Arctic hare, the stoat (*Mustela erminea*), and the polar bear, are circumpolar forms. Regarding vascular plants, there are about 310 species; forty-four are European-Asiatic and about forty are American.
*   **Europe:** In the Carpathians, vegetation is divided into four zones based on altitude: beech up to 4,000 ft., Scottish fir up to 5,000 ft., and a species of pine that disappears at 6,000 ft., leaving a zone of bare rock or moss and lichen. The fauna includes lynxes, wolves, and bears. In the Balearic Isles, the flora and fauna resemble those of the Mediterranean coasts of France or Spain.

## Regional Characteristics in the Americas
In Central America, Honduras possesses fauna and flora similar to its neighboring countries. In North America, the primeval state of Kentucky was well timbered. Its eastern forests are characterized by cedar, pine, walnut, tulip-tree (locally called "yellow poplar"), elm, chestnut, beech, maple, and oak. The western forests consist largely of sycamore, gum, tulip-tree, beech, walnut, chestnut, hickory, oak, ash, and cypress. Native fruits include the strawberry, raspberry, and grape.

## Historical Fauna
The distribution of reptiles is currently limited, as they are creatures that require warmth year-round and cannot withstand cold. During the Mesozoic, they existed as a "hothouse fauna" living amidst a "hothouse flora" that endured no frosts. This period marked the attainment of a real dry land flora and fauna, distinct from the swamp and mud fauna and flora of the previous Carboniferous period, when the world was ruled by the order of amphibia.
