# The Indo-Chinese Peninsula

The Indo-Chinese peninsula is a region of Southeast Asia characterized by high mountain ranges, fertile plains, and several major river systems. Its geography is defined by ranges extending south from the eastern extremity of the Tibetan mountains, between the 95th and 100th meridians. These ranges, which are likely permanently snowy up to approximately 27 deg. N., spread outward as they move south, eventually reaching the Malay peninsula, the east flank of Bengal, and Cochin-China.

## Hydrography and Major Rivers
The peninsula is defined by four great rivers: the Mekong, the Menam, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy. The valleys of these rivers form the primary territories of Burma, Siam (including Laos), and Cochin-China (including Cambodia and Tongking). 

Research into the "mountain wilderness" dividing Tibet and Burma from China has established that the Salween, Mekong, Yang-tsze-kiang, and Hwang-ho (Yellow River) all have their sources north of the point where the Tsanpo (which is the same river as the Brahmaputra) effects its great countermarch from east to west. The sources of these four rivers are arranged from west to east. South of this point, the dual sources of the Irrawaddy rise between the Salween and the eastern edge of the Brahmaputra basin. 

The Salween and Mekong rivers, along with the Yang-tsze, flow parallel to one another for roughly 300 miles within narrow, precipitous troughs before spreading apart north of the 25th parallel.

## Regional Geography and Political Divisions
The region is divided into several states and provinces:

*   **Burma:** A province of British India covering an area of 238,738 sq.m. It extends from the Pakchan river (9° 55' N) to the Kachin, Naga, and Chingpaw hills (between 27 and 28 deg. N). It is bounded to the west by the Bay of Bengal and to the east by the Mekong river. The province is divided into Lower Burma and Upper Burma (annexed January 1, 1886). The eastern portion consists of the Shan States, covering 59,915 sq.m.
*   **Siam:** This region includes Laos and is characterized by well-watered, populous, and fertile lands with a climate similar to eastern Bengal.
*   **Cochin-China:** This area includes Tongking and Cambodia. The people of Cochin-China are known as Anam.

The Mekong river serves as a significant political boundary, limiting British interests in Burma and French interests in Tongking. For approximately 200 miles, the river defines the limits of occupation between French and British subjects.

## The Shan State of Keng Tung
Keng Tung is the most extensive of the Shan States in Burma, covering over 12,000 sq.m. It is situated primarily east of the Salween river and west of the Mekong river, the latter of which borders French Lao territory. Known to the Chinese as Mêng Kêng and to the Burmese as "the 32 cities of the Gôn," its classical name is Khemarata or Khemarata Tungkapuri.

The terrain of Keng Tung is predominantly mountainous, with peaks exceeding 7,000 ft. and elevations rarely falling below 5,000 ft. The state is split between two drainage basins: 63% lies in the Mekong basin and 37% in the Salween basin. Key tributaries of the Salween in this state include the Nam Hka and Nam Hsim, while the chief tributary of the Mekong is the Nam Lwe.

Economically, Keng Tung produces rice as its chief crop, followed by sugar-cane, tobacco, and earth-nuts in the valleys. Hill crops include tea, poppy, and cotton. The state exports cotton to China and opium to northern Siam. It is also known for the manufacture of guns in the Sam Tao district and the production of shoes and sandals made from bullock and buffalo hide.

## Culture, Language, and History
Philologists suggest that the Burmese arrived at the headwaters of the Irrawaddy as part of a vast Indo-Chinese immigration swarm from western China. The indigenous tongues of the region are categorized into several groups:
*   **Indo-Chinese:** Including the Tibet-Burman (Burmese, Kachin, and Kuki-Chin) and Siamese-Chinese (Tai and Karen) sub-families.
*   **Môn-Annam:** Including the North Cambodian and Upper Middle Mekong (or Wa Palaung) groups.
*   **Malay family:** Including the Selung language.

Historically, the peninsula hosted various expanding and contracting kingdoms. Architectural remains from the 12th to 14th centuries—such as those at Angkor in Kamboja, Ayuthia in Siam, and Pagán in Burma—indicate that these civilizations were highly advanced in wealth and art, showing strong Hindu influence combined with individual peculiarities.
