# Baluchistan

Baluchistan is a region located within the borders of British India, deriving its name from the dominant race of its inhabitants. Geographically, it extends from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea, and from the borders of Afghanistan and Persia to the borders of Sind and the Punjab. The region is characterized by a rainless environment, sharing a continuous flora with the desert regions of northern and eastern Africa, extending from the great African desert to the southern parts of the Punjab and the Indus.

## Political Status and Administration
Baluchistan is not regarded as a distinct entity among Asiatic nations in the same manner as Afghanistan. Its independence is qualified, as the region is subject to British control. The political status of the province as a whole is similar to that of the native states of the Indian peninsula, with supreme control exercised by the agent to the governor-general of India and a staff of political assistants.

The territory is divided into two primary divisions:

*   **British Baluchistan:** An official province of British India under a chief commissioner, covering an area of 9,403 sq. m. This area consists mainly of tracts transferred from Afghan authority to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, although they had nominally been handed over to Kalat forty years prior. These districts include Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali.
*   **Foreign Territories:** These areas are under the superintendence or administration of the same officer serving as agent to the governor-general. This division is further split into three subdivisions:
    *   **Directly Administered Districts:** Covering 36,401 sq. m., these include the Bolan Pass, the eastern portion of the Quetta tahsil, and the whole of the Chagai and Zhob political agencies. Some of these are tribal areas where revenue is collected, while others are held on lease from the khan of Kalat.
    *   **Native States:** Covering 79,382 sq. m., this includes Las Bela and the native states of Kalat, which includes Kharan and Makran.
    *   **Tribal Areas:** Covering 7,129 sq. m., these are in the possession of the Bugti and Marri tribes.

## Boundaries and Buffer Systems
The Baluchistan Agency extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea. To the north and east, Baluchistan serves as an intervening state or buffer between British India and Afghanistan.

The boundary between Baluchistan and Afghanistan begins at Domandi, at the junction of the Kundar and Gomal rivers. It follows the Kundar stream southwest for approximately 40 miles before diverging north toward Lake Ab-i-Istada. It then strikes the Kadanai river, turns the northern spurs of the Toba plateau (the base of the Kwaja Amran or Kojak Range), and winds through open plains. The boundary follows the western edge of the Toba plateau—which reaches 8000 feet in height—near the New Chaman railway station. From there, it descends to the plains and is defined by hill ranges southward to Nushki.

From Nushki, the boundary cuts across the Lora hamun, leaving the frontier post of Chagai to Baluchistan. It then follows a combination of straight lines through the desert south of the Gaud-i-Zirreh salt swamps and the central mountainous water-divide to the Malik Siah Koh. This line passes 50 miles south of the Helmund, shutting off the valley and the approach to Seistan between the Helmund and the Gaud-i-Zirreh during flood seasons.

The eastern boundary of the northern section is marked by the "red line" at the foot of the frontier hills, defining the border of British India. This creates a buffer system of independent tribes. While the Indian government interferes little with the internal jurisdiction of tribal chiefs in the Suliman Range, the British maintain a line of posts on the Zhob river. This provides command of the hills and controls communication with Afghanistan, promoting a level of peace and order not found in the north.

## Geography and Infrastructure
Since 1880, a systematic survey of Baluchistan has progressed, contributing to a series of professional geographical maps extending from the Indus to the Euphrates and the Oxus. These efforts, along with Indian triangulation extensions reaching the coast of Makran and the eastern districts of Persia, have raised the accuracy of Asiatic geographical surveys.

Infrastructure developments include the extension of the railway from Quetta to Nushki and through to New Chaman on the Afghan-Baluch frontier. Additionally, a desert route between Nushki and Seistan has been developed, featuring dug wells and established serais (posts) for khafila traffic. The completion of surveys in Makran and Baluchistan has clarified ancient connections between the east and west and reopened at least one old trade route between India and Seistan.
