# Beginnings of Poetry

The origins and evolution of poetry are characterized by a transition from communal, rhythmic activities to individualized artistic expression and formalized recitation. Evidence from various cultures suggests that early poetry was often inextricably linked to dance, music, and oral tradition.

## Communal and Folk Origins
Some theories suggest that poetry began with "communal consent," emerging from a period where song, dance, and poetry were integrated. An example of this is found among the tribes of Delagoa Bay, where a singer in a dancing-circle chants words while the dancers provide a refrain. Similar song-dances have been observed in Australia. However, Australian song is not entirely communal; certain men, claiming inspiration from the All Father or dreams, devise new forms of song and dance that spread across the country.

In the British tradition, romantic ballads retain vestiges of these early dance-songs. Many of these ballads are considered communal because they were modified and interpolated by various reciters over different ages. While some ballads were derived from medieval literary romances—which in turn may have borrowed from ancient popular *Maerchen*—it is noted that not all folk-songs are simply vulgarizations of literary works.

## Oral Traditions and Memory
In Persia, oral poetry is maintained by specialized performers. The *Kurroglou-Khans* (from *khaunden*, meaning to sing) memorize and narrate the *mejjlisses* (meetings) of Kurroglou, often accompanied by a three-stringed guitar called a sitar or chungur. Similarly, there are *Shah-nama-Khans* for Ferdausi and *Koran Khans* for the prophet Mohammed. These singers are noted for their astonishing memory, capable of reciting for several hours in a single breath from any passage requested by the audience. In some instances, national bards of the Calmuck may perform songs that last an entire day.

## Epic Recitation in Ancient Greece
The early history of Greek epic recitation is evidenced by Homeric hymns showing poets competing for prizes at festivals, a law in Athens regulating recitation at the Panathenaea, and mentions of rhapsodists at Sicyon. 

In the Homeric poems, the figure is the *aoidos* (singer), who accompanies his song with a lyre (*phormigx*). In the *Iliad*, Achilles is depicted singing the stories of heroes (*klea andron*) in his tent, with Patroclus prepared to take his turn. The *Odyssey* describes singers like Phemius and Demodocus, who perform in the houses of chieftains. Their songs, often focusing on the Trojan War, are characterized as shorter improvisations.

Later, the practice evolved into "rhapsody" (*rhapsodia*). The term *rhapsodos* is post-Homeric; Pindar provided two explanations for it: "singer with the wand" (*rhabdos*) and "singer of stitched verse" (*rhapton hepeon aoidoi*), the latter being etymologically correct. The wand may have served as a symbol of the right to be heard. By the 6th century B.C., the poems of the Ionic Homer had gained national importance in the Doric parts of the Peloponnesus, as seen when Cleisthenes put down rhapsodists at Sicyon due to their poems concerning Argos and the Argives.

## Lyrical and Formal Developments
Ancient Greece established a distinction between poetry chanted by a choir and the *melos* (song proper), which expressed the sentiments of a single poet. By the 7th century B.C., poets such as Sappho and Alcaeus reached high technical perfection in Dorian-inspired song. Other poets, including Alcman, Arion, Stesichorus, Simonides, and Ibycus, wrote for choirs, leading eventually to the dithyrambic odes of Bacchylides and Pindar. Other sung forms included the satires of Archilochus, the romantic reveries of Mimnermus, and the gnomic verses of Theognis.

## Later European Developments
In 15th-century England, powerful poetry was composed in popular forms for the common people, often without a conscious artistic purpose. This included popular ballads, dramatic compositions, and songs and carols. The latter may have existed before the Norman Conquest but were modified by French lyric and musical forms. These works often served as simple expressions of personal emotion, ranging from secular subjects to religious aspirations and the blending of worship with earthly tenderness.

By the Augustan period in Holland, poetry is described as having entered a decline, characterized as "blank and dull." Notable figures of this era include Hubert Cornelissen Poot, known for his love of nature, and Dirk Smits, an elegiac singer from Rotterdam. Other works from this period include a Biblical epic on the history of Abraham by Arnold Hoogvliet and the tragic dramas of Lucretia Wilhelmina van Merken.
