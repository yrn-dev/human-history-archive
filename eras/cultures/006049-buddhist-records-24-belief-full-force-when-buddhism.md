# Buddhism: History, Teachings, and Regional Development

Buddhism originated in India and subsequently spread across Asia, becoming a dominant religious force in China and Japan. Its development involved the integration of existing local beliefs, the establishment of monastic institutions, and the evolution of its doctrines as they were adapted to different cultures.

## Core Teachings and Practices
Early Buddhist teaching incorporated elements of previous beliefs, such as ecstatic meditation. This practice, rooted in ancient animism and seen in the *Vedas*, *Upanishads*, and ancient Yoga, was followed by the Buddha's teachers and admitted into the training of the Buddhist Bhikshu. These states of rapture, known as Conditions of Bliss, are viewed as useful tools for removing mental obstacles to achieve Arahatship. Arahatship consists of thirty-seven constituent parts, with these conditions forming one group of four. However, seeking Arahatship through ecstasy alone is considered a deadly heresy, as these practices are a means to an end rather than the end itself.

A central concept in Buddhism is Nirvana. While early European writers mistakenly interpreted Nirvana as a state reached after death—viewing it as the "dying out" of a soul—canonical texts indicate a belief in salvation within this world and this life. This concept is known in Indian religious language as *Jivanmukti*.

Another significant belief is that of rebirth. In China, the prospect of being reborn as an animal or a woman serves as a deterrent against certain behaviors and encourages the softened treatment of domestic animals. This belief is supported by records of individuals who could recall previous existences, including one man who remembered the pain of having been a horse, despite the administration of a "cup of forgetfulness" in Purgatory intended to prevent such memories.

## Spread to East Asia
The transmission of Buddhism to East Asia occurred over several centuries. In China, a Buddhist priest named Fa Hsien traveled from Central China to India in A.D. 399, visiting Benares, Buddha-Gaya, and Patna to obtain images, relics, and sacred books. He returned to China via Sumatra and Ceylon, where he spent three years.

The faith reached Korea in A.D. 372, where it was met without bigotry. Within three years of the arrival of the first image of Sakiya Muni in Koma, the King and his Court converted, and two large monasteries were established. Buddhism subsequently reached Japan in A.D. 522.

## Buddhism in China
In China, Buddhism became the dominant religion and a refuge for those facing worldly trouble or sickness. Over time, the subtle doctrines originally presented in India evolved into a more clearly defined system accessible to the plain intellect. In this context, Buddha is viewed as the savior of the people through righteousness, and Buddhist saints are believed to possess intercessory powers. Despite this, the religion is often practiced with a lack of reverence; for example, people may buy and sell sweetmeats or talk loudly in temples before sacred images.

## Buddhism in Japan
In Japan, Buddhism was introduced during a period that saw the rise of the great *uji*. The faith did not entirely replace traditional ancestor worship. Instead, the *uji no Kami* (family deities) were grafted onto a Buddhist stem, and shrines became *uji-tera* (uji temples). This synthesis led to Ryobu Shinto, or mixed Shinto, where Buddhist teachers asserted that Shinto Kami were avatars, or incarnations, of various Buddhas.

The priesthood in Japan also became a vehicle for social mobility. Because official posts were largely monopolized by aristocrats, those from lower families entered the priesthood to aspire to roles as abbots or tutors to sovereigns. This led to a concentration of able men in the cloisters rather than the Court nobility. However, this success brought abuses. The tonsure was sometimes used to evade forced labor or taxes, and temples were built to acquire wealth or hide property from fiscal burdens. Eventually, Buddhism in Japan evolved into a militant power capable of appealing to force.
