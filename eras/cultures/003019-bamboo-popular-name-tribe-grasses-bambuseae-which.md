# BAMBOO

Bamboo is the popular name for the *Bambuseae*, a tribe of grasses characterized by woody stems that are often large and tree-like. These plants spring from an underground root-stock and frequently grow in crowded, dense clumps. While some species, such as *Bambusa vulgaris*, grow to heights of 20 to 50 feet, the largest species can reach 120 feet.

## Botanical Characteristics
The stem of the bamboo is slender and hollow, featuring well-marked nodes or joints where the internal cavity is closed by a strong diaphragm. The plant possesses numerous branches, which are spiny in certain species. Its leaves are typically narrow and short, featuring a short stalk and a jointed base that attaches the leaf-blade to a long sheath.

The flowering structures, known as spikelets, are usually many-flowered and arranged in panicles or racemes. The bamboo flower is distinguished from most other grasses by typically having six stamens and three lodicules. Blooming patterns vary by species; some bloom annually, while others bloom only at intervals of many years. In such cases, individuals of the same species may bloom simultaneously over vast areas. For example, on the west coast of India, the *Bambusa arundinacea* has been observed to bloom simultaneously every thirty-two years. Following the ripening of the seed, the leafless flowering culms always die.

The fruit of the bamboo varies by genus. In *Arundinaria* and *Bambusa*, the fruit resembles the grain typical of grasses. In *Dendrocalamus*, the fruit is a nut, and in *Melocanna*, it is rarely fleshy, resembling an apple in size and appearance.

## Distribution and Habitat
The *Bambuseae* consist of twenty-three genera. While they occur throughout the tropical zone, their distribution is uneven, with Tropical Asia being the richest in species and Africa having very few. Their range extends into sub-tropical and temperate zones. In Asia, they are found in Japan and at elevations of 10,000 feet or more in the Himalayas. In South America, they reach the snow-line of the Andes.

In specific regions, bamboo defines the landscape. The Lushai Hills, a mountainous district on the border of Burma and Eastern Bengal and Assam, are largely covered in rank undergrowth and dense bamboo jungle. Similarly, the district of Backergunje in British India is characterized by a flat landscape dotted with clusters of bamboos and betel-nut trees, with villages often walled by groves of these plants.

## Human Uses and Industry
In Oriental countries, the applications for bamboo are nearly endless.

**Food and Medicine**
The soft, succulent shoots are harvested as they begin to spring and served as a food similar to asparagus. To extend their period of consumption, they are earthed over. These shoots are available year-round, though most abundant in autumn, and are consumed fresh, salted with rice, pickled, or preserved in sugar. 

As the plant matures, a fluid is secreted in the hollow joints. This develops into a siliceous concretion known as *tabascheer* or *tabaxir*. While this substance possesses peculiar optical properties and was highly valued in the East for medicinal qualities, it is considered entirely inert as a medicinal agent and has not been adopted into European materia medica.

**Manufacturing and Arts**
Bamboo is a primary material for various crafts:
* **Basketry and Furniture:** The Chinese and Japanese use splits of bamboo to manufacture furniture and baskets of high finish and beauty. The Japanese specifically use bamboo wicker-work to encase delicate egg-shell porcelain.
* **Korean Industry:** In Korea, bamboo is used to manufacture mats, hats, and fine split blinds.
* **Writing Materials:** In ancient China, "books" consisted of thin bamboo slips. Characters were written on these tablets using a bamboo or wood pencil with a frayed end to apply colored liquid. Knives were used only to scratch out characters that were written incorrectly.

## Historical and Social Applications
Bamboo has served practical roles in various historical contexts. In 1462, during a period of famine and pestilence in Japan, the Buddhist priest Gwana used bamboo stretchers to carry starving people to grass huts where they were fed boiled millet.
