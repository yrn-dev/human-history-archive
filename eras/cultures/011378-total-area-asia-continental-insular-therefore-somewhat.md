# Asia: Area and Geography

## Total Area and Territorial Distribution
The total area of Asia, encompassing both continental and insular regions, is calculated to be somewhat over 16,819,000 square miles, although various authorities provide differing detailed estimates. This vast area is divided among several powers and independent territories.

The largest portion of the continent is under Russian control, covering 6,495,970 square miles. Chinese territory follows with 4,299,600 square miles. Other significant territories include those held by the British (1,998,220 sq. m.), the Dutch (586,980 sq. m.), the Turks (681,980 sq. m.), and the French (247,580 sq. m.). The United States holds 114,370 square miles, while German holdings account for 193 square miles and Japanese territories cover 161,110 square miles. Additionally, other independent territories comprise 2,232,270 square miles.

## Population Distribution
The rough population of Asia is estimated at 823,000,000 people. The distribution of this population is heavily concentrated in specific territories: 330,000,000 inhabitants reside in Chinese territory, 302,000,000 in British territories, and 25,000,000 in Russian territories.

## Hydrographic Basins
The continent can be analyzed through its hydrographic basins, which are divided into three primary divisions:

*   **Northern Division:** This area embraces approximately 6,394,500 square miles. It includes the Arctic river basins (4,367,000 sq. m.), the Aralo-Caspian basin (1,759,000 sq. m.), and the Mediterranean (268,500 sq. m.).
*   **Southern Division:** Nearly equal in extent to the north, this division totals 6,514,000 square miles, consisting of the Indian Ocean drainage (2,873,000 sq. m.) and the Pacific drainage (3,641,000 sq. m.).
*   **Interior or Inland Basins:** These amount to 3,141,500 square miles, roughly half the size of the other two divisions. This region includes the Syro-Arabian inland basin, the basin of Asia Minor, the Iranian (or Perso-Afghan) uplands, the Tibetan plateau, the Gobi depression, and lacustrine regions south of the Arctic watershed.

Among the river basins, the Ob is the largest in Asia, exceeding 1,000,000 square miles. Other major basins include the Amur (776,000 sq. m.), the Yang-tsze-kiang (685,000 sq. m.), the Ganges (409,500 sq. m.), and the Indus (370,000 sq. m.).

## Geological and Regional Features
The geology of Asia is characterized as highly complex and remains little known over wide areas, making a connected account of its development or structure difficult.

In the region of Asia Minor, historical records distinguish between continental and insular settlements. For example, Herodotus notes twelve ancient continental Æolic cities—including Têmnos, Larissa, Neon-Teichos, Kymê, Ægæ, Myrina, Gryneium, Killa, Notium, Ægiroëssa, Pitanê, and Smyrna—and distinguishes them from insular Æolic Greeks located in Tenedos, Hekatonnesoi, and Lesbos.

## External Influences
European influence on the continent has been particularly strong during two distinct periods: first following the conquests of Alexander the Great, and second from the 16th century onwards. Alexander's conquests led to the creation of Perso-Greek kingdoms that hellenized their immediate areas and influenced the religion and art of China and India. 

While the Crusades maintained communication with the Levant and established the power of the Roman Church, intercourse with the deeper parts of Asia remained limited to a few travellers. In the region of western Asia and eastern Europe, Islamic influence has remained paramount. However, improved communication has allowed western Europe to assert power across middle and eastern Asia.
