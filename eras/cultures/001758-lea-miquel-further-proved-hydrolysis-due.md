# Urea Hydrolysis and the Role of Urase

## The Process of Urea Hydrolysis
In the natural environment, hundreds of thousands of tons of urea are deposited daily. Because ordinary plants are unable to assimilate urea in its initial state, it must undergo significant changes to become useful. This process begins when bacteria bring about a simple hydrolysis, converting the urea into carbonate of ammonia (also referred to as ammonium carbonate). This bacterial process has been more clearly understood since the initial descriptions provided by Cohn, van Tieghem, and Pasteur.

## The Discovery of Urase
Further research conducted by Miquel and Lea proved that this specific hydrolysis is caused by an enzyme known as urase. This enzyme is characterized by the fact that it is difficult to separate from the bacteria involved in the process. These bacteria, capable of facilitating the change to ammonium carbonate, are found in various environments, including manure heaps, soil, and rivers.

## Agricultural and Environmental Impact
The ability of bacteria to render urea available as nitrogen provides soil with enormous stores of nutrients. In addition to urea, soil nitrogen is supplied by other living debris and by rain from the atmosphere. Furthermore, nitrogen-fixing bacteria in cultivated soils can force inert free nitrogen to combine with other elements into compounds that plants can assimilate. 

The work of Winogradsky confirmed earlier assertions by A. Laurent and M. P. E. Berthelot regarding this process. For example, *Clostridium pasteurianum* can fix nitrogen in proportion to the amount of sugar it consumes, provided it has the necessary minerals and carbohydrates for growth and is protected from free oxygen by an envelope of fungi or aerobic bacteria. This is an example of symbiosis, similar to the relationship found in the bacteria-filled nodules on the roots of Leguminosae.

## Related Bacterial Research
Beyond the hydrolysis of urea, research by Miquel, Globig, and others has explored the limits of bacterial survival. While it was previously questioned whether spores could withstand high temperatures, these researchers demonstrated that numerous bacteria can grow and divide at temperatures of at least 70°C. Examples include *B. Ludwigi* and *B. ilidzensis* from hot springs, as well as *B. thermophilus* from sewage, the latter of which remains active at 70°C. This vital activity explains the heating of vegetable products such as tobacco, hops, and hay-stacks, as well as the spontaneous combustion of sterilized cotton-waste when fresh washings are added.
