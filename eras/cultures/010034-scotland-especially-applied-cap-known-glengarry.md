# Scotland and the Glengarry

## Head-dress and the Glengarry
In Scotland, the term "bonnet"—derived from the Latin *bonetum* (a type of stuff or a cap made from it)—has survived as a common term for a head-covering. While "bonnet" was a general English term for a soft cap until the end of the 17th century, its continued use in Scotland is especially applied to the cap known as a "glengarry."

The glengarry's shape is referenced in the description of other military head-dress. The "rifle busby," a folding cap made of astrachan, is described as being somewhat similar in shape to a glengarry, although the busby is taller. Additionally, the full dress feather bonnet worn by Highland infantry is colloquially referred to as a "busby."

## Social and Architectural History
The word "booth" (connected to a Teutonic root meaning to dwell) evolved from meaning a temporary dwelling of boughs to a market stall or non-permanent erection. In Scotland, the term appears as *bothy* or *bothie*, generally referring to a cottage or hut. Specifically, it was used to describe a barrack-like room on large farms used to lodge unmarried labourers. This arrangement, known as the "Bothy system," was formerly common in northern Scotland and Aberdeenshire.

## Notable Figures and Glengarry
The name Glengarry is associated with both Scottish clan leadership and Canadian geography.

### Alestair Ruadh Macdonnell
Alestair (Alexander) Ruadh Macdonnell (c. 1725-1761) was the chief of Glengarry and a Scottish Jacobite. A branch of the clan Macdonald, his family used the spelling Macdonnell or Macdonell. Alestair's father, John, the 12th chief of Glengarry, is described as a brutal man who reportedly starved his first wife to death on a Hebrides island. 

Alestair fled to France in 1738 as a boy and joined the Royal Scots, a regiment in French service. He commanded a company in 1743 and served as a Jacobite agent in Scotland in 1744. After being captured at sea in 1745 while bringing a picquet of the Royal Scots to assist Prince Charles Edward, he was imprisoned in the Tower for twenty-two months. 

Following his release, Alestair lived in poverty for a time, having received the ruined family estates from his father in 1744. By 1749, while in London, he is believed to have become a spy for the British government under the codename "Pickle," providing information on Jacobite conspiracies and the prince. Despite betraying a man to death in 1752 and being denounced by a Mrs. Cameron, he maintained the confidence of Jacobite leaders. He succeeded to the estates in 1754, where he was known as a greedy landlord, until his death on December 23, 1761.

### John Sandfield Macdonald
In Canada, Glengarry county in Ontario is the birthplace of statesman John Sandfield Macdonald (1812-1872), born at St Raphael on December 12, 1812. Macdonald was elected to the Canadian parliament for Glengarry in 1841, holding the seat for sixteen years. He served as Speaker of the house from 1852 to 1854 and later formed a ministry that held office until February 1864. After opposing federation, he was tasked by the Conservatives in 1867 to organize the provincial government of Ontario, which he led with efficiency and economy until his defeat by the Liberals in December 1871.
