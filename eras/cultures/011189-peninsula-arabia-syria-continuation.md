# The Peninsula of Arabia and Syria

## Geography and Topography
The peninsula of Arabia, along with Syria—which serves as its continuation to the north-west—possesses geographical characteristics similar to the driest and hottest regions of Baluchistan and Persia. The region is predominantly covered by low mountains reaching altitudes of 3,000 to 4,000 feet. These mountains contain narrow valleys with scanty vegetation, though areas near the coasts exhibit greater fertility due to higher rainfall. 

In northern Syria, the landscape is more productive owing to a more copious water supply and the mountains of Lebanon, which rise to approximately 10,000 feet. The northern part of this tract is conterminous with the plain of Mesopotamia, which reaches a maximum elevation of about 700 feet above sea level. In contrast to the black, silty soil of Egypt, the soil of Syria and Arabia is described as being somewhat rocky and clayey.

## Continental Position and Boundaries
Asia, viewed as a whole, takes the form of a great isosceles spherical triangle. One of its equal sides extends from the north-eastern apex at East Cape to the southern point of Arabia. This southern point is located at the strait of Bab-el-Mandeb at 45 deg. E. The peninsula of Arabia, along with those of Cambodia and Hindostan, descends to approximately 10 deg. N.

The boundaries of the region are defined by several landmarks. To the west, the boundary passes through the Mediterranean and down the Red Sea to the southern point of Arabia. A great circle drawn from East Cape to the southern point of Arabia indicates the north-western boundary of Asia, though Asia Minor and the north-western half of Arabia lie outside this circle. Historically, some accounts describe a peninsula stretching from the land of the Persians to the Erythraian Sea, including Persia, Assyria, and Arabia, ending at the Arabian gulf.

## Flora and Climate
The rainless region of south-western Asia, which includes the peninsula of Arabia, the shores of the Persian Gulf, south Persia, Afghanistan, and Baluchistan, shares a continuous flora with the desert flora of northern and eastern Africa. This botanical zone extends from the coast of Senegal to the meridian of 75 deg. E., reaching from the great African desert to the border of the rainless tract along the southern parts of the Punjab and the Indus.

## Population and Ethnology
The inhabitants of Syria and Arabia are categorized as Semitic, a term given to races speaking languages akin to ancient Assyrian. Physically, these populations are considered branches of the Melanochroi (dark-haired people), who are themselves viewed as a result of the admixture of the Xanthochroi (fair-haired people of northern Europe) with the Australoid type. 

The Australoid family—characterized by dark skin, dark soft hair, broad flat noses, and heavy jaws—is believed to extend into Egypt and south Arabia. The Melanochroic race extends from southern Europe and North Africa through Syria, south-western Asia, Arabia, and Persia into India.

## Societal Structure and History
Historically, Arabia has been a land of nomads, with the exception of the fertile strip of the Yemen in the south. It served as the land of origin and headquarters for Semitic peoples. From this region, waves of nomads drifted into Mesopotamia, the Mediterranean coast, and Egypt. These movements included the Chaldeans from Eastern Arabia, the Arameans who established themselves in Syria with Damascus as their capital, the Hebrews, and the Phoenicians and Canaanites along the eastern Mediterranean shores.

In the more arid districts, the population has historically lived a freebooting, nomadic pastoral life under the leadership of petty chiefs. In more fertile tracts, however, people settled in towns where external commerce was established and agriculture became profitable. Except for south eastern Arabia, the tract was nominally subject to Turkey, though the people remained practically independent to a significant extent.
