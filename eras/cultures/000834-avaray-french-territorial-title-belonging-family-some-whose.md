# AVARAY

Avaray is a French territorial title associated with a family that has included several members conspicuous in history. The title is linked to the estate of Avaray, located in the province of Orleanais.

## Origins and Early History
The title originated with the Besiade family, a Bearnaise family that moved into the province of Orleanais during the 17th century. Upon their arrival, they acquired the estate of Avaray. In 1667, Theophile de Besiade, marquis d'Avaray, obtained the office of grand bailiff of Orleans. This specific office was subsequently held by several of his descendants.

## The Marquisate and the French Revolution
In the late 18th century, Claude Antoine de Besiade, marquis d'Avaray, served as the deputy for the bailliage of Orleans in the states-general of 1789. During this period, he proposed a *Declaration of the Duties of Man*, intended to serve as a pendant to the *Declaration of the Rights of Man*. Following the revolutionary period, Claude Antoine de Besiade's status continued to rise; he became a lieutenant-general in 1814, a peer of France in 1815, and was named duc d'Avaray in 1818.

## The Duchy of Ile-Jourdain
Antoine Louis Francois, comte d'Avaray, the son of Claude Antoine, distinguished himself through his devotion to the comte de Provence (who later became Louis XVIII). The comte d'Avaray assisted the prince during his emigration. In 1799, having nominally become king, the prince created the estate of Ile-Jourdain a duchy under the title of Avaray. This title was granted in favor of the comte d'Avaray, whom the prince referred to as his "liberator."

## Context of French Ducal Titles
The title of duke in France evolved significantly over time. In the 10th and 11th centuries, the title had no defined significance, and high nobility might use the terms duke, count, or marquis indifferently. By the 12th century, the title of duke was definitely regarded as superior to others and, in some cases—such as with the dukes of Brittany, Normandy, Aquitaine, and Burgundy—implied vast territorial power and practical independence from the crown.

However, as royal power strengthened, the political significance of the title diminished. By the eve of the French Revolution, a ducal title generally implied high rank and territorial wealth rather than sovereign power. Under the *ancien regime*, there were three distinct classes of dukes:
1. Dukes who were peers and held seats in the parlement of Paris.
2. Hereditary dukes who were not peers.
3. "Brevet" dukes, who were created for life only.

In republican France, existing titles are officially recognized, though they serve as badges of distinguished ancestry rather than sources of political power.
