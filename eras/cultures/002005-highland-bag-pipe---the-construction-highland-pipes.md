# The Highland Bag-pipe

The Highland bag-pipe is a musical instrument categorized as a Class I bag-pipe, distinguished primarily by the method of inflation, which is achieved via a blow-pipe rather than bellows. This separates it from instruments such as the Lowland bag-pipe or the Northumbrian bag-pipe.

## Construction and Mechanics
The construction of the Highland bag-pipe consists of a bag, a blow-pipe, a chaunter, and drones. The bag rests under the performer's arm, with the blow-pipe and chaunter positioned at opposite extremities and the drones pointing over the shoulder. 

The chaunter is a conical wooden tube that terminates in a bell. Including the reed, it measures between 14 and 16 inches. It features eight holes in total: seven located in the front and one at the back for the left thumb. The right thumb serves only to support the instrument, while the left hand fingers the upper holes, which are stopped by the under part of the finger joints. Additionally, there is a double hole near the bell that remains uncovered to regulate pitch.

The drones provide a continuous ground bass. Each drone utilizes a beating-reed—created by cutting and splitting a piece of cane or reed to raise a tongue or flap—which is fixed in a socket and inserted into the stock. Air forced from the bag by the pressure of the performer's arm causes the reed's tongue to vibrate, setting the air column in vibration. Because the drone-pipe is a cylindrical tube, it possesses the acoustic properties of a closed pipe, producing a note corresponding to a pipe twice its length. Drones are tuned using sliding-joints.

## Performance and Musicality
Because the double reed of the chaunter is not manipulated by the performer's lips, the instrument produces only nine notes. However, skilled performers can obtain harmonics by increasing wind pressure and "pinching" or partially covering the C and B holes. Some notes are approximately a quarter of a tone sharp.

A technical peculiarity of the Highland bag-pipe is that repeating the same note is difficult. To resolve this, pipers use "cuts" or "warblers," which are grace notes fingered with great rapidity. These warblers can consist of single notes or groups of three to seven notes played in leaps. These ornaments help relieve the constant discord created by the drone bass; highly skilled pipers may introduce up to eleven warblers between two beats in a bar.

## History and Notation
The archive contains evidence of the instrument's existence in the medieval period, specifically a Highland bag-pipe dated 1409 (MCCCCIX) held by Messrs J. & R. Glen of Edinburgh. This 15th-century instrument featured two drones inserted into a single stock shaped like a wide-spread fork. These drones were tuned to A, in unison with the lowest note of the chaunter.

Historically, the use of musical notation for Highland pipe tunes is a recent innovation. Pipers traditionally used verbal equivalents for notes. For example, the piobaireachd *Coghiegh nha Shie* ("War of peace") was taught to apprentices using syllabic jargon such as "Hodroho, hodroho, haninin, hiechin." While such verbal transmissions may seem like an unintelligible jumble when written down, Alexander Campbell noted that when this "syllabic jargon of illiterate pipers" is correctly translated into musical characters, it coincides exactly with musical notation.

## Comparative Context
The Highland bag-pipe shares structural similarities with other rustic instruments, such as the French *cornemuse* used by shepherds. It differs from the modern Lowland bag-pipe, which is blown by bellows, and the Northumbrian (or Border) bag-pipe, which also uses bellows and features a chaunter stopped at the lower end to allow for silence when all holes are closed.
