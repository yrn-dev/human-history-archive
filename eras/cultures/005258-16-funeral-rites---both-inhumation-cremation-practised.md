# Funeral Rites

Funeral rites encompass the various ceremonies and methods used by different societies to dispose of the dead. Because records of ancient races are often limited to their tombs, the exploration of these sites frequently provides the primary evidence for the religions, arts, institutions, and customs of vanished societies. For the great culture folks of antiquity—including the Romans, Greeks, Persians, Hindus, Egyptians, and Babylonians—literary evidence exists alongside monuments to provide a detailed understanding of their rites.

## Methods of Disposal: Inhumation and Cremation
The two primary methods of disposing of the dead are inhumation (burial) and cremation. In prehistoric times, barrow-burial was almost universally prevalent across Europe, the Mediterranean basin, Asia Minor, India, China, and Japan. Similar customs existed in the New World; in the great plains of North America, dead were buried in enormous barrows, where cremation appeared more frequently than inhumation.

In heathen times in Britain, both practices were used, though inhumation seemed to prevail everywhere. Cremation was more common in northern counties than in the south, although it was fairly numerous in the Thames valley. The *Beowulf* represents cremation as the prevailing custom. Burnt remains were typically enclosed in clay urns—often badly baked and decorated with geometrical patterns applied with a punch—which were then buried. These urns, ranging from 4 to 12 inches in diameter, closely resemble those found in northern Germany.

In Norway, the earliest age is referred to as the "Age of Burning" because all dead were consumed by fire, with standing stones raised over the ashes. Later, following the burial of Frey under a cairn at Upsala, many chiefs began raising cairns to remember relatives.

## Grave Goods and Beliefs
The practice of burying tools, weapons, and ornaments with the deceased is a characteristic of inhumations from the Quaternary epoch, suggesting a belief that death is a portal to another life. In some inhumation graves, the skeleton is laid at full length, usually with the head facing north or west, accompanied by a sword, shield, and spear, as well as valuable ornaments and brooches. Other graves are simpler, containing only a few beads, a simple brooch, or a small knife.

Certain rituals were employed to ensure the deceased could use these items in the next world. Objects were sometimes turned inside out, upside down, or carefully broken to liberate the "soul" or *manes* of the object. Similarly, properties were committed to flames during cremation. In the land of Kinsay (Hang-chau), as described by Marco Polo, relations wore hempen garments and played instruments while burning parchment representations of money, armor, camels, slaves, and horses. It was believed these effigies would become real flesh, blood, and gold in the next world. In old Peru, mothers were sometimes buried with their suckling babies to provide consolation in the afterlife.

## Religious and Cultural Variations
### Buddhist Traditions
In Japan, there is no evidence of Buddhist funeral rites until the death of the retired Emperor Shomu in 756. Cremation was introduced by the Buddhist priest Dosho, who died in 700 and ordered his body be cremated at Kurihara; the Dowager Empress Jito followed this practice two years later. 

In China, the cremation of Buddhist priests is universal. Priests facing death move to the "Abode of a Long Old Age" to enter Nirvana, a state of conscious freedom from physical and mental disturbance. The deceased priest is placed in a wooden box and carried in a procession with streaming banners to a cremation ground. Brother priests chant Buddhist liturgies in Pali—a language they often do not understand—while the box is hoisted onto an oil-drenched pyre. The resulting ashes are collected in an urn and placed in a mausoleum.

### Christian and Primitive Rites
Primitive interment was sometimes used to prevent ghosts from haunting the living or to stop vampires and ghouls from possessing the corpse. Cremation was thought to achieve the latter by removing the "nidus" for the demon. 

In Christian rites, some prayers were intended to protect the body from violation by vampires. Ancient tombs were built with architectural solidity for this reason, and graves were regularly sealed with the cross. An old Armenian rite for laymen includes a prayer asking the Lord to seal the sepulchre so that no "filthy and unclean devil" who assails the bodies of the heathen may approach.

## Social and Legal Aspects
In old Japan, an edict issued in 706 mandated that trees be planted at ancestral tombs and in the residential quarters of common people, as tending to the resting place of the dead was considered a sacred duty. Additionally, reforms during the Daika era set limits on the size of sepulchres, which reduced the amount of forced labor required of the lower classes.

In England, burial societies emerged as a form of friendly society. Members paid voluntary subscriptions to insure that money would be available for the funeral expenses of a member, their spouse, child, or the widow of a deceased member.
