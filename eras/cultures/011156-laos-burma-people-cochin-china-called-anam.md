# Indo-China

Indo-China is a term used to describe the region comprising Burma, Siam, Cambodia, and Annam. Positioned and civilized between India and China, the region exhibits varying degrees of cultural influence from both. Indian influence is predominant as far as Cambodia, where Indian alphabets are employed and Buddhism is of the Sinhalese type. In contrast, Annam and Tongking utilize Chinese institutions and script.

## Geography and Hydrography
The region is characterized by high mountain ranges extending south from the eastern extremity of the Tibetan mountains, between the 95th and 100th meridians. These ranges, which are likely permanently snowy up to 27 deg. N., spread outwards to reach the sea at the Malay peninsula, the east flank of Bengal, and various points in Cochin-China.

The Indo-Chinese peninsula is well-watered, fertile, and populous, possessing a climate similar to that of eastern Bengal. The landscape is defined by several great rivers: the Mekong, the Menam, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy. The valleys of these rivers form the primary portions of the states of Burma, Siam (including Laos), and Cochin-China (including Cambodia and Tongking).

## Burma
Burma is a province of British India, consisting of British Burma and the former independent kingdom of Burma. It is divided into Lower and Upper Burma; the latter was annexed on January 1, 1886. The province extends from the Pakchan river in the south (9° 55' north latitude) to the Kachin (Chingpaw) and Naga hills in the north (between 27 and 28 degrees north latitude). Its total area is estimated at 238,738 sq.m., with Burma proper occupying 168,573 sq.m. and the Shan States occupying 59,915 sq.m.

The Burmese are linguistically allied to Tibetans and are believed to have entered the region from the north-west as part of a vast Indo-Chinese immigration swarm from western China. Early history was marked by conflicts between the Burmese and the Talaings. Around 1750, Alompra founded a kingdom by uniting his countrymen and breaking the power of the Talaings, later invading Siam. This kingdom was eventually annexed by Britain in 1885. In the medieval period, the Chinese (who called the country Mien) under Kublai Khan overran the country as far as the Irrawaddy delta, destroying the ancient capital of Pagan.

Education in Upper Burma is notably strong in primary literacy due to indigenous monastic schools. In nearly every village, Buddhist monks known as *pongyis* provide free instruction in reading and writing. The province also shows a lack of prejudice regarding the seclusion of women, contributing to high literacy rates.

## Siam, Cambodia, and Laos
The Siamese, or Thai, are considered a late invasion from southern China, descending around the 13th century. They speak a monosyllabic language of the Chinese type, though it is written using an Indian alphabet.

The Khmers of Cambodia form a relatively ancient kingdom. Their language belongs to the Mon-Annam group. While the kingdom was reduced in recent centuries by the advance of the Siamese and later became a French protectorate, ruins dating from A.D. 800 to 1000 indicate a previously strong Hindu influence.

Laos is included within the state of Siam. Along with Burma and Siam, it is a country where Buddhism remains predominant.

## Cochin-China and Annam
The people of Cochin-China are called Anam. The region's history includes the ancient kingdom of Champa, which possessed a hinduized Malay civilization before being absorbed by Annam. Until the French took control, the history of Annam was defined by a struggle for sovereignty with the Chinese.

The Annamese are a distinct race. The Mon-Annam language group is spoken in Annam and Pegu (an ancient kingdom now confounded with Burma), suggesting these people once spread across the entire region before being divided by the advance of others, including the Siamese. In the 13th century, Kublai Khan claimed submission from Champa, leading to an attempted invasion through Tongking; however, the Mongols suffered significant losses in the resulting war.
