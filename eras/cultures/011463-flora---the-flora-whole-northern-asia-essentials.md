# Flora of Asia and Related Regions

The distribution of plant life across Asia is heavily dependent on climatic conditions, resulting in a diverse array of flora that ranges from Arctic-European types in the north to tropical assemblages in the south and east.

## Northern and Central Asia
The flora of northern Asia is essentially the same as that of northern Europe, with differences occurring primarily in species rather than genera. Notable absences east of the Ural mountains include all heaths and the oak. The primary trees found on the mountains are larch, birch, and pine, while poplars, alders, and willow dominate the lower ground. In Siberia, the pine reaches a northern limit of approximately 70 deg. N.

In the warm temperate zone extending from the Himalaya to the Mediterranean, the flora is essentially European in character. While many European species reach the central Himalaya, they are fewer in the eastern parts. Genera common to both Europe and the Himalaya are abundant and found at all elevations throughout the chain. This zone also sees a diffusion of Chinese and Japanese forms, which are most numerous in the eastern Himalaya and less frequent in the west.

## South-Western and Western Asia
The rainless region of south-western Asia—which includes Afghanistan, Baluchistan, south Persia, the shores of the Persian Gulf, and the peninsula of Arabia—shares a continuous flora with the desert regions of northern and eastern Africa. This tract extends from the coast of Senegal to the 75 deg. E. meridian, reaching the border of the rainless area along the southern Punjab and the Indus.

In northern Persia, Asia Minor, and Afghanistan, the flora approximates that of the western Himalaya. The evergreen oak of southern Europe, *Quercus Ilex*, is found in forests as far east as the Sutlej. In Persia and Afghanistan, thistles and Boraginaceae are abundant in higher regions, alongside gigantic Umbelliferae such as *Prangos*, *Bubon*, *Dorema*, *Galbanum*, and *Ferula*, some of which extend into Tibet. 

The flora of northern Persia and Asia Minor differs little from southern Europe. In areas with abundant rain, mountains are clothed in various Coniferae and forests of *Corylus*, *Carpinus*, *Acer*, *Ulmus*, *Fagus*, and *Quercus*. Among these, *Fagus* is the only genus not found on the Himalaya. This region also features an abundance of fruit trees of the plum tribe and cultivated plants typical of southern Europe.

## Eastern and South-Eastern Asia
The flora of south-eastern Asia, covering the Malayan peninsula, islands, and India (from the 65th to the 105th meridian), is estimated by Sir J.D. Hooker to comprise between 12,000 and 15,000 indigenous species. The dominant orders by numerical importance are Leguminosae, Rubiaceae, Orchidaceae, Compositae, Gramineae, Euphorbiaceae, Acanthaceae, Cyperaceae, and Labiatae. While the region is a heterogeneous mix of alpine, temperate, and tropical plants, the tropical elements are dominant. The Indian flora is noted for providing a more complete illustration of the world's chief natural families than any other country, though Compositae, Gramineae, and Cyperaceae are comparatively rare or deficient in some areas.

In East Asia, the flora of north China is akin to that of Japan and shows a relation to the American continent, with temperate forms extending as far as the Himalaya and nearly to Kashmir. South China is botanically similar to the great Indian region, with many Chinese forms penetrating it. Analysis of the Hong Kong flora shows that about three-fifths of species are common to the Indian region, while the rest are local or Chinese.

## Regional Boundaries and Transitions
A significant physical separation exists in the Malay archipelago. Along the Straits of Macassar (approximately 120 deg. E.), a boundary known as Wallace's line marks the transition where flora and fauna to the west are essentially Asiatic, while those to the south and east show a distinctly marked and eventually predominant Australian element.

Outside of Asia, other regions show similar botanical intersections. Greenland possesses about 310 species of vascular plants; forty-four are European-Asiatic, forty are American, fifteen are endemic, and the remainder are common to both Asia/Europe and America. In Europe, the flora of the Balkans corresponds with that of the Carpathians, while the Rila and Rhodope groups combine indigenous types with those of the mountains of Asia Minor and the central European Alps.
