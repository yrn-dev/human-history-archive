# The Chaunter

The chaunter, also referred to as the "chanter" or the melody-pipe, is a primary component of the bag-pipe, a complex reed instrument of great antiquity. While the bag-pipe utilizes a reservoir—an air-tight leather bag—to provide a wind supply regulated by the performer's arm, the chaunter is the specific pipe responsible for producing the melody.

## Technical Construction and Function
The chaunter is invariably designed to "speak" by means of a double-reed. Depending on the variety of the bag-pipe, the bore of the chaunter may be either conical or cylindrical. To allow the performer to manipulate the pitch and play melodies, the pipe is equipped with lateral holes and, in certain instances, a bell and keys.

In the general construction of the instrument, the chaunter and the blow-pipe (the valved tube used to fill the bag) are positioned at opposite ends of the leather bag. The chaunter is inserted into a fixed stock or short tube, which serves as a socket and an air-chamber to protect the reed.

## Regional and Historical Varieties
The configuration of the chaunter varies significantly across different cultural and historical iterations of the bag-pipe:

*   **Highland and French Instruments:** A 15th-century Highland bag-pipe (dated 1409) featured a chaunter with a thumb-hole at the back and seven finger-holes in front. Similarly, the French *cornemuse* or *chalemie* used by shepherds featured a conical bore and a double reed hidden within the stock. This specific chaunter had eight holes (seven in front and one thumb-hole) providing a compass from C to C'. When removed from the bag and played separately, the compass could be increased to E by overblowing. Another variety of *cornemuse* used with the *Hautbois de Poitou* also featured eight holes, with the lowest hole covered by a key in a perforated box.
*   **The Magdeburg Bag-pipe:** As noted by Praetorius, a specific variety of *sackpfeife* found in Magdeburg utilized two chaunters mounted in a single stock. Each of these pipes had one thumb-hole at the back and three holes in front. This arrangement allowed the performer to play simple two-part melodies; the right-hand chaunter produced the notes D, E, F, G, and A, while the left-hand chaunter produced G, A, B, C, and D.
*   **The Calabrian Bag-pipe:** This instrument utilizes two chaunters and two drones fixed in one stock. Each chaunter possesses three or four finger-holes. In the right-hand pipe, the fourth hole is covered by a key enclosed in a perforated box.
*   **The Northumbrian or Border Bag-pipe:** This variety is distinguished by a chaunter that is stopped at the lower end, rendering the pipe silent when all holes are closed. It features one thumb-hole, seven finger-holes, and a varying number of keys.
*   **The Musette de Naples (*Surdelina* or *Sampogna*):** Described by Mersenne, this complicated instrument featured two chaunters with narrow cylindrical bores, equipped with both keys and finger-holes.

## Relationship to Other Pipes
The chaunter operates in contrast to the "drones" of the bag-pipe. While the chaunter is the melody-pipe, drones are jointed pipes with cylindrical bores and no lateral holes, meaning they can only produce one fixed note. In some instruments, such as the 15th-century Highland bag-pipe, the drones are tuned to the lowest note of the chaunter. In the Northumbrian variety, the drones are tuned via stoppers, allowing them to be silenced.
