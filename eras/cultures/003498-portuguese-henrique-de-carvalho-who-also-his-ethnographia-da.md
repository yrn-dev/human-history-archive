# Portuguese Overseas Expansion and History

The Portuguese people engaged in extensive overseas exploration, trade, and colonization beginning in the late 15th century, establishing a global presence that stretched from the Americas to East Asia.

## Early Exploration and the Americas
In 1494, the Pope divided the newly discovered lands of the Americas between Spain and Portugal. Under this settlement, Portugal was granted Brazil and all territories east of a line located 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, while Spain received the remainder. 

Beyond the Americas, Portugal pushed its enterprises southward and eastward. In 1497, Vasco da Gama sailed from Lisbon, rounding the Cape and visiting Zanzibar before reaching Calicut in India. This route to India was pursued with renewed vigor following news of other discoveries in western Europe. Later, in 1519, a Portuguese sailor named Magellan, though employed by the Spanish King, sailed south of South America through the "Strait of Magellan" to enter the Pacific Ocean.

## Trading Stations and Asian Possessions
By 1515, Portuguese ships had reached the Moluccas and Java. The Portuguese established and fortified various trading stations along the coasts of the Indian Ocean. Key possessions included Goa and Mozambique, as well as two smaller holdings in India. 

In China, the Portuguese were permitted to build factories on a peninsula in 1557. In 1580, Gregory XIII established a bishopric in Macao, and a senate was organized there in 1583. Jeronimo de Silveira served as the first royal governor of Macao in 1628. For a significant period, the Portuguese in Macao remained largely under Chinese control, with Chinese mandarins maintaining authority and insisting that European criminals be handed over to them. This arrangement ended in 1849 under Governor Ferreira do Amaral, who stripped Chinese officials of their authority in the peninsula; although Amaral was assassinated on August 22, his successors continued his policy. Macao and a part of Timor remain Portuguese possessions.

## Relations with Japan
Portuguese involvement in Japan included both religious missions and trade. In the mid-16th century, the Jesuit Xavier visited Japan, utilizing the presence of Portuguese crews—who wore rich garments and carried arms—to attract the interest of leaders like Otomo. Otomo, desiring foreign wealth and effective weapons of war, granted the Jesuits liberty to preach. During his twenty-seven-month stay in Japan, Xavier and his comrades gained approximately 760 converts.

However, Portuguese activities also led to friction. Records from the Academy of History at Madrid indicate that Portuguese merchants, including "Lascars and scullions," purchased Japanese subjects and transported them into slavery in the Indies, often in brutal conditions where many died during the voyage. While the Taiko (Hideyoshi) initially exempted Portuguese merchants from banishment, he grew resentful of the Jesuits' inability to dissuade traders from such outrages.

By 1636, tensions increased when the Dutch discovered a report from the governor of Macao describing a festival honoring Vieyra, who had been martyred in Japan. The Dutch shared this with the Japanese to portray the Portuguese as traitors to the state. In 1637, the Bakufu in Yedo issued an edict forbidding foreigners from traveling in the empire to prevent Portuguese individuals from entering using passports with Dutch names.

## Historical and Literary Records
Portuguese history has been documented by various historians and authors. G.J.C. Henriques, a Portuguese historian, discovered and published the records of the trial of George Buchanan under the title *George Buchanan in the Lisbon Inquisition* (1906). 

In the 19th century, the English novelist William Henry Giles Kingston spent much of his youth in Oporto, where his father was a merchant. Kingston wrote newspaper articles on Portuguese subjects and was awarded a Portuguese order of knighthood and a pension for his role in the conclusion of the commercial treaty of 1842. His work *The Prime Minister* focuses on the days of the Marquis of Pombal, and his *Lusitanian Sketches* detail his travels within Portugal.
