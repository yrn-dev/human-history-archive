# Etymology and Evolution of Ball, Ballet, and Ballad

The terms "ball," "ballet," and "ballad" share a common linguistic origin rooted in the act of dancing. These words derive from the French *bal* and *baller*, which in turn stem from the Late Latin *ballare*, meaning "to dance." This Latin root is further connected to the Sicilian Greek *ballizein*.

## The Ball and Social Dancing
The word "ball" refers to a dance and, more specifically, a social gathering organized for the purpose of dancing. In modern society, these events are often dominated by the waltz, which comprises approximately five-sixths of most ball programmes. 

The waltz itself has a complex history. While Germany is credited with the waltz as it is currently danced—with the first German waltz tune, "Ach! du lieber Augustin," dated to 1770—its actual origin is French. It developed from the *Volte*, which originated from the *Lavolta* of Provence, a dance fashionable in the 16th century at the Valois court. The waltz was performed at the Paris opera in 1793 in the ballet *La Dansomanie* and entered English ballrooms in 1812. Although initially met with opposition, it gained popularity after being danced by Emperor Alexander at Almack's in 1816.

## Ballet and Pantomime
Ballet is defined as a performance incorporating music, dancing, and pantomime. The term passed from the Mediterranean Latin *ballare* (where *ballator* was synonymous with *saltator*) through Italian (*ballare* and *ballata*) and French (*ballet*) before entering Old English as *ballette*. 

According to Rousseau, the Old French meaning of *ballet* was "to dance, to sing, to rejoice." Over time, the meaning evolved. In its current theatrical application, a ballet is a representation where a story is conveyed solely through gesture and music, with the music providing a stronger emphasis than the human voice. While dancing is connected to the story, it is frequently incidental. To provide more precision, the archive distinguishes between the *ballet d'action* (or pantomime ballet), which tells a story, and a *divertissement*, which consists of a single scene such as a village festival.

## Ballads and Poetic Forms
The word "ballad" is derived from the Old French *baller*. Originally, a ballad was a song performed to the rhythmic movement of a dancing chorus. While the modern "ballad" is confined to singing, and the "ball" is confined to dancing, old ballads continue to be sung to dance tunes, despite no longer serving as vocal accompaniments for dances around the maypole.

Distinct from the song is the "ballade," a technical term for a complicated and fixed form of old-fashioned French poetry. Although it shares the same Low Latin root *ballare*, it has nothing in common with the song-ballad except this derivation. In the 14th and 15th centuries, it was spelled *balade*. 

The rules for the ballade were established by Henri de Croi in *L'Art et science de rhetorique* (first printed in 1493). A regular ballade consists of:
* Three stanzas and an envoi.
* A refrain repeated at the end of each stanza and the envoi.
* A limited set of three or four rhymes that must be reproduced exactly in every section.
* A specific stanza length based on the refrain; for example, a refrain of eight syllables requires *huitains* (eight-line stanzas), while ten syllables require *dizains* (ten-line stanzas).

Theodore de Banville's 1861 work, "Ballade aux Enfants Perdus," is cited as a faultless execution of this form. By 1550, Joachim du Bellay referred to these as "old French poems," and Moliere's character Philaminte later described the ballade as "fade" and out of fashion.
