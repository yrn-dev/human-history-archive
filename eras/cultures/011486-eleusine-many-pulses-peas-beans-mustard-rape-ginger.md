# Cultivated Plants and Agricultural Products

The cultivation of various grains, pulses, and oil-seeds varies significantly across different global regions, influenced by climate, soil quality, and available irrigation.

## Agriculture in the Indian Region
Indian agriculture is characterized by a combination of harvests from temperate and tropical zones. In the northern regions, winter cold allows for the cultivation of most European vegetables and cereals. Wheat is typically sown in November and reaped in early April. Conversely, the summer heat and rain create a tropical climate suitable for rice and other tropical cereals, which are generally sown in early July and reaped in September or October. In southern India and similar Asian islands, the lack of a sufficient difference between winter and summer prevents the proper cultivation of barley or wheat. In these areas, other cereals may be grown in all seasons if artificial irrigation is used, though agriculture is generally limited to the rainy months.

Cultivated plants of the Indian region include:
*   **Cereals and Grains:** Wheat, barley, rice, maize, and various millets including *Sorghum*, *Penicillaria*, *Panicum*, and *Eleusine*. In the Deccan and Khandesh, Joar (*Sorghum vulgare*) and bajra (*Holcus spicatus*) serve as staple food grains, while rice is the chief product of the Konkan. *Eleusine coracana* (Nachani) and *Paspalum serobiculatum* (kodra) are inferior grains grown on hill-sides that provide food for aboriginal tribes such as the Bhils, Kolis, and Waralis.
*   **Pulses:** The region grows many pulses, peas, and beans. Important varieties include gram (*Cicer arietinum*), tur (*Cajanus indicus*), mug (*Phaseolus Mungo*), and kulti (*Dolichos biflorus*).
*   **Oil-seeds:** These include mustard, rape, til (*Sesamum orientale*), safflower, linseed, and castor-oil.
*   **Fibres:** Cotton is a primary fibre, with improvements made through the introduction of American varieties in the southern Mahratta country and the Dharwar collectorate. In Khandesh, the superior Hinganghat variety has largely replaced the indigenous plant. Other fibres include sunn or tag (*Crotalaria juncea*) and Deccan hemp (*Hibiscus cannabinus*).
*   **Other Crops:** The region produces ginger, turmeric, pepper, capsicum, tobacco, *Sesamum*, poppy, *Crotolaria*, *Cannabis*, sugar, coffee, and tea. Various Cucurbitaceae are also grown. Fruit crops include mango, pomegranate, figs, peaches, vines, plantains, and oranges and lemons of many sorts. Common palms such as *Borassus*, *Phoenix*, and *Cocos* provide toddy and cocoa-nut.

## Botanical Characteristics of Legumes and Seeds
Plants that produce two cotyledons in the embryo are classified as dicotyledonous. In seeds such as the bean and pea, these cotyledons form the majority of the mature embryo and are exalbuminous. The texture of these cotyledons can be thick, as seen in the pea, or thin laminae with distinct venation, as seen in *Jatropha*, *Euonymus*, and *Ricinus*.

The legume, or pod, is a dry, many-seeded, unilocular monocarpellary fruit formed from one carpel. While it typically dehisces by the dorsal and ventral sutures, some legumes do not dehisce, such as the tamarind, *Cathartocarpus Fistula*, and *Arachis* (the earth-nut, which produces fruit underground). Other forms include the lomentaceous legume (*lomentum*), seen in *Entada*, *Ornithopus*, *Coronilla*, and *Hedysarum*.

Certain seeds possess an additional covering called an arillus or arillode. In the nutmeg, this is a scarlet covering known as mace. Other seeds may have cellular bodies called caruncles or strophioles, such as the Castor-oil plant (*Ricinus*). The hilum (umbilicus) is a scar on the seed; in the bean, it is black, while in many *Phaseolus* species, it is white. In the bean, a small process of integument called the embryotega is pushed up like a lid during sprouting.

## Regional Agricultural Examples
### Egypt
In Egypt, the land can bear three or four different kinds of crops in a single year. The cycle begins with cucumbers and lettuces, followed by corn, and concludes with several sorts of pulse peculiar to Egypt. Because the region is extremely hot with very little rain, these crops depend on reservoirs and canals for water. Additionally, the Nile provides rich pastures for cattle from November to March; during the Nile's inundation, cattle are fed with beans, barley, and cut straw or hay.

### Jíruft and Rudbar
In the districts of Jíruft and Rudbar, the lands yield rice, cotton, henna, dates, millet, pulse, horse-beans, French-beans, and *Palma Christi*. This region experiences a rainy season from January to March.

## Other Applications and Observations
### Apiculture
Certain plants are noted as rich sources of honey for bees. These include mustard, rape, beans, cotton, and a wide variety of trees and flowers such as the apple, cherry, peach, pear, and various maples.

### Roman Nomenclature
In Roman history, some cognomina were derived from cultivated plants. For example, the name Fabius is derived from *faba* (a bean), Lentulus from *lens* (pulse), and Cicero from the cultivation of *cicer*.
