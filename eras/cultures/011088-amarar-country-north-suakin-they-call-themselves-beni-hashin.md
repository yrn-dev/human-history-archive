# Amarar and the Ashraf

## The Ashraf of the Amarar Country
The Ashraf (also referred to as Shurefa or Sherifs) are a small, scattered tribe of African "Arabs." They are settled in several regions, including the valleys of the Baraka and Gash near Tokar, as well as the Amarar country located north of Suakin. The group refers to themselves as the Beni Hashin.

The name *sherif* (plural *ashraf*) is a title used for descendants of the prophet Mahomet, from whom the Beni Hashin claim descent. During the period of the khalifa Abdulla (1885-1898), the term Ashraf was used to identify the adherents and family of the mahdi, as the mahdi's family also claimed to be Ashraf. In the context of the Sudan troubles, the Ashraf of Tokar maintained their loyalty to Egypt.

## The Beja Peoples and the Amarar
The Amarar are identified as one of the chief Beja tribes, alongside other groups such as the Hadendoa, Bisharin, Hallenga, Hamran, Shukuria, and Ababda. 

Another related group, the Beni-Amer (or Amir), are also of the Beja family. They are described as African "Arabs" of Hamitic stock, ethnologically positioned between Nubians and Abyssinians. The Beni-Amer occupy portions of the coast-country of Eritrea north of Abyssinia and the Red Sea coast south of Suakin. They possess mixed Abyssinian and Beja blood and speak a dialect known locally as *Hassa*, which is half Tigre and half Beja. While the Beni-Amer marry women from other mountain tribes, such as the Bogos, they are too proud to allow their daughters to marry Abyssinians.

## Social Structure and Customs of the Beja
The Beja peoples, including the Amarar, practice polygyny. However, they grant their women a level of independence not found among the Arabs. This social position suggests an earlier matriarchal system. 

Within most Beja groups, a wife is permitted to return to her mother's tent at her discretion. Following the birth of a child, a wife has the power to repudiate her husband; for the husband to be re-accepted, he must provide a present. There have been recorded instances where women have used this custom to obtain all of their husband's possessions. Additionally, some tribes observe a custom called the "fourth day free," where women are considered married for only a certain number of days per week, allowing them to form whatever liaisons they choose on the odd day.
