# Bolivian Highlands

The Bolivian highlands are characterized by the Andean cordilleras, which occupy approximately two-fifths of the total area of Bolivia. These mountains cross the south-west corner of the country and project eastward toward the Brazilian highlands, forming a great obtuse angle. This region represents the massif of the Andean system, consisting of two great parallel chains accompanied by spurs and flanking ranges to the east.

## Physiography and the Great Plateau
The Andean system in Bolivia is composed of several parallel ranges that enclose deep ravines and great elevated plateaus, which are further broken by transverse ranges. To the north-east of Lake Titicaca, the terrain consists of a "knot" of lofty intersecting ridges known as the Nudo de Apolobamba, which contains some of the highest peaks in South America.

Between the two primary chains lies the great Bolivian tableland, or *alta-planicie*. This plateau extends from the Vilcanota knot in Peru in the north to the Serrania de Lipez on the Argentine frontier in the south. It measures approximately 500 miles in length and 80 miles in breadth, covering an area of about 40,000 square miles. The plateau is divided into two distinct regions: the northern part is known as the *puna*, while the southern part is the "desert of Lipez," which shares the character and appearance of the Puna de Atacama.

The *alta-planicie* is a great lacustrine basin that was once an inland sea, which previously had an outlet to the east via the La Paz gorge. The plateau's average elevation is about 12,650 feet near Lake Titicaca, descending by approximately 1,000 feet toward its southern end. The environment is described as bleak and inhospitable in the north and barren and arid toward the south, featuring great saline depressions that fill with water during the rainy season. The highest peak on the plateau is the Cerro de Tahua, reaching 17,454 feet.

## The Cordillera Occidental
The western range, known as the Cordillera Occidental, serves as part of the boundary between Bolivia and the northern provinces of Chile. This range follows the outline of the coast and acts as the western rampart of the Bolivian tableland. It is characterized by several snow-clad peaks, including:
*   Huallatiri (21,654 ft.)
*   Sajama (21,047 ft.)
*   Parinacota (20,918 ft.)
*   Pomarape (20,505 ft.)
*   Licancaur (19,685 ft.)
*   San Pedro y Pablo (19,423 ft.)
*   Oyahua (19,226 ft.)
*   Lirima (19,128 ft.)

## River Systems of the Highlands
The drainage of the Bolivian highlands is divided into three distinct regions: the Central Plateau, the Amazon, and La Plata.

The Amazon system includes rivers flowing into the Madeira. The Mamore river rises on the north-east slopes of the Sierra de Cochabamba and flows north to meet the Beni. A significant tributary of the Mamore is the Guapay (or Grande), which rises in the Cordillera Oriental east of Lake Pampa Aullaguas. Other tributaries rising on the north-east flanks of the Andes include the Secure, Chapare, Manique (or Apere), and the Yacuma. Additionally, the Yapacani rises on the east slopes of the Cordillera Real.

The south-east drainage basin discharges into the Paraguay. The primary river in this system is the Pilcomayo, which rises on the east slopes of the Cordillera Oriental opposite the southern end of Lake Pampa Aullaguas. Other streams in this region include the Tarija (or Rio Grande), which drains a district south-east of the Santa Victoria sierra, and the Parapiti, which rises on the eastern slopes of the Sierra de Misiones.

## Fauna of the Highlands
The mountainous and arid plateaus of the Andes are inhabited by members of the Camelidae family, specifically the guanaco, llama, alpaca, and vicuna. These animals are smaller than the African camel and possess no hump.

The llama and alpaca were domesticated long before the discovery of America. The llama is utilized as a pack animal, and its coarse wool is used for native garments. The alpaca is highly valued for its fine wool, which serves as a staple export for Bolivia. The guanaco and vicuna remain in a wild state. The vicuna is celebrated for its wool, which is woven into costly wearing apparel and *ponchos* (blanket cloaks), while the guanaco is hunted for its skin to create robes or rugs. The rearing of llamas and alpacas is a recognized industry in the highlands, managed entirely by the Indians.
