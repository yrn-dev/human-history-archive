# The Abhidhamma and Buddhist Canonical Works

The Buddhist canon consists of a variety of texts that preserve the doctrines, rules, and stories of early Buddhism. Because the Buddha taught through conversation and utilized the literary habits of his time, he embodied his doctrines in set phrases known as *sutras*. These served as *memoria technica*—catchwords that could be easily remembered and used to recall fuller expositions. While writing was known during this period, the lack of suitable materials made lengthy books impossible. Consequently, the Buddha used the ordinary conversational idiom of the day, Pali, rather than the Sanskrit used by the Brahmins.

## The Nikayas and Early Collections
Following the death of the Buddha, his disciples collected his sayings into the Four Nikayas, or "collections." These texts did not reach their final form until approximately fifty or sixty years after his death. A supplementary Nikaya was also created to house other sayings and verses, most of which were attributed to the disciples rather than the Buddha. Evidence indicates that slight additions were made to this supplementary Nikaya as late as the 3rd century B.C., during the time of Asoka.

For a few generations, these collections were transmitted via memory, though written memoranda and running commentaries likely accompanied the process. Approximately one hundred years after the Buddha's death, a schism occurred within the community, resulting in two schools, each maintaining its own arrangement of the canon in Pali or an allied dialect.

## The Abhidhamma
Added to the canonical books are seven works on *Abhidhamma*. These works provide a more classified and elaborate exposition of the Dhamma (doctrine) than that found in the Nikayas. The *Abhidhamma* works are considered later additions to the canon. 

Of these seven works, only one has been translated: the *Dhamma Sangani*. This text is described as an ancient manual for Buddhist students, with the translator, Mrs. Caroline Rhys Davids, estimating its date to be the 4th century B.C. The translation, published as *Buddhist Psychology*, provides a detailed account of the psychological conceptions that serve as the basis for Buddhist ethics.

## Other Canonical and Poetical Works
The Buddhist canon includes several other significant works that provide authority on early Buddhist ethics and philosophy:

*   **The Jataka Book**: A collection of 550 folk-tales current in India at the time the canon was formed. These verses are attributed to the Buddha in his previous births, with the Buddha identified as the hero in each story. While the verses are canonical, the prose is preserved only in the commentary. This collection is regarded as the most authentic, complete, and oldest extant collection of folklore, with many of its fables eventually migrating to Europe and appearing in medieval poems, story-books, and homilies.
*   **The Sutta Nipata**: A poetical work containing fifty-five poems. Most of these are short lyrics, though the last one differs in form. A separate ancient commentary on the majority of these poems is also included in the canon.
*   **The Vinaya**: A complete statement regarding the rules of the order.
