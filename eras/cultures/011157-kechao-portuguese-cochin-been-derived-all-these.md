# Cochin and Related Terms

The term "Cochin" appears in historical and geographical contexts referring to distinct regions in Southeast Asia and Southern India, as well as in relation to specific biological dyes.

## Cochin-China
Cochin-China is a region in the Indo-Chinese peninsula, described as being more nearly Chinese in all respects than neighboring populations. The area is well-watered, populous, and fertile, possessing a climate very similar to that of eastern Bengal. Its geography is defined by high mountain ranges extending south from the eastern extremity of the Tibetan mountains (between the 95th and 100th meridians), reaching the sea at various points in the Malay peninsula, the east flank of Bengal, and Cochin-China.

The states of Cochin-China include Cambodia and Tongking. The people of this region are known as Anam. It is probable that the Portuguese term "Cochin" was derived from a corruption of Kechao, which is the Anam name for the capital of Tongking. The region is served by great rivers, including the Mekong, the Menam, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy.

## Cochin State and Kodungalur
In southern India, within the presidency of Madras, lies the Cochin state. A town of considerable historical interest in this state is Kodungalur (also known as Cranganur). Although currently of little importance, it was once the best harbour on the coast and the only outlet for the Periyar and opening in the Cochin backwater until the formation of the Vypin harbour in 1314.

Kodungalur is associated with several historical traditions:
* **Religious Settlements:** Tradition identifies it as the seat of Cheraman Perumal's government and the first field of St. Thomas's labours in India in A.D. 52. While the visit of St. Thomas is generally viewed as mythical, the Syrian Church was firmly established there before the 9th century. Additionally, settlements of the sons of Israel are believed to have been even earlier, with claims to grants dating to A.D. 378.
* **Portuguese and European Influence:** In 1502, Syrian Christians sought Portuguese protection. The Portuguese built a fort in 1523 and enlarged it in 1565; however, their cruelty drove most of the sons of Israel to Cochin. 
* **Military Transitions:** The fort was captured by the Dutch in 1661, leading to forty years of contest between the Dutch, the raja of Kodungalur, and the zamorin. In 1776, Tippoo seized the stronghold. After several transfers between the Dutch, the Travancore raja, and Tippoo, the fort was destroyed by Tippoo in 1790. Today, the surrounding country is an autonomous principality tributary to the raja of Cochin.

## The Christians of St. Thomas
On the coast of Malabar, the Christians of St. Thomas were established for ages before the Portuguese opened Indian navigation. These people, who excelled the natives of Hindostan in arts, arms, and possibly virtue, were respected by the zamorin and the king of Cochin. Their society was characterized by merchants enriched by the pepper trade and husbandmen who cultivated the palm-tree.

Though they acknowledged a Gentoo as their sovereign, they were governed in temporal and spiritual matters by the bishop of Angamala, who claimed the title of metropolitan of India. This jurisdiction covered two hundred thousand souls across fourteen hundred churches. These Christians adhered to the communion of the Nestorian patriarch, with bishops ordained at Mosul. Their Syriac liturgy commemorated Nestorius and Theodore. They rejected the title "Mother of God" and viewed the Latin exaltation of the Virgin Mary as superstition. Consequently, Portuguese inquisitors viewed them as guilty of schism and heresy.

## Cochineal
Distinct from the geographical locations, cochineal refers to the dried scale-insect *Coccus cacti*. This insect lives on certain cactus plants, specifically *Opuntia coccinellifera* (sometimes called *Nopalea*) and *Opuntia tuna*. These insects are reared in "nopaleries," which can contain up to 50,000 plants. While native to Mexico, the majority of the supply is sourced from the Canary Islands and Colombia.

Cochineal is used to produce dyes, primarily for wool. The colouring matter, carminic acid, is believed by chemists to be a derivative of naphthalene. Depending on the mordant used, cochineal produces different colours:
* **Crimson:** Produced using aluminium (or by mordanting wool with tartar and alum).
* **Scarlet:** Produced using tin (specifically a mixture of oxalic acid, cochineal, and stannous chloride or nitrate of tin). A small amount of flavine may be added for a yellower shade.
* **Purple:** Produced using a chromium mordant.
* **Grey or Slate:** Produced using an iron mordant.

The industry has declined significantly following the discovery of azo scarlets derived from coal-tar.
