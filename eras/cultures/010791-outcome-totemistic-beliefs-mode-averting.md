# Taboo

## Definition and Origins
In its earliest origins, the concept of the taboo was not ethical in the modern sense of asceticism. Instead, it was characterized as a mode of averting the contaminating presence of demons and djinns. These practices were often the outcome of totemistic beliefs.

## Ritual Practices and Fasting
Fasting served as a primary method within these systems to prepare an individual for the sacramental eating of a sacred animal. This process was frequently assisted by the use of aperients and purgatives. In old Greek rites, abstaining from flesh was considered essential to avert djinns or the *Keres*, which are described as ill-regulated ghosts that return to the earth to molest living people.

Certain groups, such as the Orphic *mystae* and the Pythagoreans, maintained this abstinence from flesh throughout their entire lives. Porphyry advocated for this discipline for those who sought to live through Truth and Reason rather than merely discussing Reason, suggesting that by casting aside the body and the "tunic of the flesh," they could enter the arena and strive in the "Olympic contest of the soul."

## Totemism in Greek Culture
Theriolatry, or the worship of animals, suggests a connection to totemism. Some anthropologists, including W. Robertson Smith and Andrew Lang, have interpreted the prestige or sanctity of specific animals in Greek religion and mythology as remnants of an earlier totemistic system. However, this scientific hypothesis is considered hazardous by some, as flaws exist in the theory.

Studies of contemporary totem-tribes suggest that totemism is more significant as a social factor than a religious one. If totemism is viewed simply as a system of nomenclature—where a tribe names itself after a plant or animal—there are a few examples of Hellenic tribes that fit this description. For instance, there are traces of snake-tribes in Parium and Cyprus (the *Ophiogeneis*) and in Aetolia (the *Ophieis*). While the *Ophiogeneis* were on terms of intimacy with the snake, there is no record that they worshipped it. In the rare and doubtful cases where snakes were worshipped in Hellenic cults, the animal may have been viewed as the *avatar* of a divinity from the under-world or as the incarnation of an ancestor.

Current knowledge of ancient Greek tribal marriage laws shows no clear evidence of totemistic arrangements, and the religion of contemporary savages does not provide a natural explanation for the peculiar phenomena found in early Hellenic polytheism.
