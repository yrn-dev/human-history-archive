# Cocos, Phoenix and Borassus

In the Indian region, the most common palms are *Cocos*, *Phoenix*, and *Borassus*. These plants are primary sources of cocoa-nut and toddy.

## Phoenix and Date Sugar
The *Phoenix sylvestris*, known as the toddy palm, is closely allied to the date palm and is considered by some to be the parent stock of all cultivated varieties. This tree produces a sap or toddy that serves as the basis for several products:
* **Date Sugar:** A valuable commercial product of the East Indies obtained from the sap.
* **Arrack:** A spirit formed by fermenting and distilling the juice.
* **Fresh Consumption:** The juice may also be drunk fresh when not boiled down into sugar.

Other parts and products of the *Phoenix sylvestris* are used in the same manner as those of the date palm. Additionally, a small species called *Phoenix farinifera*, which grows in the hill country of southern India, is used to obtain date palm meal from its stem.

## Cocos and the Narghile
The *Cocos* palm, or coco-nut tree, is known in India by the name *nargil*. This name is the origin of the term *narghile*, a type of Indian pipe similar in principle to the hookah. The *narghile* consists of a pipe with a long flexible tube attached to a bowl of water (often scented) resting on a stand. The smoke of the tobacco passes through the water to be cooled before reaching the smoker. Historically, the *narghile* utilized a coco-nut as the receptacle for the water, although modern versions are frequently constructed from metal, glass, or porcelain.

## Regional Distribution and Agriculture
The distribution of these palms and related agriculture varies across the Indian region:

**Southern India and Coastal Areas**
In the Kolaba district of British India (southern division of Bombay), the sea frontage is largely fringed by a belt of betel-nut and coco-nut palms. In southern India and other Asian islands with similar climates, the difference between summer and winter is not significant enough to allow for the proper cultivation of barley or wheat. Agriculture in these areas is generally limited to the rainy months, unless artificial irrigation is used.

**Ceylon**
In Ceylon, the town of Kurunegala serves as a center for the cultivation of coco-nut, along with cocoa, coffee, and rice.

## Context of Indian Cultivated Plants
The palms *Cocos*, *Phoenix*, and *Borassus* exist alongside a diverse array of other cultivated plants in the Indian region. These include:
* **Cereals and Grains:** Rice, wheat, barley, maize, and various millets such as *Eleusine*, *Panicum*, *Penicillaria*, and *Sorghum*.
* **Fruits and Nuts:** Mango, pomegranate, plantains, figs, peaches, vines, and various sorts of lemons and oranges.
* **Other Crops:** Coffee, tea, sugar, indigo, cotton, poppy, *Cannabis*, *Sesamum*, tobacco, ginger, turmeric, pepper, capsicum, mustard, rape, and many pulses, beans, and peas.

Indian agriculture is characterized by a combination of temperate and tropical zone harvests. North of the tropic, the winter cold allows for the cultivation of European vegetables and cereals, with wheat typically sown in November and reaped in early April. Conversely, the summer heat and rain in the same region support tropical cereals like rice, which are generally sown in early July and reaped in September or October.
