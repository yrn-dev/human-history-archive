# Classification and Characteristics of Bantu Languages

The Bantu languages constitute a striking family of speech characterized by a high degree of homogeneity, particularly when compared to the linguistic diversity found in Africa north of the Bantu borderland. The study of these languages involves the classification of known forms and the determination of their geographical limits, though some small patches of languages spoken by a few villages possess isolated characters that make them difficult to include in broader groups.

## Geographical Distribution and Classification
The domain of Bantu speech is broadly divided into four primary sections:
*   **The Great Lakes and East Coast:** This group extends down to and includes the Zambezi basin.
*   **The South-Central Group:** This includes the Bechuana-Zulu languages.
*   **The South-West Group:** This extends from the southern part of the Belgian Congo to the Angola-Congo coast and Damaraland.
*   **The Western Group:** This encompasses languages of Cameroon and the Central and Northern Congo, likely including group No. 2 of the Semliki river and Albert Nyanza.

Specific groups within the Western region include the *Bangala-Bobangi-Liboko* (commercial languages of the Upper Congo such as *Ngala*, *Bangi*, *Liboko*, *Poto*, *Ngombe*, and *Yanzi*), the *Bateke* (*Batio*), the *Di-Kele* and *Benga* dialects of the Batanga coast and Spanish Guinea, and the *Duala* group of the Cameroon river estuary. The *Fan* or *Pangwe* forms of speech, found in French Gabun and German Cameroon, are noted as being so corrupt they are barely recognizable as Bantu.

Notably, the Bantu field does not cover the entire Congo; the area between Stanley Falls and the Rubi river is outside this field. The *Wamanga* and *Bondonga* languages are not Bantu, but are allied to the *Mundu* of the Egyptian Sudan and the *Mbuba-Momfu* of Nepoko and the Ituri.

## Linguistic Structure and Concord
A defining feature of Bantu languages is the system of pronominal-prefix and concord. The concord involves the repetition of pronominal particles within a sentence. For example, in a reconstructed "Old Bantu" sentence, the repetition of particles allows for a structured relationship between the subject and the verbs or adjectives. In modern *Luganda*, this is seen in the phrase "*Bo aba-ntu ba-bi ba-bota tu-ba-tia*" (They are bad people who kill; we fear them).

The language utilizes a series of prefixes, some of which persist as concords even after the prefix itself has fallen out of use as a definite article. Examples include:
*   **The 2nd prefix:** Originally *Baba-* in archaic speech (such as Mt. Elgon), it may degenerate into *Va-*, *Wa-*, *Ma-*, or *A-*.
*   **The 7th prefix:** *Ki-*, which may transition through *Ci-* into *Si-* or *Shi-*.
*   **The 10th prefix:** Originally *Ti-*, *Tin-*, *Zi-*, or *Zin-*. In the *Swahili* of Zanzibar, while the plural prefix *zinombe* has ceased to be used for "cows," the *Zi-* particle reappears in the concord (e.g., *Nombe hizi zangu*).

The Bantu verb consists of a practically unchangeable root, which serves as the second person singular of the imperative. To this root, various particles—often worn-down verbs, adverbs, or prepositions—are prefixed and suffixed to create moods and tenses. A common method for forming the preterite tense involves the suffix *-ile* or *-ili* (related to the root for "to be" or "exist"), as seen in the Zulu transition from *tanda* (love) to *tandile* (have loved).

## Archaic Dialects and Evolution
The most archaic dialects currently existing are found in the Bemba country, the north coast of Tanganyika, Ruwenzori, Mt. Elgon, Uganda, and Unyoro. Other archaic types include the *Ci-bodzo* of the Zambezi delta, the Subiya speech of the western Zambezi, and the Nkonde and Kese dialects of Lake Nyasa. While *Ki-swahili* is primitive in its roots, it is not so in its prefixes. The Zulu-Kaffir language was once considered the most archaic, but it has been deposed by the languages of the Great Lakes; it is now noted for being unusually divergent in some features.

## Origins and Migration
Linguistic research suggests that the Bantu people left their primal home in the heart of Africa approximately 2,000 years ago. This date is deduced from the common root for domestic fowl, *kuku* (and its variants *nkuku*, *ngoko*, *nsusu*, *nguku*, *nku*), which is found throughout Bantu Africa. Because the domestic fowl reached Africa via Egypt no earlier than 500 to 400 B.C., the Bantu must have adopted the bird before their southern exodus.

The creation of the pronominal-prefix type of language predates this exodus. While Bantu shows some affinities in numerals (2, 3, 4, and sometimes 5) and certain roots with languages of the Gold Coast, lower Niger, and N. Togoland, it remains a distinct division of sexless Negro speech with no obvious Hamitic influence.

Traditions of the Bantu peoples point to a northern origin. During their migration, they occupied Zanzibar and Pemba and traveled to the Comoro archipelago and the west coast of Madagascar, where they likely gave rise to the race known as the Va-zimba.
