# Language and Indo-Aryan Languages

## Terminology and Definitions
The term "Aryan" has been employed by various philologists in a confusing variety of ways. Max Muller used "Aryan" as a convenient short term for the entire body of languages more commonly known as Indo-Germanic or Indo-European. In this context, Muller defined "Aryas" simply as those who speak Aryan languages, regardless of their blood, color, or anatomical characteristics. He explicitly avoided ethnological significations, asserting that the term refers to the grammar of the language rather than hereditary traits.

Conversely, some sources note that "Aryan" was originally applied only to the Indo-Iranian people. In modern discussion, the term has been used in a wider sense because "Indo-European" and "Indo-Germanic" are considered clumsy or objectionable terms. Consequently, "Aryan" may be used to describe the entire family of languages or be restricted in a narrower sense to Indo-Iranian speech.

## The Indo-European Family and Aryan Expansion
The parent Indo-European language was spoken by a primitive race whose original home is not certainly known, though current tendencies suggest it was located in Europe. This language gradually diffused across much of Europe and portions of Asia. The Teutonic or Germanic branch—represented by the languages of England, Germany, Holland, Denmark, Iceland, and the Scandinavian peninsula—belongs to this family.

As the primitive Aryan speech spread, it broke into various daughter languages. To the west, north of the Black Sea and the Carpathians, Aryan-speaking tribes used a dialect called Slavonian, which became the source of Polish, Russian, Bulgarian, and Serbian. Other variations individualized as Armenian and Indo-Iranian. The Indo-Iranian branch eventually split into several languages, including Persian and Sanskrit.

## Indo-Aryan Languages in India
Sanskrit was the language of fair-complexioned Aryan speakers who moved eastward into India between 3000 and 1000 B.C., conquering the dark Dravidian peoples who previously possessed the land. 

Research by Dr. Grierson in his 1906 monograph, "The Pisaca Languages of North-Western India," suggests that certain dialects of the north-western frontier—specifically those of Gilgit, Chitral, Dardistan, and Kafiristan—constitute a separate group. These are regarded as descended from Aryan but independent of both Iranian and Sanskrit.

## Modern Indo-Aryan Vernaculars
The modern Indo-Aryan languages include an Eastern Group consisting of four forms of speech: Bengali, Bihari, Assamese, and Oriya. According to 1901 data for British India, the total number of speakers for this group was 90,242,167. The distribution was as follows:
*   Bengali: 44,624,048
*   Bihari: 34,579,844
*   Oriya: 9,687,429
*   Assamese: 1,350,846

Bihari is the most western of these four and is also spoken by inhabitants of the Tarai districts of Nepal. The Bihari vocabulary is characterized by "tadbhavas" (home-born words) used in both colloquial and literary speech, while "tatsamas" (words borrowed from Sanskrit) are few. There are very few borrowings from Arabic, Persian, or other languages.

## Historical and Archaeological Evidence
The separation of Aryan from other Indo-European languages remains unclear. However, discoveries by German excavators at Boghaz Keui in Cappadocia provided significant evidence. Treaties from the beginning of the 14th century B.C. between the kings of Mitanni and the Hittites appear to contain the names of Indra, Varuna, and Mitra, which are deities of early Aryan mythology.

Additionally, a previously unknown Indo-European language called Tocharish was reported from Turfan in Turkestan. Preliminary accounts up to 1909 suggest that Tocharish may be more closely related to Western groups than to Aryan, though further investigation is required.
