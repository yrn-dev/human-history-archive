# BONNET

The term "bonnet" is derived from the Latin *bonetum*, which originally referred to a specific kind of stuff and subsequently to the cap manufactured from that material.

## General History and Usage
Originally, a bonnet was a soft cap or head covering. In the English language, this remained the common term for such headgear until the end of the 17th century. This usage persists in Scotland, particularly in reference to the cap known as a "glengarry." In the 12th century, the flat bonnet was one of several common shapes of hats and caps used across all social classes, alongside the skull-cap, the brimmed hat, and the Phrygian cap.

In modern times, the term has evolved to describe a specific type of woman's head-covering. This is distinguished from a "hat" by its close fit to the head and the frequent absence of a brim, though its specific shape has varied according to fashion and period.

## Technical and Slang Applications
Beyond headwear, the term "bonnet" has been extended to other contexts:
*   **Maritime:** In the context of a ship's sail, a bonnet is an additional piece of material laced to the bottom. Historically, it appears to have referred to a piece laced to the top; records from the beginning of the 16th century use the phrase "to vail the bonnet" (from the French *avaler*, meaning to let down) to mean "strike sail."
*   **Mechanical:** The term is applied to certain protective devices used in safety-lamps or steam-engines.
*   **Slang:** In slang, a "bonnet" refers to a decoy or an accomplice to a gambler.

## Related Military and Ecclesiastical Headgear
While distinct from the general bonnet, other specialized caps share similar evolutionary or functional traits:

**The Busby**
The busby is an English name for a military head-dress made of fur. The term may be derived from "buzz wig" or associated with Dr. Busby of Westminster. In its original Hungarian form, it was a cylindrical fur cap with a colored cloth "bag" hanging from the top, which was attached to the right shoulder to protect against sword-cuts. In Great Britain, there are two primary types: the cylindrical hussar busby (worn by the Royal Horse Artillery and hussars) and the rifle busby, which is a taller folding cap of astrachan resembling a Glengarry. Both feature straight plumes in the front. Colloquially, "busby" also refers to the full dress feather bonnet of Highland infantry and the tall raccoon-and-bear-skin caps worn by fusiliers and foot-guards.

**The Biretta and College Cap**
The biretta (from the Medieval Latin *biretum*) is a square, stiff cap worn by Catholic clergy, constructed from a cardboard framework covered in silk or cloth. It features three or four arched ridges on top meeting at a central knob or tassel (*floccus*). Its color indicates rank: white for the pope, red for cardinals, purple for bishops, and black for lower clergy. 

Originally a round, low cap with a knob, the *biretum* evolved by the 16th century toward a square shape. In 17th-century England, this developed into a "square cap" with a projecting rim of thick stuff, a garment denounced by Puritans as a symbol of High Church Erastianism. By the 18th century, the top was made of card or wood, and the knob became a long tassel, resulting in the modern "college cap."
