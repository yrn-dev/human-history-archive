# Apere and Yacuma

The Apere and Yacuma are rivers located in Bolivia that serve as tributaries to the Mamore river. Both rivers originate on the north-east flanks of the Andes.

## The Yacuma River and Regional Drainage
The Yacuma river is characterized by its role in draining a specific geographical region consisting of swamps and lakes located to the north of the Sierra Chamaya. This river is part of a larger hydrographic system that traverses the llanos of Mojos and Guarayos. This extensive region was once covered by the ancient Mojos Lake and continues to contain large areas that remain undrained, such as the region of Lake Rojoagua (also known as Roguaguado).

The Yacuma is one of several rivers, including the Guapore, San Miguel, Guapay, Mamore, Beni, and Madre de Dios, that traverse these low-lying plains. The surrounding landscape is composed of flooded bottomlands, great swamps, and alluvial plains.

## The Amazon Drainage Basin
The Apere and Yacuma are situated within the Amazon river-system, one of three distinct drainage regions in Bolivia. This system encompasses more than one-half of the republic and consists of rivers that flow either directly or indirectly into the Madeira, which is a major tributary of the Amazon.

The primary rivers of this specific system are the Beni and the Mamore. These two rivers unite at 10 deg. 20' S. latitude to form the Madeira. The Mamore itself is formed by the confluence of several streams. While the Guapay (or Grande) is considered the main stream of the Mamore system, the Apere and Yacuma are among the other large Bolivian tributaries that rise on the north-east flanks of the Andes to feed into the Mamore.

## Geography of the Lowlands
The rivers flow through a region that contradicts the popular conception of Bolivia as solely a rugged, mountainous country. Approximately three-fifths of the country is made up of gently undulating forest regions, low alluvial plains, and great swamps. 

The area north of the Chiquitos sierras and the Andes—where the Yacuma and other tributaries operate—is characterized by the llanos of Mojos and Guarayos. This district is one of the richest in Bolivia and is capable of sustaining a large population. It contains extensive open plains and rich agricultural districts; cattle-raising has been successfully practiced here since the era of the Jesuit missions. However, the region is also subject to periodical inundations, and the lower slopes of the Andes toward the north-west are covered in heavy forests.
