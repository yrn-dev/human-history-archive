# BOOTH

## Etymology and Primary Meaning
The word "booth" is connected to a Teutonic root meaning "to dwell," a root that also produced the word "bower." In its primary sense, a booth is a temporary dwelling constructed from boughs or other slight materials.

## Evolution of Usage
Over time, the term expanded beyond its original meaning of a dwelling to describe non-permanent erections used for commerce. This includes market stalls or tents at fairs where goods are sold. In a more modern application, the term is used for the temporary structure where votes are registered, known as a polling-booth.

The transition from temporary to permanent structures is illustrated by the history of Old Fish Street in London. According to Stow, the houses there began as movable boards set out on market days for the sale of fish. After obtaining licenses to establish sheds, these structures evolved into shops and eventually became tall houses.

## Regional Variations and the Bothy System
In Scotland, the word appears as *bothie* or *bothy*, generally referring to a cottage or a hut. Specifically, it was used to describe a barrack-like room on large farms intended for the lodging of unmarried labourers. This arrangement, referred to as the "Bothy system," was formerly common in northern Scotland and Aberdeenshire.

## Related Teutonic Roots
The concept of dwelling is further reflected in other Teutonic derivations. The Old English word *bonda* is derived from the root of the verb *bua*, meaning to dwell or to have a house (cognate with the English "boor" and German *Bauer*). This term appears in the word "husband." 

Historically, *bonda* was used in the laws of Canute as an equivalent to the Old English *ceorl*. Following the Norman Conquest, as the position of the churl became less free, the "bond" (meaning householder) began to approximate the status of the "villein" and later the "serf." This shift in meaning toward serfdom and slavery resulted from an early phonetic confusion with the word "bond" (derived from *bindan*, meaning "that which binds"). This confusion also affected the word "bondage," which properly meant "tenure in villeinage" but came to be used as a synonym for slavery.
