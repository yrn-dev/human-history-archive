# Hues and Variations in Nature and Culture

The concept of hue and variation manifests across diverse biological species, geological formations, and human cultural expressions, ranging from the physical pigmentation of animals to the abstract "hues" of musical tuning and ethics.

## Biological Pigmentation and Variation
In the animal kingdom, hue often varies by species, subspecies, and environment. The bongo (*Boocercus eurycerus*), a West African bushbuck, is characterized by a deep chestnut male with white spots below the eye, a white crescent on the chest, and narrow white stripes on the body. The East African bongo (*B. e. Isaaei*) possesses a body hue that is richer and stronger. Unlike the true bongo, where the presence of horns in females is probable but unproven, horns are present in both sexes of the East African form.

Among birds, insular populations often differ from their continental counterparts. Birds in the Mediterranean province—including those in the Azores, Madeira, and the Canaries—tend to have stronger bills and legs and darker plumage. Specific examples include the bullfinch of the western islands (*Pyrrhula murina*), whose male has a sober mouse-coloured breast instead of the ruddy breast seen in *P. vulgaris*. Similarly, the Canary Islands' peculiar chaffinch (*Fringilla teydea*) is distinguished by a sombre hue.

In marine mammals, seal skins are divided into hair seals and fur seals. Observations indicate that in exposed regions, nature requires greater effort to secure food, resulting in coarser body coverings and stronger limbs. Conversely, in densely wooded districts, furs are softer, more silky, and darker in colour.

## Geological and Natural Diversity
The Qur'an describes the diversity of colours as a manifestation of divine action. This diversity extends to the earth's topography, where mountains contain streaks and tracts of various hues, including red, white, and those that are intensely or raven-black. ## Abstract and Cultural Applications of Hue
The term "hue" is also applied to non-visual concepts in history and the arts. In ancient Greek music, before the civilization reached its historic form, there was significant independence in lyre stringing and intonation. This included chromatic (half-tone) and enharmonic (quarter-tone) tunings, which were referred to as *chroai*, or the "hues of tuning." These modifications of tetrachords were later disused during the classic period.

In a biographical context, the term has been used to describe moral blindness. Regarding Machiavelli, it is noted that he was "colour-blind to commonplace morality," effectively merging the "hues of ethics" into a single "grey monotone of politics."

## Environmental and Material Factors
Physical richness and strength are often attributed to soil and climate. In the Grampian territory, the soil of the Howe is described as stronger and richer than that found in the Dee valley, while the most fertile regions are along the coast, characterized by deep loam resting on clay.

In human history, the "richer sort" of people have been distinguished from the poorer by their access to resources during times of crisis. During periods of famine and sedition, the wealthy might sell their possessions for wheat, whereas the poorer would sell theirs for barley. In such desperate conditions, the physical state of a person's body served as an indicator of their food supply; those who were "wasted away" were ignored by robbers, while those in "good case" were targeted under the assumption that they possessed concealed food.
