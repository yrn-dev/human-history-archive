# Baluchistan

Baluchistan is a sparsely populated region covering a larger area than any Indian province except for Bengal, Madras, and Burma. It is characterized by diverse geography and political divisions, split primarily between British and Persian administrations.

## Geography and Topography
The region is divided into two distinct parts by a railway line, its associated plains, passes, and the hills surrounding Quetta. 

North of the railway line, the territory is situated between the plains of the Indus and Afghanistan. This area consists of long ridges of picturesque highlands, including the central ranges of the Suliman system, which served as the prehistoric home of the Pathan highlander. The average breadth of this northern district is 150 miles, expanding to over 200 miles along the railway and narrowing to less than 100 miles at the Gomal. Drainage in this section either flows north toward the Gomal or moves east through the limestone axis of the hills to the Indus.

South of the railway lies a square block of territory measuring approximately 300 miles by 300 miles. This area is primarily the home of the Baluch and the Brahui. It features the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, where drainage forms deep gorges that pass east into the plains of Sind. Other streams in this southern section either flow in straight lines to the Arabian Sea north of Karachi or curve west to disappear into inland swamps.

## British Baluchistan
British Baluchistan is a distinctive province leased from Kalat and managed under the civil administration of British India. It consists of an irregularly shaped block of upland territory. Its boundaries include:
* **North:** The main water-divide between the Lora and Zhob.
* **East:** An arm extending to the Thal Chotiali valley.
* **South-East:** The basins of the Nari and Bolan as far as Sibi.
* **South:** Reaching Nushki through Shorarud.
* **West:** Resting on the Kwaja Amran (Kojak) Range, including the Chaman railway terminus.

Officially, the province includes the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts were originally Afghan but were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, though they had been nominally handed to Kalat forty years prior. 

The region is overseen by the Baluchistan Agency, which extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. While some districts within the agency maintain a high degree of independence, the province as a whole is subject to the supreme control of the agent to the governor-general of India and his political assistants. The level of independence in various districts generally varies in proportion to their distance from Quetta.

## Persian Baluchistan
Persian Baluchistan is a province of Persia covering approximately 60,000 square miles along the northern shore of the Arabian Sea. It is bounded by Kerman to the west, Seistan and the central Persian desert to the north, and independent and British Baluchistan to the east. 

The landscape is largely composed of barren hills, mountains, and arid, waterless plains—some featuring moving sands and others being stony. Only a small portion of the land is under cultivation. The principal rivers are the Bampur and Mashkid, which flow away from the sea and are lost in depressions known as *hamuns*. Rivers that flow into the sea are generally unimportant and remain dry for most of the year.

Administratively, it is a division of the province of Kerman, subdivided into twenty districts:
1. Bampur
2. Serhad
3. Dizek
4. Jalk
5. Sib
6. Irafshan
7. Magas
8. Serbaz
9. Lashar
10. Champ
11. Fannuj
12. Bazman
13. Aptar
14. Daman
15. Aprandagan
16. Asfehgeh
17. Surmij
18. Meskutan
19. Pushteh
20. Makran (the country of the Ichthyophagi), which includes the subdistricts of Bahu, Dasht, Geh, Kucheh, and Kasrkand.

The total population of Persian Baluchistan is under 200,000. The province remained practically independent until Persian troops occupied Bampur in 1849; Persian supremacy was not recognized in some extreme eastern districts until 1872.

## Climate and Ecology
Baluchistan is part of an almost unbroken desert stretching from Arabia through south Persia and Baluchistan to Sind. This region is characterized by extremely hot and dry tracts. The vegetation consists largely of plants also found in Sind and Africa, some of which extend into the hotter regions of India.

In British Baluchistan, the climate is described as dry, bracing, and exhilarating. However, it is subject to temperature extremes that can lead to severe forms of fever, though it is generally considered favorable for European existence. In contrast, the northern Pathan district is characterized by clear, bracing air with no great extremes of temperature and vegetation that is often alpine.
