# Sanskrit and Iranian Linguistic Relations

Sanskrit and the Iranian languages form part of the Aryan group, which is distinguished from other Indo-European languages by four primary characteristics. These include the representation of the short schwa-sound by *i* (seen in the word *pita*, meaning "father"), the confusion of original long and short *a* sounds with original long and short *e* and *o*, the transformation of original *s* into *s* after certain consonants and the vowels *i* and *u*, and the use of the suffix *-nam* for the genitive plural of stems ending in a vowel, a feature borrowed by analogy from stems ending in *-n*. Examples of this suffix include the Sanskrit *asvanam* ("of horses"), the Zend *aspanam*, and the Old Persian *aspanam*.

## Distinctions Between Sanskrit and Iranian

While Sanskrit and Iranian are remarkably alike, there are clear distinctions between them. One primary difference is the treatment of Aryan voiced aspirates (*gh, dh, bh*); these survive in Sanskrit but are confused in Iranian with original *g, d, b*. Further linguistic changes occurred in the language of the later parts of the Avesta. Additionally, the Aryan breathed aspirates (*kh, th, ph*) generally become spirants in Iranian, except when combined with certain consonants.

Phonetic differences also appear in the treatment of the letter *s*. In Iranian, Aryan *s* becomes *h* when it appears initially before vowels and in certain medial cases, a trait that resembles Greek (for example, the Sanskrit *sapta* corresponds to the Zend *hapta* and the Greek *hepta*, meaning "seven").

Vowel changes are also prevalent in Zend, though these are not shared with Old Persian. Some of these changes result from epenthesis or umlaut, as seen in the comparison between the Sanskrit *arya* and the Zend *airya-*. A striking difference also exists in the numeral for "one": Sanskrit uses *eka-*, while Zend uses *aeva-* and Old Persian uses *aiva-*. The Iranian forms share a stem with the Greek *oi(f)o-s*, meaning "alone."

## Comparative Philology and Scholarship

The study of the common origin of Sanskrit and other languages has been a central focus of comparative grammar. Franz Bopp, a German philologist, held the chair of Sanskrit and comparative grammar at Berlin starting in 1821. His work, *Vergleichende Grammatik des Sanskrit, Zend, Griechischen, Lateinischen, Litthauischen, Altslavischen, Gothischen, und Deutschen*, sought to trace phonetic laws, describe original grammatical structures, and investigate the origin of grammatical forms through intercomparison. Bopp's earlier research in Paris, influenced by scholars like Alexander Hamilton, led to his 1816 work comparing the conjugation system of Sanskrit with that of German, Latin, Greek, and Persian.

Other scholars have contributed to the archive of Sanskrit knowledge. Arthur Coke Burnell, an English scholar in Madras, collected 350 manuscripts for the India library and published the *Classified Index to the Sanskrit MSS. in the Palace at Tanjore* in 1880. Burnell also worked on elucidating the principles of Hindu law.

## Related Languages and Dialects

The Indo-Aryan group includes Sanskrit and Prakrit, with the synthetic declension of these languages being preserved most completely in Marathi. Prakrit simplified the complex declensional system of Sanskrit by assimilating all cases to the declension of *a*-bases.

Beyond the primary Sanskrit and Iranian branches, research by Dr. Grierson suggests that various dialects found on the north-western frontier—specifically in Gilgit, Chitral, Dardistan, and Kafiristan—constitute a separate group. While these "Pisaca Languages" are descended from Aryan, they are considered independent of both Iranian and Sanskrit.

## Cultural and Terminology Notes

The term *arya* (Sanskrit) and its counterparts *airya-* (Zend) and *ariya-* (Old Persian) were used as national names in Persia, Bactria, and India. Within Sanskrit, a distinction may be made between *arya* (the origin of "Aryan") and *arya*, which appears in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet for deities.

In the context of Hindu mythology, the Sanskrit word *avatar* means "descent" and refers to the incarnation of a deity on earth. The ten Avatars of Vishnu are prominent; these include appearances as a fish, a tortoise, a hog, a dwarf, Krishna, Buddha, and a monster (half man, half lion) created to destroy an Iranian giant. The tenth Avatar, Kalki, is described as a white-winged horse who will destroy the earth.
