# China: Geography and Physical Features

## Geographic Location and Boundaries
China is situated between the North Pacific and the eastern flank of the Tibetan plateau. Its latitudinal limits are approximately 40 degrees North in the north and 20 degrees North in the south. The country is characterized by a landscape that is generally broken up by mountains of moderate elevation, though the mountains of its western border are of greater importance.

## Climate and Land Characteristics
The climate of China is analogous to that of southern Europe, featuring hot summers and cold winters, with the northern regions experiencing decidedly severe winters. The land is generally well wooded, fertile, and highly cultivated. It is also a populous region and is well watered.

## Hydrography and the Tibetan Connection
The eastern face of Tibet serves as the source for the two great rivers of China: the Yang-tsze-kiang and the Hwang-ho. The Hwang-ho rises from the north-east angle of the Tibetan face, while the Yang-tsze-kiang rises from the south-east. In Tibet, the main stream of the Yang-tsze-kiang is known as the Dichu. Its primary feeder is the Ya-lung-kiang, which originates near the Hwang-ho and is regarded as the territorial boundary between Tibet and China.

## Bordering Mountain Systems
The western reaches of China are influenced by several complex mountain systems. The Nan-shan, or Southern Mountains, reach far into China to the 113° E meridian. The Nan-shan is the eastward continuation of the Astin-tagh. 

The Astin-tagh is viewed by several judges—including Przhevalsky, Sven Hedin, Bogdanovich, and Grenard—as a branch or subsidiary range of the main Kuen-lun range. The Astin-tagh forms the outermost border-ridge or escarpment to the north of the Tibetan plateau. It is not a single continuous chain but consists of multiple parallel main ranges: two in some areas, three in the east, and up to four to the northeast of Tsaidam. These are further flanked by offshoots, spurs, and subsidiary chains.

The Astin-tagh eventually swells into the massif of Anambaruin-ula, which is traversed by at least three minor parallel chains. East of this massif, the system contracts back to two main ranges. The northern branch of this system merges into the Nan-shan, while the southerly branch is identified as the Humboldt Range (which features a pass at 13,200 ft.) and is likely continued in the south Koko-nor Range, which overhangs the Koko-nor to the south.

## Regional Context and the Gobi Desert
To the north and north-west, China's geography is bounded by the Tian-shan and the Yablonoi Mountains. The area between these, the northern face of the Tibetan plateau to the south, and the Khingan range to the east, forms the Gobi, the great desert of central Asia. The western portion of this desert constitutes eastern or Chinese Turkestan, while the eastern portion is nearly conterminous with south Mongolia. This desert region maintains a high elevation, with no part appearing to be less than 2,000 ft. above sea level.
