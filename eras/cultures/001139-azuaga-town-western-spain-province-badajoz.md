# AZUAGA

## Geography and Location
Azuaga is a town located in western Spain within the province of Badajoz. It is situated on the Belmez-Fuente del Arco railway. The town serves as the central market for livestock originating from broad upland pastures. These pastures are watered by two rivers: the Matachel, which is a left-hand tributary of the Guadiana, and the Bembezar, which is a right-hand tributary of the Guadalquivir.

## Demographics and Industry
As of 1900, the population of Azuaga was 14,192. The town's local economy includes the manufacture of pottery and coarse woollen goods.

## Regional Context: Province of Badajoz
Azuaga is part of the province of Badajoz, which was formed in 1833 from districts previously belonging to the province of Estremadura. Badajoz is the largest province in the kingdom, covering an area of 8,451 square miles with a population of 520,246 in 1900. It is bounded by Portugal to the west, Seville and Huelva to the south, Cordova and Ciudad Real to the east, and Caceres to the north.

The landscape of the province is characterized largely by a monotonous, flat, or slightly undulating plain, though some districts contain low ranges of hills. The primary river is the Guadiana, which flows from east to west across the north of the province. The region experiences extreme temperatures, with fierce north and north-west winds in winter and intense heat in summer; the latter often brings prevalent intermittent fevers to the Guadiana valley.

Due to scanty average rainfall and limited irrigation, agriculture and the cultivation of olives and vines are in a backward state. However, Badajoz possesses more livestock than any other Spanish province, notably large herds of sheep and goats, as well as acorn-fed swine celebrated throughout Spain for their bacon and hams.

## Regional Economy and Infrastructure
The industrial sector of the province is of limited importance, consisting of the production of oils, soaps, cork, leather, and coarse cotton and woollen stuffs. Commercial interests are more significant, particularly the transit trade with Portugal, which is facilitated by seven custom-houses. 

Mineral exploitation in the province has been hindered by poor communication and a lack of water. In 1903, only 26 of nearly 600 registered mines were operational, producing lead and small amounts of copper.
