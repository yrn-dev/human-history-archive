# The Climate and Geography of Tibet

## Physical Geography and Elevation
Tibet is characterized by a mean elevation of 15,000 feet above the sea. Its geography is defined by significant mountain ranges; it is connected to the lower levels of Hindostan by the broad mountainous slopes known as the Himalaya. To the north, the slope that descends toward the central plains of the Gobi is generally referred to as the Kuen-lun, although this is apparently a Chinese designation and not the name used locally.

The region serves as a primary source for several major river systems. The two great rivers of China, the Yang-tsze-kiang and the Hwang-ho, both originate from the eastern face of Tibet. The Hwang-ho rises from the north-east angle, while the Yang-tsze-kiang (known in Tibet as the Dichu) rises from the south-east. The Ya-lung-kiang, a chief feeder of the Yang-tsze-kiang, rises near the Hwang-ho and is regarded as the territorial boundary between Tibet and China. Additionally, the Tsanpo, a great river of eastern Tibet, supplies the main stream of the Brahmaputra.

## Climatic Conditions
The climate of Tibet is marked by extreme rigour, characterized by a combination of great cold and great drought. These conditions render the country essentially very poor, with much of the territory being little more than desert. 

Despite the general cold, the region experiences a specific summer climate produced by the combination of extreme drought and a nearly vertical, hot sun. This results in a summer environment that does not differ greatly from the plains of central Asia.

## Vegetation and Flora
Due to the harsh climate, vegetation across Tibet is most scanty. Trees are rarely found, appearing only in more sheltered spots where they have been artificially planted. 

The flora of the dry regions is notable for a high relative number of Chenopodiaceae, including *Salicornia* and other salt plants. Polygonaceae are also abundant, frequently appearing in leafless forms that give the vegetation a remarkable aspect. Furthermore, peculiar forms of Leguminosae prevail. Many of these plants, along with others found in the colder desert tracts of Afghanistan and Persia, or the drier and southern regions of Siberia, extend into Tibet.

## Human Adaptation and Settlement
The population of Tibet adapts its lifestyle based on the specific climate of the local terrain. In the higher regions, where conditions are most severe, the people are nomadic shepherds who are thinly scattered over a large area. Conversely, those living in the warmer and lower valleys follow agriculture and reside in houses.

## Fauna
The animal life of Tibet and northern Asia is dominated by species restricted to temperate and cold regions. While monkeys (Quadrumana) are nearly unrepresented in the broader region, one or two species occur in Tibet and northern China. Other animals frequenting these cold climates include wolves, foxes, bears, and weasels. The region is also home to various rodents, such as pikas (tailless hares) and marmots, as well as various forms of sheep and antelope.
