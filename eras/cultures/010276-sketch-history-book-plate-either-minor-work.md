# History of the Book-Plate

The book-plate, serving as an accessory to book binding or as a minor work of decorative and symbolical art, is used as a mark of possession (referred to as *ex-libris*). The history of these tokens begins in Germany, where the earliest known examples were produced. German book-plates appeared in great numbers long before the practice spread to other nations and are noted for their high artistic interest.

## Early German Development
The early history of the book-plate is closely tied to German draughtsmen. Between 1503 and 1516, Albrecht Durer engraved at least six plates, some of which were of significant size, and he provided designs for many others. Other notable plates are attributed to Hans Holbein and Lucas Cranach. Additionally, a group known as the "Little Masters"—including Jost Amman, the Behams, Matthias Zundt, Virgil Solis, Saldorfer, and Georg Hupschmann—contributed to the medium. 

The decorative styles established by these artists influenced German design for subsequent centuries, persisting despite the introduction of French and Italian fashions during the 18th and 17th centuries. While the "heavy, over-elaborated" German style did not affect neighboring countries, Germany remained the source from which the fashion of ornamental book-plates as marks of possession spread.

## Evolution of Style and Design
For a long period, the creation of book-plates was almost exclusively the domain of the heraldic stationer, relying on routine skill. However, more recent trends have seen the composition of personal book-tokens recognized as a minor branch of a higher art. This shift has introduced a new class of designs characterized by great variety. 

In modern designs, there is a observable trend where the purely heraldic elements become subsidiary, while symbolic or allegorical elements assert themselves more strongly. This represents a departure from the more definite styles of previous eras.

## Modern Production and Artists
Historically, book-plates were largely restricted to important collections or ancestral libraries. The popularization of the medium has been aided by the development of process work, which has made the reproduction of elaborate and beautiful designs both cheaper and easier. Consequently, the majority of modern plates are reproduced via process.

Despite this, some artists continue to use the graver. In England, G.W. Eve and C.W. Sherborn are among the best-known artists who maintain this skill, producing work that compares with the finest engravers of the past. In the United States, notable engravers include E.D. French of New York City, K.W.F. Hopson of New Haven, Connecticut, and J.W. Spenceley of Boston, Massachusetts.

## Collecting and Value
The practice of collecting book-plates has sparked debate. Some enthusiastic collectors, known as "ex-librists," have claimed that "ex-librisme" is a science. Conversely, some bibliophiles have criticized the removal of book-plates from old books as an act of vandalism.

Regardless of these debates, book-plates are often considered to be of high interest and may possess a value far exceeding the volume to which they are attached. They serve as personal relics of well-known individuals or as specimens of past decorative fashions.

Beyond the early German masters, various artists' works are considered worth collecting, including those by Simon Gribelin, D.N. Chodowiecki, Hubert Francois Bourguignon (known as Gravelot), and Charles Eisen. Other notable contributors include David Loggan, W. Faithorne, W. Marshall, Francesco Piranesi, and Sir Robert Strange. Further artists of note include Cipriani, Bartolozzi, Hogarth, William Henshaw, John Keyse Sherwin, Hewitt, and Bewick (and his imitators).
