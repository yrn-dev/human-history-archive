# Baluch Highlands and Arab Influence

The Baluch highlands are part of a sparsely populated region in Baluchistan, a tract larger than any Indian province except for Bengal, Madras, and Burma. The highlands are divided by a railway line; to the north lie the long ridges of the Suliman system, while to the south is a square block of territory roughly 300 miles by 300 miles, primarily the home of the Baluch and the Brahui. This southern section includes the Jalawan highlands of the Kalat plateau, where drainage channels cut deep gorges in the hills before passing east into the plains of Sind.

## Agricultural History and Desiccation
In the Baluch highlands, there is evidence of cultivation and Arab irrigation works that attest to a period when the region experienced a flourishing agricultural condition. However, large areas of these highlands have since undergone widespread desiccation. 

While the wanton destruction of forests and ordinary processes of erosion and denudation acting on recent deposits over harder beds may have contributed to these climatic changes, the scale of the desiccation suggests more rapidly destructive principles. These climatic changes, regardless of their origin, are considered the likely cause for the disappearance of the Arabs from the fields of agriculture and trade, rather than tribal disturbances.

## Geological Activity
The region has experienced alterations of level during recent geologic periods. Historic records indicate compressions resulting in a relative rise of ground over the crests of anticlinal folds. A specific instance of this ongoing compression occurred on December 20, 1892, when a severe earthquake caused the earth's crust to yield along an old line of fault west of the Kawaja Amran range. This event resulted in a shortening of approximately 2-1/2 feet on the railway line that crossed the fault. Additionally, the coast exhibits active volcanic agency in the form of mud volcanoes.

## Ethnography of the Baluch
The Baluch are not a homogeneous race, but rather a mixture of people of Persian, Dravidian, and Arab extraction. The Arab influence is strongly impressed upon the ethnography of Baluchistan, as Arabs occupied all of southern Baluchistan and Seistan from a very early date. All Rind tribes claim to be of Koraish extraction and Arab origin. Specifically, the Bugti and Marri tribes, located on the southern buttresses of the Suliman Mountains, are Rind Baluchis almost certainly of Arab extraction; they arrived in Sind either with or after the Arab conquerors and mixed with the original Hindu inhabitants.

The "Arab type" of Baluch is found at intervals throughout the country. Characteristically, the Baluch is described as being easier to control than the Pathan due to a lack of blind allegiance to priests or bigoted fanaticism. Physically, the Baluch frame is more spare, wiry, and shorter than that of the Pathan, though they possess a bold, manly bearing. A Baluch chief of the Bugti or Marri clans is noted for imposing presence and stately dignity, with Semitic features resembling those of the Bedouin.

In terms of temperament, the true Baluch of the Derajat is described as frank, open, temperate, enduring, and faithful to his word, viewing courage as the highest virtue. However, they are noted as poor husbandmen due to a lack of industry and a pride that views manual labor as degrading. They are expert riders, and the Baluch breed of horses is celebrated throughout northern India. By tradition and descent, the Baluch is a bandit and a first-rate fighting man, though he rarely enlists in the Indian army.

## Regional Geography and Trade
The highlands are accessed via the town and district of Dera Ghazi Khan in the Punjab, which commands the direct approaches to the Baluch highlands through Fort Monro and Sakki Sarwar. Dera Ghazi Khan was founded at the end of the 15th century by Ghazi Khan, a Baluch chieftain.

In the broader region, cattle-breeding is vigorously pursued in the highlands and Hissar. While the highlands receive cereals such as wheat and rice from the irrigated lowlands, the region produces various manufactured goods, including woollen cloth, felt, saddles, and weapons. Trade in the wider area is facilitated by camels, horses, and donkeys in the hilly tracts, and by the Transcaspian railway.
