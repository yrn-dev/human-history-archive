# BAHR

The term *bahr* is the Arabic word for "sea," and its diminutive form is *bahira*. Beyond its primary meaning, *bahr* is also used to signify a river, particularly those characterized by a large body of water, such as the Nile. In some instances, the term is used to designate the dry bed of a river.

## Hydrography of the Nile Basin

In Northeast Africa, the Bahr-el-Ghazal (meaning "Gazelle river") serves as the chief western affluent of the Nile, joining it at 9° 30' N., 30° 25' E. It is a deep stream formed by the confluence of numerous rivers, the most significant of which is the Jur. The basin of the Bahr-el-Ghazal is extensive, stretching south-west to the Congo watershed and north-west to Darfur.

The Bahr-el-Ghazal is fed by several tributaries:
*   **Bahr-el-Homr:** This is the main northern feeder, passing through the country of the Homr Arabs. It is the only important affluent with tributaries originating north of the main stream. In 1906, its lower reaches were completely blocked by *sudd* (obstructions), which prevented water from entering the Bahr-el-Ghazal until the Sudan government sent engineering parties to clear the waterway.
*   **Lol (or Bahr-el-Arab):** The most westerly tributary, the Lol rises in Dar Fertit as the Telgona or Boro. It receives the Bongo, Chel, Sopo, and Raga rivers from the south and south-west. 

The nomenclature of these rivers has historically been confusing because different tribes use different names for the same streams. For example, the Lol is also called the Kir, a name also applied to the lower course of the Bahr-el-Homr.

Both the Bahr-el-Ghazal and the Bahr-el-Jebel are prone to being choked by *sudd*. During the rule of the Mahdists, the Bahr-el-Ghazal became almost blocked by these accumulations. Between 1901 and subsequent years, British officers removed *sudd* from the Jur, the Bahr-el-Ghazal, and other rivers, enabling uninterrupted steamboat communication during the flood season between Wau and Khartum, a distance of approximately 930 meters. Between 1905 and 1907, British officers—including Capt. C. Percival, Lieut. D. Comyn, and R. C. Bayldon—explored the western and northern affluents of the Ghazal to clarify the region's hydrography.

## Regional Geography and Resources

In east central Africa, the Bahr-el-Ghazal forms the southern boundary of Darfur, the westernmost state of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. The Bahr-el-Homr marks the southern frontier of Darfur for approximately 200 meters. In Darfur, various *khors* (seasonal streams) flow south and south-east toward the Bahr-el-Ghazal; during heavy rainfall, the waters of some of these *khors* reach the Bahr-el-Homr.

The Bahr-el-Ghazal province also contains significant mineral resources. Copper mines located at 9° 48' N, 24° 5' E (across the Darfur frontier) feature ore veins of silicate and carbonate, with some specimens containing 14% metal.

## Other Geographical and Political References

The term *bahr* appears in other geographical contexts, such as the Dardanelles strait, known in Turkish as *Bahr-Sefed Boghazi*. This strait connects the Aegean with the Sea of Marmora. Its European extremity features the castle of Sedil Bahr, while the "Old Castle of Rumelia" is known as Kilid-Bahr.

In British India, Bahraich (or Bharaich) is a town and district in the Fyzabad division of the United Provinces, situated on the river Sarju. The town has flourished since the introduction of the railway and is home to a Mussulman monastery, the ruined palace of a nawab of Oudh, and an American Methodist mission. It is a site of pilgrimage for both Hindus and Mahommedans due to the tomb of Masaud, a champion of Islam who was killed in battle by confederate Rajputs in 1033. As of 1901, the population was 27,304.

## Historical Figures

The name Bahr[a]m is associated with several Persian monarchs:
*   **Bahr[a]m II (277–294):** Son of Bahr[a]m I; his reign is noted for theological inscriptions and an attack by the Roman emperor Carus, who conquered Ctesiphon in 283.
*   **Bahr[a]m III:** Son of Bahr[a]m II and former governor of Sejistan (Saganshah). He reigned for only four months in 294.
*   **Bahr[a]m IV (389–399):** Son and successor of Shapur III and former governor of Kirman (Kirmanshah). He was killed by malcontents.
*   **Bahr[a]m V (420–439):** Son of Yazdegerd I. He gained the crown with the help of al-Mondhir, the Arabic dynast of Hira. He is known for systematically persecuting Christians, which led to war with the Roman Empire, though he eventually signed a treaty of religious toleration between Zoroastrianism and Christianity. Persian tradition calls him Bahr[a]m Gor ("the wild ass") for his courage and strength, though some accounts suggest he was a weak monarch who favored priests and grandees. He is credited with building many villages, gardens, and fire-temples.

Additionally, in the history of Darfur, Ahmed Bahr (1682–1722) was a monarch who made Islam the state religion and encouraged immigration from Bagirmi and Bornu, extending his rule east of the Nile to the banks of the Atbara.
