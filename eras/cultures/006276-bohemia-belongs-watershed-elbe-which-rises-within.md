# Bohemia

Bohemia (German: *Bohmen*; Czech: *Cechy*; Latin: *Bohemia*) is a kingdom and crownland of Austria. It serves as the principal province of the Austrian empire and is situated in the geographical center of the European continent. The territory covers an area of 20,060 square miles, approximately two-thirds the size of Scotland. It is bounded to the northwest by Saxony, to the northeast by Prussian Silesia, to the southeast by Moravia and Lower Austria, to the south by Upper Austria, and to the southwest by Bavaria. Enclosed by high mountains, Bohemia acts as a borderland between the Slavonic and German worlds.

## Geography and Hydrography
The interior of Bohemia is largely an undulating plateau exceeding 1,000 feet in height, consisting of terraces that slope gradually from south to north. The country is divided into highland (south of a line passing through Komotau, Prague, and Hohenmauth) and lowland (north of that line). The lowest points are found in the north within the Elbe valley. Interior mountain ranges include the Brdywald (2,798 ft.), the Tepler Gebirge (2,657 ft.), the Karsbader Gebirge (3,057 ft.), and the Kaiserwald (3,238 ft.). The northern corner is occupied by the Mittelgebirge, a volcanic massif reaching 2,739 ft.

Bohemia belongs to the watershed of the Elbe, which rises within the territory. The Elbe receives the Adler on its left, and the Iser, Polzen, Biela, and Moldau on its right, as well as the Eger and its affluent, the Tepl. Despite the Elbe's presence, the Moldau (Czech: *Vltava*) is the principal river from every perspective. The Moldau is the main stem of the hydrographic structure, while the Elbe and other rivers are lateral branches; below the point of confluence at Melnik, the Elbe follows the general direction of the Moldau. 

The Moldau is the country's primary commercial artery, as it is navigable below Budweis, whereas the Upper-Elbe is not. The Moldau's basin (11,890 sq. m.) is twice as large as that of the Elbe, and the river is both wider and deeper. It has a length of 270 miles—47 miles longer than the Upper-Elbe—though it runs through a deep, narrow valley from above Budweis to approximately 15 miles south of Prague where roads and railways are absent. The Moldau's right-bank tributaries include the Sazawa and Luzniza, while its left-bank tributaries include the Wottawa and the Beraun. The Beraun, formed by the union of the Angel, Radbusa, Mies, and Uslawa, is the third most important river in the country. Lakes are few and mostly located at high altitudes.

## Industry and Commerce
Bohemia is one of Europe's greatest manufacturing centers and ranks first among Austrian provinces industrially. The region's abundance of iron, coal, and water power fueled enormous industrial growth during the latter half of the 19th century.

*   **Glass and Porcelain:** The glass industry, introduced from Venice in the 13th century, is located near the mountains to utilize fuel and silica. Crystal-glass is produced around Steinschonau and Haida, while porcelain is concentrated around Carlsbad.
*   **Textiles:** This industry is primarily located in the valley of the Lower Elbe and the northeast corner around Reichenberg. Specific centers include Reichenberg for cloth, Trautenau and Rumburg for linen, and Aussig and Asch for woollen yarns. Lace is a home-industry in the Erzgebirge region, centered at Weipert, while Strakonitz specializes in red fezes.
*   **Metallurgy and Machinery:** Metallurgic industries are concentrated near mines. Industrial and agricultural machinery are manufactured in Prague, Pilsen, and Reichenberg; Prague also hosts a major railway rolling-stock establishment.
*   **Other Industries:** Prague and Aussig produce chemicals, Budweis produces pencils, and Graslitz and Schonbach manufacture musical instruments. Other sectors include paper, leather, dyeing, calico-printing, and sugar refining. The region's breweries produce world-appreciated beer.

Commercial development is supported by a system of good roads and an extensive railway network.

## Notable Towns and Historical Sites
**Königinhof (*Dvur Kralove*):** Located in northeastern Bohemia on the left bank of the Elbe, this city was founded by King Wenceslaus II (1278-1305) and given to his wife, Elizabeth. It is a center for cotton-weaving, corn-milling, and brewing. Historically, it was a site of conflict during the Hussite wars and the scene of a 1866 battle where Prussians defeated the Austrian army. It was also the site where Wenceslas Hanka claimed to have found an ancient Czech manuscript in 1817, though scholars later proved the document was a forgery.

**Kolin (Neu-Kolin):** A royal city on the Elbe, 40 miles east of Prague, colonized by Germans in the 13th century. It is known for the early Gothic church of St Bartholomew and industries such as steam mills, brewing, sugar-refining, and the production of syrup, spirits, potash, tin ware, and starch. In 1757, the Battle of Kolin saw the Austrians under Daun defeat Frederick the Great's Prussians, leading to the evacuation of Bohemia.

**Königstein:** While currently a town in Saxony, Germany, this location was a fief of Bohemia in 1241. It was formally ceded by Bohemia to Saxony in 1459 via the treaty of Eger. The fortress there was used as a *point d'appui* against Bohemian inroads and is often called "the key to Bohemia."
