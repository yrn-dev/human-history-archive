# Azerbaijani Carpet Weaving: Art and Heritage

## Overview
Azerbaijani carpet weaving is not merely a craft but a profound cultural expression that encodes the history, spirituality, and geography of the South Caucasus. Recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, these textiles serve as visual archives of the region's diverse ethnic and religious influences.

Historically, Azerbaijani carpets are categorized into several primary schools: Guba, Shirvan, Baku, Ganja, Gazakh, Karabakh, and Tabriz. Each school is distinguished by specific knotting techniques, color palettes, and symbolic motifs. While the Guba and Shirvan schools are known for their intricate, smaller-scale geometric patterns, the Karabakh and Ganja styles often feature bolder, larger compositions.

A central point of historical discussion among scholars concerns the origins of these patterns. While some historians emphasize the indigenous roots of the "dragon" and "floral" motifs, others argue that the art form was heavily influenced by the migration of Turkic tribes from Central Asia and the subsequent aesthetic exchanges with the Safavid Empire. This synthesis of nomadic tradition and sedentary urban artistry created a unique visual language.

## Timeline of Development

| Era | Development |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Ancient/Medieval** | Emergence of early weaving traditions; influence of Caucasian Albanian and early Turkic cultures. |
| **16th - 18th Century** | Golden Age under the Safavid Empire; integration of courtly floral styles and royal workshops. |
| **19th Century** | Industrialization and increased export to Europe and Russia; standardization of regional schools. |

## Key Facts
*   **Symbolism:** The motifs are rarely purely decorative. The *buta* (almond-shaped leaf) is one of the most iconic symbols, representing fire, eternity, or a sprouting seed.
*   **Materials:** Traditional carpets are woven using high-quality wool (both for the warp and weft) and natural dyes derived from plants, minerals, and insects (such as madder root for reds and indigo for blues).
*   **The Knot:** The predominantly used technique is the symmetrical knot (Ghiordes knot), though variations exist depending on the regional school.
*   **Regional Diversity:** The Karabakh school is particularly famous for its "Chelebi" and "Malibeyli" patterns, which often reflect the lush landscapes of the highlands.

## Did You Know?
*   **UNESCO Status:** In 2010, Azerbaijani carpet weaving was officially inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
*   **The Carpet Museum:** Baku is home to the Azerbaijan National Carpet Museum, an architectural marvel shaped like a rolled-up carpet.
*   **Natural Pigments:** Traditional Azerbaijani weavers used pomegranate peels to achieve specific shades of yellow and brown, ensuring the colors remained vibrant for centuries.
