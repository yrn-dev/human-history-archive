# Dhamma Sangani and the Concept of Karma

The *Dhamma Sangani* is one of seven later works on *Abhidhamma*, which provide a more classified and elaborate exposition of the doctrine (Dhamma) as it is presented in the *Nikayas*. It serves as an ancient manual for Buddhist students, with the translator Mrs. Caroline Rhys Davids estimating its date to be the 4th century B.C. A translation of this work has been published under the title *Buddhist Psychology*, which provides a comprehensive account of the psychological conceptions that form the basis of Buddhist ethics.

## Karma in the Dhamma Sangani
Within the discussions found in the *Dhamma Sangani*, *Karma* (defined as action or doing) is identified as a mental disposition. This disposition in one life is what determines the position of the individual in the subsequent life. 

This specific interpretation exists alongside other conflicting propositions within Buddhist theory regarding the mode of action of *Karma*. For example, in the story of the good layman Citta, *Karma* is expressed as an aspiration on a deathbed; in other dialogues, it is a thought dwelt upon during life; and in the *Vimana Vatthus* and *Peta* stories, it is usually an isolated act. Despite these contradictions, these views share the common premise that an individual's current position is caused by their own moral cause.

## Buddhist Adaptation of Transmigration
The Buddhist theory of *Karma* differs significantly from popular beliefs and brahmin theology regarding the transition between lives. While brahmin theology posits that a subtle entity called the soul leaves the body through a hole at the top of the head to enter a new, ready-made body, the Buddhist adaptation denies that any soul, memory, or consciousness passes from one body to another.

Instead, it is the craving and grasping that persist at the moment of death that cause a new set of *Skandhas* to arise. These *Skandhas* constitute the new body, along with its capacities and mental tendencies. Although the mechanism of this process is not explained, the theory attempts to address the mysteries of inherited character, the connection between all life forms, and the influence of an immeasurable past on the individual.

## Escape from Rebirth
The Buddhist framework offers a potential escape from the miseries of transmigration. This is achieved by the Arahat, who has conquered the cravings responsible for producing a new body. For the Arahat, actions are no longer classified as *Karma*, but as *Kiriya*, which does not lead to rebirth.

## General Context of Indian Thought
The Buddhist doctrine of *Karma* emerged from an Indian mental landscape characterized by the idea of cyclic recurrence, where it was believed that everything eventually comes round again. In this system, the happiness or misery of a subsequent life was determined by the good or evil of the preceding life. This world of thought, which includes the plan for an emancipated life laid down by Gautama in the sixth century before Christ, contrasts with modern scientific views which hold that change is inexhaustible and that history and generations do not repeat themselves precisely.
