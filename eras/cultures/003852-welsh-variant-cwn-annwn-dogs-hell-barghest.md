# The Barghest and Related Spectre-Hounds

The Barghest (also referred to as the Barguest or Bargest) is a creature of folklore primarily associated with the north of England, particularly Yorkshire. It is described as a monstrous goblin-dog characterized by having claws and huge teeth.

## Nature and Characteristics
The Barghest is essentially a nocturnal spectre. Its appearance is traditionally regarded as a portent of death. In certain regions, such as the Norfolk coast, the creature is believed to be amphibious, emerging from the sea at night to travel through lonely lanes. In Wales, the Barghest's counterpart is known as Gwyllgi, or "the Dog of Darkness," which is described as a frightful apparition of a mastiff possessing blazing red eyes and baleful breath. A Welsh variant of this legend is the *Cwn Annwn*, translated as the "dogs of hell."

## Regional Variations and Kinship
The phenomenon of the spectre-hound is familiar across various folk-lores under different names:
*   **England:** In Lancashire, the creature is called the Striker or Trash. In Cambridgeshire and on the Norfolk coast, it is known as Shock or Shuck. Other specific entities sharing the characteristics of the Barghest of York include the Black Dog of Winchester, the Padfoot of Wakefield, and the Demon of Tedworth.
*   **Isle of Man:** The creature is styled the Mauthe Doog. This figure is mentioned by Sir Walter Scott in "The Lay of the Last Minstrel," where the spectre-hound is described as ghastly, wan, and speechless.
*   **Norman Folklore:** The Barghest is considered a kinsman to the *Rongeur d'Os*.

Belief in these spectre-hounds persists in the wild regions of the north country of England. For example, in Nidderdale, Yorkshire, the name is used by nurses to frighten children.

## Etymology
The derivation of the word "barghest" is a subject of dispute among scholars. One theory suggests it comes from *burh-ghest*, meaning "town-ghost," noting that "ghost" is pronounced as "guest" in the north of England. Other explanations propose a German origin, suggesting *Berg-geist* ("mountain demon") or *Bar-geist* ("bear-demon"), the latter referring to the creature's alleged ability to appear at times as a bear.
