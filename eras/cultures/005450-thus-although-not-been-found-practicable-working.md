# Practical Application and Theory in History and Science

The tension between theoretical frameworks and their practical application is a recurring theme across various disciplines, including constitutional law, scientific research, and national governance. In many instances, the ideal theory of a system is recognized, yet the actual working of that system deviates due to pragmatic constraints or external pressures.

## Constitutional and Political Theory

In the British constitution, there exists a theory of direct and exclusive dependence of colonial possessions on the crown. While this theory is recognized as far as possible, it has not been found practicable to carry it out fully in operation. Under this framework, it is understood that the principal sections of the empire possess equal rights under the crown and none is subordinate to another. Theoretically, the imperial parliament only intervenes in colonial affairs when the crown's constitutional advisers require parliamentary support. To align practice with theory, it has been suggested that a council representing all important parts of the empire should be constituted to advise the crown on imperial affairs. The movement toward bringing the supreme headship of the empire into accord with modern ideas has been facilitated by the assumption of full self-governing responsibilities by principal colonies.

In a different political context, the Korean Peninsula experienced a gap between the theory of independence and practical reality. Korea was not permitted to exercise independence in practice; instead, a Chinese resident stationed in Seoul became an "imperium in imperio." Consequently, Korea behaved as a Chinese dependency, obeying the resident in all matters. This state of national irresponsibility led to a lack of security for property and life and a failure to develop national resources. The royal family of Korea subsequently viewed their power as dependent on their ability to conciliate China, which provided military assistance to suppress those who appealed to force against oppression.

## Scientific Research and Education

The distinction between theory and experimental data is evident in the work of Bunsen. While Bunsen was conversant with the chemical thought of his time, he did not associate a body of chemical doctrine with his name and took little part in theoretical discussions. He believed that one properly proved fact was worth all invented theories, preferring to devote his energy to the collection of experimental data. Despite this, he was a highly successful teacher of chemistry, particularly due to his insistence on practical work. In 1840, he instituted a regular practical course at Marburg and personally directed his pupils in the laboratory rather than delegating work to assistants.

In the field of dairy science, Babcock and Russell developed an American theory regarding the ripening of cheese. In 1897, they discovered an inherent enzyme in milk called galactase, which can digest milk casein and produce decomposition products similar to those in ripened cheese. They theorized that this enzyme is a significant factor in ripening changes and concluded that bacteria are not essential to the breaking down of casein, as they excluded bacterial action using anaesthetic agents. Their theory also emphasizes the role of rennet extract; they found that pepsin in commercial rennet extracts hastens ripening. They differentiated the proteolytic action of pepsin from that of galactase, noting that pepsin cannot produce decomposition products lower than the peptones precipitated by tannin.

## Legal and Economic Practices

The evolution of bankruptcy and damages reflects a shift from drastic practices to evolved legal principles. In primitive communities, bankruptcy systems did not exist, and creditors used drastic means to recover debts. Under the Roman law of the Twelve Tables, creditors could potentially cut a debtor's body into pieces. In Greece, Rome, and other ancient nations, selling the debtor and their family into slavery was habitually exercised. This practice was also seen among the Israelites; the Old Testament records a woman telling Elisha that a creditor had come to take her two children as bondmen because her husband had died. Over time, these severe laws were replaced by imprisonment in public prisons. In feudal Europe, the state often kept subjects free for military service, though English courts later evolved the practice of imprisoning debtors to compel them to meet obligations.

In the assessment of damages, courts have evolved rules to manage the difficulty of applying theory to concrete cases. A distinction is made between general damages, which are implied by law as flowing from a breach of right and require no proof, and special damages, which are caused in fact by the wrong. Special damages cannot be recovered unless they are specifically claimed and proved, or if the best available details were communicated to the opposing party before the trial.

## Professional and Cultural Contributions

The application of knowledge to the public sphere is seen in the career of Charles Lucas (1713-1771). An Irish physician and politician, Lucas began as an apothecary and pamphleteer, publishing papers that led to legislation for the inspection of drugs. He later challenged the encroachments of aldermen on the electoral rights of Dublin citizens. After studying medicine in Paris and receiving an M.D. from Leiden in 1752, he published a work on medicinal waters. In 1761, as a member for the city of Dublin, he wrote to Lord Halifax regarding the unrepresentative character of the Irish constitution and the grievances caused by exorbitant pensions for government officials.

In the cultural sphere, Jacob Van Maerlandt, a 13th-century Flemish poet, adapted the didactical genre to the practical character of the Flemish nation. His goal was to provide laypeople with access to knowledge previously monopolized by clergymen, writing on ethics, politics, natural history, and both sacred and profane history. Maerlandt is credited with elevating the Flemish language to a literary rank capable of expressing the national genius.
