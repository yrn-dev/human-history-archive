# Agriculture and Village Life

The development of agriculture and the structure of village life vary significantly across different eras and geographic regions, ranging from the earliest Neolithic communities to the organized systems of the Anglo-Saxons, the feudal structures of Belgium, and the administrative autonomy of Japanese villages.

## Early Agricultural Origins
Approximately twelve to fifteen thousand years ago, Neolithic human communities began spreading across the warm, well-watered regions of the Old World. These early agricultural peoples developed cultivated fields, villages, and small walled cities, often guided by a tradition of priests and priestesses. This "Heliolithic culture" (meaning Sun and Stone) is believed to have originated in the western Asiatic and Mediterranean areas. From there, it spread eastward and across Pacific islands, potentially reaching America and mingling with Mongoloid immigrants from the North.

## Anglo-Saxon Agriculture and Land Measurement
The Anglo-Saxons possessed an advanced agricultural system before settling in Britain, as evidenced by ancient rock-carvings at Tegneby in Bohuslän depicting a plough drawn by two oxen. By the time of the Domesday Book, the heavy plough drawn by eight oxen appeared to be universal. In Kent, agricultural terminology was based on this system: the *sulung* (ploughland) was the primary unit, and its fourth part was the *geocled* or *geoc* (originally a yoke of oxen). In the Danelagh, a similar analogy existed with the *carucata*, where the eighth part was the *bouata* or "ox-land."

In later times, the hide became the regular unit in most kingdoms, usually containing 120 acres and divided into four *girda* (yardlands). However, the hide originally referred simply to land pertaining to a household. The acre was later standardized with a length called *furhlang* (furlong), reckoned as one-eighth of a mile, and a breadth called *aecerbraedu* (chain). Originally, an acre was thought to be the measure of a single day's ploughing.

Regarding technology, the Anglo-Saxons initially relied on the *cweorn* (quern) for grinding corn, a task performed chiefly by female slaves, though donkey-mills (*esolcweorn*) were occasionally used. The water-driven mill (*mylen*), derived from the Romans, became common; by the 11th century, every village of significant size seemed to have one. A reference to a windmill exists as early as 833.

## Village Governance and Social Structure
In Anglo-Saxon villages, corporate life may have centered around an ale-house. However, substantial local authority rested with the lord or his steward (*gerefa* or *villicus*), who was responsible for internal peace and external obligations. While some scholars suggest villages were originally settlements of free kindreds (citing place-names ending in *-ing*, *-ingham*, or *-ington*), it is more probable these names derive from the *twelfhynde* class to whom land was granted.

In ante-feudal Belgium, a geographic divide existed: isolated farms prevailed north of a line running through Boulogne, Saint-Omer, Douai, Mons, and Maestricht, while villages were the system to the south. By the 10th century, landlords unified these under one economic organization. Domains were split into a central part—the landlord's manor worked by unfree "serfs" or laborers—and surrounding small lots given to free farmers. Under ecclesiastical landlords (bishops or abbots), conditions were often favorable; these serfs were organized into *familiae*, where a community mayor administered justice in the abbot's name.

In Japan, the Bakufu and feudatories granted significant autonomy to lower classes. Farmers elected a village chief (*nanushi* or *shoya*) for a term of one year or for life, possessing powers similar to a governor. Other officials included representatives of farmers (*hyakushodai*) and heads of guilds (*kumi-gashira*). The *gonin-gumi* (guilds of five householders) provided mutual aid and ensured taxes were paid and laws followed.

## Regional Agricultural Profiles
### Dahomey
In Dahomey proper, the soil is a fertile deep red clay. The primary product is palm-oil, with the Toffo district noted for its oil-palm orchards. While palm-wine is produced, its manufacture is discouraged because it destroys the tree. Other vegetable products include maize, cassava, yams, plantains, coco-nuts, and various fruits and spices. The economy relies heavily on the export of palm-oil and palm-kernels to Germany and Great Britain. In the interior grassy uplands, inhabitants grow cotton for their own use and raise horses and cattle.

### Burma
Agriculture is the most important industry in Burma, with nearly two-thirds of the population engaged in it. In rural villages, men, women, and children all participate in agricultural work. The typical diet consists of boiled rice with salted or dried fish, sesame-oil, chillies, onions, and various boiled vegetables.
