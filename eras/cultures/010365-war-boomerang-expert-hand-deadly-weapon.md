# Boomerang

The boomerang is a missile weapon utilized by the Australian aborigines and other peoples. The term is derived from a native name used by a single tribe in New South Wales, referred to by Captain King in 1827 as "the Port Jackson term." Contrary to some beliefs, the word is not onomatopoeic, as the weapon whistles in the air rather than making a "boom" sound. Additionally, it has been erroneously linked to the *womera*, or spear-thrower.

## Types and Utility
There are two primary types of boomerangs: the non-return (or war) boomerang and the return boomerang. Both varieties are found across most parts of Australia.

The war boomerang is described as a deadly weapon when utilized by an expert. Similarly, the lighter hunting boomerang is considered effective. In contrast, the return boomerang is primarily used for killing birds or as a plaything; it is noted that this type can be as dangerous to the person throwing it as it is to the intended target.

## Physical Characteristics
Both types of boomerangs share a general sickle-shaped form and are typically constructed from wood. In India, however, they may be made of steel or ivory. The proportions of the weapon are generally modeled so that the breadth is 12 times the thickness, and the length is 12 times the breadth. Lengths vary from 6 inches to 4 feet.

The return boomerang typically weighs approximately 8 oz. and measures between 2 and 3 feet in length. While it may have two straight arms meeting at an angle between 70 and 120 degrees, in Australia it is always curved at an angle of 90 degrees or more. Its cross-section is asymmetrical, featuring a convex upper side and a flat or nearly flat lower side. The arms possess a "skew," meaning they are twisted 2 or 3 degrees from the center plane, and the ends are raised above the plane of the weapon. This specific design requires the weapon to be thrown with the right hand. The non-return boomerang is similar in construction but features a skew in the opposite direction.

## Flight Mechanics
The flight of the boomerang is primarily dependent on its skew. To operate a return boomerang, the thrower holds it vertically with the concave side facing forward and throws it in a plane parallel to the ground, imparting as much rotation as possible. The weapon typically travels straight for 30 yards or more with nearly vertical rotation before inclining to the left, lying over on its flat side, and rising. After describing a circle with a diameter of 50 yards or more, it returns to the thrower.

Australian natives are capable of throwing the weapon so that it travels 100 yards or more before the leftward curve begins, reaching heights of up to 150 feet and circling five times before returning. It is possible for the weapon to strike the ground without affecting its return. While the non-return type is normally thrown like the return type to travel an immense distance, it can be made to return in a nearly straight line if thrown at a 45-degree angle. One recorded English throw reached 180 yards, significantly further than the same thrower's 70-yard distance with a cricket ball.

## Global Distribution
While most closely associated with Australia, similar instruments are found elsewhere:
* **North-East Africa:** A weapon closely resembling the boomerang survives here, and allied forms made of metal, known as throwing knives, have spread from this region.
* **Ancient Egypt:** General Pitt-Rivers indicated that the return form was used in ancient Egypt.
* **South India:** A boomerang-shaped instrument capable of returning is found among the Dravidians.
* **Arizona:** The Hopis (Moquis) utilize a non-return form of the weapon.

Despite these examples, it remains uncertain if the instruments found in India and Egypt bear a real resemblance to the Australian return boomerang.
