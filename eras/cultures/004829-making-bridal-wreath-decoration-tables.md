# The Making of the Bridal Wreath and Decoration of Tables

The archive's records provide various accounts of the use of wreaths and the decoration of tables across different historical contexts, ranging from ancient civic celebrations and military leadership to funerary customs and domestic traditions.

## Wreaths in Victory and Honor
Wreaths have historically served as symbols of victory, divine favor, and honor. In ancient Greece, wreaths were cast upon Dion as he entered the city through the Temenitid gate, treating him as an Olympic victor. Similarly, the general Timoleon utilized parsley to create wreaths for himself and his officers. While his soldiers initially feared parsley because it was habitually used for wreaths deposited on tombstones, Timoleon reframed the plant as a "Corinthian symbol of victory" and the sacred herb used to decorate victors at the Isthmian festival.

In the Persian Empire, the wreath appeared in the visions of Xerxes. He dreamed he was crowned with a wreath of an olive-branch, and the shoots of the olive-tree covered the entire Earth, a vision interpreted by the Magians as a sign that all men would be subject to him.

## Decorative Arts and Wreaths
Wreaths also feature prominently in various forms of decorative art. The "grotesque" style, found in ancient Roman and Greek dwellings (referred to as *grotte*), consists of fantastic shapes of animals and human beings joined together by garlands, wreaths of flowers, or arabesques. 

In the history of English book-plates from the Elizabethan to the Victorian periods, wreaths were occasionally used as simple decorations, often appearing in symmetrical arrangements alongside palms.

## Rituals and Traditions of the Bride
The term "bride" is a common Teutonic word (appearing as *bruths* in Goth and *bryd* in Old English) referring to a woman on her wedding day and during her first year of wifehood. While the provided texts do not detail the specific "making" of a bridal wreath, they describe several bridal customs related to symbols of plenty.

In the Roman form of marriage known as *confarreatio*, the bride held three wheat-ears as a symbol of plenty. In the middle ages, these wheat-ears were either carried or worn by the bride. This evolved into a custom where young girls threw grains of wheat over the bride outside the church porch. Over time, these grains became thin dry biscuits or oatmeal cakes (the latter still used in Scotland) that were broken over the bride's head. During the reign of Elizabeth, these became small rectangular cakes made of sugar, milk, eggs, spices, and currants, which guests threw at the bride as she crossed the threshold. By the time of Charles II, these evolved into a single large cake decorated with ornaments and almond paste. In some rural parishes, such as north Notts, wheat is still thrown over the couple with the cry "Bread for life and pudding for ever."

## Decoration of Tables and Festive Spaces
The decoration of tables and public spaces often signaled a state of jubilee or religious remembrance. During Dion's entry into the city, the main street of Achradina was decorated for a festival with bowls of wine and tables ready prepared, alongside victims under sacrifice to the gods.

In contrast, the Neolithic people, prior to the coming of bronze, had no evidence of tables and likely squatted on clay floors.

In more recent historical contexts, festive decorations were used in religious settings. For All Souls’ Day, Landsturm men decorated a church by hanging large green wreaths from all the pillars and transforming the chancel into a bank of foliage to remember the fallen.

## Funerary Wreaths
Wreaths are also used to express sorrow and loss. In French graveyards, crosses are often decorated with wreaths made of coloured beads. These decorations are accompanied by simple words such as *Souvenir*, *Regrets*, and *Nos Parents*.
