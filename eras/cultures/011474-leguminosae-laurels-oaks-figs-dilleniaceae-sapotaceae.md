# Leguminosae, Laurels, Oaks, and Figs

## Botanical Characteristics and Distribution
The Leguminosae, laurels, oaks, and figs, along with Sapotaceae, Dilleniaceae, and nutmegs, constitute the chief trees found in the dense forests of the western portion of the archipelago and the perennially humid regions of the Malayan peninsula. These areas are often difficult to traverse due to the presence of thorny cane, a palm of the genus *Calamus*. This specific forest flora also extends along the mountains of eastern India reaching the Himalaya, where it ascends to elevations of 3,000 to 4,000 feet in the west and 6,000 to 7,000 feet in the east.

Within the Leguminosae, certain species are better equipped to resist excessive drought and high temperatures during the northern Indian hot months (April to June) than those requiring equatorial heat. These include *Acacia*, *Bauhinia*, *Butea*, *Dalbergia*, *Bombax*, *Skorea*, *Nauclea*, *Lagerstroemia*, and *Bignonia*.

## The Legume Fruit
The legume, or pod, is a many-seeded, unilocular, dry monocarpellary fruit formed from one carpel. It typically dehisces by both the dorsal and ventral sutures, a characteristic seen in the pea and bean. However, some Leguminosae produce fruits that do not dehisce, such as the tamarind, *Cathartocarpus Fistula*, and *Arachis* (the earth-nut), the latter of which produces its fruit underground. 

Variations in legume structure include:
* **Schizocarpic forms:** Some are schizocarpic, such as *Cathartocarpus Fistula* (which has a partitioned legume) or those forming external constrictions known as lomentaceous legumes or lomentums (found in *Entada*, *Ornithopus*, *Coronilla*, and some Acacias).
* **Morphological shapes:** In *Medicago*, the legume is twisted like a snail, while in *Caesalpinia coriaria* (Divi-divi), it is vermiform or curved like a worm.
* **Seed count:** While typically many-seeded, some are reduced to two seeds (*Dalbergia* and *Pterocarpus*) or a single seed (*Geoffroya superba* and *Erythrina monosperma*).

## Paleobotanical History
The flora of the Eocene period in Northern Europe included laurels and oaks, as well as willows and chestnuts (*Castanea*), indicating a more temperate climate than the Middle Eocene. During the Middle Eocene, in Hampshire, the Isle of Wight, and adjacent continental areas, figs and palms flourished alongside cinnamon, magnolia, sequoia, cypress, ferns, and the cactus.

## Material and Symbolic Uses
### Woodwork and Furniture
Oak is noted for its durability and use in construction and furniture. In England, English oak worked smooth and usually painted is considered the best wood for half-timber work, with examples found in Bristol, Holborn Bars in London, and Moreton Old Hall near Congleton, Cheshire. 

Oak has also been used extensively in historical furniture, including:
* **English pieces:** 17th-century arm-chairs, gate-legged tables, sideboards, court cupboards, and chests (one dated 1637).
* **French pieces:** A 13th-century coffer featuring wrought iron bands.

### Symbolic and Ceremonial Use
Laurels and oaks have been utilized in crowns, wreaths, and honorary badges:
* **Ancient Rome:** Victors in the Capitol were crowned with garlands of oak leaves. The laurel crown is identified as the Delphic crown rather than the Capitoline.
* **European Orders:** The badge of the military Order of Savoy (founded 1815) features a white cross surrounded by an oak and green laurel wreath. Similarly, the badge of the Order of Leopold (Belgium, founded 1832) rests on a wreath of oak and green laurel.
