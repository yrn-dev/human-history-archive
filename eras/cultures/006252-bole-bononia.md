# BOLE AND BONONIA

## Bole: Mineral and Material
Bole is a clay-like substance characterized by red, yellow, or brown colors. Chemically, it consists essentially of hydrous aluminium silicate combined with varying amounts of iron. Unlike ordinary clay, most bole is not plastic; instead, it behaves similarly to fuller's-earth, dropping to pieces when placed in water.

Historically, bole was highly regarded for medicinal purposes. The most famous variety was the Lemnian Earth (*gae Laemnia*) from the Isle of Lemnos in the Greek Archipelago. This earth was extracted only once a year through a ceremonial process. After being mixed with the blood of goats, it was formed into small balls or cakes and stamped by priests, leading to its designation as *Terra sigillata*, or "sealed earth."

Geologically, large quantities of bole are found in the west of Scotland and the north of Ireland as red partings between successive lava flows of the Tertiary volcanic series. In these regions, it appears to have formed from the decomposition of kindred rocks and basalt via meteoric agencies during periods of volcanic repose. In Antrim, bole is associated with bauxite, lithomarge, and pisolitic iron-ore. Similar substances are found interbedded with some doleritic lavas of Etna and between the great sheets of the Deccan traps in India.

## Etymology and Linguistic Uses of "Bole"
The term "bole" appears in several distinct linguistic contexts:

*   **Botany:** In the sense of a tree trunk or stem, the word derives from the Old Norwegian *bolr* and Old German *Bohle* (meaning plank). It is likely connected to words for round objects, such as "bowl," "ball," and "boll."
*   **Zoology:** In Middle English, the words *bole* or *bolle* were used to refer to the bull (the uncastrated male of the domestic ox, *Bos taurus*), as well as the males of other large animals such as the whale or elephant.
*   **Fortifications:** The word "bulwark" (a barricade of earth and beams) is likely derived from *bol* or *bole* (tree-trunk) and *werk* (work).
*   **Colloquialisms:** The use of "bull" to describe a verbal blunder involving a contradiction in terms may be traced to a Middle English word *bul* (meaning deceit, trickery, or falsehood), which the *New English Dictionary* compares to the Old French *bole*, *boule*, or *boul*.

## Bononia
Bononia is the ancient name for the modern city of Bologna, Italy. It served as the chief town of ancient Aemilia and was a critical meeting-point for important roads, a role the modern city maintains as a railway centre.

### Early History and Origins
Classical writers attributed the city's origin to the Etruscans, claiming it was founded as Felsina by Ocnus or Aucnus from Perusia. However, recent excavations have revealed cemeteries and approximately 600 ancient Italic (possibly Ligurian) huts dating from 800 to 600 B.C. These preceded the Etruscan civilization, which existed there from roughly 600 to 400 B.C. Evidence of this Etruscan period includes an extensive necropolis discovered in the public garden, on the Arnoaldi Veli property, and at the site of the modern cemetery.

### Roman Period
By 196 B.C., the town was in the possession of the Boii. It became a Roman colony in 189 B.C. Its strategic importance grew significantly due to its position within the north Italian road system. In 187 B.C., it was connected to Placentia and Ariminum via the Via Aemilia, and a road was constructed that same year to Arretium. A further road to Aquilelia was likely built around 175 B.C.

Bononia acquired Roman citizenship in 90 B.C. In 43 B.C., Mark Antony used the city as a base for operations against Decius Brutus and settled colonists there. Later, Augustus added more colonists and built a new aqueduct from the Letta (a tributary of the Rhenus), which was restored in 1881. In A.D. 53, following a fire, Emperor Claudius provided a subvention of 10 million sesterces (L1,087,500).

### Later History and Legacy
Bononia was one of ancient Italy's most important cities, demonstrating enough strength to resist Alaric in 410 and survive the general ruin of the era. It later became part of the Greek exarchate of Ravenna. While no Roman remains exist above ground today, various discoveries have been made within the city walls.

In the modern era, the city of Bologna is noted for its liqueurs, *tortellini* (a type of macaroni), and *mortadella* or *salami* (Bologna sausage).
