# Japanese Mythology

Japanese mythology serves as a record of the nation's genesis and provides indications of the creeds of its people. For the interpreter, the mythology presents a specific difficulty regarding nomenclature, particularly the term *Kami*. While foreign writers frequently translate *Kami* as "god" or "deity" to describe an "Age of Gods" (*Kami no yo*), the term does not necessarily carry a divine import. Historically, *Kami* could refer to the head of a household among the hundreds of families in Japanese society. For example, fifty years prior to the writing of the archive's source, the Government was referred to as *O Kami* (the Honourable Head), and feudatories often held the title of *Kami* of a specific locality.

## Cosmogony and the Creation of the Land
The mythological origins of Japan involve the procreation of the islands. According to tradition, the *Kami* Izanagi and Izanami are credited with the begetting of islands. Japanese euhemerists argue that these descriptions are allegories for warlike aggression rather than literal procreation. In this interpretation, Izanagi and Izanami commanded cooperating forces that, after an initial failure to synchronize movements, eventually overran the island of Awaji and brought Shikoku under their sway. The role of Izanami as a leader of armies aligns with the broader feature of early Japan where females commonly led military forces.

The first offspring of Izanagi and Izanami was a leech, who was sent adrift in a reed boat. This element of the myth recalls the biblical story of Moses and the Accadian legend of Sargon's ark of rushes, though it has no known counterpart in Chinese mythology.

## The Sun Goddess and the Celestial Realm
The supreme being in Japanese mythology is the goddess of the Sun. This worship of a sun goddess has been noted as a possible point of communication between Japan and western Asia, mirroring the worship of the goddess Isis by Semitic tribes. 

The "river of heaven," or the Milky Way, is a prominent feature in Japanese mythology, similar to its role in Chinese myth. In the Japanese legend, the Milky Way serves as a council-place for the *Kami* following an incident where the *Kami* Susanoo committed acts of violence against the Sun goddess and her spinning maidens.

## The Role of Susanoo and the Subjugation of Japan
Susanoo is described as an insubordinate *Kami* who was expelled from heaven due to his destructive violence. Following his descent, the *Kami* of the "plain of high heaven" ceased their involvement in ruling the "ever fruitful land of reed-covered moors, and luxuriant rice-fields" (the name for Japan), leaving the land in Susanoo's hands. 

Susanoo is associated with several legends, including the rescue of a maiden from an eight-headed serpent. To intoxicate the serpent, Susanoo used sake (rice-wine) and a chop-stick he found floating down a river in Izumo. While the sake and chop-stick are viewed as products of Chinese civilization, the rescue of the maiden is considered a replica of the legend of Perseus and Andromeda.

Six generations after Susanoo, the Great-Name Possessor held supreme power over the islands from his seat in the province of Izumo. Eventually, the Sun goddess decided a sovereign should be sent to govern the land. She chose the son of the eldest of five *Kami* who had been born from her necklace during a procreation competition with Susanoo.

## Other Mythological Figures and Realms
Following certain events, a large-scale begetting of *Kami* occurred, though only a few maintained intelligible roles, such as the protector of house-entrances, the guardian of the kitchen, and the *Kami* of agriculture. Other figures include Kagase, the last of the rebellious *Kami* during the subjugation of Izumo. In terms of astronomy, the only stars specifically mentioned in the Records and Chronicles are the Pleiades, Venus, and the Weaver.

The mythology also references *Tokoyo-no-Kuni* (the Eternal Land). This realm is described in various contexts:
* As the home of "long-singing birds" used to entice the Sun goddess from her cave.
* As the final retreat of Sukuna-hikona.
* As a place reached by one of Jimmu's elder brothers.
* As the "mysterious realm of gods and genii" mentioned in the death-song of Tajima Mori.

Evidence suggests *Tokoyo-no-Kuni* may have been an epithet for China, specifically Shantung, which required a great feat of navigation to reach from Japan.

## External Influences and Material Culture
Analysis of mythological artifacts suggests continental influence. The "curved jewels" mentioned in myths were often made of jade or jadelike stone, minerals not found in Japan, indicating they originated in Asia. Additionally, the term *wani* (crocodile) is suggested by the sinologue Aston to be a corruption of the Korean word *wang-in* (king).
