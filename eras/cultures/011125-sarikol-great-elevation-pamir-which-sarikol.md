# Sarikol and the Great Elevation of the Pamir

The Sarikol range is a meridional mountain system that serves as a primary geographical and political divider in Central Asia. It is characterized as the eastern face of the great elevation of the Pamir. To the west of Kashgar, the Sarikol range limits a central depression—once a vast inland sea—that extends from the desert of Gobi through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. The level of this depression between the Sarikol range and the mountains enclosing the sources of the Hwang-ho likely never exceeds 2,000 feet above sea level.

## Geographical Structure
The Sarikol range consists of two parallel chains. The western chain serves as the water-divide of the Pamirs, while the eastern chain, also known as the Kandar or Kashgar range, is split at intervals by lateral gorges. These gorges facilitate the passage of the main drainage from the eastern slopes of the Pamir. 

The broader Pamir plateau is described as a great, broad, rounded ridge extending north and south. It is crossed by thick mountain chains with elevated valleys that slope gently toward the east but are narrow and confined with a rapid fall toward the west. The watershed of this region extends from the Tághdúngbásh Pámir, along the range dividing it from the Little Pámír, and through the Neza Tásh mountains to the Kizil Art Pass.

## Political Boundaries and Demarcation
The Sarikol range defines the political boundary between Russia and China on the western edge of the Kashgar plains. In the context of Afghan borders, the northern limits of Afghanistan were set by Boundary Commissions in 1884-1886 and 1895, extending from the Hari Rud in the west to the Sarikol mountains in the east.

The range, along with the Muztagh range, acts as a barrier that shuts off China from India and Russia. The boundary of the province of Badakshan, which includes Wakhan, also merges into the Pamir boundary after the crest of the Hindu Kush effects a junction with the Sarikol and Muztagh ranges. In the region of the Great Pamir, the Oxus river serves as the northern boundary of Afghanistan, separating it from Russia until the river reaches its source in Victoria Lake. Beyond this point, an 1895 Anglo-Russian Commission demarcated a line extending to the glaciers and snowfields overlooking the Chinese border. The actual dividing line between Chinese Turkestan and the Russian Pamirs, where the rugged Sarikol range intervenes, has remained indefinite.

## Exploration and Research
The glacial regions of the Sarikol mountains were the subject of extensive investigations by the Swedish professor Sven Hedin between 1894 and 1896. Other notable figures in the region include George N. (Lord) Curzon, who was in the Pamirs during the same period, and Captain H. Deasy, who continued the Pamir triangulation into the plains of Kashgar. This triangulation had previously been carried across the Hindu Kush by Colonels R.A. Wahab and Sir T.H. Holdich during the 1895 Pamir Boundary Commission.

Historical accounts also note the presence of Buddhist caves and excavations in the Sarikol and Balkhab valleys, verifying narratives provided by the Chinese pilgrim Hsuan-Tsang, who visited the region in A.D. 630.

## Regional Terminology and Topography
The Sarikol range was known as Taurus to the ancients and as Bolor during medieval times. Within the Pamir region, geographers recognize several distinct areas:
* Pamir Kalan (Great Pamir)
* Pamir Khurd (Little Pamir)
* Pamir Alichur
* Pamir Khargosi ("of the hare")
* Pamir Sares
* Pamir Rang-kul

Travels through the region, such as those recorded by Lieutenant-Colonel Gordon in 1873, detail the route from Kashgar to the Sirikol River. This journey involves passing through the Kaskasú Pass and the Shindi ravine to reach Táshkurgán (anciently called Várshídi). From Táshkurgán, the Sirikol Valley extends toward the Kunjút mountain range. Further travel leads through the Shindán defile to the Áktásh Valley (called Áksú by the Kirghiz), which flows through the Little Pamir to the Barkat Yássín (or Gházkul) Lake.
