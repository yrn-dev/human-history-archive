# 15th Prefix

The archive's sources do not contain information regarding a "15th prefix" that is designated as "Ku-" or one that loses its initial "K" or softens into "Hu." 

The available records regarding prefixes are primarily focused on the Bantu group of languages. These languages are characterized by the governing of unchangeable roots by prefixes. There are approximately sixteen prefixes common to the whole group, which are used as pronominal particles to indicate classes or genders of substantives. These prefixes are used in either a singular or plural sense, and with the exception of the "abstract" prefix *Bu* (No. 14), singular prefixes cannot be used as plurals and vice versa.

## Documented Bantu Prefixes

The archive provides specific details on several of these prefixes and their variations across different dialects:

*   **The 2nd Prefix:** In the most archaic Bantu speech, such as the languages of Mt. Elgon, this prefix appears as *Baba-* in its definite form. In Zulu-Kaffir, it is sometimes *Ngaba*. While the concord is *-ba-* in less corrupt tongues, this plural prefix can degenerate into *Va-*, *Wa-*, *Ma-*, and *A-*.
*   **The 4th Prefix:** The prefix *Mi-* has a concord that appears as *gi-*, *-i-*, *-ji-*, and occasionally *-mi-*.
*   **The 5th Prefix:** The most common modern form is *Li-*, though *Di-* is considered the older and more correct version. This prefix is often dropped (while the concord remains) or transforms into *Ri-*, *I-*, *Ji-*, and *Ni-*.
*   **The 7th Prefix:** The prefix *Ki-* follows a parallel course in various non-related dialects, moving through *Ci-* into *Si-* (or *Shi*).
*   **The 8th Prefix:** This prefix serves as the plural of the 7th. Its oldest form is *Ibi-* or *Mbibi-*. It is highly variable across Bantu speech, appearing as *Vi-*, *Pi-*, *Fi-*, *Fy-*, *P[vs]i-*, *[vS]i-*, *I-*, *By-*, *Bzi-*, *Psi-*, *Zwi-*, *Zi-*, and *Ri-*.
*   **The 10th Prefix:** Originally *Ti-*, *Tin-*, *Zi-*, or *Zin-*, this prefix becomes *Jin-*, *Rin-*, *Din-*, *Lin-*, [theta]*in-*, or [theta]*on-*. The *n* in this prefix is actually the singular prefix No. 9. In the Swahili of Zanzibar, this prefix is absent as a plural prefix for nouns but reappears in the concord (for example, in the phrase *Nombe hizi zangu*).
*   **The 11th Prefix:** The prefix *-Lu-* is believed to descend from an older form, *Ndu-*. While *Lu-* is the commonest type, it sometimes becomes *U-* through the loss of the *L*, and is pronounced *Du-* or *Ru-* in more archaic dialects.

## General Linguistic Features

In Bantu languages, the pronominal particle or prefix of a noun is attached to the roots of verbs, pronouns, prepositions, and adjectives connected to the governing noun. This system is referred to as "concord." These particles must always precede the nominal, pronominal, adjectival, and verbal roots, although they may follow the roots of some prepositions or auxiliary prefix-participles used in verb conjugation.

The languages do not recognize sexual gender in their concord or pronouns. Instead, sexual gender may be indicated by a male prefix (often identical to a word for "father") or a feminine prefix such as *na* or *nya* (connected to the root for "mother"), or suffixes like *ka* or *kazi* (indicating "wife" or "female"). The 1st and 2nd prefixes are usually restricted to humanity and invariably indicate living beings.

## Examples of Root Application

The root *-ntu* (originally meaning "person" or "object") demonstrates how different prefixes alter meaning:
*   *muntu*: a man
*   *bantu*: men
*   *kintu*: a thing
*   *bintu*: things
*   *kantu*: a little thing
