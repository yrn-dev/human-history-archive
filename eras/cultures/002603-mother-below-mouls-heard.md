# The Mother Below the Mouls

The phrase "the mother below the mouls heard that" appears in the context of European folk-songs, specifically those originating from Provencal and Danish traditions. It refers to a recurring thematic element in folklore regarding the state of the dead and the spiritual connection between deceased parents and their living children.

## Folk-Song Themes of the Dead
In Provencal and Danish folk-songs, there are narratives where the cries of children who are being ill-treated by a cruel step-mother are capable of reaching the departed mother. The specific lyric, "'Twas cold at night and the bairnies grat, The mother below the mouls heard that," illustrates the belief that a mother, though buried (below the mouls), remains aware of and is awakened by the distress of her children.

This concept is part of a broader collection of widespread themes found in folk-songs concerning the state of the dead. Similar motifs appear in other cultures:
* **Romaic Traditions:** In M. Fauriel's Romaic collection, the song *The Night Journey* describes a dead brother who is awakened from the "sleep of death" by the longing of love to carry his living sister from Bagdad to Constantinople in a single night.
* **Scottish Traditions:** A similar story is told of Proud Lady Margaret. Additionally, Scottish folklore mentions a "channering worm" that chides souls arriving at the gate of Paradise, where a birk grows.
* **German and Danish Traditions:** The German song converted by Buerger into *Lenore* and the Danish *Aage und Else* share these themes.
* **Brittany Traditions:** In the *Barzaz Breiz*, a dead foster-brother carries his sister to the apple close of the Celtic paradise. In Brittany, the land of death is envisioned as an island of Avalon characterized by flowering apple trees, an eternal sunset, and a fountain of forgetfulness.

## Pre-Christian Conceptions of the Afterlife
The archive notes that certain ideas regarding the place of the dead are pre-Christian. For example, the Romaic conception of the "garden of Charon" describes a place from which neither maidens nor warriors can escape, regardless of the season—whether in spring, summer, or during the autumn grape gleanings.
