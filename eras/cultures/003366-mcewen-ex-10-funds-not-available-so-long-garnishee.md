# Availability of Funds

The concept of available funds varies across legal, financial, and historical contexts, ranging from the specific obligations of banking institutions to the fiscal capacities of ancient states and imperial courts.

## Banking and Legal Obligations
In the relationship between a banker and a customer, the banker acts as a debtor and creditor. While it is sometimes suggested that a cheque is the only method for withdrawing money, this view is inconsistent with cases where a bank balance has been attached by a garnishee order as an unconditional debt, such as in *Rogers v. Whiteley* (1892). 

A banker is obligated to honor a customer's cheques provided there is a sufficient and available balance. If a banker dishonors a cheque despite having such funds, they are liable for substantial damages to the customer without requiring proof of actual injury (*Rolin v. Steward*). In instances where multiple cheques are presented simultaneously and the available balance cannot cover all of them, the banker is not required to discriminate between cheques and should pay as many as the funds allow.

Certain conditions may affect the availability of funds for drawing:
* **Processing Time:** Money paid into an account so shortly before a cheque's presentation that it cannot pass through the bank's books is not treated as available.
* **Branch Restrictions:** A person with an account at one branch cannot draw cheques on another branch if they have no account there or are overdrawn.
* **Cheque Form:** Banks may be justified in refusing cheques not drawn in the ordinary recognized form, such as those drawn to "petty cash or order" or "wages or order."

## Bills of Exchange and Funds
A bill of exchange serves as a substitute for money and an unconditional order to pay. If an instrument's payment is dependent on a contingency or a particular fund—meaning its payment relies on the continued existence of that fund—it is considered invalid as a bill, although it may still serve as an equitable assignment or agreement. In Scotland, however, a bill may operate as an assignment of funds held by the drawee.

## Historical Fiscal Availability
The availability of funds has historically dictated the ability of states and individuals to perform essential rites or strategic military expansions.

### Imperial Japan
The Imperial exchequer of Japan experienced periods of extreme impoverishment. In 1500, following the death of Emperor Go-Tsuchimikado, funds for funeral rites were unavailable, leaving the corpse in a darkened palace room for forty days until Sasaki Takayori provided the money. Similarly, no funds were available for the coronation of the successor, Go-Kashiwabara. A futile attempt to levy contributions from the daimyo failed, and the ceremony was delayed for twenty years until 1520, when the Buddhist priest Koken provided ten thousand gold ryo from the gifts of his disciples.

In other instances, the Japanese government ensured the availability of funds for religious purposes through tax exemptions. Yoritomo exonerated the Soga estate from tax duties to ensure funds were available for religious rites for the spirits of Juro and Goro.

### Ancient Athens
Athens utilized specific funds for strategic development and civic maintenance. The silver mines of Laurium in Attica provided a valuable fund that Themistoklês persuaded the Athenian people to use for building ships rather than distributing the money. 

Athens also maintained a "Theôric Fund" for festivals and distributions, which Demades described as the "cement of the political system." While some suggest this surplus could have been stored in the acropolis as an accumulating war-fund—similar to provisions made half a century prior under Perikles—the city prioritized the social, religious, and patriotic communion provided by festival expenditure.

## Public and Specialized Funds
Various legislative and administrative frameworks have been established to make funds available for specific purposes:

* **Land Improvement:** In England, the Public Money Drainage Acts (1846-1856) authorized the advance of public money to landowners for irrigation, drainage, clearing, and the construction of permanent roads and buildings. Subsequent legislation, including the Land Improvement Act 1864 and its 1899 amendment, facilitated the borrowing of money by charging costs as a rent-charge upon the land's inheritance.
* **Military Research:** During wartime, funds may be provided on a generous scale to pursue multiple lines of experimentation simultaneously, such as the development of projectiles for use against aircraft and Zeppelins.
* **Legal Restitution:** Under the Justice for All Act of 2004, federal authorities are required to notify victims of any available restitution. However, the use of a civil remedy statute in place of a restitution fund may eliminate this notification requirement.
