# Dalmatia

Dalmatia is a kingdom and crownland of the Austro-Hungarian empire located on the Adriatic Sea in the north-west of the Balkan Peninsula. It is bounded by Croatia and Bosnia to the north and north-east, and by Montenegro and Herzegovina to the south and south-east. The region covers an area of 4,923 square miles, with a maximum length of 210 miles from the north-west to the south-east. Its breadth varies significantly, measuring 35 miles between the Bosnian frontier and Point Planca, but narrowing to less than one mile at Cattaro. Near Castelnuovo and Klek, the frontier of Herzegovina reaches the sea for a total distance of 14.5 miles.

## Early History and Roman Rule
The history of Dalmatia begins around 180 B.C. when a tribe, from which the region takes its name, established a republic after declaring independence from the Illyrian king Gentius. This republic was headquartered in its capital, Delminium, and its territory extended from the Cetina to the Narenta, and later to the Kerka, where it bordered Liburnia. 

Roman involvement began in 156 B.C. with an attack that forced the Dalmatians to pay tribute. However, the land was not fully annexed until the time of Augustus (31 B.C.–A.D. 14), following the suppression of several revolts by Tiberius in A.D. 9. This led to the adoption of Latin civilization across Illyria. Following the collapse of the Western Empire, the region was subject to Theodoric and Odoacer, Gothic rulers, from 476 to 535, before Justinian added it to the Eastern Empire.

## Slavic Migrations and Social Division
In the first half of the 7th century, a great Slavonic migration occurred. While invaders such as Bulgars, Serbs, and Croats easily absorbed or expelled populations in other parts of the Balkans, they were hindered in Dalmatia by highly civilized maritime city-states. These cities, including Zara and Ragusa, relied on the support of kinsfolk in Italy. 

This resulted in a divided society: Slavs settled the country districts, while the Italian or Latin population retreated to the large towns for safety. This division was further deepened by the 1054 schism between Western and Eastern Christianity, as the Italians remained attached to the Papacy while the Slavs generally preferred the Bogomil or Orthodox creeds. These two groups did not contribute to a common civilization in the literature of Ragusa until the 15th century.

## Population and Ethnology
As of 1900, the population of Dalmatia reached 591,597. This population consisted of approximately 3% Italians and 1% foreigners. The remaining 96% are known as Morlachs, belonging to the Serbo-Croatian branch of the Slavonic race. The term "Morlachs" (or *Mavrovlachi*) originally referred to Latinized inhabitants of central Illyria—nomadic muleteers, cattle-drovers, and shepherds—who were driven into the mountains by 7th-century invaders. By the 14th century, they migrated into maritime Croatia and northern Dalmatia. Over time, they adopted the language and manners of the Slavs. In southern Dalmatia, the term is sometimes used as an expression of contempt.

The literary languages of the region are Serbo-Croatian and Italian, both of which are modified by various dialect forms. Historically, the region also contained a large Celtic element, evidenced by common Celtic place-names.

## Political Transitions after 1797
Following the fall of the Venetian republic in 1797, the treaty of Campo Formio transferred Dalmatia to Austria. During this period, the republics of Poglizza and Ragusa remained independent, with Ragusa prospering through neutrality during the early Napoleonic wars. However, the peace of Pressburg in 1805 handed the country to France. This occupation was contested by a Russian force that seized the Bocche di Cattaro and received aid from Montenegrins. Napoleon deprived Poglizza of its independence in 1807 and Ragusa in 1808.

In 1809, Dalmatia was restored to France and united with the Illyrian kingdom via the treaty of Vienna. Between 1812 and 1815, a British naval force under Captain Hoste occupied the islands of Lagosta, Lesina, and Curzola after a naval engagement off Lissa, establishing overland trade through Germany, Austria, and Dalmatia. By 1814, allied Austrian and British forces removed the final French garrison, and in 1815, Dalmatia was incorporated into the Austro-Hungarian empire. Aside from ineffectual risings near Cattaro in 1869 and 1881–1882, the region remained tranquil.
