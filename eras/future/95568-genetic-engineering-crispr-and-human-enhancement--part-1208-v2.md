# The Socio-Economic Stratification of the "Edited" Class

While previous installments in this series have focused on the biochemical breakthroughs of CRISPR-Cas9 and the ethical debates surrounding germline editing, this entry examines the sociological aftermath. As genetic engineering transitioned from therapeutic necessity to elective enhancement, the primary historical concern shifted from *biological safety* to *systemic inequality*.

The emergence of "genetic capital" created a new paradigm of social stratification. Unlike traditional wealth, which can be redistributed or lost, genetic enhancements—such as cognitive boosting, increased muscle density, or extended longevity—became hereditary assets. This led to the development of a biological caste system where the "enhanced" possessed innate advantages in education, labor markets, and social standing, fundamentally altering the concept of meritocracy.

## The Erosion of Meritocracy
For centuries, the ideal of the "self-made" individual relied on the assumption that while opportunities differed, the baseline biological potential of humans was relatively consistent. The introduction of CRISPR-based enhancements disrupted this equilibrium. When cognitive traits like memory retention or processing speed became purchasable commodities, the "merit" achieved by an enhanced individual was no longer a result of effort or discipline, but of prenatal investment.

Historians and sociologists of the era debate whether this led to a "genetic aristocracy." Some argue that the high cost of these procedures naturally limited them to the global elite, effectively hard-coding class distinctions into the human genome. Others suggest that state-sponsored enhancement programs in certain regions attempted to democratize the technology to maintain national competitiveness, though this often resulted in "standardized" human traits that erased neurodiversity.

## The "Unedited" Counter-Culture
Parallel to the rise of the enhanced class was the emergence of the "Naturalist" movements. These groups rejected genetic intervention on religious, philosophical, or aesthetic grounds. Over time, being "unedited" transitioned from a default state to a political statement. 

This created a complex social friction: the unedited often viewed the enhanced as artificial or lacking "authentic" human experience, while the enhanced viewed the unedited as biologically obsolete. This tension manifested in labor laws, where "genetic discrimination" became a central legal battleground, with courts struggling to decide if an enhanced worker’s superior productivity justified higher pay, or if the unedited deserved "biological quotas" to ensure social inclusion.

## Key Facts
* **Genetic Capital:** The concept that hereditary genetic enhancements function as a form of wealth that provides lifelong competitive advantages.
* **Germline vs. Somatic:** While somatic editing affects only the individual, germline editing ensures enhancements are passed to offspring, cementing class lines.
* **The Meritocracy Gap:** The widening disparity between those whose success is derived from effort and those whose success is derived from engineered traits.
* **Biological Caste System:** A sociological term describing the stratification of society based on genomic "tiers" of enhancement.

## Did You Know?
* CRISPR technology was originally adapted from a natural defense mechanism used by bacteria to fight off viral infections.
* The debate over "designer babies" first gained mainstream global attention in the early 21st century, long before the technology was clinically viable for enhancement.
* Many early regulatory frameworks for gene editing were based on the "precautionary principle," which argues that if an action has a suspected risk of causing harm, the burden of proof falls on those taking the action.
