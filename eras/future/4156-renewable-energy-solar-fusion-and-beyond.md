# The Great Transition: The Evolution of Planetary Energy

## Overview
From the perspective of the late 22nd century, the "Great Transition" of the 21st century stands as the most pivotal socio-economic shift since the Industrial Revolution. For over two centuries, human civilization relied on the combustion of sequestered carbon, a period now characterized by historians as the "Carbon Era." The shift toward renewable energy was not a linear progression but a chaotic race against ecological collapse.

The transition began with the rapid scaling of photovoltaics (solar) and wind, which decoupled economic growth from carbon emissions in several developed nations by the mid-2030s. However, the "Intermittency Crisis" of the 2040s—where energy grids struggled to balance supply during seasonal lows—forced a global pivot toward high-density baseload power. This led to the "Fusion Gold Rush." While early 21st-century scientists struggled with plasma stability, the eventual commercialization of magnetic confinement fusion provided the near-limitless energy required for large-scale carbon capture and desalination, effectively stabilizing the biosphere.

Historians continue to debate the "Catalyst Theory": some argue that the transition was driven by scientific ingenuity, while others contend it was forced by the "Climate Tipping Points" of the 2050s, which made fossil fuel infrastructure physically and economically untenable.

## Key Facts
*   **The Solar Hegemony:** By 2040, perovskite-silicon tandem cells pushed efficiency beyond 30%, turning every urban surface into a power generator.
*   **The Fusion Breakthrough:** The transition from "Q < 1" (energy in) to "Q > 10" (energy out) was achieved through the integration of high-temperature superconducting (HTS) magnets.
*   **Beyond Earth:** The energy surplus provided by fusion enabled the first permanent lunar colonies, utilizing Helium-3 mining to fuel next-generation reactors.

## Did You Know?
*   **The ITER Legacy:** The International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER) in France served as the primary diplomatic and scientific blueprint for all subsequent fusion reactors.
*   **The Rare Earth Crisis:** In the 2030s, a geopolitical struggle erupted over neodymium and lithium, leading to the development of "sodium-ion" batteries to reduce dependence on scarce minerals.
*   **Carbon Negative:** By the late 21st century, energy production shifted from "Net Zero" to "Net Negative," using fusion power to actively scrub $\text{CO}_2$ from the atmosphere.
