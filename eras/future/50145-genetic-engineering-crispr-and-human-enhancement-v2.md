# The Socio-Economic Divide: The Legacy of "Genetic Stratification"

While previous archives have focused on the technical triumphs of CRISPR-Cas9 and the eradication of monogenic diseases, the historical record requires a critical examination of the societal fallout. The transition from *therapeutic* editing (fixing a mutation) to *enhancement* (improving a healthy trait) did not occur in a vacuum. Instead, it mirrored and magnified the existing wealth disparities of the early 21st century.

The central tension of this era was not whether human enhancement was possible, but who had access to it. As genetic interventions moved from clinical necessity to elective optimization, a new form of systemic inequality emerged: genetic stratification.

## The Commodification of the Genome

In the early stages of CRISPR application, the high cost of somatic cell editing meant that only the wealthiest demographics could afford "optimization" packages. These enhancements—ranging from increased cognitive processing speeds to enhanced muscle density and longevity—were not distributed by merit or need, but by capital.

Historians note that this created a feedback loop. Those with the means to enhance their offspring provided them with biological advantages that further ensured their success in competitive academic and professional environments. This effectively transformed socio-economic status into a heritable biological trait, challenging the democratic ideal of social mobility.

## The Ethical Schism and Regulatory Failure

The global response to this divide was fragmented. While some nations attempted to ban non-therapeutic enhancement to maintain "human equality," these regulations often drove the practice underground or into "genetic havens"—jurisdictions with lax oversight where the wealthy could bypass ethical restrictions.

Scholarly debate continues regarding the "Inevitability Thesis." Some historians argue that genetic stratification was an unavoidable outcome of a capitalist healthcare model, while others contend that a more robust international framework, similar to the early treaties on nuclear proliferation, could have ensured equitable distribution of the technology.

## Key Facts
* **Therapy vs. Enhancement:** The critical distinction between curing a disease (e.g., sickle cell anemia) and enhancing a trait (e.g., memory).
* **Germline Editing:** The modification of embryos, which ensures that enhancements are passed down to all future generations.
* **Somatic Editing:** Modifications made to non-reproductive cells, affecting only the individual patient.
* **Genetic Havens:** Regions that ignored international ethical guidelines to attract "genetic tourism."
* **The Access Gap:** The period during which the cost of CRISPR therapies remained prohibitively high for the general population.

## Did You Know?
* The first CRISPR-based therapy to receive regulatory approval (Casgevy) targeted sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia, marking the shift from theory to clinical reality.
* The 2018 "He Jiankui affair," involving the first gene-edited babies, sparked the first major global outcry and led to a tightening of international ethical standards.
* Many early critics of enhancement drew parallels to the 20th-century eugenics movements, warning that "optimization" could lead to a new form of social Darwinism.
