# The Epstein Case: DOJ Disclosure and FBI Investigations

The United States Department of Justice (DOJ) has released a comprehensive collection of materials related to the investigations of Jeffrey Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell. These disclosures, made available at justice.gov/epstein, include a wide array of investigative files, court records, and interview transcripts.

## Scope of Public Disclosures
The DOJ's production of materials encompasses several distinct investigative files and legal records. These include:
*   **FBI Investigative Files:** Records from FBI New York regarding the 2018 criminal case for child sex trafficking and the 2019 Maxwell criminal case, as well as the 2019 investigation into Epstein's death. Additionally, the production includes the FBI Miami investigative file for the 2006 Epstein criminal case for child prostitution and the 2009 Alfredo Rodriguez criminal case for obstruction of justice.
*   **Administrative and Custodial Records:** Materials from the Bureau of Prisons (BOP) concerning Epstein's custody at the Metropolitan Correctional Center New York (MCC New York), specifically count slips, commissary records, and visitor logbooks.
*   **Legal and Judicial Materials:** Grand-jury materials from the SDNY (Epstein and Maxwell cases) and the SDFL (Epstein case), along with court records from civil and criminal cases involving the Epstein estate, Maxwell, and Epstein.
*   **Other Reports:** Investigative materials that served as the basis for the Office of the Inspector General's (OIG) June 2023 report into the death of Epstein, as well as materials produced via Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests.

## FBI Investigations and Victim Interactions (2006–2008)
Internal DOJ memoranda detail the complexities of the FBI's early investigations into Epstein. Between 2006 and 2008, FBI agents conducted numerous interviews with victims. According to a 2017 declaration by an FBI case agent, a majority of these victims expressed concern regarding the public disclosure of their identities. Other victims feared their parents would discover their involvement with Epstein or worried about the impact on relationships with boyfriends and family members.

Specific interactions in early 2008 highlight tensions regarding the status of the investigation. On January 31, 2008, a CEOS Trial Attorney named Villafafia and the FBI interviewed three victims. One victim, identified as M, stated in a 2015 declaration that she believed the FBI was still investigating her case and was unaware of any Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA). M indicated that she hoped Epstein would be prosecuted and was willing to testify. During these interviews, one victim suffered an emotional breakdown, sobbing and reporting nightmares that Epstein was "coming after her."

## Prosecutorial Discretion and the NPA
The DOJ records reveal internal debates regarding the Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA). In November 2007, an official named Sloman complained to Leowitz that private investigators for Epstein were contacting victims to ask if government agents had discussed financial settlements. Sloman noted that FBI agents were legally required to inform victims of the resolution of the matter, including Epstein's agreement to pay damages.

Villafafia expressed concern to Sloman that filing charges at that time would lead to cross-examination regarding whether victims were told they would be entitled to damages if Epstein were convicted. While the FBI and USAO continued to locate and interview potential victims—including 12 new potential victims in October and November 2007—they decided to stop informing victims about the NPA. Villafafia later told the Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR) that she believed it was accurate to tell victims the case was "back under investigation" because they were still interviewing witnesses to prepare for a potential federal trial.

## OPR Investigative Process
The Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR) conducted an extensive review of the handling of the Epstein case. This investigation involved:
*   The review of hundreds of thousands of records, including memoranda, emails, and letters from the USAO, the Criminal Division, the Executive Office for U.S. Attorneys, and the Office of the Deputy Attorney General.
*   The examination of state investigation materials, including grand jury audio recordings and transcripts.
*   More than 60 interviews with witnesses, including FBI supervisors, administrative personnel, current and former USAO staff, and former senior Department managers, such as a former Assistant Attorney General for the Criminal Division and a former Deputy Attorney General.
*   Consultations with victims and their attorneys.
