# The Socio-Economic Stratification of the "Edited" Class

While previous entries in this series have focused on the biochemical mechanisms of CRISPR-Cas9 and the ethical dilemmas of germline editing, this installment examines the sociological aftermath of human enhancement. As genetic engineering transitioned from therapeutic necessity to elective enhancement, it ceased to be a purely medical issue and became a primary driver of systemic social stratification.

The central tension of this era was not the technology itself, but the accessibility of it. When the ability to enhance cognitive function, physical longevity, and disease resistance became a commodity, the existing wealth gap was biologically encoded. This created a divergence in the human experience, where socioeconomic status determined not just one's quality of life, but one's biological potential.

## The Emergence of Biological Caste Systems

Historians often point to the "Genetic Divide" as the moment when social mobility became functionally impossible. In earlier centuries, education and networking provided the ladders for upward mobility. However, with the advent of CRISPR-driven enhancements, the "Edited" elite possessed innate advantages—such as accelerated neural plasticity and enhanced memory retention—that the "Unedited" could not replicate through effort alone.

This led to a feedback loop: those with the means to afford enhancements secured the highest-paying roles in the global economy, which in turn provided the resources to further optimize the genomes of their offspring. The result was a biological reinforcement of class boundaries, transforming temporary economic advantage into permanent hereditary superiority.

## The Scholarly Debate: Determinism vs. Plasticity

Within the archives of the period, a fierce debate raged among sociologists and geneticists regarding "Genetic Determinism." One school of thought argued that the Edited class was fundamentally a new subspecies, possessing capacities that rendered traditional democratic equality obsolete. 

Conversely, critics argued that the "Plasticity Theory" remained valid—asserting that environmental factors, culture, and lived experience still played a critical role in human development. They contended that while an edited brain might process information faster, it did not inherently possess wisdom, creativity, or empathy. This debate highlighted the struggle to define "humanity" in an age where the genome was no longer a lottery, but a design choice.

## Key Facts
* **Therapeutic vs. Enhancement:** The initial legal divide was drawn between "correcting" a mutation (therapy) and "improving" a trait (enhancement).
* **The Access Gap:** High costs of CRISPR treatments initially limited enhancements to the top 1% of the global population.
* **Cognitive Optimization:** Focus areas for enhancement included increased synaptic density and the suppression of genes linked to cognitive decline.
* **Regulatory Failure:** International treaties struggled to maintain a unified ban on germline editing, leading to "genetic tourism" in unregulated jurisdictions.

## Did You Know?
* CRISPR was originally discovered as a natural immune system used by bacteria to fight off viral infections.
* The first successful CRISPR-based gene edits in human embryos were reported in 2015, sparking the global ethical debate.
* Many early critics of enhancement feared a "Gattaca-style" future, referring to the 1997 film that predicted genetic discrimination.
