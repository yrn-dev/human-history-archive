# Quantum Computing: Solving the Unsolvable — Part 1073
## The Socio-Economic Aftershocks of the Post-Encryption Era

While previous installments in this series have focused on the technical triumphs of quantum supremacy—specifically the engineering of stable qubits and the mastery of error correction—this entry examines the profound societal disruption that followed. The transition to a quantum-capable world was not merely a scientific milestone; it was a systemic shock to the global infrastructure of trust.

For decades, the digital economy relied on the presumed hardness of integer factorization. When the first cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQCs) became operational, the "unsolvable" nature of RSA and ECC encryption vanished. This created a period of intense geopolitical instability known as the "Great Decryption," where the historical archives of every government and corporation became transparent overnight.

## The Migration to Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)

The primary focus of this era was the desperate race toward Post-Quantum Cryptography. Historians note that the transition was not uniform. While developed nations and massive tech conglomerates integrated lattice-based cryptography relatively quickly, smaller institutions and legacy systems remained vulnerable for years.

This disparity created a "security divide." Entities that could not afford the computational overhead of PQC found themselves locked out of secure global trade networks. The shift was not just a software update; it required a fundamental rethinking of how identity and authenticity are verified in a world where traditional digital signatures are trivial to forge.

## Scholarly Debate: The "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later" Legacy

A point of significant scholarly debate among digital historians is the extent of the "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later" (HNDL) strategy. It is widely accepted that adversarial actors spent years collecting encrypted data in the 2020s, knowing they could not read it at the time but betting on the eventual arrival of quantum decryption.

Some historians argue that the damage was done long before the first quantum computer was turned on, suggesting that the "Quantum Apocalypse" was a slow-motion event rather than a sudden crash. Others contend that the rapid development of Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)—which uses the laws of physics rather than math to secure data—mitigated the worst of the fallout.

## Key Facts
* **Shor’s Algorithm:** The mathematical foundation that proved a quantum computer could efficiently factor large integers, rendering RSA encryption obsolete.
* **Lattice-Based Cryptography:** One of the primary replacements for classical encryption, designed to be resistant to both classical and quantum attacks.
* **Quantum Key Distribution (QKD):** A method of communication that allows two parties to produce a shared random secret key known only to them, detectable if intercepted.
* **NIST Standardization:** The pivotal role played by the National Institute of Standards and Technology in vetting and selecting PQC algorithms.

## Did You Know?
* **The Y2Q Problem:** Much like the Y2K bug, "Y2Q" (Years to Quantum) became a shorthand for the deadline by which the world needed to migrate its encryption to avoid total systemic collapse.
* **Quantum Randomness:** Unlike classical "pseudo-random" numbers, quantum computers can generate truly random numbers, which are essential for high-security encryption.
* **The Cost of Transition:** The migration to quantum-resistant standards is estimated to be one of the most expensive infrastructure upgrades in human history, surpassing the cost of early internet adoption.
