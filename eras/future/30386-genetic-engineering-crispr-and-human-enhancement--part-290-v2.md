# The Socio-Economic Stratification of the "Edited" Class

While previous entries in this series have focused on the biochemical breakthroughs of CRISPR-Cas9 and the ethical dilemmas of germline editing, the historical trajectory of genetic engineering cannot be understood without analyzing its impact on social hierarchy. As the technology transitioned from therapeutic application (curing disease) to enhancement (augmenting capability), it ceased to be a medical tool and became a marker of socio-economic status.

The central tension of this era was the transition from "natural lottery" to "purchased advantage." For the first time in human history, the biological advantages typically distributed by chance—cognitive speed, physical endurance, and longevity—became commodities available to those with the financial means to afford them.

## The Emergence of the Biological Divide

The primary historical concern during the rollout of human enhancement was the creation of a "genetic underclass." Historians note that while public health initiatives initially aimed for universal access, the market-driven nature of biotechnology led to a tiered system of enhancement. 

Wealthier demographics were able to access "polygenic scoring" to select for complex traits, creating a feedback loop of success. Those with edited cognitive capabilities were more likely to secure high-paying roles in the burgeoning biotech economy, which in turn allowed them to afford further enhancements for their offspring. This created a hereditary meritocracy where biological superiority was bought, not earned, fundamentally challenging the democratic ideal of equal opportunity.

## Scholarly Debates on "Genetic Essentialism"

Among historians and sociologists of the period, a significant debate persists regarding "genetic essentialism." One school of thought argues that the social divide was inevitable and that CRISPR merely accelerated a process already occurring through disparities in nutrition and education. 

Conversely, critics argue that genetic engineering introduced a qualitative difference in human inequality. They contend that while a wealthy child in the pre-CRISPR era could be tutored, they were still biologically human; an "enhanced" individual, however, possessed a hardware advantage that rendered traditional social mobility nearly impossible for the unedited.

## Key Facts
* **Therapy vs. Enhancement:** The critical legal and ethical line was drawn between *somatic* editing (fixing a disease in one person) and *germline* editing (changing traits for future generations).
* **Polygenic Traits:** Most enhancements targeted polygenic traits, which are controlled by multiple genes, making the process far more complex than curing single-gene disorders like sickle cell anemia.
* **Regulatory Fragmentation:** Different global jurisdictions adopted varying laws, leading to "genetic tourism" where wealthy individuals traveled to unregulated regions for enhancements.
* **The Accessibility Gap:** The high cost of CRISPR-based therapies initially limited access to the top 1% of the global population.

## Did You Know?
* CRISPR is derived from a natural defense mechanism used by bacteria to fight off viral infections.
* The first human embryos were edited using CRISPR in 2015, sparking an immediate global debate on the ethics of "designer babies."
* Many early critics of enhancement feared the loss of "human diversity," arguing that a drive toward a standardized "ideal" would eliminate beneficial genetic variations.
