# The Great Migration: Socio-Economic Stratification in the Virtual Era

While previous installments in this series have focused on the technical evolution of neural-link interfaces and the sensory fidelity of digital realms, this entry examines the sociological fallout of "The Great Migration." As humanity shifted its primary residence from physical geography to persistent virtual environments, the nature of class, labor, and social mobility underwent a fundamental transformation.

The transition was not uniform. The emergence of digital worlds did not erase existing inequalities; rather, it translated them into a new medium. The divide shifted from who owned the land to who owned the server architecture and the bandwidth.

## The Digital Land Rush and Virtual Feudalism

In the early stages of mass migration, digital space was marketed as a democratic utopia where anyone could build a paradise. However, historians note that this quickly devolved into a system of "Virtual Feudalism." The corporations that developed the core engines of these worlds retained ultimate ownership of the underlying code. 

Users who "purchased" virtual estates were, in legal reality, merely leasing licenses. This created a new class of digital peasantry—individuals who spent their entire waking lives working virtual jobs to pay for the upkeep of their digital dwellings, all while the platform architects extracted "existence rents" through subscription models and transaction fees.

## The Physicality Gap: The "Meatspace" Divide

A significant scholarly debate persists regarding the "Physicality Gap." Some historians argue that the migration was a liberation from the constraints of biology. Conversely, a growing school of thought suggests that the ability to maintain a high-quality physical body became the ultimate status symbol.

As the wealthy transitioned their consciousness into optimized digital avatars, they paradoxically invested more in the "Meatspace" (the physical world) to ensure their biological substrates—the servers and life-support pods—were housed in the most secure, climate-controlled environments. This led to a stark geographic divide: gleaming, automated fortress-cities for the elite, and decaying urban sprawls for those whose digital access was intermittent or low-resolution.

## The Erosion of Shared Reality

The most profound impact was the fragmentation of the collective human experience. In the physical era, people of different classes still shared the same air and streets. In the virtual era, algorithmic filtering allowed users to exist in entirely different versions of reality. The wealthy lived in "High-Fidelity" zones with simulated physics and limitless resources, while the lower castes inhabited "Low-Poly" sectors characterized by lag, visual artifacts, and restrictive permissions.

### Key Facts
* **Server Sovereignty:** The shift in power from national governments to the entities controlling the hosting infrastructure.
* **Bandwidth Poverty:** A socio-economic condition where limited data speeds restricted a user's ability to interact with high-fidelity social circles.
* **The Substrate Crisis:** The ongoing struggle to maintain the physical hardware required to sustain billions of uploaded consciousnesses.
* **Avatar Dysmorphia:** A widespread psychological condition resulting from the gap between one's idealized digital form and their physical biological state.

### Did You Know?
* The term "Meatspace" originated in early cyberpunk literature before becoming the standard historical term for the physical world.
* Some early virtual colonies attempted "Open-Source Governance," but most were absorbed by larger corporate entities within a decade.
* The first "Digital Tax" was implemented not on income, but on the amount of processing power (CPU cycles) an individual consumed.
