# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must shift our focus to the logistical precursors of production. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian is not merely a story of increasing cognitive skill in shaping stone, but a story of expanding geographical horizons and strategic resource management.

The transition in toolkits reflects a profound change in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their landscape. To understand the evolution of the tool, we must first understand the evolution of the "quarry."

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Early *Homo* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. The "raw material economy" was local; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used. There was little evidence of selective transport, meaning the tool's form was often dictated by the natural shape of the available stone.

As we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), a shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective procurement." They no longer settled for the nearest rock; they sought out specific materials—such as high-quality flint or obsidian—that offered predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fractures). This suggests a mental map of the landscape and a willingness to travel several kilometers to reach a known source.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the Mousterian era, the Levallois technique emerged, requiring a highly sophisticated "prepared core." This method demanded a specific grade of stone to ensure the final flake would detach with precision. The logistical pressure increased: if a group moved into a region devoid of high-quality stone, their entire technological system was threatened.

This led to the emergence of curated toolkits. Rather than discarding a tool after a single use, Mousterian tools show evidence of extensive retouching and sharpening. This shift from "expedient" tools to "curated" tools indicates a cognitive leap in planning and a recognition of the scarcity of prime raw materials.

## Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Localism:** Oldowan tools were generally sourced within a very small radius of the habitation site.
* **Selectivity:** Later hominins bypassed inferior stones to seek out materials with superior flaking properties.
* **Transport Costs:** The movement of raw materials over long distances indicates an evolved understanding of spatial memory.
* **The Levallois Requirement:** The Mousterian prepared-core technique required high-quality, homogenous stone to be successful.
* **Curated vs. Expedient:** The shift toward retouching tools suggests a strategy to maximize the lifespan of rare materials.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some later prehistoric contexts, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers from its volcanic source, suggesting early forms of social exchange or trade.
* **The "Core" Logic:** In the Levallois technique, the "waste" was not the flake, but the core; the goal was to produce one perfect, predetermined shape.
* **Material Bias:** Some archaeological sites appear "primitive" not because the inhabitants were less skilled, but because they only had access to poor-quality local stone.
