# Western Cordillera of Bolivia

The Western Cordillera is a geological feature of the Bolivian Andes. It consists primarily of beds from the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods, which are found together with the ashes and lavas produced by the region's great volcanoes.

## Volcanic Activity and Distribution
The volcanoes of Bolivia are located almost entirely within the Western Cordillera. This distinguishes the region from the eastern ranges of the Bolivian Andes, where the great summits—such as Sorata and Illimani—are instead formed of other intrusions and Palaeozoic rocks.

## Surrounding Geological Features
The Western Cordillera is separated from the eastern ranges by an intervening plateau. This plateau is covered by terrestrial and freshwater deposits. Within these deposits, ridges of Palaeozoic rock rise, along with a series of gypsiferous marls and red sandstones. The age of these specific deposits is somewhat uncertain, though they are probably, at least in part, Cretaceous.

The depression located between the Western Cordillera and the Eastern Cordillera is covered by recent deposits. These appear to be partly of torrential origin, similar to talus-fans found at the foot of mountain ranges in other dry regions. Additionally, the lakes Pampa Aullaguas (Poopo) and Titicaca were undoubtedly more extensive in the past than they are currently.

## Comparison with the Eastern Andes
While the Western Cordillera is characterized by Jurassic and Cretaceous beds and volcanic activity, the eastern ranges of the Bolivian Andes are formed of Palaeozoic rocks with other and granitic intrusions. These Palaeozoic beds have yielded fossils from the Carboniferous, Devonian, Ordovician, and Cambrian ages. 

In the northern part of the eastern ranges, the Carboniferous and Devonian are extensively developed, whereas in southern Bolivia, the Ordovician and Cambrian beds make up the greater part of the range. Further east, the hills known as the Chiquitos, which rise from the plains of eastern Bolivia, are composed of ancient sedimentary rocks of an unknown age.

## Stratigraphy of the Eastern Region
The Palaeozoic beds in the east are directly overlaid by gypsiferous marls and red sandstones, which are similar to the *formacion petrolifera* found in Brazil and Argentina. These layers frequently feature a tuff or conglomerate of porphyritic rocks at the base. Marine fossils discovered in the middle of this series by Gustav Steinmann indicate an age no earlier than the Jurassic, and Steinmann specifically refers them to the Lower Cretaceous. However, it is considered not improbable that this series may represent more than one geological system.

While no later marine deposits have been found in the plains of Bolivia or the eastern Andes, freshwater beds of a later date and the Tertiary period occupy a wide area.
