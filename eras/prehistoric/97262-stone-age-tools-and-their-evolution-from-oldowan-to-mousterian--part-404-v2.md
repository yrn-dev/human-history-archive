# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and techniques used to create Oldowan choppers or Mousterian scrapers—we must shift our gaze from the finished object to the origin of the stone. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability, but a revolution in geological procurement and spatial planning.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a fundamental shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape. It is the story of moving from "opportunistic gathering" to "strategic sourcing."

## From Opportunism to Intentionality
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally used whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often sourced from nearby stream beds. The "cost" of transporting raw materials was high in terms of energy, so tools were typically discarded where they were made.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "raw material selectivity." Hominins began to bypass readily available low-quality stone in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. This indicates a sophisticated mental map of the environment and the ability to plan for future needs—a cognitive trait known as "executive function."

## The Logistics of the Mousterian
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly organized. The Levallois technique, a hallmark of this era, required a specific type of stone that could be predictably flaked. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported high-quality cores over several kilometers. This suggests the existence of "quarry sites" and a rudimentary form of economic planning. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individual bands simply expanded their foraging territories to secure better stone. Regardless, the shift from using "whatever is here" to "seeking the best" marks a pivotal moment in human evolution.

## Key Facts
* **Raw Material Selectivity:** The preference for fine-grained stones (like flint) allowed for sharper edges and more predictable fracturing.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools were typically sourced within a few hundred meters; Mousterian materials are often found kilometers from their source.
* **The Levallois Method:** A sophisticated "prepared core" technique that required specific geological properties to be successful.
* **Curated Technology:** Later Stone Age tools were "curated" (carried and maintained) rather than "expedient" (made and thrown away).
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The ability to remember the location of a distant quarry suggests an evolution in long-term memory and spatial reasoning.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some prehistoric regions, obsidian—a volcanic glass—has been found hundreds of kilometers from the nearest volcano, suggesting early networks of exchange.
* **The "Knapping" Sound:** Experimental archaeologists use the sound of the stone striking to determine if a core is structurally sound before they begin shaping it.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians often learn more from "debitage" (the waste flakes left over) than from the tools themselves, as the waste reveals the entire sequence of production.
