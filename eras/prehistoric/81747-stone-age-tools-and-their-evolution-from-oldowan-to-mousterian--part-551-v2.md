# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the logistical challenge of the materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. To understand the evolution of the Stone Age, we must examine the "raw material economy": how prehistoric hominins selected, transported, and valued specific minerals.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian industries was not merely a leap in cognitive ability, but a shift in how hominins interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan sites (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They typically utilized river cobbles—basalt, quartz, or quartzite—found in the immediate vicinity of their campsites. The "transport distance" was minimal; if a suitable stone was within a few hundred meters, it was used.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a pattern of strategic sourcing emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a "conchoidal fracture," meaning they break in predictable, glass-like curves, allowing for the precision required to create the thin, sharp scrapers and points characteristic of the Mousterian toolkit.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The movement of stone over long distances serves as a proxy for understanding prehistoric social structures. In later Middle Paleolithic sites, archaeologists have found raw materials that originated dozens, and sometimes over a hundred, kilometers away from the site of use.

This suggests two possibilities: either the hominins were highly migratory, carrying "cores" of stone with them as they traveled, or they had established rudimentary exchange networks with other groups. The effort required to transport a heavy piece of high-quality flint across a landscape indicates that the *value* of the tool's edge outweighed the *cost* of the energy spent transporting the material.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Availability

There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether the selection of "exotic" stones was a conscious aesthetic or qualitative choice, or simply a result of shifting migration patterns. Some archaeologists argue that the preference for specific stones in Mousterian assemblages indicates a "mental template" not just for the tool's shape, but for the material's properties, marking a significant cognitive evolution in planning and foresight.

### Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break, which is essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Transport Distance:** The measurement of how far a raw material traveled from its geological source to the archaeological site.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for and collecting stone for tool production.
* **Material Hierarchy:** The tendency for later hominins to prefer cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) over coarser stones (like quartzite).

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Tools:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded after one use, Mousterian tools show signs of "resharpening," indicating they were valued possessions.
* **Geological Mapping:** Modern archaeologists use "provenance studies" (chemical fingerprinting) to trace a single stone tool back to the exact quarry it came from thousands of years ago.
