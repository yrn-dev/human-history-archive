# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the logistics behind the stone. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their landscape and managed raw materials.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" foraging to "strategic" procurement. Understanding where the stone came from provides critical insight into the social organization and territorial range of early humans.

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers typically utilized whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often scavenging materials from riverbeds. The "cost" of transporting stone was high relative to the tool's lifespan; once a chopper became dull, it was discarded, and a new stone was sourced from a few meters away.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a distinct shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective sourcing," bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials allow for predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture), which was essential for the Levallois technique used by Neanderthals.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian toolkit (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago) reveals a sophisticated understanding of geography. Archaeological sites often contain "exotic" stones—materials not native to the immediate geology of the site. This suggests two possibilities: either the hominins traveled vast distances to quarry specific sites, or they engaged in early forms of social exchange with other groups.

The transport of high-quality cores over several kilometers indicates a cognitive shift toward "future-planning." Rather than reacting to an immediate need, Mousterian toolmakers carried "pre-forms"—partially worked stones—that could be refined into specialized scrapers or points at the moment of use.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Chance

There remains a scholarly debate regarding the "trade" of stone. Some archaeologists argue that the presence of non-local stone is simply a byproduct of wide-ranging migratory patterns. Others suggest that the consistency of specific material sources across different sites points to a structured social network or a "mental map" of the landscape passed down through generations.

### Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily utilized local river cobbles; minimal transport distances.
* **Acheulean:** Increased preference for finer-grained stones to achieve the symmetry of the hand-axe.
* **Mousterian:** Heavy reliance on high-quality flint and obsidian for precision flaking.
* **Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined shape.
* **Exotic Materials:** Non-local stones found at sites, indicating movement or exchange.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian’s Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Unlike Oldowan tools, Mousterian tools show signs of "re-sharpening," meaning they were kept and maintained over time.
* **The "Mental Template":** The transition to Mousterian tools suggests the toolmaker had a clear image of the final product in their mind before the first strike.
