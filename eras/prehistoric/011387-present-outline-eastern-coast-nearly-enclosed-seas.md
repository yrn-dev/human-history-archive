# The Present Outline of the Eastern Coast and the Nearly Enclosed Seas

## Geological Origins and Formation
The present outline of the eastern coast of Asia and the nearly enclosed seas situated between the mainland and various islands are attributed primarily to simple faulting, according to the findings of Richthofen. This geological structure is part of a broader pattern of Asian landmass formation. Geologically, Asia can be viewed as consisting of a northern mass and a southern mass, both of which were too rigid to crumple but capable of fracturing. Between these two rigid masses lay an intermediate belt of softer rock.

During the Mesozoic era, a sea existed between the northern and southern landmasses of Asia, where ordinary marine sediments were deposited. While the current belt of folding does not coincide precisely with this central sea, the correspondence is considered fairly close. The elevation of the Himalayan range, for instance, resulted from an overthrust from the north, as if the high land of central Asia had been pushed southward against the unyielding old rocks of the Indian peninsula, causing the edges of the over-riding strata to be crumpled and folded.

## General Outline of Asia
Due to the vast extent of Asia, ordinary maps often provide misleading conceptions of its form because of the distortions inherent in projecting large spherical areas onto flat surfaces. When viewed on a globe, Asia resembles a great isosceles spherical triangle. 

The north-eastern apex of this triangle is located at East Cape (Vostochnyi) in the Bering Strait. The two equal sides of the triangle each measure approximately 6,500 miles (a quadrant of the sphere). One side extends westward to the southern point of Arabia, and the other extends eastward to the extremity of the Malay peninsula. The base of the triangle, which connects these two points, occupies about 60 degrees of a great circle, or 4,500 miles. This base is deeply indented on either side of the Indian peninsula by the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea.

## Boundaries and Coastal Limits
The boundaries of the continent can be approximated by drawing great circles through East Cape:

*   **North-western Boundary:** A great circle drawn from East Cape to the southern point of Arabia follows the coastline of the Arctic Ocean, crosses the Ural Mountains, passes through the western part of the Caspian, and runs nearly along the boundary between Asiatic Turkey and Persia. Asia Minor and the north-western half of Arabia lie outside this circle.
*   **Eastern Boundary:** A great circle drawn from East Cape to the extremity of the Malay peninsula follows the coasts of Cochin-China, China, and Manchuria, departing relatively little from the actual eastern boundary.

## Regional Coastal Characteristics
In the far east, the Maritime Province's southern extremity features the valley of the Suifeng river, which enters the Pacific Ocean at the Gulf of Peter the Great. The coastal climate in this region is influenced by sea currents; while the warm Kuro-Siwo current does not reach the Siberian coasts, a cold current from the Sea of Okhotsk flows along the Pacific shore to the eastern coast of Korea, bringing chilling fogs and icy water to the coasts of Sakhalin.

In other regions, such as the Kilia delta on the eastern side, the coastline is constantly advancing and the sea is becoming shallower. This is caused by the enormous amount of solid deposits—sand and mud—brought down by the river, with the Kilia branch pouring approximately 3,000 cubic feet of these materials into the sea per minute during ordinary floods.
