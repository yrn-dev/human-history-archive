# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the materials themselves. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological procurement and spatial planning.

For the prehistoric hominin, a tool was only as effective as the stone from which it was knapped. The shift from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound evolution in territorial awareness and social organization.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever basalt or quartz was available in their immediate vicinity. The "cost" of transporting heavy raw materials was high, meaning tools were often discarded shortly after use.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian, we see a marked increase in "selective procurement." Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint or chert, which provide a more predictable fracture pattern. This suggests that early humans had developed a "mental map" of their landscape, remembering the locations of specific outcrops over several kilometers.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift
By the time the Mousterian industry emerged (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), the relationship with raw materials had become strategic. The Levallois technique—which involves preparing a core to strike off a single, predetermined flake—required high-quality stone to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian tool-makers transported raw materials over significantly longer distances than their Oldowan ancestors. This transport implies a shift in social structure: either individuals were traveling further, or there were established networks for moving stone between groups. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this constitutes "trade" in a modern sense or simply the result of wider seasonal migration patterns.

## Key Facts
* **Material Preference:** While basalt was common in early sites, flint and obsidian became highly prized for their ability to hold a razor-sharp edge.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan materials were typically sourced within 1–5 km; Mousterian materials are frequently found sourced from 10–30 km away.
* **Core Reduction:** The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian represents a shift from "expedient" tool use to "curated" technology.
* **Geological Knowledge:** The ability to identify specific rock types indicates a sophisticated understanding of mineral properties.

## Did You Know?
* **The Obsidian Trail:** In some prehistoric regions, obsidian (volcanic glass) has been found hundreds of kilometers from its only known source, suggesting vast prehistoric movement or exchange.
* **Recycling:** Some Mousterian sites show evidence of "tool recycling," where old tools were reshaped into new ones to save on the effort of sourcing new stone.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike an Oldowan chopper, a Mousterian point was often treated as a prized possession, carried for weeks and meticulously sharpened.
