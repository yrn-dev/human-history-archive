# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian point—we must shift our focus to the *geology* behind the craft. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their landscape and managed raw material logistics.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and long-term planning.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized whatever water-worn cobbles were available in their immediate vicinity, typically basalt or quartz. The "cost" of transporting these materials was high relative to their utility, meaning tools were often discarded exactly where they were made.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "selectivity." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, razor-sharp edges required for the Levallois technique.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade
The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of resource distribution. Archaeological evidence shows that high-quality raw materials were often transported over distances exceeding 10 to 50 kilometers from their original source.

This movement suggests two possibilities that remain subjects of scholarly debate: either these groups were highly mobile, carrying "cores" of stone with them as they migrated, or they engaged in early forms of social exchange with neighboring groups. The ability to curate a tool—sharpening it repeatedly rather than discarding it—indicates a psychological shift toward "future-oriented" behavior. The stone was no longer just a tool; it was a portable asset.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
*   **Oldowan:** Primarily utilized local river cobbles; minimal transport distance.
*   **Acheulean:** Increased preference for softer stones that allowed for symmetrical shaping (handaxes).
*   **Mousterian:** Heavy reliance on flint and obsidian for the production of specialized flakes.
*   **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones break (like glass), essential for creating sharp edges.
*   **Curation:** The practice of maintaining and transporting a tool over long distances rather than making a new one on-site.

## Did You Know?
*   **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel, which is why it was highly prized by Mousterian toolmakers.
*   **The "Core" Strategy:** Instead of carrying finished tools, later Paleolithic humans often carried a "core"—a large block of stone from which many smaller flakes could be struck as needed.
*   **Geological Mapping:** By tracing the chemical signature of a stone tool back to its parent quarry, modern archaeologists can map the exact migration routes of prehistoric humans.
