# BABOON

The term "baboon" is derived from the French word *babuin*, which in turn originates from *Babon*, an Egyptian deity to whom the animal was sacred. In a strict sense, the term designates the Egyptian monkey known scientifically as *Papio anubis*, characterized by a long muzzle and a medium-sized tail. In a broader sense, it refers to all members of the genus *Papio* (formerly known as *Cynocephalus*).

## Physical Characteristics and Behavior
Baboons are generally large terrestrial monkeys. They possess long, naked, dog-like muzzles with nostrils pierced in the truncated extremity. Their limbs are short and thick, adapted more for walking than for climbing. Other notable physical features include large cheek-pouches and large, often brightly colored, naked callosities located on the buttocks.

In terms of locomotion, the baboon walks on a flat foot using all its toes, with the middle toe serving as the chief throw off, a manner of walking similar to that of a bear. This differs significantly from humans, who walk on the toe and heel, and the great apes, who walk on the outer side of the foot.

Baboons typically frequent barren rocky districts in large droves. They are described as being exceedingly fierce and dangerous to approach. Their diet is opportunistic, consisting of practically anything eatable they are able to kill or capture.

## Classification and Species
The genus *Papio* is currently confined to Arabia and Africa, though it extended into India in past times. The genus is divided into several groups:

*   **The Yellow Baboon**: *P. cynocephalus* (or *babuin*) is the typical representative of the genus. It is distinguished by its small size and grooved muzzle, and its range extends from the Zambezi to Abyssinia.
*   **Subgenus *Choeropithecus***: This group includes the anubis baboon (*P. anubis*), including the subspecies *doguera*, *heuglini*, *neumanni*, and *pruinosus*—which range from Egypt throughout tropical Africa—as well as *P. sphinx*, *P. olivaceus*, the chacma (*P. porcarius*) of the Cape, and the Abyssinian *P. lydekkeri*.
*   **Subgenus *Hamadryas***: This group is typified by the South Arabian *P. arabicus* and the Arabian baboon (*P. hamadryas*) of Arabia and North Africa.
*   **Subgenus *Maimon***: This group consists of the mandrill (*P. maimon*) and the drill (*P. leucophaeus*) found on the west coast.

Additionally, a peculiar form of baboon known as *Cynopithecus* is found in Celebes; this form appears to be allied to African animals rather than Asiatic ones.

## Historical and Cultural Significance
The ancient Egyptians held the baboon in high regard. The Arabian baboon (*P. hamadryas*) was dedicated to the god Thoth. Frescoes indicate that the Egyptians tamed anubis baboons and trained them to pluck sycamore-figs from trees.

In the demonology of some "savage, barbarous and semi-civilized peoples," such as the Malays, there is a belief in a "baboon demon." This entity is said to take possession of dancers, causing them to perform wonderful feats of climbing.

## Evolutionary Context
The geological record for primates is difficult to decipher because they often live in forests or bare rocky places and are rarely covered by sediment. Primitive monkeys appeared early in the Cainozoic period, approximately forty million years ago.

Regarding the use of tools, Charles Darwin represented baboons as using stakes to prize up rocks while hunting for insects, striking blows with stones and sticks, and breaking nuts by using stones.
