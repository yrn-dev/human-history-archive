# Bayeux, Calvados

Bayeux, located in the department of Calvados, is recognized as a type district in the field of geology and is associated with several historical figures and artifacts.

## The Bajocian Stage
In geology, the term "Bajocian" was proposed in 1849 by d'Orbigny to describe rocks of Middle Jurassic age that are well developed in the vicinity of Bayeux, Calvados. The Bajocian stage is considered practically equivalent to the Inferior Oolite as defined by British geologists. It also corresponds closely with the Dogger of Oppel and the Middle and Lower Brown Jura of Quenstedt.

The rocks of the Bajocian stage in Europe are primarily various types of limestones, which are very frequently oolitic. In the type district of Bayeux, these are specifically ferruginous oolites. Other regional variations include:
* **Jura and Lorraine:** A crinoidal variety of limestone is overlain by a coral limestone.
* **Maine and Anjou:** The presence of marly beds and calcareous sandy beds.
* **Poitou:** Dolomitic limestone containing chert nodules.

Using fossil ammonites, the Bajocia strata are divided into several zones in descending order: *Parkinsonia Parkinsoni* and *Cosmoceras garantianum*; *Coeloceras subcoronatum* (*Humphriesianum*); *Sonninia Romani*; *Stephaeoceras Sowerbyi*; *Harpoceras concavum*; and *Murchisonae*. Some European geologists, however, prefer to place the *Parkinsonia* zone at the base of the overlying Bathonian.

The range of rocks of this age is extensive globally. They are found in North America (California, Oregon, Mexico, Dakota, and British Columbia) and South America (Argentina, Chile, and Bolivia). In Africa, they appear in Somaliland, north Africa, German East Africa, and north-west Madagascar. They extend through southern Europe into Turkestan, and may possibly include the Kota-Maleri beds of India's Upper Gondwana series. The Bajocian sea also encompassed Japan, Borneo, New Zealand, and parts of New South Wales, reaching as far as Franz Josef Land and the polar region of eastern Greenland.

## Historical Figures and the Bayeux Tapestry
Bayeux is linked to Odo, the Bishop of Bayeux, who was a significant landowner following the Norman Conquest, retaining estates of importance in areas such as Buckinghamshire. Odo is depicted on the Bayeux tapestry wielding a mace; this was used by medieval bishops in battle instead of a sword to adhere to the canonical rule forbidding priests from shedding blood.

The Bayeux tapestry also provides early evidence regarding the history of the British horse. One piece of the tapestry depicts a horse drawing a harrow, which is the earliest suggestion that horses were utilized in agriculture, though this was likely an exceptional case as oxen remained the primary animal for such work until a later period.

## Academic Contributions in Calvados
The region of Calvados was the site of significant palaeontological work by Eugène Eudes-Deslongchamps (1830-1889). Born in 1830, he became a professor of geology and dean, as well as a professor of zoology at the faculty of sciences at Caen around 1856. Following the death of his father in 1867, he completed a memoir on Teleosaurs titled *Prodrome des Téléosauriens du Calvados*. His other contributions to the Société Linnéenne de Normandie include studies on the geology of Calvados (1856-1863) and the *Terrain callovien* (1859), as well as his work *Le Jura normand* (1877-1878). Eudes-Deslongchamps died on December 21, 1889, at Château Matthieu, Calvados.
