# Angaraland

Angaraland is the name given by E. Suess to the northern land mass of Asia. This geological region is part of a broader continental structure that, during the Mesozoic era, consisted of a northern mass and a southern mass separated by an intervening sea. This sea is referred to by Suess as the Tethys and by Neumayr as the central Mediterranean sea.

## Geological Composition and History
The northern land mass begins in the north in the area situated between the Lena and the Yenisei rivers. In this region, Cambrian and Ordovician beds overlie folded Archean rocks; these beds generally remain undisturbed and flat. Resting upon these are the Angara series, which consist of freshwater deposits—primarily conglomerate and sandstone—containing numerous plant remains and workable seams of coal. While some of these deposits are of Permian age, others are likely Jurassic.

Evidence indicates that much of the north and east of Asia has remained land since the Palaeozoic era. With the exception of the extreme north, where Cretaceous and Jurassic marine fossils have been discovered, there is no evidence that this specific part of Siberia has been submerged beneath the sea since the early part of the Palaeozoic era.

## Tectonic Activity and Volcanism
The northern land mass is characterized as a rigid region, too rigid to crumple but capable of fracturing. Geologically, Asia is viewed as consisting of this northern mass and a southern mass (Gondwanaland), with an intermediate belt of softer rock. Due to the contraction of the earth's interior, the outer crust was forced to accommodate a smaller nucleus, causing the central softer belt to crumple and fold, while the rigid masses to the north and south yielded via faulting. This process is evident in the Himalayan range, where folds were produced by an overthrust from the north, as if the high land of central Asia had been pushed southward over the old rocks of the Indian peninsula.

The northern unfolded region has also been the site of significant volcanic activity since or during the Cretaceous period. Great flows of basic lava are found directly upon the Ordovician and Cambrian beds of Siberia; these are certainly in part of Tertiary age. Similar, though smaller, flows are present in northern China, Korea, and Manchuria. While these eruptions have almost entirely ceased, the primary volcanoes of the present day are located in the islands off the south eastern and eastern coasts.

## Eocene Continuity and Distribution
During Eocene times, there may have been greater continuity among the northern land masses than exists today. It is possible that America joined Asia via Alaska, and that Europe was united with North America through Greenland and Iceland. However, the northern land mass was divided in the region just east of the Ural mountains by a northward-extending arm of the great central Mediterranean sea.
