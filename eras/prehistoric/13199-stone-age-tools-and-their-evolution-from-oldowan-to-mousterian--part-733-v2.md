# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the logistics of the materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. To understand the evolution of the Stone Age, we must examine the "lithic economy": how prehistoric hominins selected, transported, and valued their raw materials.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a leap in cognitive skill, but a shift in how early humans interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. The "transport distance" for these tools was minimal; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used. The focus was on immediate utility rather than long-term planning.

As we transition into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked change. *Homo erectus* and later *Homo neanderthalensis* began to exhibit "selective sourcing." They no longer settled for the nearest available rock; instead, they sought out high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials fracture more predictably (conchoidal fracture), allowing for the precise thinning required to create the iconic Acheulean handaxe or the refined Mousterian scrapers.

## The Logistics of Mobility

The movement of stone serves as a proxy for understanding prehistoric social structures and territorial ranges. Archaeological evidence shows that by the Mousterian era, Neanderthals were transporting high-quality raw materials over distances of 10 to 60 kilometers. 

This suggests a sophisticated mental map of the environment. Hominins were not wandering aimlessly; they were returning to known quarries. This behavior implies a level of foresight—the ability to anticipate a future need for a tool and the willingness to invest energy in transporting heavy raw stone to a distant campsite.

## The Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing scholarly debate is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the local geology) indicates early trade networks between different hominin groups or simply the wide migratory patterns of a single group. While some archaeologists argue that the distribution of obsidian in certain regions suggests a primitive exchange economy, others maintain that these patterns are better explained by seasonal migration.

### Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Material Preference:** Flint and obsidian were prized for their ability to produce an edge sharper than a modern surgical scalpel.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools were rarely moved far from their source, whereas Mousterian materials often traveled dozens of kilometers.
* **Curation:** Later Stone Age tools show evidence of "resharpening," indicating that the material was valued and preserved.
* **Cognitive Leap:** The shift from opportunistic to strategic sourcing indicates an evolution in spatial memory and long-term planning.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian is volcanic glass; when fractured, it can create an edge only a few molecules thick, making it the sharpest natural material known.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** In the Mousterian Levallois technique, the stone core was carefully prepared so that a single, perfectly shaped flake could be struck off with one blow.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use "X-ray fluorescence" to trace a prehistoric tool back to the exact quarry where the stone originated.
