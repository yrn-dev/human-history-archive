# The Raw Material Dilemma: Lithic Procurement and Transport

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the "where" and "how" of the stone itself. The evolution of prehistoric toolkits was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in the logistics of raw material procurement.

For the early hominin, a tool was only as good as the rock it was made from. The shift from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound evolution in spatial mapping and foresight.

## From Opportunism to Planning

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was primarily opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized materials found in their immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartzite gathered from stream beds. The "transport distance" was minimal; if a suitable stone was not within a few hundred meters, the tool-making event simply did not occur.

However, as we transition into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian, we see a shift toward "selective procurement." Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials fracture more predictably (conchoidal fracture), allowing for the thinner, sharper edges required for the Mousterian scrapers and points.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with the landscape had changed. Archaeologists have identified "quarry sites" where stone was systematically extracted and then transported several kilometers to habitation sites.

This movement suggests two critical cognitive developments:
1. **Mental Mapping:** The ability to remember the location of a resource that is not currently visible.
2. **Future Planning:** The understanding that a current investment of energy (carrying heavy raw stone) would pay dividends in future efficiency (better tools for butchery).

There remains a scholarly debate regarding the extent of "trade" or social exchange during this period. While some argue that the presence of non-local stone indicates early trade networks between groups, others suggest it is simply the result of wide-ranging migratory patterns.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, creating a curved, glass-like surface essential for sharp edges.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expenditure required to move raw materials, which influenced where prehistoric camps were established.
* **Material Selectivity:** The transition from using "whatever is available" to seeking specific minerals for their superior cutting properties.
* **Lithic Caching:** The practice of leaving stockpiles of raw stone at strategic locations for future use.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian’s Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after one use, Mousterian tools show evidence of "resharpening," indicating they were valued possessions.
* **Geological Luck:** The development of certain tool cultures was often dictated by local geology; groups living in areas without flint had to innovate using softer stones or organic materials like bone.
