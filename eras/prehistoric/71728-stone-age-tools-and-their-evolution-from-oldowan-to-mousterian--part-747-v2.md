# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the acquisition of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in "raw material economy."

The ability to identify, select, and transport high-quality stone over vast distances reveals a profound shift in the prehistoric mind, moving from opportunistic scavenging to strategic planning.

## From Opportunism to Selection (Oldowan)
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), *Homo habilis* largely utilized "expedient" technology. Most tools were crafted from cobbles found in the immediate vicinity, often within a few hundred meters of the site. The selection process was rudimentary; if a stone was hard enough to produce a sharp edge, it was used.

However, as we transition into the Acheulean period, we see the first signs of selectivity. Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of materials with predictable fracturing patterns, such as quartzite or fine-grained basalt, sometimes transporting these materials several kilometers.

## The Strategic Logistics of the Mousterian
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated primarily with Neanderthals), the relationship with the landscape had fundamentally changed. The Levallois technique—a sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape—demanded high-quality flint or chert.

This necessity birthed a primitive "supply chain." Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers frequently traveled tens of kilometers to specific quarries. This suggests two critical cognitive developments: a mental map of the landscape and the ability to plan for future needs (delayed gratification), rather than reacting to immediate requirements.

## The Debate: Trade or Migration?
A point of ongoing scholarly debate is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the site) indicates a formal trade network between different hominin groups or simply the result of wide-ranging migratory patterns. While some argue that the movement of high-quality flint suggests social exchange, others maintain that these materials were simply carried by individuals as they followed seasonal game.

## Key Facts
* **Expedient Tools:** Oldowan tools were typically made from the nearest available stone.
* **Material Preference:** The shift to Acheulean and Mousterian tools saw a preference for cryptocrystalline rocks (like flint) due to their conchoidal fracture.
* **Transport Distance:** While Oldowan sourcing was local, Mousterian sourcing often spanned 10–50 kilometers.
* **Core Preparation:** The Levallois technique required specific material purity to prevent the core from shattering prematurely.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** Sourcing raw materials proves that prehistoric humans possessed advanced spatial memory.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after one use, Mousterian tools were "curated," meaning they were sharpened and reused over long periods.
* **Natural Selection:** Early humans didn't just pick any stone; they sought materials that could withstand high-impact force without crumbling.
* **Geological Markers:** Archaeologists can often trace a specific tool back to a single quarry by analyzing the trace elements in the stone.
