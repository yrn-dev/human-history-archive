# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and flaking techniques that define the Oldowan, Acheulean, and Mousterian industries—we must examine the logistical intelligence behind the stone. The evolution of toolmaking was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological procurement and spatial planning.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality minerals reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their environment and managed time.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized "local" materials—stones found within a few hundred meters of the site. If a riverbed provided basalt or quartz, that is what was used. The cognitive load was low: find a stone, strike it, and use it immediately.

However, as we move into the Acheulean era, we see the emergence of "selective sourcing." Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials, such as fine-grained flint or obsidian, which allowed for the symmetrical precision of the handaxe. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and a willingness to invest energy in transport for the sake of superior performance.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of the Hunt
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required materials with predictable fracturing patterns.

Archaeological evidence indicates that Mousterian toolmakers often transported high-quality stone over distances of 10 to 100 kilometers. This implies a "curated" technology. Rather than making a tool and discarding it, they created versatile, durable blanks that could be resharpened in the field. This shift marks the transition from a "disposable" economy to a "maintained" economy, reflecting a sophisticated understanding of future needs and resource scarcity.

## Scholarly Debate: The "Cost-Benefit" Analysis
There is ongoing debate among lithic analysts regarding the motivation behind long-distance transport. Some scholars argue that the movement of stone was driven purely by functional necessity (the need for a sharper edge). Others suggest a social or symbolic dimension, where specific types of stone—perhaps those with striking colors or rare textures—held prestige or cultural value, indicating the earliest inklings of aesthetic preference.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan:** Primarily relied on local river cobbles and opportunistic sourcing.
* **Acheulean:** Marked by the selection of materials that could support bifacial symmetry.
* **Mousterian:** Characterized by "curated" tools and the transport of materials over significant distances.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were prized for their predictable conchoidal fractures.
* **Logistics:** The shift toward distant sourcing indicates an expanded cognitive map and better planning capabilities.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late Paleolithic sites, obsidian has been found hundreds of miles from its volcanic source, suggesting early networks of exchange.
* **Core Exhaustion:** Archaeologists can tell how "precious" a material was by how much of the core was used; rare stones were flaked until almost nothing remained.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** A curated tool is one designed to be carried and reused, unlike "expedient" tools made for a single task and immediately dropped.
