# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must shift our gaze from the finished tool to the source. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in logistics and geological knowledge.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of stones to the strategic quarrying of high-quality minerals reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their environment and planned for the future.

## From Opportunism to Selection: The Oldowan Shift
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages, toolmakers were largely "opportunistic." They utilized whatever river cobbles were immediately available in their vicinity. If a basalt pebble was within reach, it was knapped. However, as we move deeper into the Lower Paleolithic, we see the emergence of "selective procurement."

Hominins began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of materials with predictable fracture patterns, such as quartzite or chert. This indicates a developing mental map of the landscape; the toolmaker was no longer just reacting to their surroundings but was actively seeking out specific geological deposits, sometimes transporting these materials several kilometers from the source.

## The Levallois Revolution and Resource Management
By the time we reach the Mousterian industry, associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*, the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape—required high-quality, homogenous stone to be successful.

This necessity drove a "logistics of quality." Neanderthals didn't just look for stone; they looked for *specific grades* of flint. This led to the establishment of quarrying sites and the transport of raw materials over vast distances. The ability to carry a "core" (a portable source of stone) allowed these hominins to venture further into unfamiliar territories, knowing they possessed the raw material necessary to create a tool on demand.

## The Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?
A point of ongoing debate among archaeologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the site where they were found) indicates early trade networks between different hominin groups or simply the wide migratory range of a single group. While some argue for a primitive form of exchange, others suggest these materials are simply "curated" tools carried over generations.

## Key Facts
*   **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint or obsidian) break, which is essential for creating sharp edges.
*   **Raw Material Transport:** Oldowan tools were typically found within 2-5 km of their source; Mousterian materials are often found dozens of kilometers away.
*   **Core Preparation:** The Levallois technique shifted the focus from the tool itself to the preparation of the stone block (the core).
*   **Lithic Analysis:** The study of stone tools to determine their origin, a process known as "sourcing."

## Did You Know?
*   **Obsidian Obsession:** Because obsidian (volcanic glass) produces the sharpest edge possible, prehistoric humans would travel immense distances to acquire it, often preferring it over more abundant local stones.
*   **Curated Tools:** Some Mousterian tools show signs of being "resharpened" multiple times, suggesting that the raw material was precious enough to be maintained rather than discarded.
*   **Geological Intuition:** Early hominins developed a "tactile library," knowing exactly how a stone would break simply by its weight and texture before the first strike.
