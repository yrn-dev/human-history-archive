# India

## Geological History and Land Masses
The geological history of the Indian peninsula is characterized by its long-term stability as a land mass. It has been conclusively proved that the peninsula of India has not been beneath the sea since at least the Carboniferous period. The Indian peninsula is considered a fragment of a larger southern land mass known as Gondwanaland, a term used by Neumayr, Suess, and others. 

This southern land mass was separated from a northern land mass, named Angaraland by E. Suess, by an intervening sea. This body of water is referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr. While the peninsula itself remained dry, the area between these two land masses experienced well-developed marine deposits of Mesozoic age and was submerged for the majority of the Mesozoic era.

## Regional Geology of Baluchistan
In the region of Baluchistan, the mountain ranges are primarily composed of Tertiary and Cretaceous beds. These beds are arranged in a series of folds that run approximately parallel to the mountain ridges, forming an extensive system that resembles a south-hanging festoon between Mount Ararat and Peshawar. Within this system, the folds loop upward at Sibi, creating a subsidiary festoon consisting of the Bugti Hills and the Suliman Hills.

The geological composition of the area includes:
* **Marine Deposits:** Most deposits, excluding the later Tertiary beds, are marine in nature.
* **Rock Formations:** Jurassic rocks contribute significantly to the hills of British Baluchistan, and _Fusulina_ limestone and Triassic beds are located along the south side of the upper Zhob.
* **Volcanic Activity:** In lower Tertiary and upper Cretaceous periods, particularly in north-western Baluchistan, there is a significant development of conglomerates and volcanic tuffs. These are likely contemporaneous with the Deccan Traps of India. 

During the Tertiary period, great masses of diorite and syenite were intruded into the region. Additionally, a line of recent volcanic cones, mostly composed of ashes and lavas that are andesitic, extends from eastern Persia into western Baluchistan. While these volcanoes appear extinct in Baluchistan, the Koh-i-Tafdan beyond the Persian frontier continues to emit vapours periodically. Mud "volcanoes" are also present on the Makran coast, though it is uncertain if they are linked to true volcanic agencies.

## Social and Religious History
In the 19th century, India saw the activities of Dayanand, who introduced views that received little acceptance from the Brahmans. Consequently, Dayanand turned his efforts toward the masses and established _Samajes_ in various parts of the country. The first of these was established in Bombay in 1875. 

Dayanand adopted the epithet Arya, choosing it because it was more dignified than the term Hindu, which he considered slightly contemptuous. Following a series of tours involving public debates with Christian missionaries and orthodox pundits, Dayanand died in 1883 at Ajmere.
