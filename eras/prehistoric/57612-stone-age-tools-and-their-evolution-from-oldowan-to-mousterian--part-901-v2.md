# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in how early hominins interacted with their landscape.

The shift from opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound change in spatial awareness and planning.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was primarily opportunistic. *Homo habilis* generally utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity, such as river cobbles. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used; if not, the toolmaker simply moved to the next available source.

However, as we transition into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see the emergence of "raw material economy." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—located kilometers away. This suggests the development of a "mental map" of the environment and the ability to plan for future needs rather than reacting to immediate requirements.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with stone had become highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique required a specific type of stone that could be predictably flaked. This necessity drove a shift toward curated toolkits.

Rather than making a tool and discarding it immediately, Mousterian toolmakers often transported "cores"—large blocks of high-quality stone—over long distances. They would then strike off small, sharp flakes as needed. This transition from "expedient" tools to "curated" tools indicates a significant increase in foresight and a social structure that likely involved the sharing or trading of materials across different groups.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing scholarly debate among archaeologists is whether the presence of non-local stone in a site indicates a primitive form of trade between hominin groups or simply the result of high mobility. While some argue that Neanderthals traded high-quality flint across territories, others suggest these materials were simply carried during seasonal migrations.

### Key Facts: Material Logistics
* **Oldowan Range:** Materials were typically sourced within 1–5 km of the site.
* **Acheulean Expansion:** Evidence shows stone transport increasing to 10–20 km.
* **Mousterian Precision:** The use of "prepared cores" allowed for maximum efficiency of the raw material.
* **Material Preference:** Flint and obsidian were prized for their conchoidal fracture, allowing for razor-sharp edges.
* **Curated Toolkits:** The shift toward carrying cores rather than finished tools reduced weight and increased versatility.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Travel:** In some prehistoric sites, obsidian has been found dozens of kilometers away from the nearest volcanic source, proving intentional long-distance transport.
* **Recycling:** Some Mousterian tools show evidence of being "resharpened" multiple times, indicating that high-quality stone was a precious resource.
* **The "Core" Concept:** A Levallois core acted like a "stone magazine," allowing a hunter to produce several standardized blades from a single piece of rock.
