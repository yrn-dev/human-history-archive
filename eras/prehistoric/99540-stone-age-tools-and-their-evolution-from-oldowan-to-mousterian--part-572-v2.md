# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shift from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we must examine the logistical intelligence behind these tools. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in the understanding of geology and spatial planning.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian reflects a growing "raw material economy." Early hominins did not simply pick up the nearest rock; they developed a sophisticated mental map of their landscape to locate specific minerals that provided the necessary fracture patterns for their needs.

## From Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, often within a few kilometers of the habitation site. The focus was on utility over quality; as long as the stone was hard enough to produce a sharp edge, it was sufficient.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked shift toward selectivity. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials allow for "conchoidal fractures," enabling the toolmaker to predict exactly where a flake would detach.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) demonstrates a sophisticated grasp of resource management. Archaeological evidence shows that high-quality stone was often transported over significant distances—sometimes dozens of kilometers—from the source to the campsite.

This suggests two critical cognitive developments:
1. **Forethought:** The ability to plan for future needs rather than reacting to immediate requirements.
2. **Curation:** The practice of maintaining and reshaping a tool (re-sharpening) to extend its life, rather than discarding it once the edge dulled.

There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport represents the earliest forms of trade between disparate groups or simply the wide-ranging foraging patterns of a single social unit. Regardless, the movement of stone indicates a structured relationship with the environment.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The glass-like break pattern essential for creating sharp edges in flint and obsidian.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of identifying, extracting, and transporting raw stone.
* **Curation:** The act of reworking a tool to preserve its utility, a hallmark of later Stone Age cognitive complexity.
* **Resource Mapping:** The mental ability of prehistoric humans to remember the locations of specific geological deposits across a territory.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Some Mousterian tools show evidence of being carried for weeks, with repeated "touch-ups" to the edge to keep them functional.
* **Geological Knowledge:** By the Mousterian era, hominins were often mining stone from deep underground or quarrying specific seams of flint, showing an advanced understanding of stratigraphy.
