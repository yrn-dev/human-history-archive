# BAGSHOT BEDS

The Bagshot Beds are a geological series consisting of clays and sands of shallow-water origin. These deposits, which include both marine and fresh-water formations, belong to the upper Eocene formation of the Hampshire and London basins in England. The series derives its name from Bagshot Heath in Surrey, though it is also well developed in the Isle of Wight and Hampshire.

## Geological Divisions
The Bagshot Beds are generally categorized into three primary divisions:

*   **Upper Bagshot Beds:** This division includes the Barton clay and Barton sand. In the Isle of Wight, these beds reach a thickness of 140 to 200 ft.
*   **Middle Bagshot Beds:** This division consists of the Bracklesham beds.
*   **Lower Bagshot Beds:** This division comprises the Bournemouth beds, Alum Bay beds, and potentially the Bovey Tracey beds.

The Lower Bagshot division is characterized by loam and pale-yellow, current-bedded sand, featuring occasional beds of flint pebbles and layers of pipeclay. In the Isle of Wight, the lower division is exposed at White Cliff Bay (140 ft.) and Alum Bay (660 ft.), where it consists of clays with ferruginous sandstone and layers of lignite, alongside sands in various shades including crimson, brown, yellow, and white.

## Regional Distribution and Characteristics
In the London basin, the Bagshot beds meet the London clay without a sharp line of junction. Lower Bagshot beds are found at Highbeech, Billericay, and Brentwood in Essex, with outliers at Harrow, Highgate, and Hampstead. In Surrey, heath-bearing Lower Bagshot beds cover tracts of London clay in areas such as Woking, Aldershot, and Weybridge. The "Ramsdell clay" located northwest of Basingstoke is also attributed to this formation.

In the Hampshire basin, the Bagshot series—along with its associated gravel and sand—occupies much of the Eocene area. The Agglestone Rock near Studland is identified as a hard mass of the Bagshot formation. Similar beds to those found in the Isle of Wight are visible near Studland, Corfe, Wareham, Bournemouth, and Poole.

The Bracklesham beds vary in thickness by location: they are 20 to 50 ft. thick in Berkshire and Surrey, and 100 ft. thick in Alum Bay. In Alum Bay, the lower portion of the Bracklesham beds contains lignite and is separated from the Barton clay by a bed of flint pebble conglomerate.

## The Barton Beds
The Barton beds are a series of Upper Eocene brown and grey softish clays with sand layers. They are found in the Hampshire Tertiary basin, with notable exposures in the Isle of Wight and the cliffs of Hordwell and Barton. The Barton beds are divided into the highly fossiliferous Barton Clay and the overlying Barton Sands (also known as Headon Hill sands), which contain few fossils. 

While the term "Upper Bagshot Beds" has been applied to these sands, the names Barton Sands or Headon Hill are considered preferable. The Barton beds are absent from the London basin; the Upper Bagshot Sands in that region likely exist at a lower horizon than the Barton Sands. For continental equivalents of this series in the Paris Basin and Hampshire basin, the term "Bartonien" was introduced by Mayer-Eymar in 1857.

## Paleontology and Economic Use
The leaf-bearing clays found in Bournemouth and Alum Bay are noted for a significant series of plant remains. These include *Populus*, *Platanus*, *Sequoia*, *Eucalyptus*, *Aralia*, *Caesalpinia*, *Osmunda*, *Polypodium*, and *Nipadites*.

The Bagshot formation has provided several materials for industrial use:
*   **Pottery:** Clays from the Wareham district are world-renowned for pottery manufacture. These are mined near Corfe and Wareham and shipped via Poole, leading to the name "Poole clays." Similarly, "Teignmouth clay" is sourced from the Bovey beds.
*   **Fuel:** Lignites have been utilized as fuel at Bovey and near Corfe.
*   **Chemicals:** Alum was previously extracted from the clays of Alum Bay.
