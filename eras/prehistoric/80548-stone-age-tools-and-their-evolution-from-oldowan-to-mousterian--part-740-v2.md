# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian technology represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding where the stone came from provides a window into the mobility patterns and social organization of early humans.

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), *Homo habilis* and early *Homo erectus* generally utilized materials found in their immediate vicinity. If a riverbed provided basalt or quartz pebbles, those were used. The "cost" of transporting heavy raw materials over long distances was too high, or the cognitive map of the landscape was too limited to justify it.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a distinct change emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials break with a predictable conchoidal fracture, allowing for the precision required to create the refined scrapers and points characteristic of Neanderthal toolkits.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian industry (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago) reveals a sophisticated understanding of resource management. Archaeological evidence shows that Neanderthals often transported high-quality stone over several kilometers—and in some cases, tens of kilometers—from the original source to the habitation site.

This suggests two critical behavioral shifts:
1. **Planning Depth:** The ability to anticipate future needs by collecting "cores" (large blocks of stone) to be worked later.
2. **Social Networking:** There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were moved by individuals during seasonal migrations or exchanged between different social groups, hinting at the earliest forms of trade.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution

*   **Oldowan Materials:** Primarily local river cobbles, basalt, and quartz.
*   **Acheulean Shift:** Increased preference for materials that could support "bifacial" flaking (working both sides).
*   **Mousterian Precision:** Heavy reliance on flint and obsidian for the Levallois technique.
*   **Transport Distance:** While Oldowan tools are usually found within meters of the source, Mousterian tools are often found kilometers away.
*   **Curation:** Later Paleolithic humans "curated" their tools, sharpening and reusing them rather than discarding them immediately.

## Did You Know?

*   **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian is volcanic glass, it can be flaked to an edge thinner and sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
*   **The "Core" Strategy:** To save energy, Mousterian toolmakers often prepared a "core" at the quarry and transported the bulky block, rather than carrying dozens of finished, fragile tools.
*   **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use "X-ray fluorescence" to trace the chemical signature of a stone tool back to the exact quarry where the rock originated.
