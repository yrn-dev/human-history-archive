# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and Transport in the Paleolithic

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must consider the logistical intelligence behind the stone. A tool is not merely a product of knapping skill; it is the result of a complex decision-making process regarding raw material procurement and the economics of transport.

The evolution from the Oldowan to the Mousterian is not just a story of better shapes, but a story of expanding mental maps. The ability to identify, source, and transport high-quality stone over vast distances reveals a profound shift in cognitive planning and social organization.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), hominins were largely opportunistic. Most tools were crafted from cobbles found in immediate proximity to the site, often sourced from nearby stream beds. The "transport distance" was minimal, suggesting a localized existence where the need for a tool was met by the nearest available rock.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian traditions, a shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective procurement." They no longer settled for any hard stone; they sought out specific materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—that offered predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). This indicates a developed understanding of geology and a willingness to bypass inferior materials in favor of superior ones.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the Mousterian period (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. Evidence shows that Neanderthals often transported high-quality stone over dozens of kilometers. This implies "curation"—the practice of keeping a tool or a core for a long period, sharpening it repeatedly rather than discarding it.

This transition suggests a shift in temporal perception. An Oldowan tool-maker lived in the "now," using what was at hand. A Mousterian tool-maker planned for the "future," carrying a curated core of flint because they knew the landscape they were entering lacked quality stone.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Chance

There remains a scholarly debate regarding the "transport" of stones in early sites. Some archaeologists argue that the presence of non-local stone in Oldowan sites may be the result of natural fluvial transport (water moving stones downstream) rather than intentional hominin carrying. However, the increasing distance and specificity of materials in later eras make the argument for intentional procurement nearly indisputable.

### Key Facts: Material Logistics
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, utilizing local river cobbles.
* **Material Preference:** Shift toward cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for sharper, more durable edges.
* **Curation:** The practice of maintaining and transporting a tool over long distances rather than making a new one on-site.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The ability to remember the location of a quarry miles away from a living site.
* **Mousterian Range:** Evidence of raw material transport extending significantly further than in previous eras.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In later prehistoric periods, obsidian was so prized for its sharpness that it became one of the first "trade" goods, moving hundreds of miles from volcanic sources.
* **The "Core" Concept:** Mousterian tool-makers used the Levallois technique, which required preparing a stone core specifically to strike off a single, perfectly shaped flake.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians determine where tools came from by analyzing "debitage" (the waste flakes), which helps map the ancient movement of prehistoric populations.
