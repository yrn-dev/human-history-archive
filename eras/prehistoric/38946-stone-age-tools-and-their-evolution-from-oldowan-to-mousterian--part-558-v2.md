# The Raw Material Economy: Lithic Procurement and Transport

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in *procurement*. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a matter of shaping stone, but of understanding the landscape, predicting resource availability, and managing the economy of raw materials.

The transition from the opportunistic gathering of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone marks a fundamental shift in prehistoric cognition and social organization.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 million years ago), hominins largely utilized "river cobbles"—stones found immediately adjacent to their living sites. The selection process was rudimentary; if a stone was hard enough to produce a sharp edge, it was used. There was little evidence of transporting materials over long distances, suggesting a highly localized existence.

However, as we move into the Acheulean period, a pattern of "selective procurement" emerges. *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials like quartzite or fine-grained volcanic rocks. This indicates a mental map of the environment and the ability to plan for future needs, rather than reacting to immediate requirements.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Leap
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality flint or chert to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported these materials over dozens of kilometers. This implies a "curation" strategy: tools were no longer disposable items discarded after one use, but valuable assets to be maintained, reshaped, and carried. The effort expended in transporting stone suggests that the efficiency of a high-quality tool outweighed the caloric cost of the journey to acquire it.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Availability
A point of ongoing debate among lithic analysts is whether the movement of stone was a result of intentional "trading" or simply the byproduct of migratory patterns. Some scholars argue that the presence of non-local stone in Mousterian sites suggests early social networks and exchange between groups, while others maintain that these materials were simply picked up during seasonal migrations.

### Key Facts: Lithic Procurement
* **Oldowan:** Characterized by "expedient technology," using materials found within a few hundred meters of the site.
* **Acheulean:** Marked by the transition to "selective procurement," favoring materials with predictable fracturing patterns.
* **Mousterian:** Involved "curated technology," where high-quality stone was transported over long distances.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were the gold standard due to their conchoidal fracture properties.
* **Cognitive Shift:** The move from local to distant sourcing indicates an evolution in spatial memory and foresight.

### Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Some Mousterian tools show evidence of being resharpened multiple times, proving they were kept for weeks or months.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Prehistoric humans didn't just find stones; they identified specific geological strata (layers) where the best flint occurred.
* **The Cost of Quality:** In some regions, Neanderthals traveled over 50 kilometers to reach a specific quarry, ignoring closer but inferior stone sources.
