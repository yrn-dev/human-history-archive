# Hornblende Schists and Metamorphic Limestone

Hornblende schists and metamorphic limestones are types of rocks found in various geological formations across the world, often associated with intense pressure, heat, and the presence of intrusive igneous bodies.

## Hornblende Schists and Epidiorites
Hornblende schists are metamorphic rocks that are sometimes used synonymously with "amphibolite" or "epidiorite." Epidiorites are a family of rocks consisting essentially of felspar and hornblende, frequently containing quartz, sphene, biotite, garnet, or epidote. These rocks typically possess a foliated structure and are produced from basic rocks such as gabbro, dolerite, and diabase.

In microscopic sections, the hornblende in these rocks is usually dark green, though it is rarely brownish. The felspar may be recrystallized and clear, or it may appear as a turbid aggregate of albite, sericite, quartz, zoisite, and epidote. These rocks are characterized by a prevalent dark green color and weather with brown surfaces. They can range in grain size from being so fine that constituents are invisible to the unaided eye to containing hornblende crystals an inch in length. Due to their hardness and toughness when fresh, they are suitable for use as road-mending stones. Additionally, epidiorites and their associated quartz veins have been found to be auriferous in many parts of the world.

Hornblende schists appear in several global regions:
* **Scotland and Ireland:** They are part of the Dalradian series, a complicated set of metamorphic rocks found in the high ground east and south of Scotland's Great Glen and traced into north-western Ireland. In these areas, they may mark beds of tuff, lava-sheets, or sills intercalated among sediments. In the southern mass near Pettigo, residual "eyes" of hornblendic rocks associated with the Dalradian series remain within the gneiss.
* **Other Regions:** They occur among the gneisses, slates, and schists of the Alps, Brittany, the Harz, and the crystalline ranges of eastern North America.
* **Africa:** In the districts of Ukamba, Nandi, and Taru, hornblende schists are noted as being rare within the prevailing Archaean rocks.

## Metamorphic Limestones and Marbles
Metamorphic limestones are produced through the agencies of pressure and heat. Many marbles are metamorphosed limestones; this process often occurs as fossil-rich limestones approach great crystalline intrusions of granite. The heat from the granite and the pressure from overlying rock masses induce this change. Experimental evidence from 1806 showed that heating chalk in a plugged gun-barrel creates carbonic acid and high pressure, converting the mass into granular crystalline marble.

In regions where sedimentary rocks have been converted into granulites, gneisses, or schists, limestones are represented by marbles, cipollins, and calc schists. These rocks are often schistose or banded, showing deformation and crushing of crystals caused by earth pressures during rock-folding. Marbles are most common in the deepest and oldest layers of the earth's crust, typically dating to the pre-Cambrian or Palaeozoic eras. They frequently occur alongside phyllites and mica schists, which originated as beds of clay alternating with the original limestone.

## Associated Geological Formations
Hornblende schists and metamorphic limestones are often found in conjunction with other complex rock assemblages:

**The Dalradian Series**
This series consists of altered sedimentary strata including graphite-schist, mica-schist, andalusite-schist, phyllite, quartzite, and limestone. It also includes chlorite-schists and hornblende schists. The total thickness of this assemblage is estimated to be many thousand feet.

**Greywackes**
Greywackes are impure, gritty, composite rocks of the Palaeozoic systems. They may occur in beds alongside limestones and slates. These rocks contain a variety of minerals, including hornblende, augite, garnet, and chlorite. Through increasing metamorphism, greywackes frequently transition into sedimentary gneisses, chloride schists, and mica-schists.

**Epidosites**
Epidosites are metamorphic rocks composed mainly of quartz and epidote. Some epidosites are derived from limestones that have undergone contact-alteration and may contain minerals such as garnet, scapolite, augite, and calcite. Other epidosites may appear as nodules, veins, or bands within masses of hornblende-schist and epidiorite.

## Regional Occurrences
* **Banffshire:** The geology includes crystalline schists of sedimentary origin. These comprise belts of phyllites, black schists, and slates with thin black limestone (sometimes containing tremolite), as well as a "main limestone" and quartzite.
* **Africa (Archaean districts):** In the districts of Taru, Nandi, and Ukamba, coarse gneiss predominates, associated with intrusive granite and garnetiferous mica-schists. In these specific regions, beds of metamorphic limestone and hornblende schists are rare. Cherty quartzites interbedded with mylonites are also found on the flanks of the Nandi hills.
