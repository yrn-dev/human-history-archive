# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian point—we must shift our perspective to the *geology* of the prehistoric landscape. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a sophisticated evolution in resource management and spatial planning.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents more than just a change in "knapping" technique; it marks a fundamental shift in how early hominins interacted with their environment and perceived the value of distance.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized "river cobbles"—stones found in immediate proximity to where the tool was needed. The distance between the raw material source and the site of use was often negligible, suggesting a "find-and-discard" mentality.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we observe a dramatic increase in "raw material transport." Hominins began to bypass readily available local stones in favor of higher-quality materials, such as fine-grained flint, chert, or obsidian, located kilometers away. This indicates a mental map of the landscape and a capacity for future planning; the tool-maker was no longer reacting to the present moment but preparing for a future need.

## The Logistics of the Levallois Technique

The Mousterian industry, closely associated with *Homo neanderthalensis*, utilized the Levallois technique—a complex method of preparing a core to strike off a predictable, pre-shaped flake. This technique demanded a specific quality of stone to ensure the flake did not shatter prematurely.

The insistence on specific materials led to the creation of informal "quarrying" sites. The logistical effort required to transport these heavy cores across territories suggests a social structure capable of coordinating movement and perhaps even the early exchange of materials between different kinship groups. Scholars continue to debate whether this constitutes a formal "trade network" or simply overlapping territorial foraging, but the evidence of non-local stone is undeniable.

## Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, utilizing local riverbed pebbles and basalt.
* **Transport Distance:** Increased from a few hundred meters in the Oldowan to tens of kilometers in the Mousterian.
* **Material Preference:** A shift toward cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) which allow for sharper, more predictable edges.
* **Cognitive Load:** The shift to distant sourcing proves "executive function"—the ability to plan for a goal that is not immediately present.
* **Waste Analysis:** The presence of "debitage" (stone chips) at home sites proves that tools were transported as cores and finished on-site.

## Did You Know?
* **The Obsidian Trail:** In some prehistoric regions, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers from its volcanic source, suggesting an incredible level of mobility or early social exchange.
* **Curated Tools:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often abandoned after one use, Mousterian tools show signs of "resharpening," meaning they were valued assets kept for long periods.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early hominins had to possess an intuitive understanding of geology to identify which rocks would fracture conchoidally (like glass) and which would simply crumble.
