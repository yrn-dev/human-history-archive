# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the *logistics* of the Stone Age. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a fundamental shift in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The transition from opportunistic scavenging of stones to the strategic sourcing of high-quality minerals reveals a growing capacity for foresight and spatial mapping.

## From Opportunism to Selection: Oldowan and Acheulean
In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized "river cobbles"—stones already rounded and weathered by water—found in the immediate vicinity of their campsites. The distance between the raw material source and the tool's discard point was typically minimal, suggesting a "use-and-discard" mentality.

As we move into the Acheulean era, characterized by the iconic handaxe, a shift occurs. *Homo erectus* began to show a preference for specific materials, such as fine-grained volcanic rocks or flint, which allowed for more precise flaking. Archaeological evidence shows that these materials were occasionally transported several kilometers from their source, indicating that hominins were beginning to plan their toolkits in advance of their needs.

## The Strategic Logistics of the Mousterian
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a predictable, pre-shaped flake—required high-quality stone to be successful. 

This necessity drove a "curated" economy. Rather than picking up any available stone, Mousterian toolmakers sought out specific quarries. Some sites show evidence of raw materials being transported over 10 to 50 kilometers. This implies not only a mental map of the landscape but also a social structure capable of transporting heavy loads of stone across diverse terrains.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Availability
A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the transport of high-quality stone was a conscious "choice" based on aesthetic or functional preference, or simply a result of wider migratory patterns. Some scholars argue that the movement of stone was a byproduct of seasonal foraging, while others suggest it represents a deliberate economic strategy to ensure survival in resource-poor areas.

### Key Facts: Material Logistics
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily local; materials were gathered within a few hundred meters of the site.
* **Material Preference:** Transitioned from general basalt/quartz to specialized flint, chert, and obsidian.
* **The Levallois Requirement:** The Mousterian technique demanded high-quality, isotropic stone to prevent unpredictable fracturing.
* **Transport Distance:** Increased from negligible distances in the Early Stone Age to tens of kilometers in the Middle Paleolithic.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Shift from "expedient tools" (made for one task) to "curated tools" (maintained and carried over time).

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late prehistoric contexts, obsidian (volcanic glass) has been found far from any volcanic source, suggesting early forms of trade or long-distance migration.
* **Core Exhaustion:** Mousterian toolmakers were so efficient that they would often flake a single core until it was completely exhausted before discarding it.
* **The "River Effect":** Many Oldowan sites are located near ancient riverbeds because the water did the hard work of collecting and smoothing the stones for the hominins.
