# Palaeozoic Era

The Palaeozoic era is one of the three great eras of geological time, alongside the Mesozoic and Cainozoic (or Cenozoic) eras. It is characterized by the formation of ancient land masses and mountain chains across several continents, including Asia, Europe, and South America.

## Geological History of Asia
The early geological history of Asia indicates that during the Ordovician and Cambrian periods, a large portion of the continent was covered by the sea. However, evidence shows that much of eastern and northern Asia has remained land since the Palaeozoic era. In Siberia, specifically the area between the Lena and Yenisei rivers, folded Archean rocks are overlaid by Cambrian and Ordovician beds. In the extreme north, there is no evidence that this region has been beneath the sea since the early part of the Palaeozoic era, except for the discovery of marine Cretaceous and Jurassic fossils.

A broad area stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the south of China contains no marine deposits later than the Carboniferous period, except in the extreme north. This suggests that the majority of this region has been land since the close of the Palaeozoic era. In the Chinese provinces of Shensi and Shansi, the geological sequence consists of folded Archean rocks overlaid by nearly horizontal Lower Palaeozoic strata, followed by marine beds from the Carboniferous period. From the Upper Carboniferous onward, no marine deposits are known in this area.

## The Indian Peninsula and Gondwanaland
It has been conclusively proved that the peninsula of India has not been beneath the sea since at least the Carboniferous period. This is evidenced by the presence of the Gondwana series—terrestrial and freshwater deposits representing the geological scale from the top of the Carboniferous to the top of the Jurassic—and the total absence of marine fossils, except near the borders of the peninsula.

The Indian peninsula is considered a fragment of a larger southern land mass called Gondwanaland (named by Neumayr, Suess, and others). This great land mass probably extended across the Indian Ocean and was once united with the south of Africa. In contrast, the northern land mass of Asia was named Angaraland by E. Suess. Between these two masses lay a sea referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr.

## Palaeozoic Formations in Europe and South America
In Europe, the "Hercynian chain" (as named by Marcel Bertrand) is composed entirely of Palaeozoic rocks. This ancient mountain chain extends through the Ardennes in Belgium and into Germany, forming the Taunus, Hunsruck, Westerwald, and Eifel. In the Ardennes, the rocks are primarily of the Devonian System, though Cambrian beds rise through them. The final elevation of this chain occurred toward the close of the Carboniferous period, with the latest beds involved in the folds belonging to the Coal Measures.

In South America, the eastern ranges of the Bolivian Andes are formed of Palaeozoic rocks with other intrusions and granitic ones. These beds have yielded fossils from the Carboniferous, Devonian, Ordovician, and Cambrian ages. In the northern part of these ranges, the Carboniferous and Devonian are extensively developed, while the Cambrian and Ordovician form the greater part of the eastern Andes in southern Bolivia. The great summits of the eastern range, such as Sorata and Illimani, are composed of these Palaeozoic rocks.

## Structural Characteristics
The structural nature of Palaeozoic regions varies by location:
* **Asia:** In Syria, Arabia, and the peninsula of India, only the oldest rocks are folded; the Mesozoic, Tertiary, and Upper Palaeozoic beds lie almost horizontally. In northern China, Mongolia, and Siberia, the region is generally free from important Mesozoic or Tertiary folding, and many mountain ranges owe their elevation to faulting rather than folding, as the folds are mostly Palaeozoic or Archaean.
* **Belgium:** The Palaeozoic area is marked by a long, narrow trough along the northern border of the Ardennes containing the coalfields of Charleroi, Mons, and Liege. This trough is separated from the older Ardennes rocks by a great reversed fault known as the *faille du midi*.
