# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the "where" and "how" of the raw materials. The evolution of tool technology was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in geological prospecting and logistics.

The ability to identify, transport, and curate specific types of stone marks a critical shift in prehistoric behavioral complexity. A tool is only as effective as the mineral from which it is knapped, and the movement of these materials reveals the expanding mental maps of early hominins.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Early *Homo* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used; if not, the tool-making ceased. The "cost" of transport was kept to a minimum.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, we see a shift toward selective procurement. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint or chert, which allow for more predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture). This suggests a developed understanding of mineralogy and a willingness to invest energy in transporting materials over several kilometers.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the Mousterian era (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became strategic. The implementation of the Levallois technique—a sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-grade stone to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian tool-makers often curated their materials. Rather than discarding a tool once it became dull, they reworked the edges or transported "blank" cores to different sites. This indicates a concept of "future-planning" and a recognition of the scarcity of prime materials. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport constitutes the earliest form of trade or simply a result of wider nomadic ranging patterns.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The key physical property of stones like flint and obsidian that allows them to break into sharp, predictable edges.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan materials are typically found within 1–2 km of their source; Mousterian materials are often found 10–20 km (or more) away.
* **Core Preparation:** The Levallois technique shifted the focus from the tool itself to the *preparation of the stone core*.
* **Material Hierarchy:** Prehistoric humans prioritized materials based on "workability," preferring flint over quartzite or basalt when available.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Highways:** In some regions, volcanic obsidian was transported hundreds of kilometers, suggesting complex social networks or highly migratory patterns.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Some Mousterian scrapers show evidence of being sharpened dozens of times, proving that a high-quality piece of stone was a prized possession.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans didn't just find stones; they often dug into the earth to mine nodules of flint, showing an awareness of where materials were hidden beneath the surface.
