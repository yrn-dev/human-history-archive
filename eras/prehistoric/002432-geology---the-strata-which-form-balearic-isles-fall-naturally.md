# Geology of the Balearic Isles and Related Geological Studies

## Stratigraphy of the Balearic Isles
The geological strata of the Balearic Isles are categorized into two primary divisions. The older series consists of beds ranging from the Devonian to the Cretaceous. This series is characterized by folding and faulting, and it constitutes all the higher hills of the archipelago. The direction of these folds varies by island: in Minorca, they run south to north; in Majorca, they run south-west to north-east; and in Iviza, they run nearly west to east. Together, these folds form an arc that is convex toward the south-east.

Within this older series, the Devonian system is visible only in Minorca; in the other islands, the Trias is the oldest system represented. The higher portion of the Cretaceous is absent, and it is believed that the principal folding of the older beds occurred during the Cretaceous period.

The newer series is of Tertiary age. These beds lie nearly horizontal and rest unconformably upon the older strata. The Eocene beds are nummulitic. While a lacustrine group was typically placed in the Lower Eocene, the presence of *Anthracotherium magnum* in interbedded lignites indicates that it is, at least in part, Oligocene. Additionally, Pliocene beds occur, and the Miocene includes a limestone containing *Clypeaster*.

## Geological Terminology and Structures
In the field of geology, a "basin" is defined as a structural feature of the bed rock equivalent to a broad shallow syncline, characterized by a centroclinal dip. This is distinct from a physiographic river basin, though the two may occasionally coincide. Examples of geological basins in England include the Hampshire basin and the London basin—both consisting of Jurassic, Cretaceous, and Tertiary rocks—as well as various coal basins such as the Forest of Dean, the N. Staffordshire coalfield, and the S. Wales coalfield. The Paris basin is composed of strata similar to those found in the London and Hampshire basins.

Conversely, "rock-basins" are smaller, steep-sided depressions scooped out of solid rock in mountainous regions, primarily through the action of glaciers. Other lake basins may be formed by crustal movements or the solution of soluble constituents in underlying strata, such as rock salt.

## Regional Geological Features
Various specific strata and formations are noted across different regions:
*   **England:** In Dorsetshire, the Puckstone and Agglestone (or Haggerstone) rocks near Studland, as well as Creechbarrow near Corfe, are formed from large indurated masses of Lower Bagshot beds. These strata are also the source of many greywethers or sarsen stones in South-East England. In the Isle of Wight and Hampshire, the Bembridge beds are part of the Oligocene fluvio-marine series. These consist of Bembridge limestone (a freshwater pool deposit containing *Chara* and snails such as *Helices, Amphidromus, Limnaea,* and *Planorbis*) topped by Bembridge marls (freshwater, marine, and estuarine clays containing *Unio, Limnaea, Melanopsis, Ostrea,* and *Meretrix*).
*   **Ireland:** In certain counties, the land is formed largely of grey Carboniferous limestone, as seen around Clane and at the Salmon Falls at Leixlip. Higher country to the south-east consists of Silurian shales, while the Leinster chain features a granite core with a margin of mica-schist. Other Silurian rocks, including basic lavas and some Old Red Sandstone, rise north of Kildare town.
*   **India:** In the Kohat district of the North-West Frontier Province, the Bahadur Khel salt-mine features an exposed mass of rock-salt extending 4 miles in length and a quarter of a mile in breadth.

## History of Geological Science
The systematic study of geology evolved from earlier observations. Aristotle recognized broad geological principles, and Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519) provided a lucid interpretation of fossils. Later, Descartes (1596-1650) speculated on the incandescent beginnings of the globe, and Steno (1631-87) described strata and collected fossils.

By the end of the eighteenth century, geology began to challenge the Bible's version of the Sumerian narrative. The French naturalist Buffon, in his 1778 work *Epochs of Nature*, extended the age of the world to 70,000 or 75,000 years, dividing this time into six figurative "days" or ages to align with the Creation story.

In the nineteenth century, Lyell published *Elements of Geology* (1838), which became a standard for palaeontological and stratigraphical geology. In *The Antiquity of Man* (1863), Lyell argued for the early appearance of humans based on flint implements found in post-Pliocene strata in the Somme valley and discussed Glacial epoch deposits. Additionally, Jules Pierre François Stanislas Desnoyers (1800-1887), a founder of the Société Géologique de France, contributed significant memoirs on the Tertiary, Cretaceous, and Jurassic strata of Northern France and the Paris Basin.
