# The Scotland Series of Barbados

The Scotland series represents the oldest rocks of Barbados. These formations are characterized as being of shallow water origin and consist of sandy clays, brown sandstones, and coarse grits. In certain areas, these beds are traversed by veins of manjak—a species of asphalt—and are in places saturated with petroleum.

## Geological Classification and Dating
The Scotland series is believed to belong to the Tertiary system. However, determining the precise homotaxis of the various formations is difficult due to a lack of characteristic fossils. Consequently, correlations regarding the age of these beds rest upon imperfect evidence.

A. J. Jukes-Browne and J. B. Harrison have ascribed the Scotland beds to either the Oligocene or the Eocene period. Their broader correlations for the island's geology include:
* **The Oceanic series:** Ascribed to the Miocene.
* **The Bissex Hill marls:** Ascribed to the Pliocene.
* **The coral limestones:** Ascribed partly to the Pliocene and partly to the Pleistocene.

## Physical Characteristics and Composition
The Scotland series has undergone processes of denudation and folding, which have resulted in the series forming the foundation upon which the island's later beds rest. The district of Scotland derives its name from a fancied resemblance to the Highlands of North Britain.

Beyond the manjak exported from the region, the district contains clays suitable for brick-making and sandstone.

## Stratigraphic Relationship to Other Formations
The Scotland beds serve as the base for subsequent geological layers. The Oceanic series lies upon the denuded edges of the Scotland beds. The Oceanic series is composed of red clay, siliceous earths, chalky limestones, and a top layer of mudstone consisting primarily of volcanic dust. Unlike the shallow water origin of the Scotland series, the Oceanic series was laid down in deep waters, containing Radiolaria, sponge spicules, diatoms, and Foraminifera such as Globigerina.

The Oceanic series is generally overlaid unconformably by coral limestones, though at Bissex Hill, a Globigerina marl rests unconformably upon the Oceanic series at the base of the coral limestones. The Coral Limestone series, while not of great thickness, covers six-sevenths of the island and reaches heights of nearly 1100 ft.
