# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of the Stone Age

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must examine the critical precursor to production: the procurement of raw materials. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped, and the transition between tool industries reflects not just a change in cognitive ability, but a sophisticated evolution in geological knowledge and logistics.

For the early hominins of the Oldowan, the relationship with stone was opportunistic. However, as we move toward the Mousterian, we see a shift from "using what is available" to "seeking what is optimal."

## From Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), raw material procurement was largely localized. Hominins typically utilized river cobbles or surface stones found within a few kilometers of their habitation sites. The focus was on accessibility; as long as the stone—often basalt or quartzite—could produce a sharp edge through percussion, it was sufficient.

By the time of the Acheulean and subsequent Mousterian industries, the spatial logic changed. Evidence suggests that *Homo heidelbergensis* and *Homo neanderthalensis* began to travel significantly further to acquire specific types of stone, such as high-quality flint or obsidian. This indicates a mental map of the landscape and a "future-oriented" mindset: the understanding that a specific geological vein miles away would yield a superior tool than the rubble at their feet.

## The Economics of Transport and Curation

The Mousterian industry (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago) is characterized by the Levallois technique, which required a high degree of predictability in how the stone would fracture. This necessity drove a shift toward "curated" toolkits. Rather than discarding a tool after a single use, Neanderthals often reworked and reshaped their tools, extending the life of the expensive, transported raw material.

There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding the extent of "trade" or social exchange of these materials. While some archaeologists argue that the presence of non-local stone suggests organized trade networks between groups, others suggest it is simply the result of wide-ranging migratory patterns. Regardless, the movement of stone proves that the evolution of the tool was inextricably linked to the evolution of the journey.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, relying on local riverbed cobbles.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were preferred for later industries due to their predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **Transport Distance:** While Oldowan materials were found within 1–5 km of sites, Mousterian materials are often found transported over 20–50 km.
* **Curation:** The practice of sharpening and reusing a tool to conserve rare raw materials.
* **Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Core" Logic:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" (the parent stone) was often treated as a precious resource, carefully shaped to maximize the number of flakes produced.
* **Geological Literacy:** Prehistoric humans possessed an intuitive understanding of mineralogy, knowing exactly which stones would shatter and which would flake.
