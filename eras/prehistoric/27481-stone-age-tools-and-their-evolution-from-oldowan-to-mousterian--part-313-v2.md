# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a shift in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

For the early toolmaker, the world was not a uniform expanse of rock, but a map of high-quality "nodes." The ability to identify, transport, and curate specific materials reveals a deepening complexity in spatial memory and forward planning.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity to their living sites, often scavenging riverbeds for basalt or quartz. The "transport distance" was minimal; if a suitable stone was available within a few hundred meters, it was used.

As we transition into the Acheulean period, associated with *Homo erectus*, we see the first signs of selective sourcing. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of materials that held a sharper edge or fractured more predictably. This suggests a mental "blueprint" of the environment, where the toolmaker knew that a superior source of chert or obsidian lay several kilometers away.

## The Mousterian and the Logic of Curation

By the time of the Mousterian industry (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago), primarily attributed to Neanderthals, the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality stone to be successful.

This necessity led to the emergence of "curated" toolkits. Rather than discarding a tool after one use, Mousterian hunters often carried "blanks" or pre-forms over vast distances. Some archaeological sites show evidence of raw materials transported over 50 to 100 kilometers. This indicates a shift from a "reactive" economy (using what is there) to a "proactive" economy (carrying what is needed).

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the presence of non-local stone at a site indicates a complex social trade network between different hominin groups or simply the wide migratory range of a single family unit. While the "trade" hypothesis is compelling, many scholars argue that the evidence currently supports high mobility rather than formal economic exchange.

### Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic; materials sourced from immediate riverine environments.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; evidence of transporting materials over several kilometers.
* **Mousterian:** High-level curation; use of "cores" to maximize the utility of a single piece of stone.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were prized for their predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **Cognitive Leap:** The transition from local to distant sourcing indicates an expansion in working memory and spatial mapping.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian (volcanic glass) creates an edge thinner than a steel scalpel, it is one of the most tracked materials in prehistoric archaeology to map ancient movement.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** Mousterian toolmakers could produce multiple specialized tools from a single prepared core, drastically reducing the amount of raw stone they needed to carry.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians identify "knapping floors" by looking for "debitage"—the tiny, useless stone chips left behind during the shaping process.
