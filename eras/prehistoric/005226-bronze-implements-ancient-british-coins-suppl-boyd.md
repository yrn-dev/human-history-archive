# Bronze Implements and Ancient British Coins

The study of ancient implements and coinage in Britain and across the globe involves the analysis of metallurgical transitions, specifically the movement from stone to copper, bronze, and eventually iron. Key scholarly contributions to this field include the works of Sir John Evans, who authored *Ancient Bronze Implements of Great Britain* (1881), *Ancient Stone Implements of Great Britain* (1897), and *Ancient British Coins* (with supplement). Other notable researchers include Boyd Dawkins, who wrote *Early Man in Britain* (1880) and *Cave-hunting and Early Man in Britain*.

## The Bronze Age and Metallurgical Development
The Bronze Age is defined by archaeologists as a stage of human culture intermediate between the Stone and Iron Ages. During this period, implements, utensils, and weapons were generally manufactured from bronze. While most authorities fix the commencement of the Bronze Age in Europe between 2000 B.C. and 1800 B.C., the period lacks absolute chronological value as it varied by region. In some areas, such as Mexico and Peru, the bronze age persisted into recent times.

The transition to bronze began with the use of copper. Approximately 6,000 or 7,000 years ago in Europe, Neolithic people produced copper implements following the patterns of their stone tools, casting them in moulds or hammering native copper. The discovery of smelting may have occurred accidentally when copper ore was placed among stones in cooking fire pits. Bronze, an alloy of copper and tin, was likely discovered because copper ore and tinstone often occur in the same veins in regions such as Cornwall, Hungary, and China. Bronze is superior to copper because it is harder, more fusible, and easier to reduce.

## Characteristics of Bronze Implements
In Europe, the Bronze Age is characterized by a distinct design and size of tools, including hammers, knives, gouges, awls, daggers, arrow-heads, and swords. The uniformity of these patterns across different parts of Europe has led some to argue that the knowledge of bronze originated in the East, noting that the arms are distinctly oriental and the handles of daggers and swords are too narrow for the large-handed races of Europe.

In Japan, bronze vestiges—including halberds, swords, and daggers—have been found in twelve provinces of southern and western Japan, including northwest Kyushu and the islands of Tsushima and Iki. Some of these items, such as those with unpierced loops or overshort tangs, suggest they were used for ceremonial purposes rather than battle.

## Ancient Coinage and Alloys
The composition of bronze in classical antiquity varied based on the intended purpose and the era. It primarily consisted of copper (ranging from 67% to 95%) alloyed with silver, lead, zinc, or tin. 

Analysis of Greek coins shows a reliance on a copper and tin alloy until 400 B.C., after which lead was used with increasing frequency; silver was rarely present. The Romans also utilized lead in their bronze coins, though they transitioned to pure copper coins under the reigns of Vespasian, Nero, Domitian, and Caligula before returning to lead mixtures.

## Archaeological Context and Burial Practices
The Bronze Age is marked by a shift in funerary customs; cremation became the standard mode of disposal for the dead, whereas burial was the rule during the Stone Age. Barrows and sepulchral mounds from the Bronze Age are generally smaller and less imposing than those of the Stone Age. These mounds often served as family or tribal cemeteries; for example, a single mound in Denmark contained seventy deposits of burnt bones.

Common finds in these barrows include:
*   Cinerary urns (typically 12 to 18 inches in height) containing burnt bones.
*   Vases of elegant form made of bronze and gold.
*   Amulets, coronets, and solid gold diadems.
*   Weapons featuring high workmanship.

Ornamentation from this period typically consists of concentric circles, bosses, and spirals. Some swords feature handles shaped like a cross or tracery believed to be the Svastika, an ancient Aryan symbol.
