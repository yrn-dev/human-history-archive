# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the refined Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and transport.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian reflects a growing sophistication in how early humans interacted with their landscape, moving from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone.

## From Opportunism to Prospecting
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was primarily opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartz river pebbles. The "cost" of transport was low; if a stone was within a few hundred meters, it was viable.

However, as *Homo erectus* developed the Acheulean handaxe, a shift occurred. The desire for symmetry and specific edge angles required materials with predictable fracturing patterns, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. Archaeologists have discovered sites where these materials were transported over several kilometers, suggesting that early humans began to maintain "mental maps" of high-quality lithic sources.

## The Mousterian and the Logic of Levallois
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became even more complex. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined shape—required high-grade stone to be successful. 

This necessity drove a "raw material economy." Neanderthals did not merely pick up stones; they sought out specific seams of flint. This led to the emergence of quarrying behaviors and, potentially, the earliest forms of social exchange. When high-quality stone was scarce, we see a fascinating adaptation: the recycling of tools. Old flakes were reworked into new points, indicating a high perceived value of the material itself.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Territory?
A point of ongoing debate among prehistoric archaeologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stone (material not native to the site) indicates early trade networks between different hominin groups or simply the result of wide-ranging migratory patterns. While some argue for a primitive economy of exchange, others suggest these materials were simply carried as "curated" tools over long distances during seasonal migrations.

### Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were preferred for their conchoidal fracture patterns.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan materials are usually local; Acheulean and Mousterian materials are often found kilometers from their source.
* **Curated Tools:** The transition to the Mousterian saw an increase in "curated" tools—items kept and maintained over long periods.
* **Core Preparation:** The Levallois technique reduced waste, maximizing the number of usable edges per kilogram of stone.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be knapped to an edge thinner and sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The Quartz Challenge:** In areas without flint, hominins used quartz, which is much harder to control and often resulted in "shattered" rather than "flaked" tools.
* **Geological Memory:** The ability to remember the location of a stone quarry across seasons is considered a key indicator of evolving cognitive memory in *Homo erectus*.
