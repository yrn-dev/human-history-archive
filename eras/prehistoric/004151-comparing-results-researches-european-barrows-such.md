# European Barrows

European barrows, also referred to as dolmens, are huge heaps of earth or stone constructed over sepulchres. These structures were created by Neolithic people before the appearance of metals to bury certain individuals with great care and distinction, though they did not bury the common herd in this manner. These mounds contribute to the scenery of many districts in Europe, as well as in America and India.

## Research and Methodology
Research into European barrows involves the comparison of physical findings with notices of barrow-burial found in early writings. These two sources of information are considered mutually illustrative. Scholars have utilized various regional studies to understand these structures, including researches into the burial mounds of East Yorkshire, the cairns of Arran and Bute, and the dolmens of Ireland. Other significant research has been conducted on the prehistoric ages of Spain and Portugal, the civilization of Sweden in heathen times, and stone age grave-finds in Denmark.

## Regional Variations and Structures
The form and use of barrows varied across different European regions and time periods:

*   **England and Scotland:** In the south of England, cremation is rare in long barrows. However, in the north of Scotland and in Yorkshire, cremation was the rule.
*   **Ireland:** The long barrow form is almost unknown in Ireland. Instead, the round barrow or chambered cairn prevailed from the earliest Pagan period until Christianity was introduced. These often occurred in groups that appear to have been royal cemeteries for tribal confederacies. An ancient Irish manuscript from approximately A.D. 1100, the *Leabhar na h-Uidhri*, enumerates eight such confederacies.
*   **Notable Examples:** One of the largest cairns in Ireland is located at New Grange on the banks of the Boyne above Drogheda. This mound is over 300 feet in diameter and 70 feet in height, featuring a circle of large standing stones at its base and a chamber 20 feet high reached by a 70-foot passage. In the Loughcrew Hills of Co. Meath, there is a group of about thirty stone barrows or cairns; many of these contain slabs ornamented with rudely incised spirals and other devices.

## Evolution of Burial Customs
The custom of burning the body began during the Stone Age, occurring before the dolmen or long barrow had ceased to be used. This shift toward cremation led to a modification of barrow structures. Originally, the chamber was viewed as a habitation for the deceased. With the practice of cremation, the chamber was downgraded to a cist used simply to receive the urn containing ashes.

This change in function resulted in a corresponding degradation of the mounds. Consequently, barrows from the Bronze Age are smaller and less imposing than those from the Stone Age. Despite their smaller size, Bronze Age barrows are often surprisingly rich in art workmanship and relics of the life of that era.

## Related Megalithic Structures
In connection with, or independent of, these burial mounds, Neolithic people erected megaliths (great stones). These were placed either singly or in groups. Among the most well-known examples of these megalithic structures are Carnac in Brittany and Stonehenge in Wiltshire.
