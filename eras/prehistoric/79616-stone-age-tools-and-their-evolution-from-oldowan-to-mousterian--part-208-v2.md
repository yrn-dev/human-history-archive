# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistics behind the stone. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived and managed their landscape.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding where the stone came from reveals as much about early human intelligence as the tools themselves.

## From Opportunism to Planning

In the earliest Oldowan sites (approx. 2.6 million years ago), tool-making was largely a matter of convenience. Hominins typically used cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used; if not, the tool-making event simply didn't happen. The "raw material economy" was local and immediate.

However, as we move into the Acheulean period (associated with *Homo erectus*), we see the emergence of selective sourcing. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials located kilometers away. This indicates a burgeoning capacity for mental mapping and future planning—the ability to recognize a resource in one location and transport it to another for later use.

## The Levallois Revolution and Material Quality

By the time the Mousterian industry emerged (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly specialized. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape—required stone with predictable fracturing patterns, such as high-grade flint or obsidian.

This necessity drove a "curation" mindset. Rather than discarding a tool once a edge dulled, Mousterian tool-users frequently reshaped and recycled their implements. This suggests that the cost of acquiring the material (in terms of energy and risk) had become higher than the cost of maintaining the tool.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among archaeologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials found far from their geological source) indicates the earliest forms of trade between hominin groups or simply the result of wide-ranging migratory patterns. While some argue that transporting flint over 50 kilometers suggests a social exchange network, others maintain it reflects the natural foraging range of highly mobile populations.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, utilizing river cobbles within immediate reach.
* **Acheulean Shift:** Evidence of selective sourcing, with materials transported over several kilometers.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were preferred for their predictable conchoidal fractures.
* **Curation:** Mousterian tools show higher rates of retouching, indicating a desire to extend the life of the tool.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expended to move stone reflects the increasing value placed on tool efficiency.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian fractures more sharply than almost any other natural stone, it was often transported the furthest distances in the prehistoric world.
* **The "Core" Logic:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" (the parent stone) was often treated as a precious resource, carefully managed to produce as many usable flakes as possible.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use X-ray fluorescence (XRF) to "fingerprint" stone tools, allowing them to trace a tool back to the exact quarry it came from.
