# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and flaking patterns that distinguish the Oldowan from the Mousterian—we must shift our lens toward the *geology*. The evolution of prehistoric technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a sophisticated evolution in how early hominins interacted with their landscape and managed raw materials.

The transition from opportunistic scavenging of stones to the strategic procurement of high-quality lithics reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and social planning.

## From Opportunism to Selection: Oldowan and Acheulean
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages, hominins typically utilized "local" materials. They gathered river cobbles or surface stones found within a few hundred meters of their activity sites. The focus was on immediate utility; if a stone could be knapped to produce a sharp edge, it was used.

However, as we move into the Acheulean period, characterized by the iconic symmetrical handaxe, we see the emergence of "selective procurement." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials—such as fine-grained quartzites or volcanic rocks—that allowed for more precise flaking. This suggests a mental map of the environment, where the toolmaker knew exactly where the best "quarries" were located, often kilometers away from their primary camp.

## The Levallois Revolution and Material Economy
The shift toward the Mousterian industry, associated largely with Neanderthals, introduced the Levallois technique. This was a "prepared core" method, where the stone was shaped specifically to strike off a single, predictable flake of a predetermined size and shape.

This technique demanded a much higher grade of raw material. Because a single mistake during the preparation of the core could waste a large piece of high-quality flint, the "cost" of material increased. This led to an intensified focus on transport. Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers transported high-quality flint over significantly longer distances than their predecessors, indicating a sophisticated logistical chain and perhaps a social structure that supported the dedicated gathering of resources.

## Scholarly Debate: Curation vs. Expediency
Among prehistoric archaeologists, there is an ongoing debate regarding "curation." Some scholars argue that the movement of high-quality stone indicates a "curated" technology, where tools were carefully maintained and carried for long periods. Others suggest a more "expedient" approach, arguing that the transport of stone was simply a byproduct of the migratory patterns of the hominins rather than a dedicated economic strategy.

### Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Local Sourcing:** Oldowan tools were generally made from materials found within immediate proximity to the site.
* **Material Preference:** Acheulean toolmakers showed a clear preference for materials that could hold a sharp edge and withstand heavy impact.
* **The Levallois Shift:** The Mousterian era saw a move toward "prepared cores," requiring higher-quality flint or chert.
* **Transport Distance:** The distance between the raw material source and the discard site increased significantly from the Oldowan to the Mousterian.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The ability to source materials from distant locations implies a developed sense of geography and planning.

### Did You Know?
* **The "Exotic" Stone:** In some Middle Paleolithic sites, stones have been found that originated over 100 kilometers away, suggesting either immense travel or early forms of trade/exchange.
* **Recycling:** Neanderthals frequently "re-sharpened" their tools, reducing a large flake to a small nub to extend the life of the material.
* **Material Constraints:** The specific shape of a tool was often dictated by the original shape of the raw stone, meaning "style" was often a compromise with "geology."
