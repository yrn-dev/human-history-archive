# BONE BED

In the field of geology, the term "bone bed" is used loosely to describe any deposit or stratum that contains bones of any kind. This general application includes osseous remains found within stalagmitic and brecciated deposits located on the floors of caves. However, geologists also use the term in a more restricted sense to refer to specific, thin layers of bony fragments that appear upon well-defined geological horizons.

## Notable Geological Bone Beds

Several specific bone beds are recognized within the geological record, particularly in Europe.

### The Ludlow Bone Bed
One of the most prominent examples is the Ludlow Bone Bed. This bed is located at the base of the Downton Sandstone within the Upper Ludlow series. In the town of Ludlow, two such beds are identified, separated by approximately 14 feet of strata. Although these layers are quite thin, the Ludlow Bone Bed can be traced from Ludlow into Gloucestershire for a distance of 45 meters. 

The composition of this bed consists primarily of scales, teeth, and fragments of spines from ganoid fish. In the Upper Ludlow rocks—which mainly consist of shales and soft mudstones—this brown layer varies in thickness from a quarter of an inch to 4 inches. In addition to fish remains, the layer contains mollusca and fragments of *Pterygotus*. Other fossils associated with this group include phyllocarids, ostracods, eurypterids, trilobites, gasteropods, pelecypods, cephalopods (such as *Orthoceras bullatum*), and numerous brachiopods, including *Chonetes striatella* and *Lingula minima*. Fish species found here include *Cyathaspis*, *Cephalaspis*, and *Auchenaspis*.

### The Bristol or Lias Bone Bed
Another recognized deposit, formerly called the "Lias" or "Bristol" Bone Bed, consists of several thin layers of micaceous sandstone. These layers contain the remains of saurians and fish and are found in the Rhaetic Black Paper Shales, which lie above the Keuper marls in south-west England. Similar bone beds on the same geological horizon have also been traced in Franconia, Hanover, and Brunswick.

### Other Deposits
Additionally, a bone bed has been observed in certain areas of south-west England at the base of the Carboniferous limestone series.

## Ossiferous Cave Deposits

Certain caves serve as significant sites for the accumulation of osseous remains. An example is Brixham Cave (also known as Windmill Hill Cavern), an ossiferous cave situated on a hill of Devonian limestone. Discovered in 1858, the cave was previously sealed by limestone breccia.

The cave contains a succession of beds that include:
*   **Stalagmite layers:** One layer, varying from one to fifteen inches thick, contains antlers of reindeer and a few bones.
*   **Red cave earth:** A layer 3 to 4 feet thick containing bones, angular limestone fragments, and worked flints.
*   **Shingle:** A layer of limestone, slate, and other local rock pebbles (five to sixteen feet thick) containing a few bones and worked flints.

The fauna unearthed from Brixham Cave includes the bones of the elephant, rhinoceros, lion, hyena, horse, and bear, as well as various small rodents and birds. A total of 1,621 bones were found, most of which were gnawed or broken. Specifically, 334 specimens of bears in various stages of growth, including sucking cubs, were discovered.

## Formation and Interpretation

Geologists suggest that the formation of cave deposits like those at Brixham occurred simultaneously with the excavation of the surrounding valley. It is theorized that small streams carried rounded pebbles into the cave. During periods of drought, animals frequented the cave, though the small quantity of remains in the shingle suggests these visits were limited. As the valley excavation proceeded and stream levels lowered, the cave became drier and was more frequently used as a den by predatory animals. Eventually, the cave was closed by the fall of angular fragments at the entrance, leaving it accessible only to a few burrowing animals, whose remains are found above the newer stalagmite floor.
