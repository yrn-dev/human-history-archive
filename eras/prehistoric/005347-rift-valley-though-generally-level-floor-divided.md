# The Rift-Valley of East Africa

The rift-valley is a broad upland feature formed by the subsidence of its floor. It is characterized by a generally level floor that is divided by transverse ridges into a series of basins. Many of these basins contain lakes that have no outlet.

## Geography and Basins of British East Africa
Within British East Africa, the southernmost section of the rift-valley consists of the arid Dogilani plains, which drain south toward German territory. At the northern end of these plains are the extinct volcanoes of Longonot (8,700 ft.) and Suswa (7,800 ft.). The ridge created by Longonot divides the Dogilani plains from the next basin, which contains Lake Naivasha. Lake Naivasha is a small fresh-water lake measuring approximately 13 miles in each direction and situated 6,135 ft. above sea level.

To the north, the basin of Lake Naivasha is closed by the ridge of Mount Buru. Beyond this ridge lie the basins of Lakes Elmenteita (5,860 ft.) and Nakuro (5,845 ft.), followed by the basins of Lakes Baringo and Hannington. Lake Baringo, located some 30 miles north of the equator, is 16 miles long and 9 miles broad with an irregular outline and brackish waters. It is fed by several small streams, including the Nyuki and Tigrish rivers, but has no outlet.

Beyond Lake Baringo, the valley drains north into Lake Sugota (located in 2° N.), which is some 35 miles long. North of Lake Sugota is the much larger Lake Rudolf, at which point the definition of the valley becomes somewhat less distinct.

## The Central African Rift-Valley
The Central African (or Albertine) rift-valley contains Lake Kivu, located about 60 miles north of Tanganyika. Lake Kivu is roughly triangular, measuring about 55 miles in length and over 30 miles at its greatest breadth, with an area of approximately 1,100 square miles. It sits at an elevation of about 4,830 ft. above sea level. The lake is deep, with shores characterized by high, bold precipitous cliffs of volcanic rock and a broken coastline, particularly on the south-east where it resembles a fjord.

Lake Kivu is separated from the Nile basin to the north by a line of volcanic peaks, a region often referred to as Mfumbiro. South of the lake, the rift-valley is blocked by huge ridges. The Rusizi River breaks through these ridges in a series of steep gorges, descending 2,000 ft. from Lake Kivu to the lacustrine plain at the head of Tanganyika.

## Geological and Volcanic Features
The rift-valley is part of a larger volcanic belt extending over 150,000 square miles. The region's geological history includes an older group of fissure eruptions that occurred along the site of the present rift-valley, resulting in lava flows over the Laikipia and Kapte plateaus. This was followed by the eruptions of larger volcanoes, including Kibo and several volcanoes within the rift-valley.

Some volcanoes in the rift-valley region have only recently become extinct, while others may be dormant. For example, Donyo Buru continues to emit small amounts of steam, and Mount Teleki, located near Lake Rudolf, was in eruption at the end of the 19th century.

## Surrounding Terrain and Escarpments
The rift-valley is bordered to the east by grassy uplands composed largely of lava, with altitudes ranging from 5,000 to 8,000 ft. The transition from these uplands to the valley floor is marked by a line of cliffs. Notable examples include the Laikipia escarpment on the equator and the Kikuyu escarpment (8,000 ft.) located just south of 1° S. A main watershed of East Africa runs close to the eastern wall of the rift-valley, separating the rivers of the east coast from the basins of inland drainage.

## Human Population and Infrastructure
The rift-valley is inhabited by a diverse population, including white settlers and Indian settlers, most of whom are agriculturists. Among the African populations, the Masai—a race of cattle-rearers speaking a Nilotic language—occupy parts of the uplands bordering the eastern rift-valley.

The region has been opened up by roads and the Uganda railway. The railway runs from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza and reaches the edge of the rift-valley at the Kikuyu Escarpment, 346 miles from its start, at an altitude of 7,600 ft. From there, the line descends across ravines via viaducts to the valley floor, reaching a level of 6,011 ft., before ascending the Mau escarpment to a summit of 8,321 ft.
