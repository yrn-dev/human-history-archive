# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the acquisition of the stone itself. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a change in technique, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived and interacted with their landscape.

The transition from the opportunistic gathering of river cobbles to the strategic mining of high-quality raw materials reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and planning.

## From Opportunism to Procurement

In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized materials found in their immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartz cobbles from nearby stream beds. The "cost" of transport was low, and the selection process was rudimentary: if a stone could be fractured to create a sharp edge, it was used.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian traditions, we see the emergence of "raw material selectivity." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—which offered more predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). This indicates a mental map of the environment and the ability to delay gratification for a higher-quality end product.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. The implementation of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-grade stone to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that these materials were often transported over several kilometers, sometimes exceeding 10 to 20 kilometers from the original source. This suggests a structured economy of movement. Whether these materials were carried by individuals during seasonal migrations or traded between small social groups remains a subject of scholarly debate, but the presence of "exotic" stone far from its geological source proves that the landscape was no longer just a place to live, but a resource map to be managed.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break, creating a curved, glass-like surface essential for sharp edges.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expended to move raw material; as cognitive ability grew, hominins were willing to accept higher transport costs for better materials.
* **Core Preparation:** The shift from simply hitting a rock to "shaping" a core before removing the final tool.
* **Material Selectivity:** The conscious choice of specific minerals over others based on their physical properties.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** Some of the earliest evidence of long-distance movement of materials involves obsidian, a volcanic glass that is naturally sharper than a modern steel scalpel.
* **Curated Tools:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after one use, Mousterian tools show signs of "re-sharpening," indicating they were valued possessions.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to possess an intuitive understanding of geology to identify "seams" of flint hidden beneath layers of soil or limestone.
