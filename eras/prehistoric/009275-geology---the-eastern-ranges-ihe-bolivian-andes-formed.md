# Geology of the Bolivian Andes

The geological structure of the Bolivian Andes is characterized by a division into two primary parallel chains—the Western Cordillera and the Eastern ranges—separated by an intervening plateau. This system forms the massif of the Andean system, consisting of parallel ranges that enclose elevated plateaus, which are further broken by deep ravines and transverse ranges.

## The Eastern Ranges and Cordillera Oriental
The eastern ranges of the Bolivian Andes are primarily formed of Palaeozoic rocks, which include granitic and other intrusions. The distribution of these Palaeozoic beds varies by region: in southern Bolivia, the Cambrian and Ordovician beds constitute the majority of the eastern Andes. In the north and particularly in the north-eastern ranges, the Devonian and Carboniferous beds are more extensively developed. Fossils from the Cambrian, Ordovician, Devonian, and Carboniferous ages have been recovered from these Palaeozoic beds.

The great summits of the eastern range, including Sorata and Illimani, are composed of these Palaeozoic rocks and their associated granitic intrusions. Further east, the hills known as the Chiquitos, which rise from the eastern plains of Bolivia, consist of ancient sedimentary rocks of an unknown age.

## The Western Cordillera and High Peaks
In contrast to the east, the Western Cordillera consists chiefly of Jurassic and Cretaceous beds. This region is also characterized by the presence of ashes and lavas from great volcanoes; indeed, the volcanoes of Bolivia are located almost entirely within the Western Cordillera. Notable snow-clad peaks in this region include:
*   Huallatiri (21,654 ft.)
*   Sajama (21,047 ft.)
*   Parinacota (20,918 ft.)
*   Pomarape (20,505 ft.)
*   Licancaur (19,685 ft.)
*   San Pedro y Pablo (19,423 ft.)
*   Oyahua (19,226 ft.)
*   Lirima (19,128 ft.)

## The Intervening Plateau
The plateau between the two cordilleras, also referred to as the *alta-planicie* or the great Bolivian tableland, is a lacustrine basin that once held an inland sea with an eastern outlet through the La Paz gorge. This plateau extends 500 miles in length and approximately 80 miles in breadth, covering about 40,000 square miles. It has an average elevation of 12,650 feet near Lake Titicaca, descending by about 1,000 feet toward its southern end.

The northern section of the plateau is called the *puna*, while the southern portion is known as the "desert of Lipez," which is part of the larger Puna de Atacama. This region is characterized by barren, arid landscapes, saline depressions that fill with water during the rainy season, and ridges such as the Cerro de Tahua (17,454 ft.).

Geologically, the plateau is covered by terrestrial and freshwater deposits. Rising through these deposits are ridges of Palaeozoic rock and a series of gypsiferous marls and red sandstones. The age of these sandstones and marls is somewhat uncertain, though they are likely, at least in part, Cretaceous.

## Stratification and Deposits
The Palaeozoic beds are directly overlaid by gypsiferous marls and red sandstones, which are similar to the *formacion petrolifera* found in Brazil and Argentina. These layers frequently feature a tuff or conglomerate of porphyritic rocks at their base. Marine fossils discovered by Gustav Steinmann in the middle of this series suggest an age no earlier than the Jurassic, with Steinmann specifically attributing them to the Lower Cretaceous.

While no later marine deposits have been found in the plains of Bolivia or the eastern Andes, freshwater beds from the Tertiary period and later occupy a wide area. Recent deposits in the depression between the Western and Eastern Cordilleras appear to be partly of torrential origin, similar to talus-fans found in other dry regions. Additionally, Lakes Pampa Aullaguas (Poopo) and Titicaca were once more extensive than they are currently.
