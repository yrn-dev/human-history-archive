# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes of the Oldowan choppers and the symmetry of Mousterian points—we must shift our perspective to the *geology* behind the craft. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in resource management and spatial planning.

For the early hominin, a tool was only as effective as the stone from which it was knapped. The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality minerals reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric humans perceived their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized river cobbles—basalt or quartz—found in the immediate vicinity of their habitation sites. The "raw material economy" was local; if a suitable stone was not within a short walking distance, tool production simply ceased.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see the emergence of "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials allow for predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture), enabling the creation of the sharp, thin edges required for the Levallois technique.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of logistics. Archaeologists have discovered lithic assemblages containing materials that originated dozens, and sometimes over a hundred, kilometers away from the site of use.

This suggests two possibilities that remain subjects of scholarly debate: either these hominins were highly mobile, carrying "curated" cores over vast distances, or they engaged in a primitive form of social exchange with other groups. The shift from "expedient" tools (made and discarded quickly) to "curated" tools (maintained and carried) marks a pivotal evolution in foresight and planning.

## Key Facts: The Lithic Economy
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, which is essential for creating a sharp edge.
* **Curated Technology:** Tools designed for longevity and transport, rather than immediate, one-time use.
* **Raw Material Radius:** The distance between a tool's find-spot and its geological source, used by historians to map prehistoric movement.
* **Core Reduction:** The process of preparing a stone block to strike off specific, predetermined flakes.
* **Material Preference:** The transition from using any available hard stone to specifically seeking out high-silica minerals.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian’s Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel, making it the gold standard for prehistoric cutting tools.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In Mousterian technology, the "core" was often more valuable than the final tool, as a well-prepared core could produce multiple standardized flakes.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use X-ray fluorescence (XRF) to "fingerprint" the chemical composition of a tool, allowing them to trace it back to the exact quarry where it originated.
