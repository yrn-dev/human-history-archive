# The Dawn of Domestication: Shaping the Prehistoric World

## Overview
The domestication of animals represents one of the most profound shifts in human history, marking the transition from a purely nomadic hunter-gatherer existence to settled agrarian societies. This process was not a single event, but a gradual co-evolutionary relationship where humans selectively bred animals for specific traits—such as docility, protection, or food production—while the animals adapted to human environments. While the "Neolithic Revolution" is often cited as the primary catalyst, the domestication of dogs occurred far earlier, during the Upper Paleolithic, suggesting that humans sought animal partnerships long before they sought stable food sources.

## Key Facts

### The Canine Exception
Dogs were the first species domesticated, descending from an extinct lineage of Pleistocene wolves. Unlike livestock, dogs were domesticated by hunter-gatherers. Historians and geneticists debate the exact origin; some argue for a single origin in Eurasia, while others suggest multiple domestication events across Europe and Asia.

### The Neolithic Livestock Shift
The domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle occurred primarily in the "Fertile Crescent" (modern-day Middle East). This shift allowed humans to move away from the uncertainty of wild game. By controlling breeding, prehistoric humans ensured a steady supply of protein and materials. The domestication of cattle, in particular, revolutionized labor through the eventual development of the plow.

### The Feline Partnership
Cats followed a different path, often described as "self-domestication." As early farmers stored grain, they attracted rodents. Wildcats (*Felis lybica*) moved into human settlements to hunt these pests. Humans tolerated the cats for their utility, leading to a symbiotic relationship. Recent archaeological finds in Cyprus suggest cats were associated with humans as early as 9,500 years ago.

## Did You Know?
* **Genetic Bottlenecks:** Domestication often leads to a "bottleneck" effect, where only a few individuals of a wild species are bred, significantly reducing the genetic diversity of the domestic descendants.
* **Morphological Changes:** Domesticated animals often exhibit "domestication syndrome," which includes physical changes like floppier ears, shorter snouts, and varied coat colors.
* **The Goat Debate:** There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether goats were domesticated independently in both the Near East and Central Asia.
