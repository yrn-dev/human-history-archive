# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of tool-making was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and transport.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian industries represents a fundamental change in how early humans interacted with their landscape. It marks the transition from "opportunistic foraging" for stone to "strategic sourcing."

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), hominins typically used whatever cobbles were available in their immediate vicinity, often gathered from riverbeds. The focus was on utility over quality; if a stone could produce a sharp edge, it was used. Consequently, Oldowan sites usually show a high concentration of local materials.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis* and early *Homo sapiens*), a pattern of selectivity emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flaking required for the complex Mousterian scrapers and points.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The presence of "exotic" stone—materials found dozens or even hundreds of kilometers away from an archaeological site—suggests a sophisticated understanding of geography and planning. The Mousterian industry, in particular, demonstrates a commitment to transporting high-quality cores over long distances.

This raises a significant scholarly debate: was this transport the result of highly mobile groups moving across the landscape, or does it indicate the earliest forms of social exchange and trade between different bands of hominins? While the "trade" hypothesis is compelling, many archaeologists argue that these patterns simply reflect the seasonal migration routes of hunter-gatherers following game. Regardless, the effort expended to acquire specific stones proves that the "mental template" of the tool existed before the stone was even found.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, creating a smooth, curved surface essential for sharp edges.
* **Core vs. Flake:** The "core" is the parent stone; "flakes" are the pieces struck off to create the tool.
* **Raw Material Radius:** The distance from a site to the nearest source of stone used, used by historians to map prehistoric mobility.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of identifying, extracting, and transporting stone for tool production.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Tools:** In the Mousterian era, tools were often "curated," meaning they were resharpened and reused multiple times rather than being discarded immediately.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to distinguish between stones that *looked* right and those that *behaved* right under a hammerstone, implying a deep empirical knowledge of geology.
