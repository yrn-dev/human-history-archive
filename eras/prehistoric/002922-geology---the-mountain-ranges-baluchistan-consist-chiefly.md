# Geology

The geological composition of the Earth's surface varies significantly across different global regions, characterized by diverse rock formations, folding systems, and volcanic activity.

## Baluchistan and Central Asia
Baluchistan is situated within a broad belt of crumpling that extends from west to east across central Asia, including Persia, Afghanistan, the Himalayas, and the Burmese ranges. In this zone, Mesozoic deposits and lower Tertiary divisions are thrown into folds that form the principal mountain ranges.

The mountain ranges of Baluchistan consist primarily of Tertiary and Cretaceous beds. These are arranged in a series of folds running approximately parallel to the mountain ridges, forming an extensive system resembling a southwards-hanging festoon between Mount Ararat and Peshawar. A subsidiary festoon, comprising the Bugti Hills and the Suliman Hills, is formed where the outer folds loop up at Sibi. Within these folds lies the stony desert of north-western Baluchistan, while the Makran coast is characterized by horizontal deposits. In the broader depressions between ridges, the beds are reported to be only slightly disturbed.

Beyond Cretaceous and Tertiary beds, Jurassic rocks contribute significantly to the hills of British Baluchistan. Triassic beds and *Fusulina* limestone are found along the south side of the upper Zhob. Most deposits, excluding later Tertiary beds, are marine. However, north-western Baluchistan shows extensive development of conglomerates and volcanic tuffs in the lower Tertiary and upper Cretaceous, likely contemporaneous with India's Deccan Traps. During the Tertiary period, diorite and syenite masses were intruded. A line of recent volcanic cones, mostly andesitic, stretches from eastern Persia into western Baluchistan; these are extinct in Baluchistan, though the Koh-i-Tafdan beyond the Persian frontier still emits vapours. Additionally, "mud volcanoes" are present on the Makran coast, though their connection to true volcanic agencies is doubtful.

## Asia and the East
In Korea, the geology is imperfectly known, but crystalline schists form all the higher mountain ranges. These schists are strongly folded and contain the country's mineral wealth. Near the Manchurian frontier, these are unconformably covered by limestones, clay-slates, and sandstones (approximately 1600 ft.) containing Cambrian fossils. Carboniferous beds of conglomerates, sandstones, and slates are found in south-eastern provinces, containing some coal, though the most important coal-bearing deposits are Tertiary. Volcanic and eruptive rocks occur in the island of Quelpart and the interior. Chinese stories claim Hal-la-san (Mount Auckland) on Quelpart erupted in 1007, but otherwise, Korea has no active volcanoes and has been remarkably free of earthquakes in historic times.

The Assam hills are geologically distinct from the Burmese ranges and the Himalayas. The mass is composed mainly of schists and gneiss. Older rocks are overlaid unconformably by Cretaceous beds (chiefly sandstones with coal seams) that thin toward the north, indicating an old shore-line. The overlying Tertiary series includes valuable coal seams and nummulitic beds. The border ranges to the south and east consist largely of Tertiary beds and belong to the Burmese system. The Assam valley is covered by Brahmaputra alluvial deposits.

In British Burma, the geology is divided into two sections. West of a line running from the mouth of the Sittang river through Mandalay, rocks are primarily Quaternary and Tertiary. East of this line, they are mostly gneissic or Palaeozoic. The western ranges feature folds in a gentle south-to-north curve. This includes an axial zone of Lower Eocene and Cretaceous, flanked by Miocene and Upper Eocene. The Irrawaddy valley is occupied chiefly by Pliocene deposits of freshwater origin containing Mammalia remains and silicified wood. The Miocene and Eocene beds are marine, with the latter containing nummulites and the former characterized by molluscan fauna.

## South America and the Balkans
The Bolivian Andes are divided by geological composition. The Western Cordillera consists of Cretaceous and Jurassic beds along with volcanic ashes and lavas. The eastern ranges are formed of Palaeozoic rocks with other intrusions and granitic ones. The intervening plateau is covered by terrestrial and freshwater deposits, including gypsiferous marls and red sandstones of uncertain age (possibly Cretaceous). Palaeozoic beds in Bolivia have yielded fossils from the Carboniferous, Devonian, Ordovician, and Cambrian ages. In the north-eastern ranges, the Carboniferous and Devonian are extensively developed, while the eastern Andes in the south are primarily Cambrian and Ordovician. The Chiquitos hills consist of ancient sedimentary rocks of unknown age. Recent deposits in the depression between the cordilleras are partly of torrential origin.

The Balkan Peninsula is divided into four distinct geological areas. A central triangular region, consisting largely of schistose and crystalline rocks, forms the core of the Rila, Perin, Belasitza, and Rhodope mountains. Here, Mesozoic and Palaeozoic beds are absent, and the earliest sedimentary deposits are Tertiary. The eastern parts of Greece are almost entirely Cretaceous beds. Surrounding this center are two belts of folded beds: the northern chain of the Balkans and the western ranges of the Ionian and Adriatic coasts. In the western belt, the folds strike N.W.-S.E. or N.N.W.-S.S.E. Carboniferous beds, the Jura, and the Trias are present in the north, while the south is formed chiefly of Cretaceous beds. Beyond the Balkans lies the Rumanian depression, occupied by undisturbed Tertiary and Cretaceous strata.
