# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shift from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and resource management.

For a prehistoric hominin, a tool was only as effective as the stone from which it was knapped. The transition between tool industries reflects a growing understanding of mineralogy and a willingness to venture further from home to secure high-quality materials.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were primarily opportunistic. They typically used whatever river cobbles or basalt fragments were available in the immediate vicinity. The "cost" of transporting heavy stones was too high to justify long-distance travel; consequently, Oldowan sites usually show a tight correlation between the tool site and the nearest natural stone source.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective sourcing." They no longer settled for the nearest rock; they sought out specific materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—that possessed a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for sharper edges and more predictable flaking.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become strategic. Evidence suggests that these hominins were transporting high-quality stone over distances of several, and sometimes dozens, of kilometers.

This transport indicates a sophisticated level of planning (foresight). A toolmaker would identify a source of high-grade flint, quarry it, and carry the "cores" to a living site to be worked into Levallois flakes. This separation of the *extraction site* from the *utilization site* marks a pivotal moment in human cognitive evolution: the ability to plan for a future need rather than reacting to an immediate one.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among archaeologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the local geology) indicates early forms of trade between different hominin groups or simply the result of wide-ranging migratory patterns. While some argue for a primitive exchange network, others maintain that the movement of stone simply mirrors the seasonal movement of the herds they hunted.

### Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Relied on local river cobbles; minimal transport of materials.
* **Acheulean:** Increased preference for finer-grained stones to create symmetrical hand-axes.
* **Mousterian:** Systematic quarrying and transport of high-quality flint/chert.
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The glass-like breaking pattern of flint that made it the "gold standard" for prehistoric tools.
* **Core Preparation:** The Mousterian technique of shaping a stone core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Toolkits:** In later stages, hominins began "curating" tools—resharpening and reusing the same stone multiple times rather than discarding it.
* **Geological Maps:** By analyzing the chemical composition of stone tools, modern geologists can pinpoint the exact hillside or riverbed where a tool was sourced thousands of years ago.
