# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For early hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a leap in cognitive ability, but a revolution in geological prospecting and transport.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding how prehistoric humans selected and moved stone provides a window into their territorial range and social organization.

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally utilized whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often scavenging from riverbeds. The "cost" of transporting stone was high relative to the benefit; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was found within a few hundred meters, it was used.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials offer a predictable "conchoidal fracture," allowing the knapper to control the edge with precision. This shift indicates a mental map of the landscape—the ability to remember the location of a specific quarry miles away and plan a journey to reach it.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) showcases a sophisticated understanding of material economy. Rather than transporting massive boulders, toolmakers began preparing "cores"—pre-shaped blocks of stone—that could be carried easily and flaked into multiple tools on-site.

There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether the presence of non-local stone in certain sites indicates long-distance migration or the earliest forms of inter-group trade. While some archaeologists argue that stones were simply carried during seasonal movements, others suggest that high-quality flint was a prized commodity, exchanged between bands to ensure survival in resource-poor regions.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The glass-like breaking pattern of flint and obsidian that made them the gold standard for prehistoric cutting edges.
* **Core Reduction:** The process of shaping a stone block to maximize the number of usable flakes produced.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools are typically found within meters of their source; Mousterian materials are often found kilometers away.
* **Material Preference:** While quartz was common in early sites, the later Paleolithic shows a strong preference for cryptocrystalline silicates (like chert).

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Toolkits:** By the Mousterian era, tools were "curated," meaning they were resharpened and reused multiple times rather than discarded immediately.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to distinguish between stones that looked similar but behaved differently under a hammerstone, showing an intuitive grasp of mineralogy.
