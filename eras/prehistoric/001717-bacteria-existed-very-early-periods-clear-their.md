# Bacteria

Bacteria, also known as schizomycetes, are minute vegetable organisms characterized by the absence of chlorophyll and an ordinary nucleus. They multiply through repeated bipartitions and typically consist of single cells. These cells may be cylindrical, oblong, or spherical in shape, or they may form other aggregates or filamentous structures. Most bacteria are colorless, though a small number secrete coloring matters. In terms of size, cells are commonly about 0.001 mm (1 micron) in diameter and two to five times that length, although both smaller and larger varieties exist.

## Biological Structure and Classification
The internal structure of bacteria has been a subject of significant discussion. While a nucleus typical of higher plants has not been demonstrated in the majority of forms, some of the largest bacteria appear to possess nuclei with typical structures and modes of division. Other forms may have a "diffuse nucleus" or "chromatin-granules," though the latter's similarity to the chromatin of higher forms is considered doubtful.

Many bacteria exhibit independent movement, which is distinct from Brownian movement. This motility is achieved via cilia or flagella—fine protoplasmic outgrowths of the cell. These structures are invisible under ordinary illumination in living cells and require special staining methods to be seen.

Due to their lack of ordinary sexual reproduction and their method of division, bacteria resemble Schizophyceae (blue-green algae), and both are often grouped in the class Schizophyta. However, the phylogeny of bacteria is considered doubtful as they may be a heterogeneous group. Some forms resemble green algae (Palmellaceae), others suggest a relationship with Myxomycetes, and some—particularly branching forms—may be reduced fungi. Additionally, the formation of endospores and the presence of ciliated micrococci suggest analogies with flagellate Protozoa or Saccharomycetes.

## Distribution in Time and Space
The presence of bacteria in fossils indicates that they have existed from very early periods. Research by van Tieghem and Renault has shown that large numbers of bacteria existed during the Devonian and Carboniferous periods, and likely earlier.

## Metabolic Roles and Environmental Impact
Nearly all bacteria are parasitic or saprophytic forms. They play critical roles in the circulation of various elements in nature:

**Carbon Circulation:** Bacteria are essential in breaking down cellulose. While some anaerobic bacteria decompose the middle lamella (pectin compounds) to isolate cellulose fibers, others in the mud of rivers, marshes, and cloacae decompose the cellulose itself, splitting it into carbon dioxide and marsh gas.

**Sulphur Circulation:** Sulphur bacteria, including genera such as *Thiothrix*, *Chromatium*, *Spirillum*, and *Monas*, are found in estuaries, sulphur springs, and marshes. Some thrive by oxidizing hydrogen sulphide (SH₂) and storing sulphur in their protoplasm; if SH₂ is unavailable, they may oxidize the stored sulphur into sulphuric acid. Conversely, anaerobic forms like *Spirillum desulphuricans* attack and reduce sulphates.

**Nitrogen Circulation:** In the soil and rivers, nitrifying bacteria oxidize ammonia to nitrous acid and then to nitric acid. This process can be reversed by de-nitrifying bacteria, which reduce nitrates to nitrites, and in some cases, further reduce them so that ammonia or free nitrogen escapes.

## Symbiosis and Industrial Processes
Bacteria engage in complex symbiotic relationships with plants. For example, the motile rod-like *Bacterium radicicola* enters the root-hairs of leguminous plants, creating hypertrophied tissues known as nodules. In these nodules, the bacteria absorb nitrogen from the air and hand it on to the host plant. The plant restricts the bacteria to these nodules and eventually absorbs them for its own use.

In industrial contexts, the production of vinegar is driven by the oxidation of alcohol into acetic acid by bacteria such as *Bacterium aceti*, *B. pasteurianum*, *B. kutzingianum*, and *B. xylinum*. These organisms form the "mother of vinegar" (zoogloea) and employ alcohol as a respirable substance.

## Interaction with Immune Systems
The phenomenon of agglutination occurs when molecules in bacteria (agglutinogens) unite with corresponding agglutinins in a fluid, a process that requires the presence of salts. Agglutinins and immune bodies are believed to be the result of reactive processes. The bacterium, as a complex organic substance, can trigger the formation of various antagonistic substances, such as antitoxins, which have a specific combining affinity for the introduced organic substance.
