# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and resource management.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian reflects a growing willingness to travel further and a more discerning eye for mineral quality.

## From Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized "river cobbles"—stones found in immediate proximity to their living sites. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available in a nearby stream bed, it was used. The "cost" of transport was kept low; tools were often discarded as soon as they became dull because the raw material was ubiquitous.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked shift toward *selective sourcing*. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flaking necessary to create the refined scrapers and points characteristic of the Mousterian toolkit.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian era (associated largely with Neanderthals) reveals a sophisticated understanding of landscape geography. Archaeologists have discovered tool assemblages composed of materials that originated dozens, and sometimes over a hundred, kilometers away from the site of deposition.

This suggests two possibilities that remain subjects of scholarly debate: either these hominins were highly mobile, carrying "cores" (large blocks of raw stone) with them as they migrated, or they engaged in a primitive form of social exchange or trade with other groups. The effort required to transport heavy stone over vast distances indicates that the efficiency of a high-quality tool outweighed the caloric cost of hauling the material.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break, creating a smooth, curved surface, which is essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Core vs. Flake:** The "core" is the parent stone; the "flake" is the piece struck off to create the tool.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for, selecting, and extracting raw stone from the environment.
* **Resource Radius:** The distance between a tool's find-spot and its geological source, used by historians to map prehistoric movement.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Obsession:** Because obsidian is volcanic glass and incredibly sharp, it was often transported further than any other material in the Paleolithic.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were "expedient" (made and thrown away), Mousterian tools were often "curated," meaning they were resharpened and kept for long periods.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to distinguish between stones that *looked* right and stones that *behaved* right under a hammerstone, demonstrating an early form of empirical scientific observation.
