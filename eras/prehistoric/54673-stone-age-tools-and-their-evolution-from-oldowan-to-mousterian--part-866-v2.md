# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical decisions behind the materials themselves. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a shift in shaping techniques, but a revolution in "raw material economy."

The ability to select, transport, and curate stone reveals a profound evolution in prehistoric planning and spatial awareness.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Early hominins typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity to their campsites, often sourced from nearby stream beds. The "cost" of transport was low, and the tools were frequently discarded shortly after use.

However, as we transition into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a shift toward selectivity. Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, razor-sharp edges required for more complex tools.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with stone had become strategic. Archaeologists have discovered "caches" of raw materials—stockpiles of high-quality stone transported over several kilometers. 

This indicates a capacity for "future-oriented planning." A Neanderthal toolmaker did not simply find a stone and shape it; they identified a source, extracted the material, and carried it to a location where it would be needed later. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and a social structure capable of managing resources across time and space.

## The Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of scholarly debate remains regarding the presence of "exotic" stones found far from their geological source. Some historians argue this is evidence of early trade networks between disparate hominin groups. Others suggest it is simply the result of high mobility—that groups carried their preferred materials with them as they migrated across the landscape. Regardless of the cause, the movement of stone proves that the Paleolithic world was far more connected than a simple "hunter-gatherer" label suggests.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic; materials were used where they were found.
* **Material Preference:** Flint and obsidian were prized for their predictable fracturing patterns.
* **Curated Technology:** Mousterian tools show evidence of "curation," meaning they were maintained and carried rather than discarded.
* **Transport Distance:** Some Mousterian raw materials have been traced to sources over 10 kilometers away.
* **Cognitive Leap:** The shift from local to distant sourcing marks an evolution in abstract planning and memory.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian’s Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge only a few molecules thick, making it sharper than a modern surgical steel scalpel.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** In Mousterian technology, the "core" was carefully prepared so that a single, perfect flake could be struck off with one blow.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use X-ray fluorescence (XRF) to "fingerprint" stones, allowing them to trace a tool back to the exact quarry it came from.
