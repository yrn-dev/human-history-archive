# The Raw Material Logic: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must shift our gaze from the finished product to the origin. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a revolution in geological literacy and spatial planning.

For the prehistoric hominin, the world was not just a landscape of prey and predators, but a map of mineral resources. The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in how early humans perceived their environment.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized "river cobbles"—stones already rounded and weathered by water—which required minimal effort to flake. The focus was on immediate utility; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, we see the emergence of "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of materials with superior conchoidal fracture patterns, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. This indicates a mental blueprint: the toolmaker knew what the ideal material was before they even began the knapping process.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The most striking evidence of cognitive evolution is the distance between the tool's find-spot and its geological source. In later Mousterian sites, archaeologists have discovered tools made from materials sourced dozens, sometimes hundreds, of kilometers away.

This suggests two possibilities that remain subjects of scholarly debate: either early humans were migrating vast distances while carrying their "toolkits" with them, or they had established rudimentary social networks for the exchange of high-value minerals. The effort required to transport a heavy core of raw flint across a landscape implies that the tool was viewed as a long-term investment rather than a disposable convenience.

## The Mastery of the Core

By the Mousterian era, the Levallois technique demonstrated a sophisticated understanding of geometry. Instead of simply hitting a rock until it looked like a tool, the knapper carefully prepared a core to strike off a single, predetermined flake of a specific size and shape. This "prepared core" technique reduced waste and maximized the utility of precious, imported raw materials, marking the dawn of true industrial efficiency.

### Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break in smooth, curved shells, essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Raw Material Transport:** Evidence shows some Middle Paleolithic hominins transported stone over 100km from its source.
* **Material Hierarchy:** Flint and obsidian were preferred over quartzite or basalt due to their predictability and sharpness.
* **Resource Mapping:** The shift to selective sourcing indicates an improved long-term memory and spatial mapping of the environment.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian’s Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Toolkit:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often left behind after one use, Mousterian tools show signs of "re-sharpening," proving they were valued possessions.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early humans could identify high-quality stone nodules hidden inside weathered limestone crusts, requiring a deep intuitive knowledge of geology.
