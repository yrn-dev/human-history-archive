# Western Asia and the Basin of the Obi

## Geological History
The early geological history of Asia indicates that a large portion of the continent was submerged beneath the sea during the Ordovician and Cambrian periods. While the peninsula of India has remained above the sea since at least the Carboniferous period, and the north and east of Asia have been land since the Palaeozoic era, other regions experienced different cycles. 

Between these ancient land masses—named Gondwanaland in the south and Angaraland in the north—lay a sea referred to by Suess as the Tethys and by Neumayr as the central Mediterranean sea. During the later stages of the Cretaceous period, the sea covered the drainage area of the Aral Sea, the basin of the Obi, and the greater part of western Asia, including Arabia, Persia, Baluchistan, and Afghanistan. However, it is probable that a considerable portion of this specific region was dry land during Jurassic times.

## Hydrography and Regional Divisions
Asia's hydrographic basins are categorized into three primary divisions. The northern division, which encompasses the Aralo-Caspian depression (or the Mediterranean), the drainage falling into the Arctic Sea, and the Mediterranean, covers approximately 6,394,500 sq. m. This total is comprised of the Mediterranean (268,500 sq. m.), the Aralo-Caspian basin (1,759,000 sq. m.), and the Arctic river basins (4,367,000 sq. m.). Within this division, the basin of the Ob is the largest river basin in Asia, exceeding 1,000,000 sq. m. in extent.

The southern division is nearly equal in size at 6,514,000 sq. m., divided between the Indian Ocean (2,873,000 sq. m.) and Pacific drainage (3,641,000 sq. m.). The interior or inland basins cover 3,141,500 sq. m., roughly half the size of the other two divisions. These inland basins include the Syro-Arabian inland basin, the Iranian (Perso-Afghan) uplands, the Tibetan plateau, the Gobi depression, the basin of Asia Minor, and the lacustrine regions south of the Arctic watershed.

## Environmental and Human Transitions
Over the last ten thousand years, these regions have undergone a process of elevation and drying. Approximately ten thousand years ago, a continuous water barrier likely existed between the Aral-Caspian sea and the basin of the Obi. As this barrier dried and the marshy terrain transitioned into steppe-like country, the region became an accumulation point for barbaric peoples. 

Mongolian nomads from the east and Nordic nomads from the west met and mixed in this area, and the riding horse was reintroduced to the western world. These populations were loosely attached to their lands, residing in wagons and tents rather than permanent houses. Their population levels fluctuated based on environmental conditions; a few years of health and plenty, or the presence of a strong ruler who ceased tribal warfare, led to population increases, while two or three hard years would force the tribes to wander again in search of food.

## Regional Geography and Borders
In the extreme west of the continent lies Asia Minor, a peninsula forming part of the empire of Turkey. It is bounded by the Mediterranean to the south, the Aegean to the west, and the Black Sea to the north, separated from Europe only by the Dardanelles and Bosporus straits. While not a term used by classical geographers, the region is often described as extending east to the Euphrates near Malatia and the Armenian plateau. Natives use the term *Anadol* (Anatolia) for the western part of the peninsula.

Further east, the Kuen-lun Mountains act as the "backbone" of the continent, stretching nearly 2500 m. from east to west. These mountains are older than the Tian-shan to the north and the Himalayas to the south. Between the Kuen Lun and the Thien Shan lies the Tarim Basin (roughly Eastern Turkestan), characterized by rivers that end in intermittent lakes and swamps rather than reaching the sea.

## Ethnic Migrations
The northern regions of Asia and Europe were formerly occupied by an obsolete and savage race known as the Fennic race. Evidence of the Ugri or Igours is found on the western borders of China, and their migration to the banks of the Irtish is noted in Tartar evidence. Remains of these Fennic tribes are scattered thinly from the shores of Lapland to the sources of the Oby.
