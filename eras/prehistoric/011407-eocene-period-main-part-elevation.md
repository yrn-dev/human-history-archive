# The Eocene Period

The Eocene, meaning the "dawn of recent life," is a geological period within the Cainozoic era. It was characterized by exceptional global warmth and significant transformations in the Earth's crust, biological life, and geography.

## Geological Transformations and Mountain Building
The Cainozoic period began with a gigantic upheaval of mountain ranges and a crumpling of the earth's crust, an era marked by great earthquakes and active volcanoes. While the Eocene was an age of warmth, it set the stage for subsequent mountain building. The Miocene is identified as the great age of mountain building, but the elevation of the Himalayas occurred primarily after the Eocene period. Evidence for this is found in the presence of nummulitic limestone at heights of 20,000 feet.

The formation of the Himalayas and other central Asian mountain chains isolated portions of a former central sea. These forces eventually led to the union of the old continents of Gondwana and Angara. However, Gondwanaland did not survive long; the section located between South Africa and India sank beneath the waves during Tertiary times.

## Global Stratigraphy and Marine Deposits
During the Eocene, marine seas were extensive. In Asia, a broad belt of marine deposits exists between China and India. Marine Tertiary beds are found in Burma, and a nearly complete series of marine deposits from the Carboniferous to the Eocene exists in south Tibet and the Himalayas. In India, Eocene strata are extensively developed, including the marine Ranikot beds in Sind—consisting of sandstones, shales, and clays with lignite and gypsum—and the massive nummulitic limestones of the Kirthar and Nari groups.

In North America, marine Eocene rocks are most prominent around the Gulf of Mexico, extending into the Mississippi valley and along the Atlantic coastal plain to New Jersey. On the Pacific coast, formations such as the Tejon series (containing oil and lignite) and the Escondido series are present. In Washington, the Puget formation contains a great series of sediments, largely of brackish water origin, with a total estimated thickness of 20,000 feet, though only the lower portion is likely Eocene.

In Europe, the Eocene is marked by various deposits. The London-Paris-Belgian basin saw formations that shifted from fresh-water and brackish origin to marine, and back again. In the central and northern Apennines, Eocene strata reach thicknesses of over 5,500 feet. In North Africa, nummulitic sandstones and limestones are widespread, including the Libyan group and the Mokattam stage of Egypt.

## Continental Geography and Volcanism
It is possible that northern land masses were more continuous during the Eocene than they are today. Europe may have been united with North America via Greenland and Iceland, while Asia may have joined America through Alaska. Conversely, a great central mediterranean sea divided the northern land mass by sending an arm northward east of the Ural mountains. South America, Africa, and Australia may have been connected to the Antarctic continent.

Volcanic activity was local and intermittent throughout the period. Evidence includes tuffs in the Vicentin and Veronese Alps, as well as diabases, gabbros, and serpentines in the Apennines. Igneous rocks, including basalts and tuffs, are also found in various U.S. states such as Idaho, Colorado, California, Oregon, Washington, and Montana, as well as in South and Central America.

## Flora and Fauna
The Eocene marked a stark contrast in fauna compared to preceding periods. The Saurian reptiles, which dominated the Mesozoic, disappeared by the start of the Eocene. They were replaced by placental mammals, which rapidly became the dominant group. Early Eocene mammals were generalized types; for example, the Condylarthra presaged herbivorous groups and the Creodonta foreshadowed the Carnivora. 

Prominent Eocene creatures included:
* **Amblypoda:** Such as *Coryphodon* and the *Dinoceratidae* (including *Dinoceras*), which were elephantine creatures with massive skulls and huge canine teeth.
* **Carnivores:** Including *Hyaenodon*, *Cynodon*, *Arctocyon*, and *Palaeonictis*. True cats did not arrive until later, though *Eusmilus* appeared in the Upper Eocene of France.
* **Non-placental mammals:** While taking a secondary role, the primitive opossum *Didelphys* had a wide geographical range.
* **Birds:** Large flightless forms like *Gastornis* and *Eupterornis* were prominent, alongside ancestors of modern owls, eagles, herons, and gulls.

The flora showed less of a sharp break from the Cretaceous. A mixed assemblage of tropical palms and mild-temperate trees existed. In Northern Europe, early Eocene plants like willows, oaks, and chestnuts indicated a temperate climate. By the Middle Eocene, the climate became warmer, with cinnamon, figs, palms, sequoia, and magnolia flourishing in areas like Hampshire and the Isle of Wight.
