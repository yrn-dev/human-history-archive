# Volcanic Activity and Geological Formations

Volcanic activity has played a significant role in shaping the landmasses of various regions across the globe, contributing to the creation of plateaus, islands, and specific rock formations throughout different geological periods.

## Volcanic Regions of Asia and the East
During or since the Cretaceous period, three distinct regions of the continent have experienced great volcanic eruptions. In the southern region, the "harras" of Arabia and the extensive flows of the Deccan Trap in India consist of lavas found in unfolded beds. The central folded belt contains volcanoes that are now mostly extinct, located in Persia, Armenia, Baluchistan, and Asia Minor. Additionally, Burma contains at least one extinct volcano. 

In the northern unfolded region, basic lava flows lie directly upon Ordovician and Cambrian beds in Siberia; these are certainly partially of Tertiary age. Smaller flows of a similar nature occur in northern China, Korea, and Manchuria. While eruptions in these areas have almost ceased, current volcanic activity is concentrated in the islands off the south eastern and eastern coasts. In Asia Minor, the interior tableland of Lycaonia features minor volcanic mountain groups, including the Karadja Dagh, which presents a range of volcanic cones, and the Kara Dagh, which rises above 7,000 ft.

## Volcanic Formations in Africa
In British East Africa, volcanic rocks form a belt covering over 150,000 sq. m., extending from beyond the northern to beyond the southern territorial limits. These are divided into older and newer sets. The older group began with fissure eruptions along the site of the present rift-valley, resulting in repeated lava flows over the Laikipia and Kapte plateaus. During this period, or shortly after, eruptions occurred at Chibcharagnani, Elgon, Kimawenzi, and Kenya. These outbursts are post-Jurassic and likely extended through the Cretaceous into early Tertiary times.

This period was followed by the eruptions of the larger rift-valley volcanoes and Kibo. The lavas from Kibo, which include leucite and nepheline basanite rich in soda felspars, resemble Norwegian "Rhombenporphyrs." More recent volcanic activity produced the Kyulu mountains, Donyo Longonot, the northern lower slopes of Kilimanjaro, and various craters in the rift-valley. Some of these have only recently become extinct, while others remain dormant. Mount Teleki was in eruption at the close of the 19th century, and Donyo Buru continues to emit small quantities of steam.

## Volcanic Islands and Oceanic Formations
Krakatoa, located in the Sunda Strait between Sumatra and Java, is a small volcanic island known for a stupendous eruption in 1883. Originally, a large volcano existed in the center of the tract, which was blown away in a prehistoric explosion, leaving only a broken ring of islands at its base. Subsequent eruptions created new small cones, the most significant reaching 2,623 ft. above sea level. In 1680, the volcano erupted, ejecting large quantities of pumice and causing great earthquakes. In 1883, after several years of frequent earthquakes, the volcano broke out in May. On August 26 and 27, paroxysmal explosions occurred; the four most violent took place on the morning of the 27th, blowing away the northern and lower portion of the island and leaving a vertical cliff that revealed the volcano's inner structure.

Kerguelen Island is also of volcanic origin, primarily composed of basaltic lavas with occasional dikes. It is reported to have hot springs and an active volcano in its south-west. Fossil remains of trees suggest the island was once thickly wooded before being denuded by submergence, volcanic action, and possible climate changes. The Kerguelen plateau rises to within 1,500 fathoms of the sea surface in some areas.

## Early Earth and Other Geological Observations
In the earliest ages of the Earth, the land was a barren place without soil or vegetation, upheaved by violent volcanic forces. The atmosphere was denser, with frequent storms and cloud masses that obscured the sun. Incessant rain-storms carried sediment to the sea, where it hardened into shales, slates, and sandstones.

Other specific geological notes include the presence of volcanic rocks of Wenlock age in the Upper Silurian series of the Dingle promontory. Additionally, the theory of coral reef formation has been debated; while Darwin proposed subsidence, others like Karl Semper and Louis Agassiz provided evidence of recent upheaval. Sir J. Murray noted that submarine elevations rising from depths of 2,000 fathoms or more provide habitats for deep-sea corals and organisms, which gradually raise the banks until reef-building corals can flourish and eventually form atolls.
