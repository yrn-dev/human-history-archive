# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a leap in cognitive ability, but a revolution in geological prospecting and resource management.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding how early humans selected, transported, and curated stone provides a window into their spatial awareness and social organization.

## From Local Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was primarily opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used; if not, the toolmaking stopped. There was little evidence of "planning" for future needs.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), a dramatic shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective sourcing." They no longer settled for the nearest available rock; instead, they sought out high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials allow for predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture), which is essential for the Levallois technique—the sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, pre-determined flake.

## The Logistics of Transport and Curation

The movement of stone across the landscape indicates a growing mental map of the environment. In Mousterian sites, archaeologists frequently find "exotic" materials—stones that do not occur naturally in the local geology. This suggests that Neanderthals were transporting raw materials over several kilometers, sometimes even tens of kilometers, to ensure they had the best tools for the hunt.

This led to the concept of "curation." Rather than discarding a tool after a single use, the Mousterian toolmaker would resharpen and rework an edge multiple times. This behavioral shift suggests a foresight that was absent in the Oldowan era: the understanding that a high-quality resource is finite and must be preserved.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing scholarly debate concerns the presence of these exotic stones. Some archaeologists argue that the movement of high-quality flint indicates early forms of social exchange or "trade" between different kinship groups. Others maintain that it simply reflects the wide migratory patterns of nomadic bands following seasonal game.

### Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan Materials:** Primarily local river cobbles, basalt, and quartz.
* **Mousterian Materials:** Preference for flint, chert, and obsidian for superior sharpness.
* **Transport Distance:** Shifted from <1 km (Oldowan) to potentially >10 km (Mousterian).
* **The Levallois Technique:** A "prepared core" method that required specific stone qualities to succeed.
* **Curation:** The practice of retouching and reusing tools to extend their lifespan.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Geological Knowledge:** By the Mousterian era, hominins likely understood which strata of earth contained the best flint nodules.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians study "debitage" (the waste flakes left over from toolmaking) to determine if a tool was made on-site or brought in finished.
