# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was an evolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding where the stone came from reveals as much about early human intelligence as the tools themselves.

## From Opportunism to Planning

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were generally opportunistic. They typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used. The "cost" of transport was kept low, and the tools were often discarded immediately after a single task, such as butchering a carcass.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), we see a dramatic shift. Hominins began to seek out specific types of high-quality stone—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—that allowed for more predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture). This required a mental map of the landscape, as the desired material was often not available at the site of use.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian

By the Mousterian period, the Levallois technique demanded high-quality cores to produce standardized flakes. This necessity drove a "raw material economy." Archaeological evidence shows that Neanderthals transported high-quality stone over distances of several kilometers, and in some documented cases, significantly further.

This transport implies a level of foresight and social organization previously underestimated. It suggests that groups planned their movements based on the location of lithic resources, or established trade-like networks to acquire materials from other groups. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport was a result of nomadic wandering or a deliberate, structured system of "quarrying" and caching materials for future use.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, which is essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for and collecting stone raw materials.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools are typically found near their source; Mousterian tools are often found far from their geological origin.
* **Material Selectivity:** Later hominins ignored "easy" local stones in favor of "superior" distant stones to ensure tool efficiency.
* **Caching:** The practice of leaving piles of raw stone at strategic locations for later retrieval.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Travel:** In some Late Paleolithic sites, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers away from the only known volcanic source, proving extensive transport or trade.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were "expedient," Mousterian tools were often "curated," meaning they were sharpened and reused multiple times.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to distinguish between different types of rock based on texture and hardness, demonstrating an early form of empirical geological science.
