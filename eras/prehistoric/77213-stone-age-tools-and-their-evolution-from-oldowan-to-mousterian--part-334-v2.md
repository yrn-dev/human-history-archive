# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a shift in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

In the earliest phases of tool use, the relationship between the tool-maker and the material was opportunistic. However, as we move toward the Middle Paleolithic, we see the emergence of a "raw material economy," where the selection of stone became a deliberate, strategic act.

## From Opportunism to Selection
The Oldowan industry (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago) is characterized by "localism." Most Oldowan tools were crafted from whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often within a few hundred meters of the site. The focus was on immediate utility; if a riverbed provided basalt or quartz, that is what was used.

As *Homo erectus* developed the Acheulean industry, a shift occurred. We begin to see evidence of "selective procurement." Hominins were no longer just picking up the nearest stone; they were seeking out materials with specific fracturing properties (such as high-quality flint or obsidian) that allowed for the symmetrical shaping of handaxes. This suggests a mental map of the environment and a capacity for forward planning.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the transport of raw materials had become a sophisticated logistical operation. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a predictable, pre-shaped flake—required high-quality stone to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian tool-makers often transported high-grade materials over distances of several kilometers, and in some cases, tens of kilometers. This implies a social structure capable of coordinating movement and a cognitive ability to forgo immediate gratification (using a low-quality local stone) in favor of a superior tool produced from distant materials.

## The Debate on Trade and Exchange
A point of ongoing scholarly debate is whether the presence of "exotic" stones at distant sites indicates individual foraging trips or the earliest forms of social exchange between groups. While some archaeologists argue for simple mobility patterns, others suggest that the movement of high-quality flint may represent a primitive trade network, hinting at complex social bonds between disparate Neanderthal populations.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan Localism:** Early tools were typically made from materials found within a very short radius of the habitation site.
* **Material Preference:** The shift to Acheulean tools saw a preference for cryptocrystalline silicates (like chert) due to their predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **The Levallois Leap:** The Mousterian industry required specific stone qualities to execute the complex "prepared core" technique.
* **Transport Distance:** While Oldowan materials moved meters, Mousterian materials often moved kilometers.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** Sourcing materials from distant quarries indicates a developed spatial memory and planning capability.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian fractures more sharply than almost any other natural stone, it was highly prized and is often found far from its volcanic source.
* **Curated Tools:** In later stages, tools were "curated," meaning they were resharpened and reused multiple times to conserve the precious imported stone.
* **Experimental Archaeology:** Modern historians use "knapping" experiments to determine exactly how much effort and time it took prehistoric humans to transport and shape specific stone types.
