# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how early hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

For the prehistoric toolmaker, the world was not a uniform expanse, but a map of geological "hotspots." The transition from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic mining of high-quality raw materials reveals a profound shift in spatial memory and planning.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartz pebbles from nearby stream beds. The "transport distance" for these tools was minimal, suggesting a lifestyle tied closely to the immediate availability of resources.

However, as we move into the Acheulean era, we see the emergence of selective sourcing. *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of materials that fractured more predictably, such as flint or fine-grained volcanic rocks. This indicates a mental "blueprint" of the landscape; the hominin knew that a superior material existed several kilometers away and planned their movement accordingly.

## The Mousterian Logistics and Trade
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. The Levallois technique required high-quality stone to produce the precise, predetermined flakes characteristic of the era. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported high-quality flint over distances exceeding 10 to 50 kilometers. This suggests two possibilities: either a highly organized seasonal migration pattern or the earliest forms of social exchange between disparate groups. The ability to curate a "tool kit" and carry raw cores across vast territories demonstrates a capacity for long-term foresight that far exceeds the immediate needs of the moment.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Availability
A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the transport of high-quality stone was always a conscious choice. Some scholars argue that "curated" tools were simply the result of hominins following game migrations that happened to lead them past quality stone sources, rather than a deliberate "mining expedition." However, the increasing rarity of local materials in later sites suggests a deliberate preference for quality over convenience.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic; materials were gathered from the immediate environment.
* **Acheulean Shift:** Evidence of selective sourcing, where specific rock types were sought out for their flaking properties.
* **Mousterian Transport:** High-quality materials were often carried dozens of kilometers from their original source.
* **Material Preference:** Flint, chert, obsidian, and quartzite were the most prized materials due to their conchoidal fracture patterns.
* **Cognitive Link:** The distance between a tool's find-spot and its geological source serves as a proxy for measuring prehistoric planning capabilities.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late Paleolithic sites, obsidian (volcanic glass) has been found hundreds of kilometers from the nearest volcano, suggesting complex early trade networks.
* **The "Core" Concept:** Mousterian toolmakers didn't just carry finished tools; they carried "cores"—large chunks of raw stone—allowing them to manufacture fresh edges on demand.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians identify sourcing patterns by studying "debitage" (the waste flakes left behind), which reveals whether a tool was made on-site or brought from afar.
