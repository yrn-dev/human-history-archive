# The Dawn of Domestication: Shaping the Prehistoric World

## Overview
The transition from nomadic hunting and gathering to settled agriculture was one of the most profound shifts in human history. Central to this "Neolithic Revolution" was the domestication of animals. Domestication is not merely the taming of a wild animal, but a genetic process where humans selectively bred species for specific traits—such as docility, size, or productivity—creating a symbiotic relationship that fundamentally altered human diet, security, and social structures.

## Key Facts

### The First Companion: The Dog
Dogs were the first species domesticated, occurring well before the advent of farming. Historians and geneticists debate the exact timeline and origin; some argue dogs descended from a now-extinct wolf lineage in Europe, while others point to East Asia. Regardless of the location, the relationship likely began as a "commensal" one, where wolves scavenged near human camps, and those with lower aggression were tolerated and eventually integrated into hunting parties.

### The Livestock Revolution
The domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle in the Fertile Crescent provided humans with a reliable source of protein, milk, and wool. This shifted human society from a "boom-and-bust" cycle of foraging to a surplus-based economy. The ability to store wealth in the form of livestock led to the emergence of social hierarchies and permanent settlements.

### The Opportunistic Cat
Unlike dogs, which were actively integrated into human labor, cats are believed to have "domesticated themselves." As early farmers stored grain, they attracted rodents. Wildcats (*Felis lybica*) moved into these settlements to hunt the pests. Humans tolerated the cats for their utility, leading to a gradual genetic shift toward docility.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Founder Effect":** Most modern domestic sheep and goats can be traced back to a very small number of wild ancestors in the Near East.
* **Genetic Markers:** Domestication often causes "domestication syndrome," which can include physical changes like floppier ears, shorter snouts, and varied coat colors.
* **The Aurochs:** Modern cattle are descendants of the Aurochs, a massive, aggressive wild ox that is now extinct.
