# Minerals of Basalt

Basalt is one of the oldest known rock names, believed to be derived from the Ethiopian word *basal*, meaning a stone that yields iron. According to Pliny, the first basalts were obtained in Ethiopia. In modern petrology, the term describes a variety of basic igneous rocks that are dark in color, weather to brown, and are comparatively rich in iron and magnesia.

## Common and Porphyritic Minerals
Basalts frequently exhibit a porphyritic structure, characterized by larger crystals—phenocrysts—set within a finely crystalline groundmass. The most common porphyritic minerals are augite and olivine. Augite is pitch-black, while olivine is yellowish or green; the latter often weathers into green or brown serpentine. Porphyritic plagioclase felspars are also very common, typically not exceeding a quarter of an inch in length, though some can reach one or two inches. Fresh plagioclase is dark grey with smooth, lustrous cleavage surfaces, but it becomes turbid and assumes greenish or grey shades upon decomposition.

In the groundmass, or second generation of crystals, ingredients are smaller and less perfect. This matrix often consists of minute prisms of augite and small lath-shaped felspars crowded together. Some basalts contain brown, isotropic glass in the groundmass, while vitreous basalts may consist primarily of a dark brown, almost opaque glassy material. This glass is generally charged with skeleton crystals of felspar or augite, spherulites, perlitic cracks, steam vesicles, or black grains of magnetite.

## Less Common and Rare Minerals
Beyond the primary constituents, several other minerals may be present in basaltic rocks:

*   **Amphiboles and Micas:** Black hornblende (which appears dark brown in thin sections and is often corroded) is not uncommon, particularly in intrusive basalts. Black mica, also known as biotite, is not infrequently observed.
*   **Felspathoids:** These occur in many basaltic rocks. Nepheline and leucite are the most common. If nepheline partially replaces felspar, the rock is termed nepheline-basanite; if the replacement is total, it is nepheline-basalt. Similarly, leucite-basalts and leucite-basanites exist. Nepheline typically appears as small six-sided prisms, often too small to be seen without a microscope, and may decompose into zeolites and analcite. Other minerals that tend to occur in these varieties include nosean, sodalite, hauyne, and melilite.
*   **Melilite:** A lime-alumina-silicate characteristic of rare melilite-basalts. In thin sections, it is colourless or pale yellow and produces characteristic dark blue polarization colours.
*   **Rare Minerals:** Sapphire, zircon, and garnet are rare. Perofskite may also appear in small dark brown forms.
*   **Native Iron:** Native iron has been found enclosed in basalt as nodules. Some of these nodules exhibit Widmannstatten figures similar to those found in iron meteorites.

## Related Rock Types and Structures
Certain basaltic rocks are categorized based on their crystalline state and structure. Dolerites are basaltic rocks that are nearly or quite holocrystalline and comparatively coarse-grained. They often occur as sills and dikes. A common structure in dolerites is the ophitic structure, where idiomorphic felspars are enveloped by shapeless masses of augite.

Some geologists have used terms such as melaphyre, diabase porphyrite, *diabas-mandel-stein*, or anamesite to describe older, pre-Tertiary basalts or altered dolerites. Another general term used by travellers and geologists for the whole suite of basaltic rocks is "trap," derived from a Swedish word for "a stair," referring to the step-like weathering patterns of superposed basalt sheets.

## Weathering and Secondary Minerals
Basaltic lavas are frequently pumiceous or spongy near their surfaces. Over time, steam cavities may be filled with secondary minerals, including zeolites, chlorite, and calcite. The weathering of primary minerals produces various pseudomorphs: olivine alters to serpentine, augite weathers to uralite, calcite, or chlorite, and plagioclase felspar may produce kaolin, white micas, epidote, or calcite.
