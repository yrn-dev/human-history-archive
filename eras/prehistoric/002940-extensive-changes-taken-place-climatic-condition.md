# Climatic Changes in Earth's History

Climatic conditions on Earth have been characterized by continuous and complex changes that have both stimulated and checked the progress of life. These changes are not regular but consist of slow fluctuations between heat and cold, as well as alternating periods of great wetness and great dryness. Contrary to the idea that the world has simply cooled since its crust grew solid, evidence of intense cold exists even in the Azoic or Archæozoic Age.

## Astronomical and Telluric Causes
The causes of climatic shifts are complex and involve the interplay of several independent wavering systems. Three primary astronomical systems include the eccentricity of the orbit, the change in the obliquity of the equator to the orbit, and the precession of the equinoxes. When the orbit is most nearly circular, the equator is at its least inclination, and the spring and autumn are at perihelion and aphelion, the climate becomes warm and uniform with minimal difference between summer and winter. Conversely, when the orbit is most eccentric, the equator is most tilted, and summer and winter occur at aphelion and perihelion, climates reach their extremes. In these periods, winter is at its bitterest, leading to great accumulations of ice and snow that reflect summer heat back into space, preventing the ice from melting.

Telluric and geographical factors further influence these conditions. The shifting of the poles of rotation, changes in the shapes of continents, and fluctuations in the warmth of the sun contribute to long periods of ice or equable warmth. Internal activity, such as volcanic eruptions and upheavals, can rearrange continental outlines, increasing mountain heights and sea depths, which exaggerates climatic extremes. These "high and deep" ages are followed by "low and level" ages of quiescence, where rain, frost, and rivers wear down mountains and fill sea bottoms with silt. Additionally, localized forces such as mountain chains interfering with moisture-bearing winds and ocean currents carrying warmth from equatorial to temperate latitudes play significant roles.

## Geological Periods and Regional Shifts
During the Cainozoic period, a time of extreme volcanic activity and the formation of the Alps, Himalayas, Rockies, and Andes, the world's climate began as austere. It grew warmer before entering a series of extremely cold cycles known as the Glacial Ages, from which the earth is apparently now slowly emerging.

In Europe and Asia, the last 10,000 years have seen significant transitions. The recession of glacial ice gave way to steppe or prairie-like conditions over the great plain of Europe, which transitioned into forest conditions approximately 10,000 to 12,000 years ago. This shift influenced human development, as Solutrian horse hunters were succeeded by Magdalenian fishers and forest deer hunters, and later by Neolithic agriculturists and herdsmen. Between 3,000 and 4,000 years ago, Europe and western Asia experienced a more genial climate and greater vegetable luxuriance; forests existed in south Russia and Western Turkestan, areas that are now deserts and steppes. However, the Aral-Caspian region was likely drier and its seas smaller between 1,500 and 2,000 years ago.

## Recent and Localized Climatic Alterations
Evidence of recent climatic changes is found in various specific regions:

*   **The Baluch Highlands:** Large areas have experienced widespread desiccation. While denudation, erosion, and the destruction of forests may have contributed, the presence of Arab irrigation works and cultivation suggests a once-flourishing agricultural state. It is probable that climatic changes, rather than tribal disturbances, caused the disappearance of the Arabs from agriculture and trade.
*   **Bangkok:** The climate has recently become hotter and less humid. While January and December still record minimums below 60°F, maximums now exceed 100°F during hot months and the start of the rains; prior to 1900, 93°F was considered unusually high. This is attributed to the removal of vegetation and extensive drainage near the town.
*   **Kerguelen Island:** Fossil remains of trees indicate the island was once thickly clothed in woods. This vegetation was lost through submergence, volcanic action, and possibly climatic changes. The island also shows evidence of powerful glaciation.

## Biological Adaptation
Because conditions are always changing, there is no finality in adaptation; living things must constantly adapt or perish. Some species exhibit seasonal adaptations to cold climates; for example, the stoat or ermine (_P. ermineus_) changes its fur color from brown in summer to white in winter, though the tip of the tail remains black.
