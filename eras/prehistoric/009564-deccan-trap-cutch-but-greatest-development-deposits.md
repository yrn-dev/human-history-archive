# Deccan Trap in Cutch and Related Formations

The Deccan Trap is a geological series characterized by horizontal lava flows. In the Bombay Presidency, these flows cover nearly the entirety of Bombay and spread across a significant portion of the Kathiawar peninsula, extending further into Cutch.

## Geological Composition and Structure
The term "trap" is derived from a Swedish word meaning "a stair," a name given because superposed sheets of basalt often weather into terraced or step-like features. This designation serves as a general term for the suite of basaltic rocks. These formations are composed of basaltic rocks, which British petrologists often refer to as dolerites when the mass is thoroughly crystallized without glassy material. In other basaltic rocks, a dark brown, almost opaque glassy material may be present, often containing steam vesicles, perlitic cracks, spherulites, or skeleton crystals of felspar and augite.

Within the Deccan traps of India, a clay-like substance known as bole—consisting essentially of hydrous aluminium silicate with varying amounts of iron—occurs as red partings between the great sheets of lava.

## Stratigraphy in Cutch and Kathiawar
In the regions of Cutch and Kathiawar, the Deccan Trap flows are underlaid by Neocomian and Jurassic beds. The Jurassic beds are marine in nature and contain numerous Ammonites. The Neocomian beds consist of shales and sandstones containing plant remains, some of which are identical to forms found in the upper portion of the Gondwana system.

Overlying the Deccan Trap in Cutch are shales, sandstones, and limestones of the Tertiary period. While these Tertiary deposits are present in Cutch, the greatest development of deposits of this age is found on the western side of the Indus.

## Regional Geography and Related Formations
The Deccan Trap formation is associated with the Western Ghats (or Sahyadri), a range of hills running down the coast with an average elevation of approximately 1,800 ft. These hills descend to the plains in terraces with abrupt fronts, a characteristic typical of trap formations. The crest of this range is sometimes only slightly raised above the level of the Deccan plateau.

Beyond the Bombay Presidency, in north-western Baluchistan, there is an extensive development of conglomerates and volcanic tuffs in the lower Tertiary and upper Cretaceous periods. These are believed to be contemporaneous with the Deccan Traps of India.

## Economic and Natural Derivatives
The disintegration of the Deccan traps has resulted in the formation of agate-gravels. In the neighborhood of Ratanpur, near Broach, these gravels are a source of carnelian, a red variety of translucent chalcedony used as an ornamental stone for seals. The red color of this stone is caused by the presence of ferric oxide.
