# Geology

## General Concepts and Terminology
In the fields of physical geography and geology, the term "horst" refers to a block of the earth's crust that has remained stationary while the surrounding land has either sunk or been crushed against it within a mountain range. Examples of the former include the Black Forest and the Vosges, while the Dole, Jura, and the Table are results of the latter. Additionally, the term is applied to larger stable areas characterized by horizontal table-land stratification, such as India, Arabia, Central South Africa, and the Russian plain, distinguishing them from folded regions like the Eurasian chains.

## Regional Geological Profiles

### Scotland and Ireland
In certain regions of Scotland, the hilly areas in the south are part of the Silurian tableland. This area features a Silurian belt extending from the Merrick range to the mouth of Loch Ryan, composed of shales, greywackes, and grits, including black shales with Upper Llandeilo age graptolites. In the Ballantrae region, there is a significant development of Arenig age volcanic rocks, including agglomerates, tuffs, and lavas. These are pierced by granite, gabbro, and serpentine. The latter is typically developed on Balhamie Hill near Colmonell. Other features include Radiolarian cherts and mudstones of Arenig age, and a sequence of Llandovery-Tarannon to Llandeilo-Caradoc strata south of the river Girvan.

In Ireland, specifically within certain counties, the landscape is largely formed of typical grey Carboniferous limestone, as seen in the flat land around Clane and the natural steps of the Salmon Falls at Leixlip. The higher country to the south-east consists of broken Silurian shales rising toward the Leinster chain. The granite core of this chain appears around Castledermot, featuring a margin of mica-schist. North of Kildare town, a ridge of Silurian rocks—including basic lavas—rises from the plain, with Old Red Sandstone on its flanks. The limestone in this specific ridge contains fossils of Bala age. The low ground is further characterized by masses of glacial gravel and eskers, particularly at the sandy plateau of the Curragh, while other areas contain extensive bogs.

In County Kilkenny, the area forms the south-eastern extremity of Ireland's great central plain. However, the plain is interrupted in the north by a hilly region that is part of the Castlecomer coal-field, which also extends into Tipperary and Queen's County.

### Asia and Korea
The geology of Korea is imperfectly known, though crystalline schists form all the higher mountain ranges and contain the country's mineral wealth. These schists are strongly folded. Near the Manchurian frontier, they are unconformably covered by limestones, clay-slates, and sandstones (approximately 1600 ft.) containing Cambrian fossils. In the south-eastern provinces, Carboniferous beds of conglomerates, sandstones, and slates are present. While these contain some coal, the most important coal-bearing deposits are from the Tertiary period. Volcanic and eruptive rocks occur in the interior and on the island of Quelpart. On Quelpart, Hal-la-san (Mount Auckland) was reportedly in eruption in 1007 according to Chinese stories; otherwise, the region has been free of earthquakes in historic times and has no active volcanoes.

In Burma, the province is divided into natural divisions. Arakan consists of a strip on the seaward slopes of the Arakan Yomas, with barren hilly country in the north and rich alluvial plains in the south and west. The main body of Burma lies in the Irrawaddy basin, which includes a highland tract of rugged hills and narrow gorges at the sources of the Irrawaddy and Chindwin. Within the Shan States, there are fertile open plateaus, including the Pyin-u-lwin plateau where Maymyo stands, approximately 3500 ft. above sea level.

## Notable Figures in Geology
Thomas George Bonney (1833–) was an English geologist and professor at University College, London. He served as president of the Mineralogical Society and the Alpine Club, and was secretary and president of the Geological Society (1884-1886). His research focused on metamorphic and igneous rocks in various locations, including the Scottish Highlands, Wales, Charnwood Forest, Salcombe, and the Lizard, as well as Alpine regions. His scientific publications include *The Story of our Planet* (1893), *Cambridgeshire Geology* (1875), *Ice Work, Past and Present* (1896), *Volcanoes* (1899), and *Charles Lyell and Modern Geology* (1895).
