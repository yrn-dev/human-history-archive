# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the procurement of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

In the earliest stages of toolmaking, the relationship between the maker and the material was opportunistic. However, as cognitive capacities expanded, so did the distance between the quarry and the campsite, signaling a shift toward strategic planning and foresight.

## From Opportunism to Curation
The Oldowan industry (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago) was characterized by "expedient" technology. *Homo habilis* typically utilized whatever river cobbles or basalt fragments were immediately available. If a suitable stone was found, it was knapped; if not, the toolmaker moved on. There is little evidence of long-distance transport, suggesting a mental map limited to the immediate vicinity.

By the time of the Acheulean (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), *Homo erectus* began demonstrating "raw material selectivity." Handaxes were not just made from any stone, but from specific materials—such as quartzite or flint—that offered predictable fracturing patterns. Archaeologists have found evidence of these materials being transported several kilometers from their source, indicating that hominins were beginning to prioritize quality over convenience.

## The Mousterian Logistics Network
The transition to the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) marked a sophisticated shift toward curation. The Levallois technique required a high-quality core to produce a specific, predetermined flake. Because these cores were labor-intensive to prepare, they became valuable assets.

Neanderthals didn't just seek out stone; they managed it. We see evidence of "curated" toolkits, where cores were carried over long distances and carefully reworked to maximize every gram of material. This suggests a sophisticated understanding of geology and a level of foresight—planning for a need that would arise days or weeks in the future—that was absent in the Oldowan era.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?
A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the site) proves the existence of early trade networks between different hominin groups, or if it simply reflects the wide migratory ranges of a single group. While some argue for a proto-economic exchange of high-quality flint, others maintain that these patterns are purely a result of nomadic movement.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic; materials sourced within meters of the tool's use.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; materials transported over several kilometers.
* **Mousterian:** High curation; strategic sourcing of high-quality flint to support the Levallois technique.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were preferred for their predictable "conchoidal" fractures.
* **Cognitive Shift:** The move from expedient to curated tools reflects an evolution in executive function and foresight.

### Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Core:** Some Mousterian cores show evidence of being carried for over 10 kilometers, a significant distance for a prehistoric hominin.
* **Materiality:** Not all stone is created equal; basalt is durable but harder to flake, whereas flint is brittle but allows for surgical precision.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians study "debitage" (the waste flakes left behind) to determine if a tool was made on-site or brought in finished from a quarry.
