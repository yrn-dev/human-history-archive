# Bryophyta and Related Botanical Groups

## Relationship to Thallophyta and Pteridophyta
The relationship between the Bryophyta, the Thallophyta, and the Pteridophyta is a subject characterized by speculation and a lack of decisive evidence. In a general sense, Bryophyta may be viewed as having derived from an algal ancestry, although the specific geological period of their emergence and the nature of their ancestral forms remain unknown.

Previous research suggested a close comparison between the alternation of generations in *Riccia* and certain Algae, such as *Coleochaete*. However, more recent studies have indicated that the body resulting from the segmentation of the fertilized ovum in these two cases is not as strictly comparable as was previously believed.

Regarding the internal development of Bryophytes, the existence of increasingly complex sporogonia can be explained by an hypothesis of progressive sterilization of sporogenous tissue, as proposed by Bower. Conversely, some indications of reduction in the Bryophyte sporogonium suggest that an alternative view of its origin is possible. While the alternating generations of Bryophyta and Pteridophyta are strictly comparable, no evidence of an actual relationship between the two groups has been found.

## Vegetative Propagation in Mosses
Vegetative propagation is common among mosses. A protonema is always formed during the development of a new plant. The social growth seen in many mosses results from the formation of numerous plants on the original protonema and from developments originating from the rhizoids.

Reproduction can also occur through gemmae, which may form at the apex, on the leaves, or on the protonema. Certain mosses possess specialized shoots to improve the distribution or protection of these gemmae. For example, in *Georgia*, multicellular stalked gemmae are located at the ends of shoots and are surrounded by a rosette of larger leaves. In *Aulacomnium androgynum*, gemmae are raised on a leafless, elongated region of the shoot.

Additionally, new plants can arise from detached shoots or leaves; almost any fragment of a moss can serve for reproduction if artificially divided. In rare instances where the sexual generation develops without spore production from the sporogonium tissues, a protonema can form from intact sporogonia still attached to the plant or from cut pieces of the seta. This process is known as apospory, a phenomenon first discovered in mosses and also found in several ferns.

## Pteridophyta: Lycopodium and Equisetum
The Pteridophyta include various vascular cryptogamous plants, such as those in the genera *Lycopodium* and *Equisetum*.

### Lycopodium
*Lycopodium* is the primary genus of the Lycopodiaceae, a natural order of the Fern-allies. These flowerless herbs feature stems that may be creeping, prostrate, or erect and widely branched, covered thickly in small simple leaves. Spore-cases (sporangia) containing a single kind of spore are borne in the axils of "fertile" leaves, which are arranged in cones. The spore develops into a prothallium, which is a subterranean mass of tissue of considerable size containing female organs (archegonia) and male organs (antheridia). There are approximately one hundred species distributed across tropical and temperate climates. In Britain, five species known as club-mosses occur on moors and heaths, primarily in mountainous areas. *L. clavatum* is specifically known as stag-horn moss.

### Equisetum
*Equisetum* (Horsetail) is the sole genus of the natural order Equisetaceae. These herbaceous plants grow from a much-branched underground rootstock. They produce slender, ribbed, green aerial shoots with a whorl of leaves reduced to a toothed sheath at each node. Whorls of more slender branches also spring from these nodes.

The shoots are categorized as either sterile or fertile. Fertile shoots bear a fructification at the apex—a cylindrical, oblong, oval, or dense conical spike. This spike consists of shortly-stalked peltate scales, with a circle of sporangia attached to the under surface of each scale; these sporangia open via a longitudinal slit on the inner side. 

The spores of *Equisetum* differ from those of ferns because their outer coat (exospore) is split into four hygroscopic club-shaped threads called elaters, which straighten when dry and curl when moist. While fertile and sterile shoots are alike in most species, they differ in some; for instance, in *E. arvense*, the fertile shoots are unbranched, not green, and appear first in the spring. Because any broken portion of the underground rhizome can produce a new plant, *Equisetum* is difficult to eradicate. There are 24 known species of this genus, which is distributed universally.
