# Geological and Structural Folding

Folding is a primary geological process responsible for the creation of various mountain ranges and structural features across the globe. This process involves the crumpling or bending of the earth's crust, often resulting in parallel ranges, arcs, or complex zones of deformation.

## Folding in North America
In British Columbia, the landscape is essentially mountainous, characterized by a series of parallel mountain ranges separated by long, narrow valleys. This region comprises nearly the entire width of the North American Cordillera (or Cordilleran belt). The area is dominated by two ruling systems: the Rocky Mountains to the northeast and the Coast Range to the southwest. Between these two systems lie the "Interior Plateau"—an elevated tract of hilly country—and various subordinate ranges. These ranges were produced by successive foldings of the earth's crust in a direction parallel to the Pacific Ocean border, resulting in a common trend that runs south-east and north-west. Additionally, the Queen Charlotte Islands and Vancouver Island are remnants of another mountain range that ran parallel to the coast but is now almost entirely submerged.

## Folding in Asia
The tectonic structure of Asia is characterized by a broad central belt of folding situated between two more rigid masses to the north and south. This intermediate belt consists of softer rock capable of folding, whereas the northern and southern masses are too rigid to crumple and instead yield through faulting.

### Central and Southern Asia
The principal mountain ranges of central Asia, including Afghanistan, Persia, Asia Minor, Baluchistan, the Tian-shan, the Himalayas, and the Burmese ranges, form a series of arcs extending from west to east. The Himalayan range's elevation was specifically produced by an overthrust from the north, as if the high land of central Asia was pushed southward against the unyielding old rocks of the Indian peninsula. 

The Kuen-lun Mountains act as the backbone of the continent, stretching nearly 2,500 miles from east to west. In the west, these ranges are folded and squeezed by lateral compression into a breadth of 150–200 miles, forcing summits to higher altitudes. In the east, they are less folded, spreading to a breadth of 600 miles and remaining flatter and lower.

### Northern and Eastern Asia
North of the central folded belt, including Mongolia, northern China, and much of Siberia, the region is generally free from important Mesozoic or Tertiary folding. While some mountain ranges in this area are formed of folded beds, these are mostly ancient (Palaeozoic or Archaean), and the ranges' elevation is often due to faulting rather than folding. In southern China, the structure consists largely of folded mountain chains, though marine deposits of Tertiary or Mesozoic age are absent except near the Burmese and Tibetan borders.

## Folding in Europe
### The Carpathians
The Carpathians are divided into an inner zone of older rocks and an outer zone of newer beds. The outer zone is remarkably uniform, consisting of Cretaceous and Tertiary sandstones and shales (Carpathian Sandstone) thrown into isoclinal folds that dip constantly to the south; this folding occurred during the Miocene period. The inner zone is more complex; in the western Carpathians, Carboniferous and older rocks were folded and denuded before later strata were deposited. In the eastern Carpathians, Permian and Mesozoic beds are only heavily folded near the outer margin of the zone.

### The Balkan Peninsula
The Balkan Peninsula features two belts of folded beds bordering a central region of crystalline and schistose rocks. To the north, these folds form the chain of the Balkans, and to the west, they form the mountain ranges of the Ionian and Adriatic coasts. In the western folded belt, the strike of the folds is N.N.W.-S.S.E. or N.W.-S.E. The involvement of Sarmatian beds indicates that this folding was not completed until Pliocene times.

## Non-Geological Applications of Folding
The term "folding" is also applied in other contexts within the archive's records:
*   **Botany:** The number and nature of divisions in a fruit depend on the extent to which the edges of the carpels are folded inwards. For example, in flax (*Linum*), a five-celled ovary becomes a ten-celled fruit through the folding inwards of the back of the carpels.
*   **Bookbinding:** Around the 5th century, the method of folding vellum into pages was adopted. Sheets were folded once to create gatherings of four or more sheets, which were then sewn together.
*   **Agriculture:** In ancient legal codes, "folding" refers to the practice of penning livestock. For instance, a shepherd who turned livestock into standing corn when they ought to have been folded was required to pay twelve-fold damages.
