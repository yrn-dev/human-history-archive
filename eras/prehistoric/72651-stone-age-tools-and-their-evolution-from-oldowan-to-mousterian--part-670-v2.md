# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the refined Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and transport.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding how early humans selected and moved stone provides critical insight into their territorial range and social organization.

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

During the Oldowan period (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), *Homo habilis* generally utilized whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often scavenging materials from riverbeds. The "cost" of transporting stone was high relative to the cognitive planning of the time; consequently, tools were often discarded where they were made.

As we move into the Acheulean era, characterized by the iconic hand-axe, we see a marked increase in selectivity. *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials—such as quartzite or fine-grained basalt—that could hold a sharper edge and withstand more percussion. This indicates a mental map of the landscape, where hominins remembered the locations of specific outcrops across several kilometers.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of Quality

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique, a hallmark of this era, required a specific type of stone with predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). 

Archaeological evidence suggests that Mousterian toolmakers were not merely gathering stone; they were managing it. We see evidence of "curated" tools—pieces that were carried over long distances and resharpened multiple times rather than being discarded. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this represents the earliest form of trade or simple territorial expansion, but the result is clear: the tool was no longer a disposable object, but a valued asset.

## Key Facts: Materiality in the Stone Age

*   **Conchoidal Fracture:** The essential property of stones like flint, chert, and obsidian that allows them to break in predictable, shell-like curves.
*   **Transport Distance:** Oldowan materials are typically found within meters of their source; Mousterian materials are often found kilometers away.
*   **Curation:** The process of maintaining a tool through retouching, extending its lifespan and reducing the need for new raw materials.
*   **Lithic Analysis:** The scientific study of stone tools to determine the geographic origin of the raw material.

## Did You Know?

*   **Obsidian Obsession:** Because obsidian (volcanic glass) produces the sharpest edge possible, some prehistoric groups transported it over 100 kilometers, suggesting high value and potential social exchange.
*   **The "Core" Strategy:** In the Mousterian period, hominins would often prepare a "core" (a large piece of stone) at a quarry and carry that core with them to strike off smaller, usable flakes as needed.
*   **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans possessed an intuitive understanding of mineralogy, knowing exactly which stones would shatter and which would crush under a hammerstone.
