# Alum Bay and White Cliff Bay

Alum Bay and White Cliff Bay are locations in the Isle of Wight where the lower division of the Bagshot beds is well exposed. These geological formations are part of a series of sands and clays of shallow-water origin—some marine and some fresh-water—belonging to the upper Eocene formation of the Hampshire and London basins.

## Geological Composition
At Alum Bay, which reaches a height of 660 ft., and White Cliff Bay, which reaches 140 ft., the lower division of the Bagshot beds consists of clays and unfossiliferous sands. These sands appear in a wide variety of colors, including white, brown, yellow, and crimson, as well as every intermediate shade. The formation also includes layers of ferruginous sandstone and lignite.

In Alum Bay specifically, the Bracklesham beds—which are sometimes classified as Middle Bagshot—reach a thickness of 100 ft. The lower portion of these beds contains lignite. In this location, the Bracklesham beds are sharply distinguished from the Barton clay by a bed of conglomerate composed of flint pebbles. The Upper Bagshot beds, which include the Barton clay and Barton sand, range from 140 to 200 ft. in thickness across the Isle of Wight.

## Paleontology and Natural History
The clays found at Alum Bay, as well as those at Bournemouth, are recognized as leaf-bearing clays. These deposits have provided a large and interesting series of plant remains. Identified specimens include:
*   *Populus*
*   *Platanus*
*   *Sequoia*
*   *Eucalyptus*
*   *Caesalpinia*
*   *Aralia*
*   *Osmunda*
*   *Polypodium*
*   *Nipadites*

## Economic Use
The geological materials of the region have historically served various industrial purposes. Alum was formerly extracted from the clays of Alum Bay. Additionally, the lignites found within these formations have been utilized as fuel, specifically near Bovey and Corfe.
