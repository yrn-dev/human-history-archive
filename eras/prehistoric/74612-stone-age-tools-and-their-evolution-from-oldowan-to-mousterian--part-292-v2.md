# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shift from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of tool technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The transition from the Lower to the Middle Paleolithic is marked by a shift from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding where a stone came from reveals as much about the mind of the toolmaker as the tool itself.

## From Localism to Long-Distance Transport

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), hominins generally utilized "local" materials. They gathered cobbles from nearby stream beds or surface deposits, often selecting any stone that could produce a sharp edge, regardless of its quality. The "cost" of transporting heavy raw materials over long distances was too high, or the cognitive mapping required to return to a specific quarry was not yet developed.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a dramatic shift. Hominins began to exhibit "selective sourcing," bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials allow for predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture), which is essential for the Levallois technique used in Mousterian toolkits.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Neanderthals and early *Homo sapiens*, the transport of raw materials had become a sophisticated economic activity. Archaeological evidence shows that high-quality stone was often carried over dozens, and sometimes hundreds, of kilometers from its original source.

This suggests several critical behavioral evolutions:
1. **Planning Depth:** The ability to anticipate future needs by carrying "cores" (unworked stone) to be knapped later.
2. **Territorial Mapping:** A mental map of the landscape that identified specific geological veins of high-quality stone.
3. **Social Exchange:** There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were transported by individuals or traded between social groups, which would indicate a complex social structure.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic; materials sourced within a few hundred meters of the site.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that could support the symmetry of the handaxe.
* **Mousterian:** High-precision sourcing; focus on materials capable of producing thin, sharp flakes.
* **Lithic Analysis:** Modern historians use "sourcing" or "provenance studies" to track the movement of prehistoric populations.
* **Transport Costs:** The distance stone traveled is often used as a proxy for the size of a group's home range.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian is volcanic glass and only found in specific regions, it is one of the best materials for historians to track ancient migration and trade routes.
* **Curated Tools:** In the Mousterian era, tools were often "resharpened" multiple times, reducing the tool's size over time to avoid the need for a new piece of raw stone.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** Rather than carrying finished tools, later Paleolithic humans often carried "prepared cores," which acted like a portable toolkit from which multiple blades could be struck.
