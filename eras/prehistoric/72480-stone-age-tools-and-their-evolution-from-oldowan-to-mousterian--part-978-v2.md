# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of tool technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The ability to identify, acquire, and transport high-quality stone is a primary indicator of evolving spatial memory and social planning. The shift from "opportunistic" gathering to "strategic" sourcing marks a critical turning point in human evolution.

## From Local Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. Most artifacts are found in close proximity to the original source of the stone—usually river cobbles or surface outcrops. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used. The "economy" of the Oldowan was one of immediate need; tools were often discarded as soon as they became dull.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective procurement." They no longer settled for any hard stone; they sought out materials with specific conchoidal fracture patterns, such as flint, chert, or obsidian, which allowed for sharper edges and more predictable flaking.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) reveals a sophisticated understanding of resource management. Archaeological evidence shows that high-quality stone was often transported over distances of several kilometers, and in some cases, tens of kilometers, from its source.

This transport implies several cognitive advancements:
1. **Forethought:** The toolmaker anticipated a future need for a tool in a location where stone was unavailable.
2. **Curation:** Instead of discarding tools, hominins began "resharpening" or retouching edges to extend the life of the tool.
3. **Social Exchange:** There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether the presence of non-local stone indicates individual travel or the earliest forms of social trading between different bands of hominins.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily utilized local river cobbles; minimal transport distances.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity for materials that could support the symmetry of the handaxe.
* **Mousterian:** High reliance on "prepared cores" (Levallois technique), requiring specific, high-quality stone.
* **Lithic Analysis:** Modern historians use "sourcing" or "provenance studies" to track where stone originated.
* **Material Preference:** Flint and obsidian were prized for their ability to produce edges thinner than a surgical scalpel.

## Did You Know?
* **The Obsidian Trail:** In some prehistoric sites, obsidian has been found hundreds of miles from the nearest volcanic source, suggesting vast networks of movement or exchange.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Some Mousterian tools show signs of being carried for weeks, with repeated "touch-ups" to the edge to keep them functional.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to possess an intuitive understanding of geology to distinguish between "usable" stone and "brittle" stone before they ever struck the first blow.
