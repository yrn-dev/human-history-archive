# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian point—we must shift our focus to the *provenance* of the stone itself. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in logistics and geological knowledge.

For the early hominin, a tool was only as effective as the material used to create it. The transition from the opportunistic gathering of river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality flint and obsidian reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric humans interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartz pebbles from stream beds. The "transport distance" was minimal, suggesting a nomadic existence tied closely to immediate resource availability.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, we see the emergence of "raw material selectivity." Hominins began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates (like chert or flint) that provided a more predictable conchoidal fracture. This indicates a mental map of the environment; the toolmaker was no longer just looking at the ground, but remembering where the "good stone" lived.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the Mousterian period (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The introduction of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required materials of exceptional quality. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers transported high-quality stone over distances of several kilometers, and in some cases, tens of kilometers. This suggests a planned economy of movement. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individual groups maintained seasonal quarrying sites. Regardless, the movement of stone implies a level of foresight and social organization previously unseen in the Oldowan era.

## Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The key physical property of stones like flint and obsidian that allows them to break into sharp, curved edges.
* **Transport Cost:** The energy expended to move raw materials, which increased as hominins became more selective about stone quality.
* **Core Preparation:** The shift from using a stone as a "hammer" (Oldowan) to treating a stone as a "source" for multiple blades (Mousterian).
* **Geological Literacy:** The evolved ability of prehistoric humans to identify specific rock strata and outcrops.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some later prehistoric contexts, obsidian has been found hundreds of miles from its volcanic source, suggesting early complex trade networks.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after use, Mousterian tools were often "curated," meaning they were resharpened and carried for long periods.
* **Material Bias:** Some archaeologists argue that our understanding of early tools is skewed because we only find tools made of durable stone; tools made of wood or bone decayed long ago.
