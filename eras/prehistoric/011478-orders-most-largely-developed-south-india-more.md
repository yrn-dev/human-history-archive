# Flora, History, and Development of India

## Botanical Characteristics of South India
The flora of the Indian peninsula and Ceylon exhibits specific developmental patterns, particularly regarding certain plant orders. The orders most largely developed in south India, and more sparingly in other regions, include the Aurantiaceae, Dipterocarpaceae, Balsaminaceae, Ebenaceae, Jasmineae, and Cyrtandraceae. While these are prominent, few of these orders contain as many as 100 species peculiar to India. The Balsaminaceae, specifically, are very abundant in the peninsula but rare in the eastern region.

A distinct connection exists between the flora of Ceylon and the peninsula and that of eastern tropical Africa. This is evidenced by the identity of genera and families and a similarity in tropical forms. Most remarkably, the mountain flora of this part of Africa closely resembles that of the peninsula, with several species believed to be identical to Abyssinian forms. This relationship is further supported by the absence of cycads, conifers, and oaks in both areas. The absence of the latter two is a notable feature of the peninsula and Ceylon, as these families are abundant to the north and east in mountains of similar elevations. Additionally, many principal forms of the eastern flora are rare or absent in Ceylon and the peninsula.

Other notable plants in the region include the genus *Begonia* (of the order Begoniaceae), which consists of succulent undershrubs or herbs. This genus is especially prevalent in India and South America, with approximately 350 species found in tropical moist climates.

## Early Human Development and Prehistory
The origins of Neolithic peoples—the ancestors of white and whitish peoples—are not yet fully known, but they likely developed their Later Palæolithic arts in south-western Asia, India, north Africa, Persia, Arabia, Asia Minor, or regions now submerged beneath the Indian Ocean or Mediterranean Sea. While Neanderthal men lived in glaciated Europe, these southern peoples mastered cattle domestication, the taming of the dog, and agriculture. Approximately 12,000 years ago, these Neolithic peoples, who were the most advanced in the world at the time, were scattered across Asia, north Africa, and Europe.

## Religious and Intellectual History
Indian history prior to Mahommedan times is characterized by the prominence of religious ideas and intellectual development over political events. National unity among Hindu peoples was maintained through the penetrating influence of the Brahman caste; kings were generally viewed as adventitious tyrants rather than national representatives. Consequently, great dynasties left few traces, and minor kingdoms are difficult for historians to disinter. However, the art, literature, and religion of India influenced all of Asia, from Japan to Persia.

In the realm of Buddhism, early development can be traced through the first eight or nine centuries. While many early works were composed in the north of India—specifically north of or a few miles south of the Ganges—significant scholarship also emerged from the south. Fourteen volumes of commentaries by 5th-century scholars in Ceylon and south India are accessible in Pāli. These include works by Dhammapāla of Kancipura (the ancient name of Conjeeveram) and Buddhaghosa of Budh Gaya, providing historical data on the religious and social life of the period.

## Political Transitions and Invasions
India experienced various waves of foreign incursions. Hungry nomads entered through the Khyber Pass from central Asia, leading to a period where the empire of Asoka was broken up. The "Indo-Scythians" established the Kushan dynasty, which ruled over North India for a time. In the fifth century A.D., the Ephthalites (or White Huns) terrorized India, levying tribute on small princes while pasturing in western Turkestan during the summer.

In 1526, the Moguls seized the throne of Delhi after descending from Transoxiana. While they founded a brilliant empire in the north under rulers such as Shah Jehan and Akbar, they never subjugated the south. This empire began to decline after 1707, leading to the rise of a powerful Mahratta confederacy in central India and invasions by Ahmed Shah and Nadir Shah of Persia. The authority of the Mogul empire lasted in name until the Mutiny of 1857.

## British India and Colonial Administration
The British presence in India was established by a trading company, reflecting a broader imperial pattern where trade served as the primary cause of expansion. In Bengal, the town of Barrackpur became a significant magisterial subdivision and the largest cantonment in Lower Bengal. Established in 1689 by Job Charnock (the founder of Calcutta), it became a military station in 1772. The town features a parade ground, a large bazaar, and a park created by Lord Wellesley, which contains the Government House and Lady Canning's tomb. By 1901, the population of south Barrackpur was 19,307 and north Barrackpur was 12,600. Barrackpur was also a site of importance during the Sepoy mutinies of 1824 and 1857.

## Paleontology
Fossil records in India include the Order Struthiones; the genus *Struthio* (ostrich) is found in the Pliocene of north-west India. Additionally, the Pliocene of the Sivalik Hills contains *Hypselornis* of the Order Casuarii.
