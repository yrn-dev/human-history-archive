# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the "where" and "how" of the raw materials. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a revolution in geological prospecting and logistical planning.

To understand the shift from the Lower to the Middle Paleolithic, we must look beyond the finished tool and examine the movement of the stone itself.

## The Localism of the Oldowan
For the early *Homo habilis*, the world was small. Oldowan tool production was characterized by extreme "expediency." Hominins typically utilized whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often scavenging materials from dry riverbeds. There is little evidence of selective transport; if a stone could be knapped to produce a sharp edge, it was used. The cognitive load was focused on the *act* of striking, not the *acquisition* of the medium.

## The Acheulean Expansion and Selective Transport
As *Homo erectus* developed the Acheulean handaxe, a shift occurred. The symmetry and durability required for these "Swiss Army knives" of the Pleistocene demanded higher-quality materials, such as quartzite or flint. 

Archaeological evidence suggests that Acheulean hominins began transporting raw materials over significant distances—sometimes several kilometers—from the source to the habitation site. This indicates a burgeoning capacity for foresight. They were no longer reacting to their environment; they were planning for future needs, selecting specific stones based on their fracturing properties.

## The Mousterian and the Levallois Strategy
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with stone had become highly strategic. The introduction of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape—required a high grade of cryptocrystalline silicate.

This era saw the emergence of "quarrying." Rather than opportunistic gathering, Middle Paleolithic humans sought out specific geological veins. This logistical sophistication suggests a complex mental map of the landscape and a social structure capable of organizing the transport of heavy raw cores across territories.

## Key Facts
* **Expediency:** Oldowan tools were typically made from the nearest available stone, regardless of quality.
* **Selective Transport:** Acheulean toolmakers began choosing specific materials, moving them across the landscape.
* **The Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated "prepared core" method that minimized waste and maximized the utility of the raw material.
* **Lithic Sourcing:** Modern historians use "sourcing" (chemical analysis of stone) to track prehistoric migration patterns.
* **Material Bias:** The archaeological record is biased toward durable stones; tools made from wood or bone rarely survive.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Some Acheulean handaxes have been found hundreds of kilometers away from their geological source, suggesting they were prized possessions or traded.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The transition to Mousterian tools implies that Neanderthals possessed "spatial memory," allowing them to remember the locations of high-quality flint mines over many seasons.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians study "debitage" (the waste flakes left over from tool making) to determine if a site was a workshop or a place where finished tools were simply used.
