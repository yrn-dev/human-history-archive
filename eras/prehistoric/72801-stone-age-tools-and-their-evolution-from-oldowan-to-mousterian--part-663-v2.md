# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shift from the crude Oldowan choppers to the refined Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and spatial planning.

For a prehistoric hominin, a tool was only as effective as the stone from which it was knapped. The transition across these three industries reflects a growing sophistication in how early humans interacted with their landscape and managed their resources.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was primarily opportunistic. Early *Homo* typically used whatever river cobbles or basalt fragments were immediately available in their vicinity. The "cost" of transporting heavy stones over long distances was too high, meaning the toolkits were ephemeral—made, used, and discarded within a few hundred meters of the source.

As we move into the Acheulean period, characterized by the iconic hand-axe, we see the emergence of selective sourcing. Hominins began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of materials with predictable fracturing patterns, such as quartzite or fine-grained volcanic rocks. This suggests a mental map of the environment, where the maker knew that a superior material existed several kilometers away and planned their journey accordingly.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of Quality
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly strategic. The Levallois technique—a sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality flint or chert to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers frequently transported high-grade stone over distances of 10 to 30 kilometers. This indicates a shift from "immediate return" foraging to a "delayed return" economy. The effort spent quarrying and carrying stone was an investment in the future efficiency of the hunt.

## Scholarly Debate: The "Curated" Toolkit
There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding the degree of "curation" in these early toolkits. Some archaeologists argue that the transport of stone suggests a high level of foresight and social organization. Others suggest that these movements were simply a byproduct of wider migratory patterns, arguing that the stones were collected incidentally during seasonal movements rather than through targeted quarrying.

***

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic sourcing; tools rarely moved far from the raw material source.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that allowed for symmetrical shaping.
* **Mousterian:** High-investment sourcing; strategic transport of flint and chert over significant distances.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) were prized for their "conchoidal" fracture, allowing for razor-sharp edges.
* **Logistics:** The shift in sourcing reflects an evolution in cognitive mapping and long-term planning.

### Did You Know?
* **Natural Selection:** Some early hominins utilized "geofacts"—stones naturally broken by river currents—before they learned to intentionally flake them.
* **The Flint Trade:** Some evidence suggests that late Paleolithic groups may have exchanged high-quality stone between different bands, hinting at the earliest forms of trade.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians determine where tools were made by looking for "debitage" (the tiny waste flakes left over), which is rarely transported away from the site of production.
