# The Dawn of Domestication: Shaping the Prehistoric World

## Overview
The transition from nomadic hunting and gathering to settled agriculture was one of the most profound shifts in human history. Central to this transformation was the domestication of animals—the process of adapting wild species for human use. This was not a single event, but a gradual evolutionary process involving selective breeding and symbiotic relationships. While dogs were the first to join human camps for mutual survival, the later domestication of cats and livestock fundamentally altered human nutrition, labor, and social structures.

## Key Facts

### The Canine Partnership
Dogs (*Canis lupus familiaris*) are unique because they were domesticated before the advent of agriculture. Historians and geneticists debate the exact timing and location, with some arguing for a single origin in Eurasia and others suggesting dual origins in Europe and Asia. The relationship likely began as "commensalism," where less aggressive wolves scavenged near human camps, eventually evolving into a cooperative partnership for hunting and protection.

### The Livestock Revolution
The domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle in the Fertile Crescent (the "Neolithic Revolution") allowed humans to move from a precarious reliance on wild game to a stable food supply. This surplus of calories supported population growth and the rise of permanent settlements. The selection of traits—such as docility and higher milk yield—gradually changed the morphology of these animals over generations.

### The Feline Symbiosis
Unlike dogs, cats (*Felis catus*) likely domesticated themselves. As early farmers stored grain, they attracted rodents. Wildcats (*Felis lybica*) moved into these settlements to hunt the pests. Humans tolerated the cats for their utility, leading to a slow transition from wild predators to household companions.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Founder Effect":** Most modern livestock descend from a very small number of wild ancestors, which is why domestic breeds often look significantly different from their wild counterparts (e.g., domestic sheep vs. mouflon).
* **The First Evidence:** Some of the earliest evidence for dog domestication comes from the Bonn-Oberkassel dog in Germany, dated to approximately 14,000 years ago.
* **Milk Evolution:** The domestication of cattle coincided with a genetic mutation in some human populations that allowed adults to digest lactose, a trait known as lactase persistence.
