# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

For the early toolmaker, the world was not a uniform expanse, but a map of geological "hotspots." The transition from opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic mining of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in spatial memory and planning.

## From Opportunism to Curation
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. The "raw material economy" was local; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used. There was little evidence of long-term planning or the transport of stone over great distances.

As we move into the Acheulean era, characterized by the iconic handaxe, we see the emergence of "curation." *Homo erectus* began to select specific materials—such as fine-grained flint or obsidian—that allowed for more precise flaking. Archaeologists have discovered tool assemblages where the stone sourced originates from quarries several kilometers away, suggesting that hominins were beginning to anticipate future needs rather than reacting to immediate requirements.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of Quality
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique required a specific type of "core" stone that could withstand precise preparation. This necessitated a rigorous screening process: not every rock was suitable.

This era saw the rise of strategic transport. Neanderthals often carried high-quality lithic materials over 10 to 20 kilometers, bypassing inferior local stones. This behavior indicates a complex cognitive map of the environment and a social structure capable of coordinating the movement of resources. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between groups or if individual foragers simply possessed an intimate knowledge of distant geological veins.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily local, utilizing river cobbles and opportunistic finds.
* **Acheulean Shift:** Increased selectivity for materials that could hold a sharp edge longer.
* **Mousterian Logistics:** Evidence of long-distance transport of high-quality flint and obsidian.
* **Cognitive Leap:** The shift from "immediate use" to "curated toolkits" indicates advanced foresight.
* **Material Preference:** Fine-grained cryptocrystalline silicates were preferred for their predictable fracturing patterns.

## Did You Know?
* **The Obsidian Trail:** In some prehistoric sites, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers from its volcanic source, suggesting early networks of exchange.
* **The "Wrong" Stone:** Early hominins occasionally attempted to make tools from unsuitable materials, leaving behind "failed" cores that help historians understand their learning process.
* **Geological Literacy:** To a Mousterian toolmaker, a landscape was read not just for prey, but for the specific "strike" and "grain" of the earth.
