# The Dawn of Domestication: Shaping the Prehistoric World

## Overview
The transition from nomadic hunting and gathering to settled agriculture was one of the most profound shifts in human history. Central to this transformation was the domestication of animals—a process of selective breeding where humans altered the genetics and behavior of wild species to serve specific needs. While the process was not a sudden "event" but rather a gradual co-evolution, it fundamentally altered human nutrition, security, and social structures.

## Key Facts

### The Canine Exception
Unlike livestock, dogs were domesticated during the Paleolithic era, long before the advent of farming. Historians and geneticists debate whether dogs were "tamed" by humans or if they "self-domesticated" by scavenging around human campsites. Regardless, the symbiotic relationship provided humans with superior tracking abilities and protection, facilitating the survival of early *Homo sapiens*.

### The Neolithic Revolution and Livestock
The domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle occurred primarily during the Neolithic period in the Fertile Crescent. This shift allowed humans to move from a precarious reliance on wild game to a stable supply of protein and fats. The introduction of cattle was particularly revolutionary, as they provided "traction"—the physical power needed to plow larger fields, which in turn supported larger human populations.

### The Feline Partnership
The domestication of cats is viewed by historians as a commensal relationship. As early farmers stored grain, they attracted rodents; these rodents attracted wildcats (*Felis lybica*). Humans tolerated the cats for their hunting skills, leading to a gradual domestication. Recent archaeological finds in Cyprus suggest cats were associated with humans as early as 9,500 years ago, though full domestication likely solidified later in Egypt.

## Did You Know?
* **Genetic Bottlenecks:** Domestication often leads to a "bottleneck" effect, where only a few individuals of a wild species are bred, significantly reducing the genetic diversity of the domestic descendants.
* **The Aurochs:** Modern domestic cattle are descended from the Aurochs, a massive, aggressive wild ox that is now extinct.
* **The "Dog-First" Theory:** Most historians agree that dogs were the first species domesticated, predating all livestock by thousands of years.
