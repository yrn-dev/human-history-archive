# London and Hampshire Basins

## Geological Definition of a Basin
In the field of geology, a basin is defined as a broad shallow syncline. It is a structure inherent to the bed rock of a specific district. This geological definition is distinct from a physiographic river basin, although the two may occasionally coincide. Strictly speaking, a structural basin is composed of rock beds that demonstrate a centroclinal dip; an elongated narrow trough or syncline does not qualify as a basin.

Other types of basins include "rock-basins," which are steep-sided, relatively small depressions scooped out of solid rock in mountainous areas primarily by glaciers. Additionally, some basins are formed by the removal of soluble constituents, such as rock salt, from underlying strata, or directly through crustal movements, and these are sometimes occupied by lakes.

## The London and Hampshire Basins
The London and Hampshire basins are two well-marked synclinal basins located in the south-eastern portion of England. They consist of Jurassic, Cretaceous, and Tertiary rocks. While these two basins are conterminous in the western area—specifically in Berkshire and Hampshire—they are separated toward the east by the denuded anticline of the Weald. The Paris basin is composed of strata similar to those found in the London and Hampshire basins.

## Eocene Stratigraphy
Except for the leaf-bearing beds of Mull and Antrim and the Bovey beds, Eocene rocks in Britain are confined to the south-eastern region of England within the London and Hampshire basins. Evidence from plant remains suggests the Eocene period began with a temperate climate in northern latitudes, though later indicators—including the Bournemouth beds, Alum Bay, and the London Clay—suggest temperatures were at least subtropical.

The strata within these basins are grouped as follows:

### The London Basin
The lower formations include the Thanet sands, which are pale yellow and greenish sands with clay streaks at the base, resting on a surface of chalk with a layer of green-coated, well-rounded chalk flint pebbles. Above these are the Woolwich and Reading beds, which contain estuarine and marine fossils. In western Kent, the Oldhaven beds (or Blackheath pebbles) consist of well-rounded flint pebbles in sand and are located between the Woolwich beds and the London Clay. The London Clay is a marine deposit 500 feet thick, consisting of brown or blue clay with septarian nodules and sandy layers.

### The Hampshire Basin
The Hampshire basin contains equivalents to many of the London strata. The Bognor Clay is the equivalent of the London Clay and is well exposed on the Sussex coast. However, the Thanet sands have not been recognized in the Hampshire basin.

The Hampshire Tertiary basin also contains the Barton Beds, which are absent from the London basin. These consist of Upper Eocene softish brown and grey clays with sand layers, particularly well exposed in the Isle of Wight and the cliffs of Hordwell and Barton. These are divided into the highly fossiliferous Barton Clay and the overlying sandy series known as the Barton Sands (or Headon Hill).

## The Bagshot Beds
The Bagshot Beds are a series of clays and sands of shallow-water origin, some marine and some fresh-water, belonging to the upper Eocene formation of both the Hampshire and London basins. They are well developed in the Isle of Wight, Hampshire, and derive their name from Bagshot Heath in Surrey.

The generally accepted divisions of the Bagshot Beds are:
*   **Upper Bagshot Beds:** Including the Barton clay and Barton sand.
*   **Middle Bagshot Beds:** Including the Bracklesham beds.
*   **Lower Bagshot Beds:** Including the Bovey Tracey beds (?), Alum Bay beds, and Bournemouth beds.

In the London basin, the Upper Bagshot Sands are likely situated at a lower horizon than the Barton Sands found in the Hampshire basin.
