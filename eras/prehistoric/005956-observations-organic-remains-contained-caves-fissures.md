# Observations on the Organic Remains contained in caves, fissures, and diluvial gravel

The study of organic remains found within caves, fissures, and diluvial gravel was significantly advanced by the work of Dr. Buckland. His primary contribution to this field was the publication of *Reliquiae Diluvianae, or Observations on the Organic Remains contained in caves, fissures, and diluvial gravel attesting the Action of a Universal Deluge* in 1823 (with a second edition in 1824). In this work, Buckland detailed observations regarding the remains of extinct animals discovered in the cavern of Kirkdale in Yorkshire and presented his views on how these findings related to the Biblical account of the Deluge.

## Bone Beds and Geological Deposits
In geological terms, a "bone bed" is a general term used to describe any stratum or deposit containing bones. This includes stalagmitic and brecciated deposits found on cave floors. In a more restricted sense, the term refers to thin layers of bony fragments on specific geological horizons. Examples include:
*   **The Ludlow Bone Bed:** Located at the base of the Downton Sandstone in the Upper Ludlow series, this bed consists primarily of scales, teeth, and spines of ganoid fish and extends 45 m from Ludlow into Gloucestershire.
*   **The "Bristol" or "Lias" Bone Bed:** Found in the Rhaetic Black Paper Shales above the Keuper marls in south-west England, containing remains of saurians and fish. Similar beds have been traced in Franconia, Hanover, and Brunswick.
*   **Carboniferous limestone series:** Bone beds have also been observed at the base of this series in parts of south-west England.

## Kent's Cavern
Known also as Kent's Hole, this is the largest of the English bone caves and is located about a mile east of Torquay harbour in a hill of Devonian limestone. It is noted for providing evidence that humans existed in Devon contemporaneously with animals that are now extinct or no longer indigenous. 

The cave consists of four distinct strata:
1.  **Surface:** Dark earth containing Roman pottery, medieval remains, and evidence of use during the Neolithic, Bronze, and Iron Ages.
2.  **Stalagmite floor:** A layer varying from 1 to 3 ft. in thickness.
3.  **Red earth:** This layer contains bones of the lion, mammoth, rhinoceros, and hyaena. It also contains an engraved antler and flint implements, proving human habitation. In some areas, a black band of soil containing charcoal, animal teeth and bones (some perforated for ornament), and numerous flint implements exists between the red earth and the stalagmite.
4.  **Hard breccia:** Filling the bottom of the cave (up to 12 ft. thick), this layer contains flint implements—generally ruder than those in higher strata—and the remains of bears.

The most notable animal remains found here are those of the Sabre-toothed tiger (*Machairodus latidens*). Scientific examination began in 1825 with Rev. J. McEnery, whose findings were later verified by Godwin Austen and a Committee of the British Association under Wm. Pengelly from 1865 to 1880.

## Brixham Cave
Also called Windmill Hill Cavern, this ossiferous cave is situated near Brixham in Devonian limestone. Discovered by chance in 1858, it had been sealed by limestone breccia until it was excavated by Dr. Hugh Falconer and a committee of geologists.

The cave's succession of beds, in descending order, includes:
*   Shingle (5 to 16 ft.) containing worked flints and a few bones.
*   Red cave earth (3 to 4 ft.) containing bones, worked flints, and angular limestone fragments.
*   An old stalagmitic floor (approximately 9 inches thick).
*   Black peaty soil (up to one foot thick).
*   Angular debris (1 to 10 ft.).
*   Stalagmite (1 to 15 inches) containing antlers of reindeer and a few bones.

Geologists suggest the cave formed simultaneously with the excavation of the valley, with streams carrying rounded pebbles into the cave. Animal remains are scarce in the shingle, suggesting animals only visited during droughts. As the valley excavation proceeded and the stream level lowered, the cave became a drier den for predatory animals. Eventually, it became a resort for bears; 334 specimens of bears in various stages of growth, including sucking cubs, were discovered. In total, 1,621 bones were found, including 691 from small rodents and birds of more recent times, as well as bones from the elephant, rhinoceros, horse, hyena, and lion. Seventeen roughly-chipped flint implements resembling the Mousterian period were also recovered.
