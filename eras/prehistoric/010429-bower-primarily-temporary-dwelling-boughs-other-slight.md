# Boughs and Temporary Dwellings

The use of boughs and slight materials has historically served various purposes across different eras and cultures, ranging from primitive housing and agricultural tools to military camouflage and commercial structures.

## Early Human Habitation and Tools
In the early history of the primitive Aryan, dwellings were often of a flimsy nature, likely constructed from mud and wattle. Evidence from ancient Aryan burial mounds (round barrows) indicates that their rounded huts featured thatched roofs, as represented by the house-shaped urns containing the ashes of the departed. 

Beyond housing, boughs were utilized in early agriculture. Before the development of more advanced methods, the primitive Aryan used a rough wooden hoe for cultivation, which typically occurred in garden patches near the home rather than in open fields. Once the use of cattle for draught purposes was discovered, the first real ploughs were created using a suitably bent tree bough.

## The Evolution of the Booth
The term "booth" is connected to a Teutonic root meaning to dwell (which also relates to the word "bower"). Originally, a booth was a temporary dwelling constructed from boughs or other slight materials. Over time, the meaning of the word evolved to describe non-permanent erections used for commerce, such as market stalls or tents at fairs where goods were sold.

In some instances, these temporary structures became permanent. In Old Fish Street, London, houses that began as movable boards used on market days for selling fish eventually became sheds, then shops, and finally tall houses after licenses were procured. The term "booth" was later applied to polling-booths, the temporary structures used for registering votes. 

In Scotland, the term appeared as *bothy* or *bothie*, generally referring to a cottage or hut. Specifically, it described a barrack-like room on large farms used to lodge unmarried labourers, a practice known as the "Bothy system" which was common in northern Scotland and Aberdeenshire.

## Military Applications
Boughs and branches have been employed by military forces for protection and concealment. During conflicts in France, French soldiers constructed small, low huts from boughs and branches in copses and woods to seek shelter from cold and rain. Additionally, the French utilized the leafy tops of trees to create concealed positions for machine guns.

In other military contexts, branches and twigs were used to disguise equipment. Provision wagons with light canvas awnings were covered in twigs and branches to make them look like "leafy bowers," a tactic necessitated by the need to hide from enemy air scouting. In contrast, German infantry utilized shelter-tents made of brown tarpaulin, which soldiers carried as part of their equipment and joined together to form temporary shelters.

## Natural and Ritual Contexts
Boughs are also mentioned in the context of natural disasters and ancient rituals. In the account of the calamities brought upon Egypt when Pharaoh refused the will of God, a severe hail—larger than that found in the north-western or northern regions during spring—fell and broke down boughs laden with fruit.

In the study of Neolithic peoples from 12,000 to 20,000 years ago, there was a primitive association between sowing and blood sacrifice. This entanglement of ideas, where a chosen youth or maiden was sacrificed as a ritual god-king during seed time, is explored in the work *The Golden Bough* by Sir J. G. Frazer.
