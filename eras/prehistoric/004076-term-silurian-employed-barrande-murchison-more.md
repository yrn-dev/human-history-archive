# The Silurian System and the Work of Joachim Barrande

The Silurian system refers to a period of geological formations characterized by specific fossiliferous strata. In Great Britain, the uppermost subdivision of these rocks is known as the Ludlow group, or Ludlovian. This group is primarily shaly, though it becomes more sandy toward the top, where it passes gradually into the base of the Old Red Sandstone. The Ludlow group consists of several formations in descending order: the Tilestones, Downton Castle sandstones (90 ft.), Ledbury shales (270 ft.), Upper Ludlow rocks (140 ft.), Aymestry limestone (up to 40 ft.), and Lower Ludlow rocks (350 to 780 ft.).

## The Ludlow Group and Fossil Records
The Upper Ludlow rocks are composed mainly of shales and soft mudstones, interspersed with harder sandy beds. Some of these sandy layers exhibit annelid tracks and ripple-marks, including the "Fucoid bed," named for its abundance of seaweed-like impressions. Near Ludlow, a "Ludlow Bone bed" exists—a brown layer ranging from a quarter of an inch to 4 inches in thickness—containing mollusca and fragmentary fish remains associated with *Pterygotus*. 

Common fossils within this group include:
*   **Plants:** *Chondrites* and *Actinophyllum*.
*   **Invertebrates:** Ostracods, phyllocarids, eurypterids, gasteropods, pelecypods, cephalopods (such as *Orthoceras bullatum*), and numerous brachiopods including *Chonetes striatella* and *Lingula minima*. Trilobites are present but are less common than in older groups.
*   **Fish:** *Cyathaspis*, *Cephalaspis*, and *Auchenaspis*.

Additionally, the Tilestones, Ledbury shales, and Downton Castle Sandstone are sometimes grouped as "Downtonian." Originally placed in the Old Red Sandstone system by Sir R. I. Murchison, these are now recognized as passage beds between the Silurian and Old Red Sandstone.

## Joachim Barrande and the Bohemian Silurian
Joachim Barrande (1799–1883) was an Austrian geologist and palaeontologist born in Saugues, Haute Loire. After receiving engineering training at the Ecole Polytechnique in Paris, he served as a tutor to the duc de Bordeaux. Following the abdication of the king in 1830, Barrande moved to England, Scotland, and eventually Prague in 1831. 

In 1839, the publication of Murchison's *Silurian System* prompted Barrande to conduct systematic research on equivalent strata in Bohemia. Between 1840 and 1850, Barrande conducted a detailed study, employing workmen to collect fossils. He obtained over 3,500 species, including fishes, crustacea (specifically trilobites), mollusca, brachiopoda, and graptolites. His primary work, *Systeme silurien du centre de la Boheme*, began publication in 1852 with a volume on trilobites; he issued twenty-one quarto volumes of text and plates by 1881, with two final volumes appearing after his death. Barrande is estimated to have spent nearly £10,000 on these works, for which he was awarded the Wollaston medal by the Geological Society of London in 1855.

## Stratigraphy and the "Colonies" Theory
Barrande employed the term "Silurian" in a comprehensive sense, dividing the rocks of Bohemia into stages A through H. The two lowest stages (A and B) were Azoic, lacking fossils. Stage C included the primordial zone, which is now recognized as part of Sedgwick's Cambrian. 

In the fourth stage (Etage D), the true lower Silurian, Barrande identified isolated patches of strata containing organic remains similar to those of the Upper Silurian. He termed these assemblages "Colonies," theorizing that species from neighboring districts had been introduced early, became locally extinct, and later reappeared. 

This interpretation was challenged in 1854 by Edward Forbes, who suggested that the occurrence of newer fossils among older ones was better explained by the crumplings, overturns, and disturbances of the older rocks. In 1880, Dr. J. E. Marr provided field evidence indicating that these repetitions were actually due to faults. Furthermore, Emanuel Friedrich Heinrich Kayser later demonstrated that Barrande's stages F, G, and H were actually Devonian. Despite these modifications, Barrande is credited with making Bohemia a classic location for studying the oldest fossiliferous formations.

## Global and Regional Occurrences
Silurian formations appear in various global contexts. In Russia, the Lower Silurian has yielded spines or small teeth (*Archodus* and *Palaeodus*) that appear to belong to fishes. Morphologists have noted a superficial resemblance between the mailed fishes of the Silurian period (Ostracodermi) and Arthropods, though this is generally regarded as mimetic resemblance.

In other regions, Silurian rocks are associated with specific geological features:
*   **Ireland:** In the south-east, broken ground of Silurian shales forms higher country, and a granite core in the Leinster chain is surrounded by mica-schist produced by the metamorphism of Silurian beds. A ridge north of Kildare town contains Silurian rocks and basic lavas, with limestone rich in fossils of Bala age. In the south, the high ridge of Slievenaman has a Silurian core, and the Silurian inlier on the range provides quarried slates at Kilmoganny. Cambro-Silurian rocks in several places yield high-quality hematitic iron.
