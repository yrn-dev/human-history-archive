# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the acquisition of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian is not merely a story of better shapes, but a story of increasing spatial awareness and the strategic management of raw materials.

In the earliest stages of tool use, hominins were largely opportunistic. However, as cognitive capacities expanded, so did the distance between the tool-user and the geological source of the stone. This shift reveals a fundamental change in how prehistoric humans perceived their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

The Oldowan industry (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago) is characterized by "expedient" technology. Most Oldowan tools were crafted from cobbles found in immediate proximity to the site, often gathered from nearby riverbeds. The focus was on immediate utility; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used.

By the time of the Acheulean industry (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), we see the emergence of selective sourcing. *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of higher-quality materials that could sustain the extensive knapping required to produce a symmetrical handaxe. This suggests a mental "map" of the environment, where specific geological outcrops were remembered and revisited.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Transition

The transition to the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) marked a revolution in raw material economy. The Levallois technique—a sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a predictable flake—required high-quality, fine-grained stone like flint or chert to be successful.

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported high-quality stone over significant distances—sometimes tens of kilometers. This indicates a shift toward "curated" technology. Rather than making a tool and discarding it, Neanderthals invested time in transporting a high-quality core, which could be used to produce multiple specialized tools (scrapers, points, knives) as needed. This logistical planning suggests a level of foresight and social organization previously unseen in the Oldowan era.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Drift
There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether the transport of stone was a conscious "economic" choice or a byproduct of expanding home ranges. Some archaeologists argue that as hominins migrated, they simply happened to find better stone, while others maintain that the specific selection of non-local materials proves a deliberate strategy of resource management.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic sourcing from immediate surroundings.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that allow for bifacial symmetry.
* **Mousterian:** High reliance on curated cores and long-distance transport of flint/chert.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) were prized for their predictable fracture patterns.
* **Cognitive Shift:** The move from "expedient" to "curated" tools reflects an evolution in foresight and planning.

### Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** A curated tool is one that is maintained, resharpened, and carried over long distances, unlike an expedient tool made for a single task and tossed aside.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to understand the difference between "conchoidal" fractures (which create sharp edges) and granular fractures (which crumble).
* **Transport Costs:** The energy spent carrying heavy stone cores over kilometers suggests that the resulting tools provided a massive survival advantage in hunting and processing hides.
