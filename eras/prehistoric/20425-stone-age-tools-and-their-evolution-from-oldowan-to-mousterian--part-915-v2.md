# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and flaking patterns of Oldowan, Acheulean, and Mousterian technologies—we must shift our gaze from the finished tool to the raw material itself. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and logistical planning.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Prospecting

In the earliest **Oldowan** industry (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever cobbles were available in nearby stream beds. The "transport distance" for these materials was minimal, often measured in a few hundred meters. The focus was on immediate utility: find a stone, strike it, and use it.

However, as we move into the **Acheulean** era, we see the emergence of selective sourcing. *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of materials with superior conchoidal fracture properties, such as quartzite or fine-grained basalt. This indicates a mental map of the environment; the toolmaker was no longer just reacting to their surroundings but planning for a future need.

## The Levallois Revolution and Material Value

By the time of the **Mousterian** industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The introduction of the **Levallois technique**—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—demanded high-quality flint or chert. 

Because these materials were not available everywhere, Neanderthals engaged in systematic transport. Archaeological evidence shows that high-quality stone was often carried over several kilometers, suggesting a "curated" tool kit. Rather than discarding a tool after one use, these hominins maintained and reshaped their implements, treating the raw material as a precious resource.

## The Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the site) proves the existence of early trade networks between different hominin groups, or if it simply reflects the wide migratory range of a single group. While some argue for a proto-economic exchange of flint, others maintain that these materials were simply gathered during seasonal migrations.

### Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic; materials gathered from immediate vicinity (stream beds).
* **Acheulean Selection:** Shift toward selective sourcing of durable materials like quartzite.
* **Mousterian Curation:** High emphasis on "curated" tools, reducing waste through the Levallois technique.
* **Transport Distance:** Increased from a few hundred meters (Oldowan) to several kilometers (Mousterian).
* **Material Preference:** Preference for cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) due to their predictable fracturing.

### Did You Know?
* **The "Knapping" Sound:** Experimental archaeology shows that the sound of a stone striking another can be heard from a significant distance, potentially acting as a social signal to other group members.
* **Recycling:** Mousterian sites often show evidence of "re-tooling," where an old scraper was reworked into a point to save on raw material.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early hominins effectively acted as amateur geologists, identifying specific strata in the earth where the best tool-grade stone resided.
