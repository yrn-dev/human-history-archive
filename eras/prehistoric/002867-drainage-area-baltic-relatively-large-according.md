# The Baltic Sea: Drainage and Physical Characteristics

## Drainage Area and River Systems
The drainage area of the Baltic is relatively large. Based on measurements provided by Sir J. Murray, it extends to 461,450 square sea miles, which is equivalent to 611,700 square English miles. The eastern region contains the largest river-basin within this area, that of the Neva. In the south, the Vistula and the Oder are the next largest basins in size. A notable feature of the drainage system in western Finland and Sweden is the presence of narrow parallel troughs that run at right angles to the coast.

## Surface Levels and Wave Patterns
Measurements taken from Swinemuende indicate that the mean surface level of the Baltic at that location is 0.093 metres (0.305 ft.) below the surface of the North Sea at Ostend (0.066 metres or 0.216 ft.) and Amsterdam (0.093 metres or 0.305 ft.). Conversely, a line of levels from Swinemuende through Eger to the Adriatic shows the Baltic's mean surface level to be 0.499 metres (1.6 ft.) above that of the Adriatic Sea.

The mean level of the surface rises approximately 0.5 metres (1.6 ft.) from Memel to the coast of Holstein, a phenomenon likely caused by prevailing westerly winds. This difference is reversed or disappears during easterly winds and is exceeded during strong westerly winds. Navigation in the Baltic is often endangered by waves that are typically irregular and short. Additionally, destructive waves known as *Seebaren* (similar to English "bores") have been recorded, which are believed to be caused by distant earthquakes.

## Geography and Bathymetry
The Baltic basin proper is separated from the depression between the Aland Isles and Sweden—known as the Aland Haf—by an unbroken ridge extending from Hango in Finland to Stockholm. Further north, another ridge defines the southern edge of the Bothnian Sea. This area is separated from the Gulf of Bothnia, the most northerly division, by a ridge across the Kvarken (or Quarken) Strait.

The Baltic includes several distinct arms and inlets:
* **The Gulf of Finland:** An arm running eastwards for about 250 miles, separating Esthonia from Finland; the Gotland deep extends directly into this gulf.
* **The Gulf of Riga:** A shallow, roughly circular inlet between Courland and Esthonia, measuring about 100 miles in diameter and reaching a maximum depth of 27 fathoms.

Recent computations place the total area of the Baltic at 166,397 square miles, including the Kattegat and Skagerrak. Its volume is 6907 cubic miles, resulting in a mean depth of 36 fathoms. This mean depth is markedly less than that of any other arm of the sea with a similar area. The seabed varies by location: the shallow banks and low coasts typically feature small pebbles and fine sand of brown, yellow, or white color, while the deeper hollows in the south consist of hard clay or soft grey and brown mud.

## Geological History and Evolution
The Baltic has undergone significant transformations due to glacial subsidence and upheaval. During the last great subsidence in glacial times, an arm of the sea submerged much of the littoral up to the Gulf of Bothnia, including the present-day lakes Malar, Hjelmar, and Vener. During this era, the northern Baltic was salty enough to support oysters.

Subsequent upheaval restricted communication with the open sea to the Danish channels, causing the waters to become fresher. While oysters disappeared, seals and various crustaceans and cold salt-water fishes became acclimatized. Some have suggested this indicates communication with the Arctic Ocean, though this is considered an unnecessary assumption given the severe climatic conditions of the time.

Further elevation transformed the Baltic into a vast freshwater lake known as the *Ancylus* lake (named for the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*). This lake is thought to have covered approximately 220,000 square miles, including Lake Ladoga, a large part of Finland, and the entire present Baltic area. A following period of subsidence re-established communication via the Danish channels and increased salinity, allowing for forms such as *Littorina littorea* and *Cardium edule*. During this epoch, the Gulf of Bothnia suffered greater depression than the Baltic proper, evidenced by deposits 100 metres (328 ft.) thick near Hernosand compared to only 25 metres (82 ft.) near Gotland.

The process of elevation that gave the Baltic its current physical form and condition followed this subsidence and appears to still be in progress. Dr. R. Sieger's isobasic lines (lines of equal rate of elevation) indicate that while movement is almost nil along the Gulf of Finland and the axial lines of the Baltic, the amplitude increases toward the main ridge of the southern Sweden *massif* and northwards toward the Gulf of Bothnia.
