# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the logistical challenge behind the craft. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. To understand the evolution of the Stone Age, we must examine the "raw material economy": how prehistoric hominins selected, transported, and valued specific minerals.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a revolution in resource management.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Early *Homo* typically used whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often scavenging riverbeds for basalt or quartz. The "transport distance" for these materials was minimal, suggesting a lifestyle tied closely to the immediate availability of resources.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with Neanderthals and early *Homo sapiens*), a pattern of strategic sourcing emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a "conchoidal fracture," meaning they break in predictable, glass-like curves, allowing for the precise edges required for Levallois technique flakes.

## The Logistics of Movement and Trade

The transport of raw materials indicates a fundamental shift in prehistoric planning. Archaeologists have discovered tool-stone deposits located kilometers away from the nearest natural geological source. This suggests that hominins were not just reacting to their environment but were planning for future needs.

The Mousterian period, in particular, shows evidence of curated toolkits. Rather than discarding a tool after a single use, Neanderthals often reshaped and recycled their flakes. This "curation" suggests that the effort required to acquire the stone—whether through long-distance trekking or early forms of social exchange—outweighed the cost of maintaining the tool. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport constitutes "trade" in a formal sense or simply the result of wide-ranging migratory patterns.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, which is essential for creating sharp, predictable edges.
* **Transport Distance:** The measurement of how far a stone tool was moved from its original geological source.
* **Curated Tools:** Tools that are maintained and resharpened over time rather than discarded.
* **Material Selectivity:** The conscious choice to ignore local stone in favor of superior materials found further away.
* **Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated Mousterian method of preparing a stone core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian (volcanic glass) creates an edge thinner and sharper than a modern steel scalpel, it was one of the most highly prized materials in the prehistoric world.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" (the main block of stone) was often treated as a precious resource to be carefully managed to produce as many flakes as possible.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use "X-ray fluorescence" to trace the chemical signature of a tool back to the exact quarry it came from, revealing ancient travel routes.
