# Archaeological Interest of Early Britain

The study of early Britain encompasses the archaeological and historical investigation of the region during its Pre-Roman, Roman, and Anglo-Saxon periods. The term "Britain" is the anglicized version of the classical name for Scotland, Wales, and England, and is occasionally used to refer to the British Isles as a whole. The Greek (*Pretanikai nêsoi*, *Brettania*) and Roman (*Britannia*) names are believed to be attempts to reproduce a Celtic original, though the exact form of that original remains a subject of dispute.

## Roman Britain and Archaeological Study
Significant archaeological interest has been directed toward the Roman occupation of Britain and Scotland. Key areas of study include the fortifications and infrastructure established during this era.

### Fortifications and Walls
The wall of Hadrian is a primary focus of archaeological research, with documented studies by John Hodgson in his *History of Northumberland* (1840) and J.C. Bruce in *Roman Wall* (1867). Further excavations regarding this structure were reported by F. Haverfield in the *Cumberland Archaeological Society Transactions* between 1894 and 1904, while R.C. Bosanquet provided a detailed account of the Roman Camp at Housesteads in 1904. Other Roman forts have also been analyzed, such as Hardknott (detailed by R.S. Ferguson) and the Roman Fort of Gellygaer (detailed by J. Ward in 1903).

Regarding the Roman occupation of Scotland, research includes the *Antonine Wall Report* (1899) by Haverfield, J. Macdonald's *Roman Stones in Hunterian Mus.* (1897), and Stuart's *Caledonia Romana* (1852). Additional Scottish excavations are recorded in the *Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland* (volumes xx.-xl.), including J. Macdonald's work on Bar Hill.

### Urban Centers and Settlements
Archaeological data on Roman urban centers has been published in various journals and texts. For example, research on Silchester and Caerwent can be found in *Archaeologia* (1890–1908), and Charles Roach Smith authored *Roman London* (1859). The study of Roman villages is supported by General Pitt-Rivers' four-volume work, *Excavations in Cranborne Chase, &c.* (1887–1908), and the *Proc. Soc. of Ant.* xviii.

## Scholarly Contributions and Literature
The "Romanization" of Britain has been a central theme for scholars such as Professor F. Haverfield, who authored *The Romanization of Roman Britain* (1906) and contributed to the *Victoria County History*. Other academic perspectives on the Roman provinces are found in the works of Mommsen and the *Edinburgh Review* (1899).

The transition and end of Roman Britain have been explored in the *English History Review* (1904), Professor Bury's *Life of St Patrick* (1905), and P. Vinogradoff's *Growth of the Manor* (1905). Additionally, the *English History Review* (1896) contains research regarding Christianity in Roman Britain.

## Costume and Material Culture

* *Archaeologia*
* M. Didron's *Annales archeologiques*
* The *Journals* of the Archaeological Societies
* Various county histories
* The *Monumenta Vetusta* of the London Society of Antiquaries

## Related Historical Contexts
While the archaeological focus remains on the British Isles, the name "Britain" influenced other regions; for instance, Brittany in western France derived its name from Britain due to migrations occurring in the 5th and 6th century A.D. Furthermore, the personification of the region as "Britannia," depicted as a female figure, appears as early as the coins of Antoninus Pius and Hadrian in the early 2nd century A.D.
