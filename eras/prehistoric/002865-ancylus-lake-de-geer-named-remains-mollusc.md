# Ancylus Lake of G. de Geer

The Ancylus lake, named by G. de Geer, represents a specific stage in the geological history of the Baltic region. It is characterized as a vast freshwater lake that formed as a result of further elevation of the land following previous glacial periods.

## Formation and Characteristics
The Ancylus lake was created through a process of elevation that transformed the Baltic into a freshwater body. This lake is named after the remains of the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*. It is estimated to have covered an area of approximately 220,000 square miles. The extent of the lake included the entirety of the present-day Baltic area, as well as Lake Ladoga and a large portion of Finland.

## Geological Context and Succession
The emergence of the Ancylus lake was part of a series of environmental shifts in the region:

*   **Preceding Stages:** During the last great subsidence of glacial times, an arm of the sea extended across Sweden, submerging much of the littoral up to the Gulf of Bothnia and including the present lakes Malar, Hjelmar, and Vener. At that time, the northern Baltic waters were salty enough to support oysters. A subsequent upheaval restricted communication with the open sea to the Danish channels, causing the waters to become fresher. While oysters disappeared, seals and various crustaceans and cold salt-water fishes became acclimatized.
*   **The Ancylus Stage:** Further elevation then transformed the region into the freshwater Ancylus lake.
*   **Subsequent Stages:** Following the Ancylus stage, a period of subsidence occurred. This re-established communication through the Danish channels and increased the salinity of the Baltic, allowing for the presence of forms such as *Littorina littorea* and *Cardium edule*. During this subsidence, the Gulf of Bothnia experienced a greater depression than the Baltic proper; deposits from this epoch reach 100 metres (328 ft.) near Hernosand, compared to only 25 metres (82 ft.) near Gotland.

## Current Geological Trends
Following the period of subsidence, a process of elevation began that gave the Baltic its current physical condition and form. This process appears to be ongoing. Research by Dr. R. Sieger involving isobasic lines (lines of equal rate of elevation) for Finland and Sweden indicates that this movement is currently almost nil along the Gulf of Finland and the axial lines of the Baltic. However, the amplitude of the movement increases toward the Gulf of Bothnia and in the direction of the main ridge of the southern Sweden *massif*.
