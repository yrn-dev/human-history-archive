# Gondwanaland and the Early Geological History of Asia

The geological history of Asia is characterized by the existence of ancient land masses and intervening seas. While much of the continent was covered by the sea during the Ordovician and Cambrian periods, evidence indicates that the north and east of Asia have been land since the Palaeozoic era. Specifically, it has been conclusively proved that the peninsula of India has remained above the sea since at least the Carboniferous period.

## The Ancient Land Masses: Angaraland and Gondwanaland

Geologists, including E. Suess and Neumayr, identified two primary ancient land masses. The northern land mass is named Angaraland by E. Suess. The southern land mass is called Gondwanaland by Neumayr, Suess, and others; the Indian peninsula is described as a fragment of this larger mass.

These two land masses were not originally connected. They were separated by a broad belt containing well-developed marine deposits of Tertiary and Mesozoic age. This intervening sea is referred to by Neumayr as the central Mediterranean sea and by Suess as the Tethys.

## Marine Deposits and the Central Sea

The sea separating the land masses left significant geological markers across Asia:
* **Burma:** Marine Tertiary beds are present.
* **South Tibet and the Himalayas:** A nearly complete series of marine deposits exists, spanning from the Eocene back to the Carboniferous.
* **Afghanistan:** Mesozoic beds are found in both fluviatile and marine forms.

The waters of this central sea reached their greatest extension during the Upper Cretaceous period. At this time, the sea spread across the whole of western Asia and encroached slightly upon the Indian land. While a considerable part of this region was likely dry land during Jurassic times, the later stages of the Cretaceous period saw the sea cover Arabia, Persia, Baluchistan, Afghanistan, the drainage area of the Aral Sea, and the basin of the Obi. The Eocene sea is noted to have been not much inferior in extent to the Upper Cretaceous sea.

## Elevation and the Union of Continents

The elevation of the main part of the Himalayas occurred after the Eocene period. This is evidenced by the presence of nummulitic limestone at altitudes of 20,000 ft. The formation of the Himalayas and other great mountain chains in central Asia resulted in the isolation of portions of the former central sea. These same geological forces eventually led to the elevation of the entire region and the union of the old continents of Gondwana and Angara.

## The Decline of Gondwanaland

Despite the union of the continents, Gondwanaland did not survive for long. During Tertiary times, the portion of Gondwanaland located between South Africa and India sank beneath the waves.
