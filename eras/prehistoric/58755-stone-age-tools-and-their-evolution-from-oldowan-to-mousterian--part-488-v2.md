# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the *logistics* of the Stone Age. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian was not merely a leap in cognitive skill, but a fundamental shift in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their landscape and managed raw materials.

The transition from opportunistic scavenging of stones to the strategic quarrying of high-quality minerals reveals a deepening understanding of geology and a growing capacity for long-term planning.

## From Opportunism to Selection (Oldowan)
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized whatever river cobbles or basalt fragments were immediately available in their vicinity. The "transport distance" for these materials was typically minimal, often measured in a few hundred meters. At this stage, the tool was a temporary convenience; once the edge dulled, the stone was discarded, and another was plucked from the nearest stream.

## The Expansion of the Home Range (Acheulean)
As *Homo erectus* developed the Acheulean handaxe (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), the relationship with raw materials changed. The handaxe required specific types of stone—such as flint, quartzite, or fine-grained volcanic rock—that could withstand the heavy percussion required for symmetrical shaping.

Archaeological evidence shows that Acheulean hominins began transporting high-quality stone over much greater distances, sometimes several kilometers. This suggests a mental map of the landscape, where toolmakers remembered the location of specific "quarries" and planned their movements to ensure they had the correct material for the task at hand.

## The Strategic Economy of the Mousterian
By the Mousterian period (associated largely with Neanderthals, approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago), the approach became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a predictable, pre-shaped flake—demanded high-grade materials. 

This era saw the rise of "curated" toolkits. Rather than making a tool and discarding it, Mousterian hunters often carried a few high-quality cores and carefully retouched the edges of their flakes to extend the tool's life. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether Neanderthals engaged in true "trade" or social exchange of exotic stones, but the presence of non-local materials in many sites suggests a complex network of movement and resource acquisition.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily local, opportunistic river cobbles.
* **Acheulean Shift:** Increased selectivity for materials that allow for bifacial symmetry.
* **Transport Distance:** Increased from a few hundred meters (Oldowan) to several kilometers (Acheulean/Mousterian).
* **Curated Technology:** The Mousterian period emphasized the longevity of the tool through retouching.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Evolution of tools mirrors a growing prehistoric understanding of mineral properties.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Exotic" Stone:** Some Mousterian sites contain stones found dozens of kilometers away, suggesting either massive migratory ranges or early forms of social exchange.
* **Material Bias:** Archaeologists must account for "preservation bias," as softer stones used by early humans may have eroded, leaving only the hardest minerals in the record.
* **Core Efficiency:** The Levallois technique allowed toolmakers to get more cutting edges out of a single piece of stone than any previous method.
