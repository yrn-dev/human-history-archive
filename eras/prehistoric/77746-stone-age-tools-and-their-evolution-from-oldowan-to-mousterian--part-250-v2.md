# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and flaking patterns that distinguish an Oldowan chopper from a Mousterian point—we must turn our attention to the logistical precursors of production. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is born. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability, but a sophisticated evolution in geological prospecting and resource management.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality minerals reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized whatever water-worn cobbles were available in their immediate vicinity, typically basalt or quartz. The "cost" of procurement was low; the stone was often found within a few hundred meters of the site of use.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, we see a marked increase in "raw material selectivity." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flakes necessary for the Levallois technique.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade
The Mousterian era (associated largely with Neanderthals) showcases a sophisticated understanding of distance and value. Archaeological evidence indicates that high-quality stone was often transported over dozens of kilometers. This implies one of two things: either a highly mobile population with a mental map of geological deposits, or the earliest inklings of social exchange networks between disparate groups.

There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport represents "curation"—the act of carefully maintaining a tool to extend its life because the material is rare—or a cultural preference for specific stones based on perceived prestige or superior performance. Regardless, the shift from "find and use" to "seek and transport" marks a pivotal cognitive evolution in planning and foresight.

## Key Facts: Material Logistics
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break in smooth, curved surfaces, essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Raw Material Radius:** The distance between a tool-use site and the original geological source of the stone.
* **Curation:** The process of sharpening and reworking a tool rather than discarding it, common when raw materials are scarce.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The strategic act of locating, extracting, and transporting stone for tool production.
* **Core Reduction:** The process of removing flakes from a larger "core" stone to create a specific tool shape.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge only a few molecules thick, making it significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Geological Maps:** By mapping the source of flint found at Mousterian sites, archaeologists can effectively reconstruct the migratory routes and territorial ranges of prehistoric humans.
* **The "Expensive" Tool:** In areas where high-quality stone was absent, hominins occasionally used softer materials like antler or bone, though these required different processing techniques.
