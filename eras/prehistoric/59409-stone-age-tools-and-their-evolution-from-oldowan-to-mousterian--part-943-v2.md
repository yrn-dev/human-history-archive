# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and techniques used to create Oldowan choppers or Mousterian points—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the procurement of the stone itself. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a shift in knapping skill, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived and navigated their landscape.

The transition from the opportunistic use of local river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality minerals reveals a profound shift in spatial memory and planning.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever raw materials were immediately available in their vicinity, such as basalt or quartz found in stream beds. The "cost" of transporting heavy stones was high, meaning tools were often discarded shortly after use.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian traditions, a pattern of "selective procurement" emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—which offered more predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). This suggests that *Homo erectus* and later *Homo neanderthalensis* possessed a mental map of their environment, knowing exactly where high-quality deposits were located, sometimes kilometers away from their primary campsites.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the Mousterian era (associated primarily with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became even more sophisticated. The introduction of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—demanded high-grade stone. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian tool-makers didn't just find stone; they managed it. They curated their toolkits, transporting "blank" cores over long distances to be finished later. This indicates a capacity for future-oriented planning: the ability to envision a tool that would be needed days or weeks in the future in a different location.

There remains a scholarly debate regarding the extent of "trade" or social exchange of these materials. While some archaeologists argue that the presence of non-local stone proves early trade networks between groups, others suggest it is simply evidence of larger home ranges and migratory patterns.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The glass-like break pattern essential for creating sharp edges, found in flint, obsidian, and quartzite.
* **Curated Toolkits:** The practice of maintaining and transporting tools rather than discarding them, marking a cognitive leap in planning.
* **Raw Material Radius:** The distance from a site to the nearest source of stone, used by historians to estimate the mobility of prehistoric groups.
* **Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated "prepared core" method that required high-quality stone to be successful.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The Weight of Labor:** Transporting raw stone cores over several kilometers required significant caloric expenditure, proving the high value placed on quality materials.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use "trace element analysis" to pinpoint the exact quarry a prehistoric stone came from, often identifying sources dozens of miles away.
