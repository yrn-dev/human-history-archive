# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shift from the crude choppers of the Oldowan to the refined flakes of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of tool technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The transition from the Lower to the Middle Paleolithic is marked by a sophisticated shift in "lithic procurement." Early toolmakers were opportunistic, but later species developed complex strategies for sourcing, transporting, and conserving high-quality stone.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was primarily opportunistic. *Homo habilis* generally utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity to their living sites, often scavenging riverbeds for basalt or quartz. The "cost" of transporting stone was high, and the willingness to carry materials over long distances was low.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked change. *Homo erectus* and later *Homo neanderthalensis* began to seek out specific materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—that offered predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fractures). This indicates a mental map of the landscape, where hominins remembered the locations of high-quality outcrops miles away from their primary campsites.

## The Economics of the Levallois Technique
The Mousterian industry is defined by the Levallois technique, a method of preparing a stone core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape. This was not just a technical achievement; it was an economic one. 

By maximizing the number of usable edges produced from a single piece of stone, Neanderthals reduced their dependence on constant raw material access. This "curation" of tools allowed for greater mobility. Rather than abandoning a tool when a small piece broke, they refined their retouching techniques to extend the life of the implement. Some scholars debate whether this indicates a formal trade network between groups or simply a wider foraging radius, but the evidence of "exotic" stones found far from their source suggests a highly organized approach to resource management.

## Key Facts
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for and collecting stone for tool production.
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The way certain stones (like flint) break in smooth, curved surfaces, essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools are typically found near sources; Mousterian tools are often found kilometers away from the original quarry.
* **Core Preparation:** The Levallois technique shifted the focus from shaping the tool to shaping the *source* (the core).
* **Material Selectivity:** Later hominins bypassed common stones in favor of higher-quality minerals that allowed for thinner, sharper blades.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some Middle Paleolithic sites, obsidian has been found dozens of kilometers from the nearest volcanic source, suggesting early forms of social exchange or migration.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Some Mousterian scrapers show evidence of being sharpened dozens of times over several weeks, proving they were prized possessions rather than disposable items.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Prehistoric toolmakers had to understand the internal stress and grain of a rock before striking it, displaying an intuitive grasp of geology.
