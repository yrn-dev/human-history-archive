# The Dawn of Domestication: Shaping the Prehistoric World

## Overview
The transition from nomadic hunting and gathering to settled agriculture was one of the most profound shifts in human history. Central to this transformation was the domestication of animals—the process of adapting wild species for human use. This was not a single event, but a gradual evolutionary process involving selective breeding and symbiotic relationships. While dogs were the first to join human circles during the Paleolithic, the domestication of cats and livestock occurred later, coinciding with the Neolithic Revolution and the rise of early urban centers.

## Key Facts

### The Canine Partnership
Dogs were the first species domesticated, likely from an extinct lineage of Pleistocene wolves. Historians and geneticists debate the exact timing and location, with some arguing for a single origin in Eurasia and others suggesting multiple domestication events across different continents. The relationship likely began as a commensal one, where less aggressive wolves scavenged near human camps, eventually evolving into a cooperative partnership for hunting and protection.

### The Neolithic Livestock Revolution
The domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle transformed human sociology. By shifting from hunting to herding, prehistoric humans secured a stable supply of protein, milk, and wool. This surplus allowed for population growth and the development of permanent settlements. Most of this occurred in the "Fertile Crescent," though independent domestication events happened in China (pigs) and South America (llamas).

### The Feline Symbiosis
Unlike dogs, cats likely "domesticated themselves." As early farmers stored grain, they attracted rodents. Wildcats (*Felis lybica*) moved into human settlements to hunt these pests. Humans tolerated the cats for their utility, leading to a gradual genetic shift toward docility. This relationship is evidenced by archaeological finds in Cyprus dating back nearly 9,500 years.

## Did You Know?
* **Genetic Shifts:** Domesticated animals often undergo "domestication syndrome," which can include physical changes like floppier ears, shorter snouts, and varied coat colors.
* **The Milk Mutation:** The ability for adults to digest lactose (lactase persistence) is a biological adaptation that evolved in human populations *after* the domestication of cattle and goats.
* **Ancient Bonds:** One of the oldest known burials of a dog with a human dates back roughly 14,000 years, suggesting a deep emotional bond existed long before agriculture.
