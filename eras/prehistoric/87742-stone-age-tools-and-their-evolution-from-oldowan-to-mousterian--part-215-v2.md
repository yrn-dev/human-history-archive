# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the "where" and "how" of the stone itself. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in geological prospecting and logistical planning.

To understand the leap from the Oldowan to the Mousterian, we must look beyond the finished tool and examine the procurement strategies of prehistoric hominins. The ability to identify, transport, and curate high-quality raw materials is a primary indicator of evolving cognitive complexity.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used; if not, the tool-making event simply did not occur. The "cost" of transport was kept low, reflecting a limited mental map of the landscape.

As we transition into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), we see a dramatic shift. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint or chert, which allow for more predictable fracturing. Evidence suggests that later Paleolithic groups transported these materials over dozens, and sometimes hundreds, of kilometers. This implies a sophisticated understanding of geology and a capacity for long-term planning.

## The Curated Toolset and the Levallois Technique

The Mousterian industry is defined by the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape. This process is resource-intensive. Because the Levallois method requires specific stone properties to work effectively, the "logistics of lithics" became paramount.

Scholars debate the extent to which these materials were traded between groups versus gathered through seasonal migration. However, the presence of "exotic" stones far from their geological source indicates that stone tools were no longer disposable gadgets made on a whim. They became curated assets, sharpened and repurposed over long journeys, marking a fundamental shift in how prehistoric humans interacted with their environment.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, relying on local river cobbles.
* **Material Preference:** A shift toward flint, chert, and obsidian for their superior conchoidal fracture properties.
* **Transport Distance:** Mousterian tool-makers often transported raw materials over significant distances, indicating advanced spatial mapping.
* **Cognitive Load:** The transition from "found" to "sought" materials suggests an increase in foresight and planning.
* **Resource Management:** The Levallois technique required high-quality stone to minimize waste during the core-preparation phase.

## Did You Know?
* **The Obsidian Trail:** In some prehistoric regions, obsidian (volcanic glass) has been found hundreds of miles from the nearest volcano, suggesting early networks of exchange.
* **Core Exhaustion:** Archaeologists can tell how "stressed" a group was by looking at the size of the remaining cores; very small, exhausted cores suggest the group was far from a raw material source.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans didn't have textbooks, but they developed a "tactile library," recognizing the weight and texture of high-quality stone even when it was buried under soil.
