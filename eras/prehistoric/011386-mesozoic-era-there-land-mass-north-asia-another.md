# Geological Land Masses of Mesozoic Asia

During the Mesozoic era, the geological structure of Asia was characterized by the existence of two distinct land masses separated by a central sea. These masses, along with the intervening marine environment, played a critical role in the eventual formation of the continent's current topography.

## Angaraland and Gondwanaland
The northern land mass of Asia was named Angaraland by E. Suess. This region stretched from the Arctic Ocean as far south as the south of China. Evidence for its existence as land since the close of the Palaeozoic era is found in the presence of terrestrial and freshwater deposits of Mesozoic age. In the north, between the Lena and Yenisei rivers, the Angara series consists of sandstone and conglomerate containing numerous plant remains, some of which are Jurassic or Permian in age. While marine Jurassic and Cretaceous fossils appear in the extreme north, most of this region remained dry land. The limits of Angaraland are indicated by marine Mesozoic deposits found to its east in Japan, to its west in the western Tian-shan, and to its south in Tibet.

The southern land mass was named Gondwanaland by Neumayr, Suess, and others. The peninsula of India is a fragment of this larger mass, which probably extended across the Indian Ocean and was once united with the south of Africa. The Indian peninsula has remained above sea level since at least the Carboniferous period, as evidenced by the Gondwana series of terrestrial and freshwater deposits and a general absence of marine fossils, except near its borders.

## The Central Sea
Between Angaraland and Gondwanaland lay a broad belt that was beneath the sea for the greater part of the Mesozoic era. This body of water is referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr. In this region, marine deposits of Mesozoic and Tertiary age are well developed.

The sea reached its maximum extension during the Upper Cretaceous period, at which time its waters spread across the whole of western Asia and slightly encroached upon the Indian land mass. This marine environment also extended into Burma, south Tibet, and the Himalayas, where a nearly complete series of marine deposits exists from the Carboniferous to the Eocene. In Afghanistan, the Mesozoic beds are a mixture of marine and fluviatile deposits.

## Continental Collision and Mountain Formation
The current elevation of Asia's great mountain chains resulted from the eventual union of Angaraland and Gondwanaland. Geologically, Asia can be viewed as consisting of a northern and southern mass that were too rigid to crumple, separated by an intermediate belt of softer rock. As the earth's interior contracted, the outer crust was forced to accommodate a smaller nucleus, causing the central softer belt to yield through crumpling and folding.

The Himalayan range provides clear evidence of this process; its elevation was produced by an overthrust from the north, as if the high land of central Asia had been pushed southward against the unyielding old rocks of the Indian peninsula. This process crumpled and folded the edges of the over-riding strata. The main elevation of the Himalayas occurred after the Eocene period, evidenced by nummulitic limestone found at heights of 20,000 feet.

## Regional Geological Characteristics
The results of these geological forces are visible in three distinct regions:

*   **The Southern Region:** In Arabia, Syria, and the Indian peninsula, only the oldest rocks are folded. The Mesozoic, Tertiary, and Upper Palaeozoic beds lie almost horizontally, characterizing a region of faulting or quiescence rather than folding.
*   **The Central Folded Belt:** This broad belt of crumpling includes the Himalayas, the Tian-shan, the Burmese ranges, Persia, Afghanistan, Baluchistan, and Asia Minor. It consists of Mesozoic and lower Tertiary deposits thrown into arcs extending from west to east.
*   **The Northern Region:** Including Mongolia, northern China, and much of Siberia, this area is generally free from important Mesozoic or Tertiary folding. Its configuration is largely due to faulting, which created trough-like depressions and mountain ridges.

Following the union of the continents, Gondwanaland did not survive intact; the portion situated between South Africa and India sank beneath the waves during Tertiary times.
