# Malar and the Baltic Region

The geographical history of the Baltic region is characterized by significant fluctuations in land elevation and sea levels, resulting in dramatic changes to the landscape and the composition of its waters.

## Geological Evolution and the Malar Region
During glacial times, a period of great subsidence occurred, allowing an arm of the sea to extend across Sweden. This sea submerged a large portion of the littoral area, reaching as far as the Gulf of Bothnia. This prehistoric sea included the areas that are now the lakes Hjelmar, Vener, and Malar. During this specific era, the waters of the northern Baltic contained enough salt to support the flourishing of oysters.

Subsequent upheaval restricted the direct communication between these waters and the open sea, limiting it to the Danish channels. As a result, the Baltic waters became fresher, leading to the disappearance of the oyster. However, seals and various crustaceans and cold salt-water fishes became acclimatized to the environment. While some have suggested these animals indicate a connection to the Arctic Ocean to the north, the severe climatic conditions of the time make such an assumption unnecessary.

## The Ancylus Lake and Subsequent Changes
In a later stage of its history, further elevation transformed the Baltic into a vast freshwater lake known as the *Ancylus* lake (named for the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*). This lake is believed to have covered approximately 220,000 square miles, encompassing the current Baltic area, Lake Ladoga, and a large part of Finland.

This was followed by another period of subsidence that re-established communication through the Danish channels and increased salinity, allowing for the presence of *Littorina littorea* and *Cardium edule*. During this epoch, the Gulf of Bothnia experienced a greater depression than the Baltic proper; deposits near Hernosand show a thickness of 100 metres (328 ft.), compared to only 25 metres (82 ft.) near Gotland.

Following this, a process of elevation began that gave the Baltic its current physical condition. This movement appears to be ongoing. Dr. R. Sieger's study of isobasic lines (lines of equal rate of elevation) indicates that while movement is almost nil along the Gulf of Finland and the axial lines of the Baltic, the amplitude increases toward the main ridge of the southern Sweden *massif* and northwards to the Gulf of Bothnia.

## Physical Geography and Bathymetry
The Baltic proper consists of a series of troughs and hollows east of the plateau connecting Ruegen and the coast of Mecklenburg with the islands of Moen and Falster. These include:
*   **The Bornholm deep:** Located east of the island of Bornholm.
*   **The Gotland deep:** Separated from the Bornholm deep by the Middelbank.
*   **The Danziger Tiefe:** An isolated depression to the south-east.
*   **The Gotland basin:** The deepest and largest of the basins, extending northeast toward the Gulf of Finland.

Along the Swedish coast, a deep channel runs northward from outside the island of Oland. This channel is cut off to the east and south by a bank extending from near Karlskrona, upon which Gotland stands. The deepest point in the Baltic is located near the junction opposite Landsort, reaching 420 metres (230 fathoms).

## Hydrography and Water Circulation
The circulation of water in the Baltic is divided between the Baltic proper and the channels connecting it to the North Sea.

### The Baltic Stream
The Baltic receives more water from river discharge and rainfall than it loses through evaporation, creating a surplus known as the "Baltic Stream." This is a surface current that originates from fresh water and, due to the earth's rotation, remains close to the Scandinavian peninsula. This stream is periodic; it is abundant enough to inundate the Skagerrak and Kattegat in spring and summer, but is largely dried up in winter when land water freezes. Inflowing water from the North Sea contains more than 32 *pro mille* of salt, while outflowing water contains less than 30 *pro mille*.

### Internal Circulation
Within the great basins from Ruegen to the Gulfs of Finland and Bothnia, the upper layers of water (30 to 70 metres thick) maintain consistent salinity through vertical convection currents. Beneath these are stagnant, saltier masses of water that are deficient in oxygen and surcharged with carbonic acid. These deeper waters originally entered via the plateau between Mecklenburg and Falster, though their horizontal movement is restricted by ridges separating the deep hollows.
