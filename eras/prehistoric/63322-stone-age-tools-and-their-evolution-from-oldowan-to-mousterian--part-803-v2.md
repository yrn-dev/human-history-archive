# The Raw Material Economy: Lithic Procurement and Transport

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the logistical intelligence behind the tools. The evolution of the Stone Age was not merely a progression of shapes, but a revolution in how early hominins perceived and managed their environment. The shift from opportunistic scavenging of stone to strategic quarrying reveals a profound cognitive leap in planning and spatial memory.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used; if not, the tool-making event simply did not occur. The "raw material economy" was local and immediate.

However, as we transition into the Acheulean period, we observe the first signs of selective procurement. *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials—such as fine-grained volcanic rocks—that could hold a sharper edge and withstand more percussion. This indicates a mental map of the landscape, where the tool-maker remembered the location of a specific outcrop miles away from their current camp.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. The Levallois technique, a hallmark of this era, required a specific type of stone that could be predictably flaked. This necessity drove hominins to transport high-quality flint or chert over significant distances.

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian tool-makers often carried "cores"—large blocks of raw stone—to different sites, rather than making every tool on the spot. This suggests a shift from "immediate-return" behavior to "delayed-return" planning. They were no longer just reacting to their environment; they were preparing for future needs.

## Scholarly Debate: The "Cost-Benefit" Model

There is ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists regarding the "cost" of transport. Some scholars argue that the distance materials were carried is a direct proxy for cognitive complexity. Others suggest that the movement of stone was a byproduct of larger migratory patterns or social exchange between groups, suggesting that stone tools may have functioned as early social currency long before the advent of formal trade.

***

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan:** Characterized by "localism," utilizing materials found within immediate reach.
* **Acheulean:** Marked by the first evidence of selective sourcing and short-distance transport.
* **Mousterian:** Featured the transport of prepared cores, indicating advanced foresight.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) were prized for their predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **Cognitive Link:** The transition from local to distant sourcing mirrors the growth of the hominin prefrontal cortex.

### Did You Know?
* Some Acheulean handaxes have been found made from materials sourced over 10 kilometers away from the site of discovery.
* The "Levallois" technique is named after a site in France where these sophisticated prepared-core tools were first identified.
* Not all prehistoric humans used flint; in regions like East Africa, obsidian and quartzite were the primary materials due to geological availability.
