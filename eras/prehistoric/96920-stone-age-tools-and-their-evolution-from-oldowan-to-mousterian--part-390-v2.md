# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and flaking patterns that distinguish the Oldowan from the Mousterian—we must shift our gaze from the finished product to the raw material. The evolution of prehistoric technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability, but a sophisticated evolution in geological prospecting and logistical planning.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in how early hominins perceived their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Prospecting

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized "river cobbles"—stones found in immediate proximity to their campsites. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used. The "economy" of the Oldowan was one of immediate need; the cost of transporting heavy stone outweighed the benefit of a slightly sharper edge.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see the emergence of "raw material selectivity." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flaking necessary to create the symmetrical hand-axes of *Homo erectus* or the refined Levallois flakes of *Homo neanderthalensis*.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian toolkit (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago) represents a revolution in spatial planning. Archaeological evidence shows that Neanderthals often transported high-quality stone over distances of 10 to 100 kilometers. This suggests a mental map of the environment and a capacity for "delayed gratification"—the ability to invest energy in transporting a heavy core today to ensure a superior tool tomorrow.

There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport was the result of nomadic movement or an early form of social exchange. Some archaeologists argue that the presence of non-local stone in specific sites suggests a rudimentary trade network between kinship groups, while others maintain it was simply the result of seasonal migration patterns.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily utilized local river cobbles; minimal transport distances.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that allowed for bifacial symmetry.
* **Mousterian:** High-precision sourcing; use of "prepared cores" to maximize the utility of a single piece of stone.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline rocks (flint, obsidian) were prized for their predictable fracturing.
* **Cognitive Shift:** The move from local to distant sourcing indicates advanced spatial memory and foresight.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The Core Strategy:** The Levallois technique used in the Mousterian era allowed a toolmaker to "pre-determine" the shape of a flake before it was even struck from the core.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians often study "debitage" (the waste flakes left over from toolmaking) to determine if a tool was made on-site or brought in from a distant quarry.
