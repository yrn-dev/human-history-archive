# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian point—we must shift our gaze from the finished product to the source. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in logistics and geological knowledge.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic mining of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape. The "where" of the stone is often as telling as the "how" of the knapping.

## From Opportunism to Intentionality

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They utilized "river cobbles"—stones already rounded by water—found in the immediate vicinity of their camps. The proximity of the raw material to the site of use suggests a limited range of transport and a "use-and-discard" mentality.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a pattern of "selective procurement" emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. This indicates a mental map of the environment; the toolmaker knew that a superior material existed several kilometers away and planned their journey accordingly.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The implementation of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a predictable, pre-shaped flake—demanded specific stone properties. 

This necessity drove the development of early transport networks. Archaeologists have found "non-local" stones in Mousterian sites, suggesting that materials were carried over significant distances. This implies not only a higher value placed on the tool but also a social structure capable of coordinating the movement of resources. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between groups or if individual foragers traveled vast distances to known quarries.

## Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Raw Material Bias:** The choice of stone (e.g., obsidian vs. quartzite) often dictated the sharpness and durability of the final tool.
* **Transport Costs:** The distance between a quarry and a campsite is a primary metric used by historians to estimate the mobility patterns of early humans.
* **Core Reduction:** The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian saw a massive increase in "efficiency," meaning more cutting edge was produced from a single kilogram of stone.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early humans developed an intuitive understanding of conchoidal fracture, knowing exactly which stones would break predictably.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern surgical steel scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often abandoned quickly, Mousterian tools show evidence of "re-sharpening," proving they were valued possessions.
* **Natural Selection:** Some researchers argue that the need to find and transport high-quality stone may have contributed to the cognitive evolution of spatial memory in early hominins.
