# Gulf of Bothnia

The Gulf of Bothnia is the most northerly division of the Baltic basin. It is separated from the Bothnian Sea to the south by a ridge that crosses the narrow Quarken (or Kvarken) Strait.

## Ice and Navigation
The Gulf of Bothnia experiences significant winter freezing, though the extent varies by region. The northern part of the gulf is frozen every winter. In the southern part, ice covers the coasts every winter, but it rarely, if ever, covers the central part. In the northern Quarken, traversable ice occurs every third or fourth year.

Navigation within the gulf is frequently interrupted by drifting ice, typically from the beginning of May back to the middle of November. However, some locations exhibit different patterns; for example, the port of Hernosand has been known to remain open throughout an entire winter. In contrast, the seaport of Luleå, located at the north-west corner of the gulf, is generally closed by ice from November until the end of May.

## Geological History
The Gulf of Bothnia has undergone significant physical changes due to periods of subsidence and elevation during glacial times. During a great subsidence, an arm of the sea extended across Sweden and submerged much of the littoral up to the Gulf of Bothnia, including what are now the lakes Malar, Hjelmar, and Vener. At this time, the northern Baltic waters were salty enough to support oysters.

Subsequent upheaval restricted communication with the open sea to the Danish channels, causing the Baltic waters to become fresher and leading to the disappearance of the oyster. The region later transitioned into a vast freshwater lake known as the *Ancylus* lake (named for the mollusc *Ancylus fluviatilis*), which covered a large part of Finland, Lake Ladoga, and the entire present Baltic area.

A following period of subsidence re-established communication via the Danish channels and reintroduced salinity. During this epoch, the Gulf of Bothnia suffered a greater depression than the Baltic proper; deposits from this period reach a thickness of 100 metres (328 ft.) near Hernosand, compared to only 25 metres (82 ft.) near Gotland.

## Current Physical Condition
The Baltic and the Gulf of Bothnia have since been shaped by a process of elevation that appears to still be in progress. Research by Dr. R. Sieger using isobasic lines (lines of equal rate of elevation) indicates that while movement is almost nil along the axial lines of the Gulf of Finland and the Baltic, the amplitude of this elevation increases northwards toward the Gulf of Bothnia.
