# The Dawn of Domestication: Shaping the Prehistoric World

## Overview
The transition from nomadic foraging to settled agriculture was one of the most profound shifts in human history. Central to this transformation was the domestication of animals—a process of selective breeding and behavioral modification that altered the biological trajectory of several species. While early humans relied on hunting for survival, the shift toward domestication provided a stable source of food, protection, and labor, fundamentally restructuring human social hierarchies and settlement patterns.

## Key Facts

### The Canine Partnership
Dogs were the first species domesticated, occurring well before the advent of agriculture. Historians and geneticists debate whether dogs were "tamed" by humans who captured wolf pups or if "self-domestication" occurred, where less aggressive wolves sought food scraps near human camps. Regardless of the origin, dogs provided critical advantages in hunting and camp security.

### The Rise of Livestock
The domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle occurred primarily during the Neolithic Revolution in the Fertile Crescent. This shift allowed humans to move from a "boom-and-bust" hunting cycle to a predictable supply of protein, milk, and wool. Cattle, in particular, revolutionized prehistoric life by providing traction for plowing, which exponentially increased crop yields.

### The Feline Symbiosis
Unlike dogs or livestock, cats likely entered a commensal relationship with humans. As early farmers stored grain, they attracted rodents; wildcats (*Felis lybica*) followed the rodents. Humans tolerated the cats for their pest-control services, leading to a gradual domestication process. Recent archaeological finds in Cyprus suggest cats were associated with humans as early as 9,500 years ago.

## Did You Know?
* **Genetic Shifts:** Domestication often leads to "domestication syndrome," where animals develop physical changes such as floppier ears, shorter snouts, and varied coat colors compared to their wild ancestors.
* **The Goat Debate:** Some archaeologists argue that goats were domesticated independently in multiple regions, including the Zagros Mountains and the Indus Valley.
* **The First "Herds":** Early livestock management wasn't always about slaughter; many prehistoric groups practiced "selective management," protecting wild herds from other predators before fully enclosing them.
