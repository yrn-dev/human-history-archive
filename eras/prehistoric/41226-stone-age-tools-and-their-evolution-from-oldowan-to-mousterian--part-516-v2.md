# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely overlooked the logistics of the materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. To understand the evolution of prehistoric technology, we must examine the "raw material economy": how early hominins identified, acquired, and transported stone.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian was not just a leap in cognitive ability regarding shape, but a revolution in spatial awareness and resource management.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, often within a few hundred meters of the site. The selection process was rudimentary; as long as the stone was cryptocrystalline (like basalt or quartzite) and could produce a sharp edge, it was used.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective sourcing." They no longer settled for the nearest available rock; instead, they sought out high-quality flint, chert, or obsidian, which offered more predictable fracturing patterns. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and a capacity for forward planning.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian industry, associated largely with Neanderthals, reveals a sophisticated understanding of transport costs. Archaeologists have discovered tool assemblages containing materials sourced from dozens of kilometers away. This implies one of two things: either the groups were highly migratory, or they had established rudimentary networks of exchange with other bands.

The effort required to carry heavy raw nodules over long distances indicates that the *quality* of the edge was now more valuable than the *convenience* of the source. This transition marks the beginning of a "curated" technology—where tools were designed to be resharpened and maintained over time, rather than discarded immediately after a single use.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Chance

There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether the transport of high-quality stone was a conscious "trade" or simply a byproduct of seasonal migration. Some researchers argue that the presence of exotic stones at specific sites suggests "social signaling," where possessing a rare material denoted status or group identity. Others maintain a more functionalist view, arguing that the movement of stone was purely a matter of survival and resource availability.

### Key Facts
* **Cryptocrystalline Structure:** The preferred quality of stone for toolmaking, allowing for a "conchoidal fracture" (a smooth, curved break).
* **Oldowan Radius:** Most Oldowan tools were sourced within a very short distance (often <1km) from the site of use.
* **Curated Tools:** A shift in the Mousterian era toward tools that were maintained and carried, rather than made and thrown away.
* **Material Preference:** Flint and obsidian were highly prized for their ability to produce razor-sharp edges.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expenditure required to move raw materials, which influenced the settlement patterns of prehistoric humans.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge only a few molecules thick, making it sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** In the Mousterian period, hominins prepared a "core" of stone specifically to strike off a single, perfectly shaped flake, reducing material waste.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use "trace element analysis" to match a stone tool found in a cave to the exact quarry it came from miles away.
