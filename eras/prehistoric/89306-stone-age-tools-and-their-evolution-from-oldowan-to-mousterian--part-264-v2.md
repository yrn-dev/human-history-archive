# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and techniques used to create Oldowan choppers or Mousterian scrapers—we must shift our gaze from the finished product to the raw material. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in logistics, geology, and spatial planning.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a fundamental shift in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their landscape. To understand the tool, one must first understand the stone.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized river cobbles—basalt, quartz, or quartzite—found in their immediate vicinity. The "raw material economy" was local; if a suitable stone was not within a few hundred meters of the activity site, the tool was simply not made.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis* and early *Homo sapiens*), we see the emergence of "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials offer a predictable "conchoidal fracture," allowing for the precise, thin flakes essential for the Levallois technique.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The shift to the Mousterian era reveals a sophisticated understanding of geography. Archaeological evidence shows that raw materials were often transported over significant distances—sometimes tens of kilometers—from the original quarry to the habitation site. 

This movement suggests two critical cognitive developments:
1. **Forethought:** The ability to plan for future needs rather than reacting to immediate requirements.
2. **Spatial Mapping:** A mental map of the landscape that identified "resource hotspots."

There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were moved by individuals during seasonal migrations or if they represent the earliest forms of social exchange (trade) between disparate groups. While "trade" may be too modern a term, the distribution of non-local flint suggests a complex social network.

## Key Facts: The Materiality of the Stone Age
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The glass-like breaking pattern of flint and obsidian that allowed for the creation of razor-sharp edges.
* **Core vs. Flake:** Oldowan tools focused on the "core" (the main rock), while Mousterian tools focused on the "flake" (the piece struck off).
* **Transport Costs:** The distance a material traveled is often used by archaeologists to estimate the home range of a prehistoric group.
* **Material Preference:** High-silica stones were preferred for their durability and ability to hold a sharp edge.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian’s Edge:** Obsidian can be fractured to an edge only a few molecules thick, making it sharper than a modern surgical steel scalpel.
* **Curated Tools:** In the Mousterian period, tools were often "curated," meaning they were resharpened and reused multiple times rather than discarded.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern geologists can use "trace element analysis" to pinpoint the exact quarry a 100,000-year-old flint tool came from.
