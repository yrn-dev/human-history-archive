# The Raw Material Economy: Lithic Procurement and Transport

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical effort behind the materials themselves. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a shift in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived and managed their landscape.

The transition from opportunistic foraging to strategic procurement reveals a growing capacity for foresight and spatial mapping. To understand the tool, one must first understand the stone.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally utilized whatever cobbles were available in their immediate vicinity, often sourced from nearby stream beds. The selection process was rudimentary; as long as the stone was hard enough to produce a sharp edge, it was viable.

However, as we move into the Acheulean period, a distinct shift occurs. Hominins began to exhibit "selective procurement," bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials like quartzite or fine-grained basalt. This suggests that *Homo erectus* possessed a mental map of their territory, knowing exactly where superior raw materials were located, sometimes kilometers away from their primary campsites.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique—which requires a carefully prepared core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—demanded materials with predictable fracturing patterns, such as flint or chert.

This necessity led to the emergence of "curated" toolkits. Rather than discarding a tool after a single use, Mousterian toolmakers invested significant energy into transporting high-quality stone over vast distances. Some archaeological sites show evidence of "exotic" stones transported over 50 kilometers, indicating either long-distance migration or an early form of social exchange between groups.

## The Scholarly Debate: Trade or Travel?
A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the presence of non-local stone indicates "trade" or simply "mobility." Some scholars argue that the transport of high-quality flint suggests a social network where materials were exchanged between bands. Others maintain that it simply reflects the wide home ranges of Neanderthals, who carried their "tool kits" with them as they followed migrating herds.

### Key Facts: Lithic Procurement
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic; materials sourced within a few hundred meters of the site.
* **Acheulean:** Evidence of selective sourcing; preference for materials that allowed for symmetrical hand-axes.
* **Mousterian:** High reliance on cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for precision flaking.
* **Transport:** The distance raw materials were carried increased significantly from the Early to the Middle Paleolithic.
* **Curation:** The shift from "expedient" tools (made and thrown away) to "curated" tools (maintained and carried).

### Did You Know?
* **The "Flint" Standard:** Not all regions had flint; in areas lacking it, prehistoric humans adapted their techniques to work with tougher materials like quartzite, though it required more effort to shape.
* **Core Exhaustion:** Archaeologists can tell how "precious" a stone was by how much of the core was used; Mousterian cores are often worked down to tiny fragments to extract every possible flake.
* **Mental Templates:** The ability to seek out a specific stone type before needing the tool is considered a major milestone in the evolution of the hominin prefrontal cortex.
