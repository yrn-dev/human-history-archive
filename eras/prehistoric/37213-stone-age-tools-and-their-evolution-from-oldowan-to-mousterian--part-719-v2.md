# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must shift our focus to the *provenance* of the stone itself. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a revolution in geological knowledge and spatial planning.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian reflects a growing sophistication in how early hominins interacted with their landscape, moving from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic mining of high-quality raw materials.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartz pebbles from nearby stream beds. The "cost" of transporting stone was high relative to the energy gained, meaning tools were often discarded as soon as they became dull.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "raw material selectivity." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials provide a predictable "conchoidal fracture," allowing for the precision required to create the refined scrapers and points characteristic of the Mousterian toolkit.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with the landscape had fundamentally changed. Archaeological evidence reveals that raw materials were often transported over several kilometers—and in some cases, tens of kilometers—from their original source.

This shift implies two critical cognitive developments: 
1. **Future Planning:** The ability to envision a need for a tool long before the task began.
2. **Mental Mapping:** A sophisticated understanding of geography and the location of specific geological veins.

There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individuals traveled vast distances to quarry sites. Regardless, the movement of stone suggests a structured social organization and a level of environmental mastery far beyond the simple "smash-and-use" method of the earliest ancestors.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily used local river cobbles; minimal transport distances.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; introduction of larger, more durable handaxes.
* **Mousterian:** High preference for flint and obsidian for superior edge sharpness.
* **Transport:** Evidence shows Mousterian hominins moved stone across diverse terrains to reach quality quarries.
* **Waste Analysis:** "Debitage" (stone flakes) patterns show that tools were often roughed out at the source and finished at the camp.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern surgical steel scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, Mousterian tools show signs of "re-sharpening," meaning they were valued objects kept for long periods.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early humans had to distinguish between "look-alike" stones to ensure the material wouldn't shatter unpredictably during the knapping process.
