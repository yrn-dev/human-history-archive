# Angaraland and the Northern Land Mass

## Overview of the Northern Land Mass
The northern land mass, referred to by E. Suess as Angaraland, is a significant geological region of Asia. It begins in the north within the area situated between the Lena and the Yenisei rivers. Geological evidence indicates that much of the north and east of Asia has remained land since the Palaeozoic era.

## Geological Composition and Stratigraphy
The foundation of this region consists of folded Archean rocks. These are overlaid by beds from the Ordovician and Cambrian periods, which generally remain undisturbed and flat. Resting upon these strata are patches of freshwater deposits, primarily composed of conglomerate and sandstone, which contain numerous plant remains and workable seams of coal. These deposits are categorized under the general name of the Angara series; while some appear to be of Permian age, others are likely Jurassic.

In addition to the plant beds, the region features extensive outflows of basic lava that rest directly on the Ordovician and Cambrian strata. Although the exact date of these eruptions remains uncertain, they likely continued into a very recent period.

## Marine History and Environmental Changes
Except for the extreme north, where Cretaceous and Jurassic marine fossils have been discovered, there is no evidence that this part of Siberia has been submerged beneath the sea since the early part of the Palaeozoic era. This contrasts with other parts of the Asian continent, as a large portion of Asia was covered by the sea during the Ordovician and Cambrian periods.

## Continental Relations and Global Context
Angaraland is one of two primary ancient land masses. The southern land mass, of which the Indian peninsula is a fragment, is known as Gondwanaland (a term used by Neumayr, Suess, and others). The Indian peninsula itself has been proven to have never been beneath the sea since at least the Carboniferous period.

Between Angaraland and Gondwanaland lies an area that was beneath the sea during the majority of the Mesozoic era, characterized by well-developed marine deposits of Mesozoic age. This intervening sea is referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr.

## Related Regional Geology
While the northern land mass remained largely dry, other regions of western Asia experienced different conditions. During the later stages of the Cretaceous period, the sea covered the drainage area of the Aral Sea, the basin of the Obi, Arabia, Persia, Baluchistan, and Afghanistan, though a considerable portion of this area was likely dry land during Jurassic times.
