# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For early hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and transport.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding how prehistoric humans selected and moved stone provides a window into their territorial range and social organization.

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used. The "cost" of transport was kept low, suggesting a highly localized existence.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "selectivity." Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials allow for more predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture), which was essential for the Levallois technique—the sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) reveals a complex relationship with the landscape. Archaeological evidence shows that high-quality stone was often transported over distances of 10 to 50 kilometers. This implies several critical behavioral shifts:

1. **Mental Mapping:** The ability to remember the location of a specific quarry across seasons.
2. **Planning Depth:** Carrying "blank" cores—semi-finished stones—to be refined later at a hunting site.
3. **Social Exchange:** There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were moved by individuals or exchanged between small kinship groups, hinting at the earliest forms of social networking.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily used local river cobbles; minimal transport distance.
* **Acheulean:** Increased preference for finer-grained stones to achieve the symmetry of the hand-axe.
* **Mousterian:** High reliance on flint and obsidian for precision flaking.
* **Levallois Technique:** A "prepared core" method that required high-quality, homogenous stone to be successful.
* **Transport Radius:** Expanded from a few hundred meters (Oldowan) to dozens of kilometers (Mousterian).

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** By the Mousterian era, tools were "curated," meaning they were resharpened and reused multiple times rather than discarded immediately.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early humans possessed an intuitive understanding of geology, knowing exactly which rock layers yielded the most durable materials for heavy-duty chopping versus fine scraping.
