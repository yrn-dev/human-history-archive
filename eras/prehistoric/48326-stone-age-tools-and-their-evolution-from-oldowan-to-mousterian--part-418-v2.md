# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must examine the logistical intelligence behind the stone. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in resource management and spatial planning.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a shift from "opportunistic foraging" to "strategic procurement." To understand the Stone Age, we must look not just at the tool, but at the distance between the tool and its geological source.

## From Local Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 million years ago), hominins generally utilized materials found in their immediate vicinity. If a riverbed provided basalt or quartz, that is what was used. The "transport distance" was minimal, suggesting a lifestyle tied closely to the immediate environment.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian traditions, a pattern of selective procurement emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint or obsidian. These materials produce a more predictable "conchoidal fracture," allowing for the precise edges required for Levallois technique flakes.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the Mousterian era (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), the transport of raw materials became a hallmark of social organization. Evidence shows that high-quality stone was often carried over several kilometers, and in some instances, tens of kilometers, from the source to the habitation site.

This suggests two critical evolutionary developments:
1. **Forethought:** The ability to plan for future needs rather than reacting to immediate requirements.
2. **Territorial Mapping:** A sophisticated mental map of the landscape, identifying "quarry sites" as permanent landmarks.

There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether this transport was the result of nomadic movement (carrying stone while migrating) or intentional "mining" expeditions where groups traveled specifically to acquire raw materials.

## Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Raw Material Selectivity:** Early hominins preferred materials like quartzite and basalt; later groups prioritized flint and obsidian for their superior sharpness.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools are typically found within meters of their source; Mousterian tools often show transport distances of 10km or more.
* **Curation:** As tools became more complex to make, they became more "curated," meaning they were sharpened and reused rather than discarded.
* **The Levallois Leap:** The Mousterian Levallois technique required a specific type of stone "core" that could be shaped to produce a predictable flake.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** Some of the earliest evidence of "trade" or long-distance exchange is found in the movement of obsidian, a volcanic glass that is incredibly sharp but geographically rare.
* **The "Core" Logic:** In the Mousterian tradition, the "core" (the parent stone) was often carried as a portable toolkit, allowing the user to strike off new blades wherever they traveled.
* **Experimental Archaeology:** Modern historians use "knapping" (the act of striking stone) to determine how much energy and time were required to transport and shape these tools.
