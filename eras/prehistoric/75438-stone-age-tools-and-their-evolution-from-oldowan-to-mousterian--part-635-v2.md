# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of tool technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian industries represents a fundamental change in "lithic economy." Early toolmakers were opportunistic, while later hominins became strategic geologists.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was characterized by local opportunism. *Homo habilis* typically utilized whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often scavenging riverbeds for basalt or quartz. The "cost" of transport was high, and the willingness to travel for specific stone was low.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked shift toward selectivity. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flaking necessary to create the refined scrapers and points of the Mousterian toolkit.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade
The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) reveals a sophisticated understanding of resource management. Archaeological sites show that raw materials were often transported over distances of several kilometers, and in some cases, dozens of kilometers, from their original source.

This suggests two critical behavioral evolutions:
1. **Planning Depth:** The ability to envision a future need for a tool and secure the material in advance.
2. **Social Networking:** There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether the presence of non-local stone indicates individual long-distance foraging or the earliest forms of inter-group exchange (trade). While "trade" may be too modern a term, the movement of high-quality stone across territories implies a complex spatial awareness and social organization.

## The Efficiency of the Core
The transition to the Levallois technique—a hallmark of the Mousterian—was as much about material economy as it was about tool shape. By carefully preparing a stone core to strike off a single, predetermined flake, hominins maximized the amount of cutting edge produced from a single piece of raw material. This efficiency was vital when the distance to the nearest high-quality quarry was great.

### Key Facts: Lithic Sourcing
* **Material Preference:** High-silica stones (flint, chert, obsidian) were preferred for their predictable fracturing.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools were typically sourced within a few hundred meters; Mousterian materials often traveled many kilometers.
* **The Levallois Method:** A "prepared core" technique that reduced waste and increased the utility of rare raw materials.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Later Paleolithic hominins demonstrated an ability to identify specific geological strata containing the best stone.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after use, Mousterian tools show evidence of "curation"—being carried, reused, and resharpened.
* **The "Quartzite Challenge":** In regions lacking flint, hominins adapted their techniques to work with tougher quartzite, proving that cognitive flexibility was as important as the material itself.
