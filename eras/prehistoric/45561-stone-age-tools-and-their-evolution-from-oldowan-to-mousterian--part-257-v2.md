# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes of the Oldowan choppers or the symmetry of Mousterian points—we must shift our focus to the *geology*. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a sophisticated evolution in the understanding of raw materials and the logistics of procurement.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a fundamental shift in how early hominins interacted with their landscape. Tool-making was not a spontaneous act; it was a calculated economic activity involving the identification, extraction, and transport of specific minerals.

## From Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized river cobbles—basalt, quartz, or quartzite—found in the immediate vicinity of their campsites. The "cost" of transport was low, and the selection criteria were basic: the stone simply needed to be hard enough to hold an edge.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass readily available local stones in favor of higher-quality materials, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a cryptocrystalline structure, allowing for a predictable "conchoidal fracture," which is essential for the precise flaking required to create the refined scrapers and points of the Mousterian toolkit.

## The Logistics of the Landscape

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) reveals a complex spatial awareness. Archaeological evidence shows that raw materials were often transported over several kilometers—and in some cases, tens of kilometers—from the original quarry to the site of use. 

This suggests two critical developments: first, a mental map of the environment that allowed hominins to remember the locations of high-quality outcrops; and second, a social structure capable of planning for future needs. The shift from "find-and-use" to "source-and-transport" indicates a transition from immediate survival to strategic foresight.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing scholarly debate concerns the presence of "exotic" stones found far from their geological source. Some archaeologists argue that this evidence points to early trade networks between disparate groups of hominins. Others suggest a more nomadic explanation, arguing that the stones were simply carried as "curated cores" during long-distance seasonal migrations.

### Key Facts: Lithic Procurement
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The key physical property of flint and obsidian that allows them to be flaked into sharp, predictable edges.
* **Curated Cores:** Large pieces of raw stone carried by hominins to be flaked into smaller tools as needed during travel.
* **Oldowan Range:** Typically characterized by low-distance transport, often within a few hundred meters of the water source.
* **Mousterian Range:** Evidence of raw material transport extending several kilometers, indicating planned foraging.
* **Material Preference:** The shift from generic basalt/quartzite to specialized flint/chert.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge only a few molecules thick, making it sharper than a modern surgical steel scalpel.
* **The "Cost" of Stone:** Archaeologists use "Least Cost Path" analysis to determine the most likely routes early humans took to reach quarries.
* **Recycling Tools:** In some Mousterian sites, evidence shows that tools were repeatedly reshaped and "recycled" to extend the life of the raw material.
