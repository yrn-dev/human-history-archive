# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in *sourcing* the stone. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a change in shaping technique, but a revolution in spatial awareness and resource management.

For the early hominins of the Oldowan tradition, tool production was largely opportunistic. They utilized "local" materials, often gathering cobbles from nearby stream beds. However, as we transition toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian, we see a sophisticated shift: the intentional selection of high-quality raw materials and the transport of these materials over vast distances.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan period (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), the primary goal was functionality. If a basalt or quartz pebble was available and could be flaked to create a sharp edge, it was used. There was little evidence of "curation"—the act of keeping a tool for long-term use or transporting raw stone to a future site.

By the Acheulean era, associated with *Homo erectus*, a cognitive shift occurred. Hominins began to distinguish between different types of stone based on their fracturing properties. They sought out cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint or chert, which allow for more predictable, razor-sharp flakes. This indicates a mental "template" not just for the finished tool, but for the ideal raw material required to achieve it.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian
The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) represents the pinnacle of Paleolithic raw material economy. Through the Levallois technique, toolmakers prepared a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake. This process is wasteful if the stone is of poor quality.

Consequently, Mousterian sites often reveal "exotic" stones—materials not native to the immediate geology of the camp. This suggests that Neanderthals engaged in strategic planning, scouting quarry sites and transporting heavy raw cores several kilometers to their living sites. This behavior implies a sophisticated understanding of the landscape and a capacity for delayed gratification.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Foraging?
A point of ongoing scholarly debate is whether the presence of non-local stone indicates a primitive form of trade between different hominin groups or simply a wider foraging range. While some archaeologists argue that the movement of high-quality flint suggests social exchange networks, others maintain that it reflects the migratory patterns of highly mobile hunter-gatherers.

### Key Facts
* **Local Sourcing:** Oldowan tools were typically made from materials found within a few hundred meters of the site.
* **Material Preference:** Acheulean toolmakers showed a marked preference for fine-grained stones that could support symmetrical bifacial flaking.
* **The Levallois Leap:** The Mousterian technique required high-quality stone to ensure the "prepared core" did not shatter prematurely.
* **Transport Distance:** Evidence shows some Mousterian raw materials were transported over 10–20 kilometers.
* **Curation:** Later Stone Age tools show signs of "resharpening," indicating that the material was valued enough to be maintained rather than discarded.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Appeal:** In certain regions, volcanic obsidian was prized above all else because it can be flaked to an edge thinner than a modern steel scalpel.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" (the main block of stone) was often treated as a precious resource, carefully shaped over hours to produce multiple tools.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early humans had to understand the internal stress lines of a rock to prevent it from cracking unpredictably during the knapping process.
