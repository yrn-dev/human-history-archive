# Global Vegetation and Biological Affinities

The distribution of plant and animal life across the globe is often characterized by affinities between geographically separated regions, frequently influenced by climate, elevation, and historical geological changes.

## Regional Affinities in Asia and Europe
There is a strong resemblance between the general assemblage of plants and animals found in northern Asia and those in adjacent parts of Europe with similar climates. In Siberia, north of the 50th parallel, the climate is similar to corresponding portions of Europe, although the summers are hotter and winters more severe. Moderate rainfall in this region is sufficient to support abundant vegetation and maintain the water supply of great rivers flowing to the Arctic Sea.

A similar affinity exists between southern Europe and a zone of Asia extending from the Mediterranean to northern China and the Himalaya. This belt—which includes northern Persia, Afghanistan, and Asia Minor—possesses a temperate climate due to its elevation. Rainfall in this zone is sufficient to maintain vigorous vegetation, and while winters are severe and summers hot, they are not extreme. Consequently, the plants and animals here show a marked similarity to those of south Europe, with which the zone is virtually continuous.

## Mountain Vegetation and the Himalaya
In the Himalaya, vegetation is most abundant and varied on the eastern side, where luxuriant forest extends to an elevation of 12,000 ft. Above this level, a sub-alpine region begins, often covered in rhododendron scrub up to 14,000 ft. Lower levels are abundant in palms, *Pandanus*, bamboos, plantains, and tree-ferns. Epiphytal orchids are frequent between 4,000 and 10,000 ft.

The western side of the Himalaya has a drier climate, resulting in less dense and luxuriant forests. Below 8,000 ft., forests are not commonly dense except in secluded low-elevation valleys, and are often covered with rhododendron, oaks, or *Pinus longifolia*. From 8,000 to 12,000 ft., a thick forest of deciduous trees is almost universal, with vegetation continuing up to 18,000 ft. or more. Certain eastern forms, such as tree-ferns, do not reach west of Nepal, while the deodar (cedar) is rarely indigenous east of the sources of the Ganges.

## Island Flora and Fauna
### Kerguelen Island
Kerguelen Island is of volcanic origin, primarily composed of basaltic lavas. While fossil remains suggest it was once thickly clothed in woods, it has been denuded by submergence, volcanic action, and possible climate changes. The current climate is not favorable to permanent vegetation; the island is subject to constant moisture from rain and the cold Antarctic drift, with a mean annual temperature of approximately 39° F.

Despite this, rank vegetation exists on lower mountain slopes, including *Azorella*, *Cotula plumosa*, and a rank grass, *Festuca Cookii*, which grows up to 300 ft. Sir J. D. Hooker identified twenty-one species of flowering plants and seven species of ferns, lycopods, and *Characeae*, along with numerous lichens, *Hepaticae*, and mosses. The Kerguelen's Land cabbage was once abundant but has been reduced by introduced rabbits. The island's fauna includes sea-leopards, sea-elephants, various penguins, and a peculiar teal (*Querquedula Eatoni*). Many insects are present, with a predominance of flightless forms. According to Hooker, this vegetation has no affinities with Africa and may have originated from the American continent.

### Malayan Islands
The vegetation of the Malayan Islands largely resembles that of the hotter, wetter regions of India, though uniform humidity and temperature lead to a predominance of tropical forms like nutmeg and liquidambar. The latter is a conspicuous tree in Java. Due to the proximity of Australia, Australian types have diffused into the region; for example, the casuarina is abundant on the eastern mountains of Java. Australian genera extend further to southern China and the Philippines. In Borneo and Sumatra, rhododendrons descend to sea level, and temperate Australian forms appear on higher mountains.

## Arctic and Temperate Vegetation
In certain arctic regions, wild vegetation in summer is profuse in individual plants but scanty in species, consisting of types identical or allied to those in Lapland or the highest British hills. In the north, the ground is covered by creeping dwarf willows, crow-berries, mosses, and flowers such as the yellow poppy, andromeda, *pyrola*, and *pedicularis*. In South Greenland, vegetation is more abundant and less arctic, with willows growing higher than a man and dwarf birches reaching a few feet in sheltered areas.

In other temperate regions, such as the department influenced by the gulf of Gascony, a mild climate and abundant rainfall support diverse agriculture. This includes the production of wheat, maize, potatoes, flax, and vegetables, as well as the rearing of cattle, sheep, pigs, and horses. Vineyards are grown on lower slopes sheltered from the north wind.
