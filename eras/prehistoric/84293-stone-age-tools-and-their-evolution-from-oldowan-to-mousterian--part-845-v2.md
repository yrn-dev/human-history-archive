# The Raw Material Economy: Lithic Procurement and Selection

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the critical precursor to production: the acquisition of raw materials. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability, but a sophisticated evolution in geological knowledge and logistical planning.

For the prehistoric toolmaker, not all stones were created equal. The transition from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic mining of high-quality stone reflects a fundamental shift in how early hominins interacted with their environment.

## From Opportunism to Selection (Oldowan)
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), raw material selection was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized whatever water-worn cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartz. The "cost" of transporting stone was high, meaning tools were often discarded shortly after use. However, evidence suggests that even at this stage, there was a nascent preference for materials with a predictable fracture pattern (conchoidal fracture), indicating a basic understanding of lithic properties.

## The Logistics of Acheulean Transport
With the rise of *Homo erectus* and the Acheulean industry, we see a marked increase in "transport distance." Handaxes were not always made from local stone; archaeological sites often reveal materials sourced from kilometers away. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and a shift toward "curated" technology. Rather than making a tool and dropping it, hominins began carrying high-quality cores over long distances, treating the stone as a valuable resource to be managed over time.

## The Mousterian and the Specialized Quarry
By the Mousterian period (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with stone became highly specialized. The Levallois technique required high-quality flint or chert to achieve the precise "prepared core" necessary for predictable flake production. 

This era saw the emergence of targeted procurement. Neanderthals did not just look for surface stones; they sought out specific geological seams. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding the extent of "true mining" versus the gathering of surface nodules, but the consistency of material in Mousterian sites suggests a disciplined approach to sourcing that minimized waste and maximized edge durability.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The key property of stones like flint and obsidian that allows them to break in predictable, shell-like curves.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of locating and acquiring raw stone for tool production.
* **Curated Tools:** Tools designed for long-term use and transport, as opposed to "expedient" tools made and discarded on the spot.
* **Material Preference:** The shift from generic basalt/quartz (Oldowan) to specialized flint/chert (Mousterian).

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some later prehistoric contexts, obsidian (volcanic glass) was transported hundreds of miles, suggesting early forms of social exchange or trade networks.
* **The "Cost" of Stone:** Archaeologists use "Least Cost Path" analysis to determine how far prehistoric humans traveled to find specific stone sources.
* **Waste Analysis:** By studying "debitage" (the waste flakes left over), historians can tell if a tool was made on-site or brought in as a finished product.
