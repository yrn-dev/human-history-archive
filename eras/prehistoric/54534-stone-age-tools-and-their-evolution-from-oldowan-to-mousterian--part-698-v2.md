# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must shift our gaze from the finished object to the raw material itself. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in geological prospecting and logistical planning.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality flint and obsidian reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Procurement

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized whatever river cobbles or volcanic rocks were immediately available in their vicinity. The "cost" of transporting material was high relative to the tool's lifespan; once a core was exhausted, it was discarded where it stood.

However, as we move into the Acheulean era, we see the emergence of "raw material selectivity." Hominins began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of materials that fractured more predictably (conchoidal fracture). This indicates a mental map of the environment, where specific outcrops were remembered and revisited, marking the beginning of a primitive supply chain.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with stone had become sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality material to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported high-grade flint over dozens of kilometers. This suggests a level of foresight and social organization previously underestimated. They were no longer just reacting to their environment; they were managing resources. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between disparate groups or if individuals traveled vast distances to secure "premium" stone, but both theories point to a complex social network.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily used local river cobbles; minimal transport distances.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; evidence of transporting materials several kilometers.
* **Mousterian:** High-precision sourcing; use of specialized quarries for flint and obsidian.
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like chert or obsidian) break, which was essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Core Reduction:** The process of removing flakes from a larger stone, which became more efficient over millions of years.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Later Paleolithic humans practiced "curation," meaning they sharpened and reused tools repeatedly rather than discarding them, to save on the effort of sourcing new stone.
* **Geological Literacy:** Prehistoric toolmakers possessed an intuitive understanding of geology, knowing exactly which strata of earth to dig into to find the highest quality nodules of flint.
