# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shift from the crude choppers of the Oldowan to the sophisticated flakes of the Mousterian—we must examine the critical logistical aspect of prehistoric technology: the procurement of raw materials. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped, and the evolution of stone tools is inextricably linked to the evolution of spatial cognition and territorial range.

For the early hominins of the Oldowan tradition, toolmaking was largely opportunistic. They utilized whatever river cobbles or volcanic rocks were immediately available in their vicinity. However, as we transition into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a profound shift in how prehistoric humans interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the Oldowan era (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), the "transport distance" for raw materials was typically minimal. Most stones were sourced within a few kilometers of the site. The focus was on functionality over quality; if a stone could produce a sharp edge, it was sufficient.

By the time of the Acheulean (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), *Homo erectus* began demonstrating a preference for specific lithic materials. They sought out stones with predictable fracturing patterns, such as fine-grained basalt or quartzite, to create the symmetrical handaxe. This suggests a mental map of the environment—the ability to remember where high-quality stone outcrops were located and plan a journey to retrieve them.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of Quality

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) represents a peak in raw material management. The Levallois technique, which involved preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake, required high-quality flint or chert. 

Archaeological evidence indicates that Mousterian toolmakers were not merely scavenging; they were quarrying. In some regions, materials have been found transported over 50 to 100 kilometers from their source. This implies a complex social structure involving either long-distance migration or early forms of trade and exchange between groups. There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport was a result of seasonal nomadic movements or a deliberate "economic" effort to secure superior materials for specialized hunting tools.

## Key Facts
* **Lithic Sourcing:** The study of where stone originated is called "provenance analysis."
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan materials were usually sourced locally; Mousterian materials often traveled vast distances.
* **Material Preference:** Fine-grained stones like flint and obsidian were prized for their "conchoidal fracture," allowing for razor-sharp edges.
* **Cognitive Leap:** The transition from opportunistic gathering to planned quarrying indicates an evolution in foresight and memory.
* **The Levallois Shift:** This technique reduced waste, making the transport of raw stone more efficient.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some prehistoric sites, obsidian (volcanic glass) has been found hundreds of miles from the nearest volcano, suggesting very early trade networks.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded after one use, Mousterian tools show signs of "re-sharpening," indicating they were valued possessions.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" (the main block of stone) was often carried as a portable source of raw material to be used as needed.
