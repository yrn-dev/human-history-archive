# Copper and Bronze Implements in Human History

The transition from the use of stone tools to metal implements marks a significant stage in human cultural development. This progression involves the discovery of native metals, the development of smelting, and the eventual creation of alloys such as bronze and brass.

## The Emergence of Copper Use
In Europe, Neolithic people began utilizing copper in specific centers approximately 6,000 or 7,000 years ago. During this initial phase, copper implements were crafted to follow the same patterns as existing stone tools. These early makers may have first hammered native copper into shape or used moulds designed to match the shape of stone implements to cast the metal.

Over time, humans discovered how to extract copper from ore. One theory, suggested by Lord Avebury, proposes that the secret of smelting was discovered accidentally when lumps of copper ore were placed among the ordinary stones used in cooking fire pits. Evidence for this method has been found at a prehistoric copper smelter site in Spain.

## The Development of Bronze and Brass
Bronze is an alloy consisting of copper and tin. It is more fusible, easier to reduce, and harder than pure copper. The discovery of bronze may have occurred through "dirtiness" rather than intentional skill; in regions such as Cornwall, Hungary, and China, copper ore and tinstone occur naturally in the same veins. Consequently, ancient smelters may have produced bronze by smelting these associated ores together.

Notably, there is little evidence that early humans recognized tin as a separate metal, and no known implements made of tin exist. Most implements described as "pure-copper" actually contain a small amount of tin. Similarly, in India, where copper ore and zinc occur together, the alloy brass was discovered.

## The Bronze Age
Archaeologists use the term "Bronze Age" to describe the cultural stage between the Stone and Iron Ages, characterized by the general use of bronze for utensils, implements, and weapons. This period does not have a fixed chronological value, as different races passed through it at different times. In Europe, most authorities fix the commencement of the Bronze Age between 2000 B.C. and 1800 B.C., though it occurred earlier in some countries and later in others. In recent times, the Mexicans and Peruvians were still in their Bronze Age.

The duration and prevalence of the Bronze Age in any given region were determined by the accessibility of the metals required for the alloy. In some areas, bronze remained an extremely valuable substance even after the Iron Age had begun.

## Archaeological Finds and Overlap
The existence of a distinct Bronze Age has been debated by some archaeologists who point to "mixed finds" in prehistoric burial places, where weapons from different ages are found side by side. However, these finds are explained by the fact that the Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages overlapped in many countries.

Several factors contribute to the mixing of implements in tumuli and sepulchral mounds:
* **Social Status:** In tombs containing multiple bodies, more valuable bronze objects were typically placed only with the remains of the wealthier tenants.
* **Re-use of Sites:** Sepulchral mounds were often opened over successive ages for fresh interments, leading to a mixture of implements from different periods.

A notable observation regarding European finds is the remarkable absence of copper implements among the examined materials.

## Other Metal and Tool Implementations
Beyond the Bronze Age, other metals appeared in the archaeological record. Gold, the first known metal, appears alongside amber and jet in bone ornaments, with Irish Neolithic remains being particularly rich in gold. In Japan, historical and archaeological records indicate the use of iron spades, hand-ploughs, axes, and wooden hoes shod with either iron or bronze. Japanese manufacturing industries also included workers skilled in copper, iron, gold, and silver.

In the far prehistoric past, before the use of metals, "Eoliths" or dawn stones—flints and stones intentionally chipped for scraping, hammering, or fighting—appeared in European deposits between half a million and a million years old.
