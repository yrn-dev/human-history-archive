# Geological Folding and Zonal Structures in Asia

The geological composition of Asia is characterized by distinct regional variations in the arrangement of rock beds, ranging from areas of total quiescence to zones of intense crumpling and folding.

## The Central Asian Folded Belt
A broad belt of geological activity exists north of the southern regions of Asia. In this zone, the Mesozoic deposits and the lower divisions of the Tertiary system have been thrown into folds. These folds extend in a series of arcs from west to east and constitute the principal mountain ranges of central Asia. This belt encompasses several regions, including:
*   Asia Minor
*   Persia
*   Afghanistan
*   Baluchistan
*   The Himalayas
*   The Tian-shan
*   The Burmese ranges (though these differ in direction)

Additionally, the Nan-shan, Kuen-lun, and the mountain ranges of southern China are situated within this same belt, although they may be of an earlier date. While the zone as a whole is characterized as one of crumpling, it is not true that the beds are folded throughout the entire width of this zone; there are considerable tracts that remain but little disturbed, though these are enclosed within the arcs formed by the folds.

## Regions of Quiescence and Faulting
Contrasting with the central belt are regions where folding is absent or limited to ancient strata. In the south—specifically in Arabia, Syria, and the peninsula of India—only the oldest rocks are folded. In these areas, the Tertiary, Mesozoic, and Upper Palaeozoic beds lie almost horizontally, marking a region of faulting or quiescence.

Similarly, a large area including northern China, Mongolia, and the greater part of Siberia is generally free from important folding of Tertiary or Mesozoic age. While mountain ranges exist in this northern region, they are often formed by folds of ancient date (mostly Palaeozoic or Archaean). In many instances, the direction of the mountain chains differs from the direction of the folds, suggesting that their elevation is due to causes other than folding. The configuration of this northern region is largely the result of faulting, which created intervening strips that stand as mountain ridges and trough-like or tray-like depressions. In northern China and large parts of Siberia, Cambrian beds remain as horizontal as they were when first laid down.

## Northern Folding and Burma
In the extreme north, specifically in the mountains of the Taimyr peninsula and the Verkhoyansk range, there are indications of another zone of folding dating from the Mesozoic or later, though available information on these ranges is scanty.

In British Burma, the geology is divided into eastern and western divisions by a line running from the mouth of the Sittang river along the railway to Mandalay and continuing northward. The western mountain ranges feature beds thrown into a series of folds forming a gentle curve from south to north, with the convexity facing westward. This area contains an axial zone of Lower Eocene and Cretaceous, flanked by Miocene and Upper Eocene. The valley of the Irrawaddy is primarily occupied by the Pliocene.
