# Geological and Geographical History of Persia and Arabia

## Geological History and Marine Deposits
The early geological history of Asia is characterized by significant periods of marine submersion. During the Cambrian and Ordovician periods, a large portion of the continent was covered by the sea. While the peninsula of India has remained land since at least the Carboniferous period, and the north and east of Asia have been land since the Palaeozoic era, the region between these masses contains well-developed Mesozoic marine deposits. This intervening area, referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr, was beneath the sea for the majority of the Mesozoic era.

During the later stages of the Cretaceous period, the sea covered a large part of western Asia. This submerged region included Arabia, Persia, Baluchistan, Afghanistan, the drainage area of the Aral Sea, and the basin of the Obi. However, it is probable that a considerable portion of this same region was dry land during Jurassic times.

## Physical Geography and Climate
The peninsula of Arabia, along with its north-western continuation in Syria, shares characteristics with the hottest and driest parts of Baluchistan and Persia. Much of this territory is covered by low mountains reaching altitudes of 3,000 to 4,000 feet, featuring narrow valleys with scanty vegetation. In northern Syria, the mountains of Lebanon rise to approximately 10,000 feet, and the increased water supply makes the land more productive.

In the south-western mountainous regions of Arabia, known as Arabia Felix, summits reach 6,000 or 7,000 feet. The rainfall here is sufficient to support more luxuriant vegetation, with a flora similar to that found in the less elevated parts of Baluchistan, Afghanistan, and southern Persia. This vegetation partakes of the character of the hotter Mediterranean region, where aromatic shrubs are abundant. Trees are rare, consisting primarily of poplars, the date palm, and the genera *Celtis*, *Dodonaea*, and *Pistacia*. The dry hills are covered with prickly forms of *Astragalus* and *Statice*.

## Flora and Agriculture
The cultivated plants in Arabia are largely similar to those found in northern India, including barley, wheat, cotton, indigo, lemons, and the common *Sorghum*, as well as dates. Coffee cultivation is specifically restricted to the slopes of the western hills. During the spring, the region sees abundant herbaceous vegetation and bulbous plants, some of which are identical to those in the Syrian region and extend as far as the Himalaya.

## Human Development and Early History
The ancestors of the white and whitish Neolithic peoples may have developed their Later Palaëolithic arts in south-western Asia, or in regions now submerged beneath the Indian Ocean or the Mediterranean Sea. While Neanderthal men lived in glaciated Europe, these southern peoples mastered the domestication of cattle, the taming of the dog, and the practice of agriculture. Evidence of these early developments is expected to be found in Arabia, Persia, India, north Africa, or Asia Minor. Approximately 12,000 years ago, Neolithic peoples—who were the most advanced in the world at the time—were scattered across Asia, north Africa, and Europe.

## Political and Regional History
Historically, Arabia has been positioned as a wedge between the Euphrates-Tigris country, Palestine, and Egypt. It served as a reservoir for nomadic tribes who traded, raided, and collected tribute from caravans. Various powers have claimed suzerainty or protection over Arabia, including Macedonia, Rome, Syria, Constantinople, Egypt, and Persia. Under the Roman Emperor Trajan, a province of "Arabia" existed, extending as far as Petra and including the fertile Hauran region.

Persia claimed ascendancy over Arabia during the time of Chosroes II, maintaining tax collectors and officials in the Yemen. Prior to this, the Yemen had been ruled by Abyssinian Christians, and for seven centuries before that, it was governed by native princes who professed the Jewish faith. In the Roman and Persian records, desert Arabs eventually became known as Saracens following the destruction of Palmyra.

## Geographical Surveys
Geographical knowledge of western Asia, excluding Arabia, was largely freed from perplexity by the close of the 19th century. This was achieved through Indian triangulation and minor extensions that pushed outwards through Baluchistan and Afghanistan to the eastern districts of Persia, and along the coast of Makran to Arabia. These surveys raised the accuracy of Asiatic geographical data to levels previously deemed unattainable.
