# Domesticated Animals

The history and distribution of domesticated animals vary across different geological eras, geographical regions, and human civilizations, reflecting the evolution of human needs from survival and hunting to organized agriculture and industry.

## Prehistoric Domestication
In the late Palæolithic period, the domestication of animals was limited. It remains an open question whether humans tamed the horse during this time; while carvings of horse heads and drawings of horses with marks suggestive of bridles exist, the animals known then were wild ponies with beards under their chins, likely incapable of carrying a man for any distance. During this era, humans had no dogs and little interaction with domesticated cattle or sheep. It is considered improbable that these early humans used animal milk as food.

The Neolithic stage marked a significant transition as humans evolved from huntsmen into herdsmen. This period is characterized by the domestication of cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs. The dog appeared very early in this stage. Additionally, while the Neolithic man began a form of agriculture and the use of seeds, hunting remained of great importance. Notably, during this period, the horse was no longer eaten.

## Regional Varieties and Species
Domesticated animals differ significantly by geography. In Asia, the elephant is a notable domesticated animal, though it does not breed in captivity and is not found wild west of the Jumna river in northern India. The horse is produced in the hot, dry countries of western Asia and Arabia in the highest perfection, while ponies are most esteemed from hilly tracts and wetter eastern regions. 

Horned cattle in Asia include the buffaloes and humped oxen of India and the yak of Tibet. In Tibet and the Himalaya, a hybrid of Indian cattle and the yak, known as the zo, is commonly reared. In the mountains of Tibet, goats and sheep are utilized as beasts of burden. In northern Siberia, reindeer are used for draught and the saddle.

In Britain, following the invasion, ordinary domestic animals remained unchanged. Cattle and sheep were kept on common lands belonging to the village, while pigs—which were particularly numerous in Kent—were kept in the woods. Bee-keeping was also practiced.

## Canine Evolution and Breeds
It is suggested that all dogs may have originated from one common source, with variations in form resulting from cross-breeding, food, and climate. While savage tribes possess only one form of dog, civilization has led to the creation of many breeds. Wild dogs in northern Europe and Asia bear a resemblance to the ordinary British dog.

In northern regions, semi-domesticated wolf-like dogs exist. The Eskimo dog, which howls rather than barks and is fed on meat, game, and fish, is regarded by some as a reclaimed wolf; the Eskimo maintain its strength by crossing it with wolves. These dogs are cunning hunters but are so rapacious that they cannot be trained to avoid attacking goats, sheep, or other small domestic animals. Similarly, the Hare Indian dog of the Mackenzie river and Great Bear Lake resembles the coyote and is practically unable to bark.

## Economic and Military Utility
Domestic animals have served critical roles in sustenance and economy. In the North, nations of shepherds rely on their herds and flocks. For military forces, such as the Tartars, flocks and herds provide a sure, increasing supply of milk and flesh, which is more efficient than transporting bulky corn. 

In modern industrial contexts, such as in Idaho in the early 20th century, livestock was a primary animal product. By 1908, Idaho's livestock included 3,575,000 sheep, 150,000 horses, 130,000 swine, 69,000 milch cows, and 344,000 other neat cattle. Sheep were particularly valuable for wool production.

In military logistics, such as those seen with German armies in the West, cattle depots and slaughterhouses were used to supply troops. The daily requirements for 1,000 men were calculated as either 19 sheep (average weight 40 kg), 6 pigs (average weight 90 kg), or 2 oxen (average weight 500 kg).

## Terminology and Classification
The term "bull" refers to the male of animals in the family *Bovidae*, specifically the uncastrated male of the domestic ox (*Bos taurus*). The term is also applied to the males of other large animals, such as the whale or elephant. The word "bullock," derived from the Old English *bulluc* (originally meaning a young bull), now refers to a young castrated male ox kept for beef slaughter.
