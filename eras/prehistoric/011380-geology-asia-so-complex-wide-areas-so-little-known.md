# The Geology of Asia

The geology of Asia is characterized by extreme complexity and remains little known over wide areas. Due to these factors, providing a connected account of the continent's development or structure is difficult, and scientific descriptions are generally limited to its broader features.

## Early Geological History and Land Masses
Knowledge of Asia's early geological history is limited, though it is established that a large portion of the continent was submerged under the sea during the Ordovician and Cambrian periods. Evidence indicates that the eastern and northern regions of Asia have been land since the Palaeozoic era. Specifically, the peninsula of India has remained above the sea since at least the Carboniferous period.

Between these ancient land masses lay an area that was beneath the sea for the majority of the Mesozoic era, characterized by well-developed marine deposits. These land masses and the intervening sea have been identified by various researchers:
* **Angaraland:** The name given by E. Suess to the northern land mass.
* **Gondwanaland:** The name used by Neumayr, Suess, and others for the southern land mass, of which the Indian peninsula is a fragment.
* **Tethys / Central Mediterranean Sea:** The intervening sea, referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr.

During the later stages of the Cretaceous period, the sea covered much of western Asia, including Arabia, Persia, Afghanistan, Baluchistan, the drainage area of the Aral Sea, and the basin of the Obi. However, it is probable that a considerable portion of this region was dry land during Jurassic times.

## The Geology of Asia Minor
In Asia Minor, the geological structure varies significantly between the central plateau and the surrounding regions.

### The Central Plateau
The central, or Lycaonian, plateau consists of limestones and freshwater marls of Neogene or late Tertiary age. The strata here are nearly horizontal. Along the south-eastern margin, in front of the Taurus mountains, is a line of now-extinct volcanoes stretching from Argaeus to Kara-Dagh, which were likely active until the end of the Tertiary period.

### Mountain Chains and Coastal Regions
The mountain chains surrounding the plateau form a complex system of intensely folded beds.
* **The Taurus and Anti-Taurus:** These chains form a crescent facing south. The Anti-Taurus contains Devonian and Carboniferous fossils, and its folding affects the Eocene but not the Miocene. In the Taurus, Miocene beds were elevated to great heights without significant folding; limestones of Cretaceous or Eocene age make up a large part of this range.
* **The Pontian Arcs:** North of the Lycaonian plateau are the West and East Pontian arcs. In the east, a mountain system runs from Sinope to Batum, nearly parallel to the Black Sea coast. This system involves folds in the Oligocene, with serpentine and Cretaceous limestones playing a large role in its formation.
* **Other Regions:** West of Sinope, Cretaceous beds run parallel to the shore. Carboniferous rocks are found at Eregli (Heraclea Pontica), where they have been used for coal. Additionally, Carboniferous fossils have been found at Balia Maden in Mysia, and Devonian fossils have been located near the Bosporus. South of the Sea of Marmora, Triassic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous beds form a band. Further south are zones of schistose and crystalline rocks, as well as serpentine, some of which are likely Palaeozoic.

Coastal areas feature flat-lying deposits of Tertiary age that sometimes extend high into the mountain regions.

## Geographic and Territorial Context
The geological study of the continent occurs within a vast area totaling somewhat over 16,819,000 square miles (though estimates vary). This area includes diverse territories, such as those dependent on extra-Asiatic powers (including the Russian, British, Dutch, French, U.S.A., and German powers) and independent or nominally independent territories (including Chinese, Japanese, Turkish, and others). 

In certain regions, such as Arabia, geographical knowledge has progressed slowly. While Colonel Wahab conducted surveys regarding the Aden hinterland boundary, knowledge of the interior desert remains limited to the accounts of foreign travellers and Englishmen such as Sir Lewis Pelly, Sir Richard F. Burton, Wm. Gifford Palgrave, Karsten Niebuhr, John Lewis Burckhardt, Joseph Halevy, Wilfrid S. Blunt, and Charles Doughty.
