# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the acquisition of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in "raw material economy."

For the early hominin, the choice of stone was not arbitrary. The transition from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality flint and obsidian reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and planning.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity—often basalt or quartz cobbles gathered from nearby stream beds. The "transport distance" was minimal, suggesting a lifestyle tied closely to the immediate availability of resources.

However, as we move into the Acheulean era and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), we see the emergence of selective sourcing. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials located kilometers away. This indicates a mental map of the landscape and the ability to delay gratification—carrying a heavy piece of raw flint for miles because they knew its superior conchoidal fracture properties would yield a sharper, more durable edge.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the Mousterian period, the "prepared core" technique (Levallois) demanded high-quality, predictable stone. You cannot reliably execute a Levallois strike on a low-grade, grainy river stone. This necessity drove Neanderthals to seek out specific geological veins.

Archaeological evidence suggests that some Mousterian groups established semi-permanent quarrying sites. This implies a social structure capable of coordinating the transport of bulk materials back to habitation sites. The shift from "finding a stone" to "managing a resource" marks a critical cognitive evolution: the transition from reactive survival to proactive environmental management.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among prehistoric archaeologists is whether the presence of non-local stone at a site indicates a sophisticated trade network between different hominin groups or simply the migratory patterns of a single band. While some argue that the movement of obsidian across vast distances suggests early "exchange economies," others maintain that these materials were simply carried during seasonal migrations.

### Key Facts: Raw Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily used local river cobbles; minimal transport distances.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that allowed for symmetrical bifacial shaping.
* **Mousterian:** Heavy reliance on high-quality flint and obsidian to support the Levallois technique.
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The key physical property (glass-like breaking) that made flint and obsidian the "gold standard" for prehistoric tools.
* **Transport Cost:** The energy expenditure required to move stone, which became a primary driver in site selection.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge only a few molecules thick, making it sharper than a modern surgical steel scalpel.
* **Curated Tools:** In later periods, tools were often "re-sharpened" multiple times, showing that high-quality stone was viewed as a precious, curated asset.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Mousterian toolmakers demonstrated an intuitive understanding of geology, often identifying hidden flint nodules beneath layers of limestone.
