# Marine Fossils and Geological Deposits

The study of marine fossils and geological strata provides evidence regarding the historical composition of landmasses and the evolution of early life. The presence or absence of marine deposits in specific regions allows geologists to determine whether an area was submerged or remained land during various geological eras.

## Regional Evidence of Landmasses
In certain parts of the world, the total absence of marine fossils serves as a primary indicator that a region has been land for an extended period. In the peninsula of India, marine fossils are absent except near its borders. This region contains terrestrial and freshwater deposits of the Gondwana series, which span the geological scale from the top of the Carboniferous to the top of the Jurassic. This evidence suggests that the region has been land since the Carboniferous period and was likely part of a larger landmass that extended across the Indian Ocean and was once united with southern Africa.

Similarly, a broad area stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the south of China shows no marine deposits later than the Carboniferous, with the exception of the extreme north. While Mesozoic age terrestrial and freshwater deposits are found in many places, the limits of this landmass are defined by marine Mesozoic deposits found in Tibet to the south, the western Tian-shan to the west, and Japan to the east. The Verkhoyansk Range's Triassic deposits further indicate that this land did not reach the Bering Sea.

In Bolivia, the eastern ranges of the Andes consist of Palaeozoic rocks. While marine fossils found by Gustav Steinmann in the middle of a series of red sandstones and gypsiferous marls indicate an age no earlier than the Jurassic (and are referred to the Lower Cretaceous), no marine deposits later than these have been found in the plains of Bolivia or the eastern Andes. Instead, a wide area is occupied by freshwater beds of Tertiary and later date.

## Marine Formations and Fossil Records
Various geological beds provide a record of marine and estuarine life. In England, the London Clay is a marine deposit 500 feet thick, consisting of brown or blue clay with sandy layers. Its equivalent in the Hampshire area is known as the Bognor Clay. Other formations, such as the Woolwich and Reading beds, contain a mixture of estuarine and marine fossils. The Thanet sands are also a marine formation, though fossils are scarce except in East Kent.

The Bembridge beds in Hampshire and the Isle of Wight represent a fluvio-marine series of Oligocene age. The Bembridge marls, which consist of marine, estuarine, and freshwater clays and marls, contain forms such as *Melanopsis*, *Ostrea*, and *Meretrix*.

## Early Marine Life and the Azoic Era
The earliest rocks in the geological record are termed Azoic rocks because they contain no traces of life. These rocks, found in great uncovered areas of North America, represent at least half of the 1,600,000,000 years assigned to the total geological record. While these rocks show rain marks and ripplings, they contain no vestiges of living things.

Following this period, the Cambrian period saw the emergence of diverse marine life. Fossil records from this era include:
* **Trilobites**: Such as *Paradoxides* and *Olenellus*.
* **Other organisms**: Jellyfishes, *Humenocaris*, *Protospongia*, *Orthoceras*, *Hyolithes* (swimming snails), *Obolella* (lampshells), *Bryograptus*, and *Archæocyathus* (coral).

## Specialized Marine Fossil Finds
Marine deposits have also preserved animals that were not primarily aquatic. The lithographic stone of Solenhofen in Bavaria is a deposit of marine origin containing numerous and perfect remains of gigantic dragon-flies. Because these fossils show no traces of subaquatic conditions—which are typically present in other strata—it is concluded that the insects were drowned in the sea and subsequently washed ashore.

Additionally, the Miocene strata of Oregon have yielded a skull of a fossil sea-lion, named *Pontoleon magnus*. This skull is considerably larger than any existing sea-lion skull, with a probable basal length of about 20 inches.
