# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a fundamental shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

For the early toolmaker, the world was not a uniform expanse of rock, but a map of high-quality "nodes." The ability to identify, transport, and curate specific materials reveals a growing capacity for foresight and spatial memory.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Early *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity to their campsites, often sourced from nearby riverbeds. The "transport distance" was minimal; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used.

However, as we transition into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "raw material selectivity." Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—that allowed for sharper edges and more predictable fracturing. This suggests a mental shift from "using what is here" to "going where the quality is."

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. Archaeologists have discovered evidence of "quarrying," where specific sites were revisited over generations. 

More impressively, the transport distances increased significantly. Some Mousterian tools were crafted from materials sourced tens of kilometers away from the site of use. This implies a complex logistical chain: the material had to be harvested, transported (possibly in caches), and then carefully curated. Because the stone was "expensive" in terms of the energy required to move it, the Mousterian technique of producing multiple flakes from a single prepared core (the Levallois technique) was an essential innovation in material economy.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among prehistoric archaeologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stone (material not native to the immediate area) proves the existence of early trade networks between different hominin groups, or if it simply reflects the wide migratory ranges of a single group. While the "trade" hypothesis is compelling, most scholars currently lean toward high mobility and strategic caching as the primary drivers.

### Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic sourcing; low transport distances; reliance on local river cobbles.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that support symmetrical bifacial flaking.
* **Mousterian:** Systematic quarrying; high-quality material preference (flint/obsidian); significant transport distances.
* **Curated Technology:** The shift from "expedient" tools (made and discarded) to "curated" tools (maintained and carried).
* **Materiality:** The choice of stone was dictated by "conchoidal fracture," the property that allows stone to break in predictable, shell-like curves.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The Energy Cost:** Transporting heavy raw stone over long distances required a caloric investment, making the "efficiency" of the tool's design a matter of survival.
* **Geological Maps:** By mapping the source of stone tools, modern historians can effectively reconstruct the "home ranges" and migratory paths of extinct hominin species.
