# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shift from the crude Oldowan choppers to the refined Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical intelligence required to acquire the stone itself. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in geological prospecting and spatial planning.

For the early hominin, a tool was only as effective as the material from which it was knapped. The transition from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic mining of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric humans perceived their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Procurement

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely "opportunistic." Hominins typically utilized materials found in their immediate vicinity, such as basalt or quartz river pebbles. The distance between the raw material source and the site of use was minimal, suggesting a limited mental map of the surrounding territory.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see the emergence of "selective procurement." Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—which offered more predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fractures). This indicates a sophisticated understanding of mineralogy and a willingness to travel several kilometers to secure a specific resource.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the "raw material economy" had become complex. The implementation of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality stone to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence suggests that Mousterian tool-makers were not just gathering stone; they were transporting "prepared cores." By carrying a pre-shaped block of stone rather than a finished tool, they maximized the utility of the weight they carried, allowing them to produce multiple specialized edges on the move. This represents a transition from immediate-return foraging to a delayed-return strategy, marking a critical milestone in cognitive evolution.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Territory?

A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the site) indicates early trade networks between different hominin groups or simply an expanded home range. While some argue that obsidian transport suggests a primitive form of exchange, others maintain it reflects the high mobility of nomadic bands following migratory game.

### Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The glass-like break pattern essential for creating sharp edges, found primarily in flint, chert, and obsidian.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expenditure required to carry heavy raw materials, which influenced the development of "curated" tools (tools designed to last).
* **Core Preparation:** The shift from using the whole stone to preparing a "core" to produce multiple flakes.
* **Sourcing:** The process of identifying the geological origin of a stone tool to map prehistoric movement.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after one use, Mousterian tools show signs of repeated sharpening (resharpening), proving they were valued possessions.
* **Geological Maps:** By analyzing the chemical composition of stone tools, modern historians can pinpoint the exact quarry used by hominins hundreds of thousands of years ago.
