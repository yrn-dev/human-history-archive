# History and Evolution of Bankruptcy Systems

## Early Practices and Primitive Communities
In primitive communities, formal bankruptcy systems were unknown. When debtors failed to meet their obligations, individual creditors were left to pursue remedies sanctioned by the law or practice of the community. These remedies were generally characterized by savage severity. Under the Roman law of the Twelve Tables, creditors could, as a final resort, cut the body of the debtor into pieces, with each creditor taking a proportionate share. While some doubt exists regarding the literal interpretation of this specific law, the practice of selling the debtor and their family into slavery was habitually exercised in Rome, Greece, and other nations of antiquity.

Even among the Israelites, whose legislation was comparatively more humane, such practices occurred. This is illustrated in the Old Testament by the account of a woman who sought help from Elisha, stating that her husband had died and a creditor had come to take her two children as bondmen.

## Transition to Imprisonment and Feudalism
As popular rights grew and more humane ideas developed, the severe laws of antiquity were found to be inconsistent with social progress. In Greece and Rome, these harsh laws created serious political disturbances between the plebeians, who comprised the majority of debtors, and the patricians, who generally formed the wealthier creditor class. Consequently, later legislation replaced the right to sell the debtor's person with imprisonment in a public prison.

Under the feudal systems of Europe, the state generally required that subjects remain free for military service. Because of this, debts could not be enforced against the person of the debtor. However, as trade developed, it became necessary to apply personal pressure to compel debtors to meet their obligations. In the English courts of law, the right of a creditor to imprison a debtor gradually evolved, though no express legal enactment for this practice appears to have existed at the time.

## National Bankruptcy Systems

### United Kingdom and the British Empire
In most parts of the British Empire, bankruptcy law is modeled on the English system, specifically in New Zealand and Australia. Victoria, Western Australia, and South Australia follow existing English acts, while New South Wales, Tasmania, and Queensland follow the English act of 1869, which allows for more creditor management and less officialism. 

A distinction exists regarding the discharge of bankrupts. In the United Kingdom, a bankrupt is not obligated to apply for discharge, resulting in a population of 70,000 undischarged bankrupts. In contrast, New Zealand, Victoria, and New South Wales require bankrupts to apply for discharge within a fixed period or face a charge of contempt of court.

In Ireland, bankruptcy is regulated by statutes from 1857 and 1872, including the Irish Debtors Act 1872. While it shares features with older English enactments and the English act of 1869, Ireland never adopted the system of liquidation by arrangement and composition without court approval. Additionally, an 1888 act established local bankruptcy courts in certain Irish districts.

### United States
The United States bankruptcy system is embodied in the National Bankruptcy Act of 1898 and the act of 1903. Acts of bankruptcy include transferring property to hinder creditors, preferring one creditor over others while insolvent, making a general assignment for creditors' benefit, or admitting in writing an inability to pay and a willingness to be adjudicated a bankrupt. Notably, the mere non-payment of debts is not an act of bankruptcy; a debtor can only be adjudicated bankrupt on the grounds of indebtedness with their own written consent.

### France
Regulated by the Commercial Code of 1807 and the law of June 9, 1838, French bankruptcy is exclusively for the protection of commerce. Only traders or commercial companies can be declared bankrupt. The law distinguishes between *faillite* (simple insolvency of a trader) and *banqueroute* (insolvency involving fraud or misconduct). A trader may be adjudicated bankrupt if they have suspended payment, even if they are not yet insolvent. 

The process involves the appointment of a *syndic provisoire* to manage the estate and a *juge commissaire* for supervision. Bankruptcy ends through a sale of assets (*union*), an ordinary composition (*concordat*), or relinquishment of assets. A distinct feature of the French system is that unpaid balances remain a debt of honour; until every penny is paid, the trader is not rehabilitated and remains under various disabilities.

### Germany
Germany adopted a unified code in 1877 to replace a chaotic system of individual state laws. Like France, Germany distinguishes between simple bankruptcy (caused by extravagance, gambling, or disorderly book-keeping) and fraudulent bankruptcy (involving the falsification of books or concealment of property). Fraudulent bankruptcy is punishable by penal servitude or imprisonment. 

In the German system, creditors meet within one month of adjudication and may appoint a committee to advise the trustee. Assets are typically sold by auction to ensure rapid liquidation and dividend payment. Compositions may be accepted by a majority in number and three-fourths in value of the creditors, subject to court approval.

## Administration and Costs
The administration of bankruptcy is designed to be self-supporting. Costs are covered by fees charged to bankrupt estates, interest on balances in bankruptcy estates accounts, and interest on unclaimed funds from former acts. These funds pay for official receivers' salaries, county court registrars' remuneration, pensions for retired officers, prosecution costs, and incidental expenses such as rent and stationery.
