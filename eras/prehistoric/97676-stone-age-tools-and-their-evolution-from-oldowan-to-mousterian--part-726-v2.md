# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian is not merely a story of better shapes, but a story of expanding geography and strategic planning.

For the early hominin, a stone tool was only as effective as the mineral it was carved from. The shift in sourcing patterns reveals a profound evolution in spatial memory and social organization.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing
The Oldowan industry (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago) was characterized by "opportunistic" sourcing. Early *Homo habilis* generally utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, often within a few hundred meters of the habitation site. The focus was on immediate utility; if a suitable river pebble was available, it was used.

However, as we transition into the Acheulean period, we see the emergence of "selective" sourcing. *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials, such as fine-grained basalt or quartzite, often transporting these materials several kilometers. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and the ability to delay gratification—carrying a heavy raw stone today because they knew they would need a superior edge tomorrow.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of Quality
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique required a specific type of stone—one that could be carefully prepared to strike off a predictable flake. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often traveled vast distances to reach specific quarries. This implies a structured "raw material economy." There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individuals traveled in seasonal circuits to harvest stone. Regardless, the transport of high-quality flint over dozens of kilometers indicates a level of foresight and territorial knowledge far beyond the Oldowan baseline.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, relying on local riverbed cobbles.
* **Acheulean Shift:** Introduction of selective sourcing, with materials transported over several kilometers.
* **Mousterian Precision:** High demand for specific lithic properties (like cryptocrystalline silica) to support the Levallois technique.
* **Cognitive Load:** The transition from local to distant sourcing indicates an evolution in spatial memory and long-term planning.
* **Material Preference:** Preference shifted from any "hard stone" to specific minerals that provided predictable fracturing patterns.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after one use, Mousterian tools were often "curated," meaning they were sharpened and reused multiple times to preserve the precious imported stone.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans developed an intuitive understanding of geology, identifying "nodules" of flint hidden within limestone layers.
* **Transport Costs:** Historians use "Least Cost Path" analysis to determine how prehistoric humans moved raw materials across rugged terrains.
