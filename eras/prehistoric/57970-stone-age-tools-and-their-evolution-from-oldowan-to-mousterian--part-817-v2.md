# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and transport.

The transition from the Early to the Middle Paleolithic represents a shift from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding how early humans selected their materials provides a window into their spatial awareness and social organization.

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally relied on "local" materials. If a riverbed provided basalt or quartz pebbles, those were used. The distance between the raw material source and the site of use was typically minimal. The focus was on immediate utility; the goal was a sharp edge, regardless of the stone's long-term durability or aesthetic quality.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a distinct change emerges. Hominins began to bypass readily available local stones in favor of higher-quality materials, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a "conchoidal fracture," meaning they break in predictable, glass-like curves, allowing for the precision required to create the Levallois flakes characteristic of the Mousterian industry.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The movement of stone over long distances suggests a sophisticated mental map of the landscape. In some Middle Paleolithic sites, archaeologists have found raw materials sourced from over 100 kilometers away. This implies one of two things: either the hominins were highly migratory, carrying their "tool kits" with them, or they had established rudimentary exchange networks with other groups.

There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether this transport was a result of planned "quarrying" or simply the byproduct of seasonal migration. However, the evidence of "curated" tools—pieces that were resharpened and reused multiple times—suggests that high-quality stone was viewed as a precious commodity, worth the caloric cost of transport.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break, which is essential for creating the razor-sharp edges of Mousterian tools.
* **Curated Tools:** Tools that show evidence of repeated sharpening, indicating the owner valued the material enough to maintain it over time.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for, selecting, and extracting raw stone from the environment.
* **Material Bias:** The tendency of early humans to prefer cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for precision work.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expenditure required to move heavy raw stone, which influenced the settlement patterns of prehistoric groups.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** In the Mousterian tradition, the "core" was carefully prepared so that a single, perfectly shaped flake could be struck off with one blow.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early hominins demonstrated an intuitive understanding of geology, often seeking out specific seams of flint hidden beneath soil layers.
