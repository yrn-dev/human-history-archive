# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the logistics of the materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. To understand the evolution of Stone Age technology, we must examine the "lithic economy": how prehistoric hominins selected, transported, and curated their raw materials.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian was not just a leap in cognitive skill, but a fundamental change in how early humans interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity to their living sites, often scavenging riverbeds for basalt or quartz. The "transport distance" for these materials was minimal, suggesting a lifestyle tied closely to immediate resource availability.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a pattern of strategic sourcing emerges. *Homo erectus* and later *Homo neanderthalensis* began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flaking required for the complex Mousterian scrapers and points.

## The Logistics of Curation

The Mousterian industry (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago) reveals a sophisticated understanding of "curation." Rather than discarding a tool once it became dull, Neanderthals frequently reshaped and recycled their tools. This behavior indicates that the raw material was viewed as a valuable asset, often transported over dozens of kilometers from the original quarry to the habitation site.

There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding the extent of this transport. Some archaeologists argue that the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the immediate area) proves the existence of early trade networks between hominin groups. Others suggest it simply reflects larger home ranges and a more nomadic migratory pattern. Regardless, the move from "use-and-discard" to "transport-and-maintain" marks a critical cognitive shift in planning and foresight.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, which is essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for and extracting high-quality stone from the earth.
* **Transport Distance:** The measurement of how far a tool's raw material traveled from its source to its final discard site.
* **Curation:** The practice of sharpening or reshaping a tool to extend its lifespan.
* **Material Preference:** The transition from using any available river cobble to specifically seeking out flint or obsidian.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** In the Mousterian period, the "core" (the main piece of stone) was carefully prepared so that a single, perfect flake could be struck off with one blow.
* **Geological Mapping:** By analyzing the chemical signature of stone tools, modern geologists can pinpoint the exact quarry used by humans hundreds of thousands of years ago.
