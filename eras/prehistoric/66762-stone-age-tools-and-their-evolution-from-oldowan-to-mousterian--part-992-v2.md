# The Raw Material Revolution: Lithic Procurement and Logistics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of Stone Age tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the "where" and "how" of the stone itself. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in geological knowledge and logistical planning.

To create a tool, a hominin first had to identify a suitable raw material. Not all stones are created equal; the ability to produce a sharp, predictable edge depends on the mineral's conchoidal fracture pattern. The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian industries reflects a growing sophistication in how prehistoric humans interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool procurement was largely opportunistic. Early *Homo* typically gathered cobbles from nearby stream beds or surface deposits. The "transport distance" for these materials was generally minimal, suggesting that tools were made on the spot and discarded quickly.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a shift toward strategic sourcing. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, or obsidian, often transporting these materials over several kilometers. This indicates a mental map of the environment and a capacity for "future-oriented" planning—gathering materials today for a need that may arise tomorrow.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly specialized. The Levallois technique required a specific type of core that could be carefully prepared to strike off a single, predetermined flake. 

This necessity drove a "curation" economy. Rather than treating stone as a disposable resource, Mousterian tool-makers carefully maintained their cores, reshaping them multiple times to maximize the utility of a single piece of high-quality stone. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding the extent of trade networks during this era; some archaeologists argue that the presence of non-local stone suggests early forms of social exchange, while others believe it simply reflects larger seasonal migration patterns.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way materials like obsidian or flint break, allowing for the creation of razor-sharp edges.
* **Transport Distance:** The measurement of how far a raw material traveled from its geological source to the site where it was used.
* **Curation:** The practice of maintaining and reshaping a tool over time rather than discarding it.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of identifying, extracting, and transporting stone for tool production.
* **Material Selectivity:** The cognitive ability to reject low-quality stone in favor of superior materials found further away.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In Mousterian tools, the "core" was often more valuable than the final flake because it served as the "factory" for future tools.
* **Geological Literacy:** Prehistoric humans had to distinguish between visually similar rocks to find those with the correct mineral structure for knapping.
