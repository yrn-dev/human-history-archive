# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a matter of shaping stone, but of understanding geology, planning for the future, and managing the transport of raw materials across prehistoric landscapes.

For the early hominin, a stone tool was only as effective as the material used to create it. The transition from opportunistic foraging to strategic sourcing marks a pivotal shift in prehistoric cognitive evolution.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. The "raw material radius" was small; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), a shift occurs. We begin to see evidence of "selective sourcing." Hominins started bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials—such as fine-grained flint or obsidian—that allowed for more precise flaking and sharper edges. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and the ability to delay gratification for a superior tool.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals, approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique required specific types of stone that could be predictably flaked. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported high-quality stone over several kilometers, sometimes crossing significant geographical barriers. This implies a structured "raw material economy" where certain sites were designated as quarries and others as workshops or habitation zones. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individuals traveled vast distances to secure their own supplies.

## Cognitive Implications
The movement from "use what is underfoot" to "travel ten kilometers for the best flint" represents a massive leap in executive function. It demonstrates foresight, spatial memory, and an understanding of the physical properties of different minerals. The evolution of the tool was, therefore, as much about the *journey* to the stone as it was about the *strike* of the hammerstone.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan Radius:** Early tools were typically made from materials found within a very short distance of the site.
* **Material Preference:** Hominins preferred cryptocrystalline silicates (like chert and flint) because they break with a predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **The Levallois Leap:** The Mousterian Levallois technique required a "prepared core," making the quality of the initial stone critical.
* **Transport Costs:** The effort spent transporting stone indicates that the value of a high-quality tool outweighed the caloric cost of the journey.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late prehistoric sites, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers away from its volcanic source, suggesting early networks of exchange.
* **Curated Tools:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately, Mousterian tools show signs of extensive "re-sharpening," indicating they were prized possessions.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to distinguish between stones that looked similar but behaved differently under pressure, showing an intuitive grasp of mineralogy.
