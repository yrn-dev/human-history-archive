# Brixham Cavern and Prehistoric Cave Usage

## Geological Formation of Brixham Cavern
Brixham cavern was formed simultaneously with the excavation of its surrounding valley. Geologists have determined that small streams flowing down the upper ramifications of the valley entered the cavern through its western opening. These streams then traversed fissures in the limestone before escaping through lower openings located in the chief valley. Evidence of this process is found in the rounded pebbles located within the shingle bed, which were carried into the cave by these streams.

## Animal and Human Presence
The frequency of habitation in Brixham cavern changed as the landscape evolved. Initially, the cave was frequented by animals only during times of drought; this theory is supported by the small quantity of animal remains found in the shingle. During this early period, implements made by humans were relatively more common, with seventeen chipped flints discovered. These implements are of a roughly-chipped type that resembles those of the Mousterian period.

As the excavation of the valley continued, the course of the stream was diverted and its level lowered. This caused the cave to become drier, leading to more frequent habitation by predatory animals. At this stage, the cavern functioned essentially as an animal den, though rare flint implements indicate that humans visited occasionally. Eventually, the cave became a resort for bears. Evidence of this includes the discovery of 334 specimens of bears in all stages of growth, including sucking cubs.

## Palaeontological Findings
The fauna discovered at Brixham cavern closely resembles that of Kent's Hole. Excavations unearthed 1,621 bones, nearly all of which were gnawed and broken. These remains include:
*   Elephant
*   Rhinoceros
*   Lion
*   Bear
*   Hyena
*   Horse
*   Various small rodents and birds (691 of the total bones belonged to these groups and are of more recent times).

## General Patterns of Early Human Cave Use
Broader historical records indicate that early humans used caves for squatting and hiding. Because they lacked means of lighting the recesses, they typically did not venture deep into caverns, instead staying far enough inside to be protected from the weather. They utilized odd corners for storing food and wood and may have barricaded the mouths of the caves. To enter deeper into the caverns, torches would have been their only available light source.

Early humans shared these spaces with other creatures, such as the cave hyena, cave bear, and cave lion. To secure these locations, humans had to drive these animals out and keep them away, likely using fire as an effective method of protection and eviction.

## The Geological Record and Caves
Much of the current knowledge regarding the earliest humans is derived from a few caves where they left traces. This is because the Geological Record primarily contains traces of creatures that were easily drowned or lived in marshes. Until the hard Pleistocene times, humans lived and died in the open, where their bodies decayed or were consumed. Consequently, caves provide some of the most critical remaining vestiges of human progenitors, though it is suggested that thousands of other deposits, particularly in the East Indies or India, may remain untouched.
