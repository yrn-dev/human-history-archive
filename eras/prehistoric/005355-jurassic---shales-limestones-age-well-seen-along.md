# Jurassic

The Jurassic period is represented in the geological record by various sedimentary formations across different global regions, most notably characterized by the presence of limestones and shales. These deposits often contain marine fossils, such as ammonites and belemnites, which serve as key indicators of the period's environmental conditions and geological age.

## Formations in British East Africa
In British East Africa, the geological structure is divided into four distinct regions. The foot plateau is composed of sedimentary rocks that date from the Trias to the Jurassic. Specifically, the Jurassic formations in this region consist of shales and limestones, which are well observed along the railway near Changamwe. 

These strata are noted for containing plentiful belemnites and gigantic ammonites. According to Dr. Waagen, these ammonites exhibit a striking analogy to forms found in the Acanthicus zone of East India.

## Deposits in India
Jurassic beds are also present in India, specifically underlaying the horizontal lava flows of the Deccan Trap series in Cutch and Kathiawar. These Jurassic beds are marine in nature and contain numerous ammonites. They are situated beneath Neocomian beds, which consist of shales and sandstones containing plant remains, some of which are identical to forms found in the upper portion of the Gondwana system.

## The Cornbrash of England
In England, a specific member of the Jurassic formation is known as the Cornbrash. This represents the uppermost member of the Bathonian stage. The term "Cornbrash" originated as an English agricultural name used in Wiltshire to describe a variety of loose rubble, or "brash," which provides good soil for the cultivation of corn.

Geologically, the Cornbrash is a thin band of shelly limestone, typically ranging from 10 to 25 feet in thickness. Despite its thinness, it is remarkably persistent and can be traced from Weymouth to the Yorkshire coast. In certain areas, such as north Lincolnshire, it is very thin and likely dies out near the Humber, though it reappears as thin beds in locations including Langdale, Newton Dale, Wheatcroft, Cayton Bay, and Gristhorpe Bay. In the inland exposures of Yorkshire, the Cornbrash is difficult to track because it often transitions into dark shales, referred to as the "clays of the Cornbrash," which contain *Avicula echinata*.
