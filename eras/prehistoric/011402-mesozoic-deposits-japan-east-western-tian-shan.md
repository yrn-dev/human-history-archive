# Mesozoic Deposits of Japan and the Western Tian-shan

The geological history of Asia during the Mesozoic era is characterized by a distinction between vast ancient land masses and intervening regions that were submerged beneath the sea. The presence of marine Mesozoic deposits in specific regions, including Japan to the east and the western Tian-shan to the west, serves as a primary indicator for determining the limits of these land masses.

## Continental Limits and Marine Deposits
Evidence suggests that a broad area stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the south of China remained land from the close of the Palaeozoic era onward. In this region, freshwater and terrestrial deposits of Mesozoic age are found in many locations, while marine deposits later than the Carboniferous are absent, except in the extreme north. 

The limits of this land mass are defined by the presence of marine Mesozoic deposits in three key directions:
* **East:** Japan
* **West:** The western Tian-shan
* **South:** Tibet

These marine deposits indicate that while the interior of the northern land mass (referred to by E. Suess as Angaraland) remained dry, these peripheral areas were subject to marine influence. Similarly, the Verkhoyansk Range contains Triassic deposits showing that the land mass did not extend to the Bering Sea.

## The Central Marine Zone
Between the northern land mass (Angaraland) and the southern land mass (Gondwanaland)—of which the Indian peninsula is a fragment—lies an area where marine deposits of Mesozoic age are well developed. This region was evidently beneath the sea for the majority of the Mesozoic era. This intervening sea has been referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr.

In western Asia, including the drainage area of the Aral Sea, Persia, Arabia, Afghanistan, and Baluchistan, the land was covered by the sea during the later stages of the Cretaceous period, although a significant portion of this region was likely dry land during Jurassic times.

## Geological Structure of the Tian-shan Region
The Tian-shan, or Celestial Mountains, form a critical part of the geological and hydrographic landscape of central Asia. Located between 40 and 43 degrees North and 75 and 95 degrees East, these mountains serve as the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. Some summits in this range exceed 20,000 feet in altitude.

Structurally, the Tian-shan is part of a broad belt of crumpling where Mesozoic deposits and lower Tertiary system beds have been thrown into folds. These folds extend in a series of arcs from west to east, forming the principal mountain ranges of central Asia. This belt of folding also includes:
* Asia Minor, Persia, and Afghanistan
* Baluchistan and the Himalayas
* The Burmese ranges

## Regional Comparisons
The Mesozoic characteristics of the Tian-shan and Japan contrast with other Asian regions:
* **The Gobi and Mongolia:** North of the folded belt, including much of Mongolia north of the Tian-shan, there are no marine beds of Mesozoic or Tertiary age. This area consists chiefly of Archean rocks and plant-bearing deposits of the Angara series.
* **China:** In the provinces of Shansi and Shensi, no marine deposits are known from the Upper Carboniferous onward. Similarly, southern China lacks marine deposits of Mesozoic or Tertiary age, except near the borders of Burma and Tibet.
* **India:** The peninsula of India has been land since the Carboniferous period, evidenced by the Gondwana series of terrestrial and freshwater deposits and a general absence of marine fossils except near its borders.
