# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a shift in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

For the early toolmaker, the world was not a uniform expanse, but a map of geological "hotspots." The transition from opportunistic gathering to strategic sourcing reveals a deepening capacity for foresight and spatial memory.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. The "raw material economy" was local; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used. There was little evidence of selective transport, suggesting a "find-and-use" mental model.

As we move into the Acheulean era, the behavior shifts. *Homo erectus* began to demonstrate a preference for specific materials that yielded more predictable edges. We begin to see evidence of "curated" tools—handaxes that were carried over long distances and reshaped multiple times. This indicates that the value of the tool was no longer just in its immediate utility, but in its potential for future use.

## The Mousterian Logistics and Trade
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique required high-quality flint or chert to produce the precise, predetermined flakes characteristic of the era. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often bypassed mediocre local stone in favor of superior materials located several kilometers away. This suggests a sophisticated mental map of the territory. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individual bands traveled vast distances to quarry sites, but the result is the same: a transition from a local economy to a regional one.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, relying on river cobbles within immediate reach.
* **Acheulean Curation:** The practice of transporting and maintaining a tool over time, rather than discarding it.
* **Material Preference:** A shift from general "hard stones" to specific cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for sharper edges.
* **Spatial Memory:** The ability to remember the location of high-quality quarries across seasonal migrations.
* **The Levallois Requirement:** This advanced technique demanded high-grade stone, forcing hominins to expand their sourcing radius.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Exotic" Stone:** Some Mousterian sites contain raw materials sourced from over 10 kilometers away, a significant distance for a prehistoric foraging group.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians determine where tools were made by looking for "debitage" (stone chips). If the chips are present but the raw material isn't local, we know the stone was transported.
* **Recycling:** In resource-poor areas, Neanderthals have been found to "recycle" old tools, reshaping worn-down scrapers into new implements.
