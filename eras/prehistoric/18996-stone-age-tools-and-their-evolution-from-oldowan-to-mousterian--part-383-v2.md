# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and flaking patterns that distinguish the Oldowan from the Mousterian—we must examine the logistical intelligence behind the tools. A handaxe is not merely a product of skill, but a product of geography. The evolution of stone tools is inextricably linked to the cognitive ability of early hominins to identify, select, and transport high-quality raw materials over vast distances.

For the prehistoric toolmaker, not all stone was created equal. The transition from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of specific minerals reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and planning.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever basalt or quartz was available in the immediate vicinity. The "cost" of transporting heavy stones was too high to justify moving them far from the source. Consequently, Oldowan sites are usually clustered tightly around natural outcrops or stream beds.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean era, we see the emergence of "selective procurement." *Homo erectus* began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of materials that offered predictable fracturing patterns, such as fine-grained flint or obsidian. This suggests a mental "map" of the landscape, where the toolmaker knew that a superior material existed several kilometers away and planned their journey accordingly.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became even more sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality stone to be successful. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported raw materials over 10 to 50 kilometers. This indicates a transition from a "immediate-return" economy to a "delayed-return" economy. They were no longer just making a tool for a current task; they were stockpiling resources for future needs. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different bands of hominins or if individual groups maintained strict territorial control over specific quarries.

## Key Facts
* **Material Preference:** Fine-grained cryptocrystalline silicates (like chert and flint) were preferred for their predictable "conchoidal" fracture.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools were typically sourced within 1–2 km; Mousterian materials are often found dozens of kilometers from their source.
* **The Levallois Requirement:** The complexity of Mousterian tools necessitated higher-grade stone, as low-quality rock would shatter unpredictably during core preparation.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The shift in sourcing proves that early humans developed advanced spatial memory and long-term planning capabilities.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian (volcanic glass) creates an edge sharper than a modern steel scalpel, it is one of the most frequently transported materials in the prehistoric record.
* **Curated Tools:** In later stages of the Paleolithic, tools were "curated," meaning they were resharpened and reused multiple times rather than discarded, to conserve precious raw materials.
* **Natural Selection:** The choice of stone wasn't just about sharpness; it was about "knapping" efficiency—the amount of usable cutting edge produced per kilogram of raw stone.
