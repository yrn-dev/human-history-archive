# Zhob

The Zhob is a river system in Baluchistan characterized by a diverse geological landscape, ranging from fertile upper valleys to rugged, desolate lower reaches. The region's geography is defined by a complex system of mountain ridges and folds that run approximately parallel to one another.

## Regional Geology and Formations
The mountain ranges of Baluchistan are primarily composed of Tertiary and Cretaceous beds. These beds are arranged in a series of folds that form an extensive system, resembling a festoon hanging southwards between Mount Ararat and Peshawar. This system includes a subsidiary festoon comprising the Bugti Hills and the Suliman Hills, where the outer folds are looped up at Sibi. 

While the deposits are mostly marine—with the exception of later Tertiary beds—there is a significant development of conglomerates and volcanic tuffs in the lower Tertiary and upper Cretaceous, particularly in north-western Baluchistan. These are likely contemporaneous with the Deccan Traps of India. Additionally, Jurassic rocks contribute to the formation of the hills in British Baluchistan. Along the south side of the upper Zhob, Triassic beds are located, and *Fusulina* limestone has been identified in this area.

## Topography of the Zhob Valley
The landscape of the Zhob varies significantly between its upper and lower sections:

*   **The Upper Zhob:** The upper parts of the valley are comparatively open and fertile. This area is characterized by flourishing villages and cultivation that has seen great development under British rule. The valley is bounded by long, gentle, sweeping spurs covered in wild olive woods featuring trees of immense size.
*   **The Lower Zhob:** In contrast, the lower reaches of the Zhob and the Kundar are hemmed in by rugged limestone walls. This region is described as a wilderness of stony desolation, featuring deep gorges and serrated, banded walls.

## Surrounding Peaks and Ridges
The geological structure of the region is visible from the summit of the Kaisargarh peak, which offers a view of the width of northern Baluchistan. To the west, toward Afghanistan, the landscape consists of line upon line of broken, jagged ridges. These are folds in the Cretaceous series that are overlaid by shales and coarse sandstones. 

Immediately west of the Kaisargarh is the Shingarh Mountain, which is a geological repetition of the Kaisargarh ridge. The Shingarh Mountain is crowned with crags of coral limestone and appears black toward the summit due to the presence of pines. Beyond this peak lie the grey, close-packed ridges that enclose the Kundar and the lower reaches of the Zhob.

## Tectonic and Volcanic Activity
The region has been shaped by significant upheaval and volcanic activity. The minor ridges and "wrinkles" seen east of the Kaisargarh were formed during the upheaval of the Suliman Mountains at the end of a great volcanic epoch. 

The area's volcanic history includes:
*   **Intrusions:** Great masses of diorite and syenite were intruded during the Tertiary period.
*   **Volcanic Cones:** A line of recent volcanic cones, mostly composed of andesitic ashes and lavas, stretches from eastern Persia into western Baluchistan. While these appear extinct in Baluchistan, the Koh-i-Tafdan beyond the Persian frontier continues to emit vapours.
*   **Mud Volcanoes:** These occur on the Makran coast, though it is uncertain if they are connected to true volcanic agencies.

## Bordering Regions
To the east, facing the Indus plains, the landscape is marked by the Siwaliks, a higher and more rugged ridge of hills. The Baluch Siwaliks provide evidence of a recent geological past, having been inhabited by the same gigantic mammals that once lived in the forests below the Himalaya. To the west, the narrow valleys of the grid-iron formation widen toward their sources, where denudation has filled hollows with fruitful soil, eventually meeting the central water-divide on the Quetta plateau.
