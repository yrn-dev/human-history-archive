# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude choppers of the Oldowan to the refined flakes of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the logistics of the materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. To understand the evolution of the Stone Age, we must examine the "raw material economy": how prehistoric hominins selected, transported, and valued specific minerals.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a leap in cognitive skill, but a shift in how hominins interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. They typically utilized river cobbles or surface stones found within a few hundred meters of their habitation site. The focus was on immediate utility; if a stone could be fractured to produce a sharp edge, it was used.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, obsidian, or quartzite. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the predictable, razor-sharp edges necessary for the Levallois technique—the sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade
The distance between the stone source (the quarry) and the site of use is a key indicator of cognitive evolution. In Mousterian sites associated with Neanderthals, archaeologists have found materials transported over dozens of kilometers. This suggests several critical behavioral shifts:

1. **Planning Depth:** The ability to envision a future need and secure materials in advance.
2. **Territorial Mapping:** A sophisticated mental map of the environment.
3. **Social Exchange:** While debated, some scholars suggest that the presence of non-local stone indicates early forms of trade or social gifting between disparate groups.

There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether this transport was a result of nomadic migration (carrying stones as they moved) or targeted "expeditions" to specific quarries. Regardless, the movement of stone proves that toolmaking was no longer a reaction to the immediate environment, but a planned economic activity.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The glass-like breaking pattern essential for creating sharp edges in flint and obsidian.
* **Sourcing:** The process of identifying the geological origin of a stone tool to determine how far it traveled.
* **Curated Tools:** High-quality tools that were maintained and carried over long distances, rather than discarded after one use.
* **Lithic Analysis:** The scientific study of stone tools to reconstruct prehistoric behavior.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** By the Mousterian era, hominins often carried "prepared cores" (blocks of stone) rather than finished tools, allowing them to produce fresh, sharp flakes on demand.
* **Waste as Evidence:** Archaeologists often learn more from "debitage" (the waste flakes left over from toolmaking) than from the tools themselves, as it reveals the exact sequence of the maker's thoughts.
