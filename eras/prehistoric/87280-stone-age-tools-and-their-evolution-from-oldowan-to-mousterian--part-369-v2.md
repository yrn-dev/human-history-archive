# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and flaking techniques that define the Oldowan, Acheulean, and Mousterian traditions—we must shift our gaze from the finished tool to the raw material itself. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological procurement and spatial planning.

The ability to identify, acquire, and transport high-quality stone reveals a sophisticated understanding of the landscape and a burgeoning capacity for foresight that transcends simple opportunistic scavenging.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest **Oldowan** assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers were largely opportunistic. Most cores and flakes were fashioned from cobbles found in immediate proximity to the site, often sourced from nearby stream beds. The "economic radius" was small; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used.

However, as we transition into the **Acheulean** period, a shift occurs. *Homo erectus* began to exhibit "selective procurement." Archaeological evidence shows that hominins bypassed mediocre local stones in favor of higher-quality materials—such as fine-grained volcanic rocks or flint—located kilometers away. This suggests a mental map of the environment and the ability to plan for future needs, transporting "blank" cores to be worked later at a different location.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of Quality

By the time of the **Mousterian** industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became even more complex. The introduction of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—demanded materials with predictable fracturing patterns.

This necessity led to the establishment of "quarrying" behaviors. Rather than relying on surface finds, Mousterian toolmakers often sought out specific geological seams. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding the extent of "trade" or social exchange of these materials between different groups, but the presence of non-local stone in many cave sites suggests that high-quality lithics were highly valued assets, moved across the landscape through either migration or social networking.

## Key Facts: Lithic Procurement
* **Raw Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like chert and flint) were preferred for their predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools were typically sourced within 1–5 km; Acheulean and Mousterian materials are often found transported over 10–50 km.
* **Curated vs. Expedient:** "Expedient" tools are made and discarded quickly; "curated" tools are carefully maintained and carried over long distances.
* **Geological Knowledge:** The shift to the Mousterian indicates a sophisticated understanding of mineralogy and the physical properties of different stone types.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Levallois technique, the "waste" was not the flake, but the core; the entire stone was meticulously shaped just to produce one perfect, razor-sharp edge.
* **Recycling:** Evidence suggests that some prehistoric groups "recycled" old tools, re-sharpening exhausted edges to extend the life of a transported stone.
* **Material Bias:** Our understanding of the Stone Age is skewed because we only find tools made of durable stone; tools made of wood, bone, or hide rarely survive the millennia.
