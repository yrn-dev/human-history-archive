# Mineral Resources and Geological Surveys

The study and extraction of mineral resources are documented through various geological surveys and specialized texts, focusing on the distribution of metallic and non-metallic minerals, the economic value of mining, and the geological composition of specific regions.

## Mineral Resources of Kentucky
The mineral resources of Kentucky are described as important and valuable, although they have seen very little development. In 1900, the total value of all manufactures in the state was $154,166,365, with $25,204,788 of that value based on products from quarries or mines. By 1907, the total value of mineral products reached $19,294,341.

Bituminous coal serves as the principal mineral of the state. In 1907, Kentucky was ranked eighth among the coal-producing states in the Union. The output of coal grew significantly over several decades:
*   1889: 2,399,755 short tons
*   1902: 6,766,984 short tons
*   1907: 10,753,124 short tons

Production is divided between two primary coalfields. The eastern coalfield covers approximately 11,180 square miles and includes counties such as Greenup, Boyd, Carter, Lawrence, Johnson, Lee, Breathitt, Rockcastle, Pulaski, Laurel, Knox, Bell, and Whitley. The western coalfield covers 5,800 square miles and includes Henderson, Union, Webster, Daviess, Hancock, McLean, Ohio, Hopkins, Butler, Muhlenberg, and Christian counties. In 1907, the western district produced 6,295,397 tons, while the eastern district produced 4,457,727 tons. The highest producing counties in 1907 were Hopkins (2,064,154 short tons) and Muhlenberg (1,882,913 short tons) in the west, and Bell (1,437,886 short tons) and Whitley (762,923 short tons) in the south-western part of the eastern field.

Kentucky is the largest producer of cannel coal in the Union. Production figures for cannel coal were 65,317 short tons in 1902, 72,856 tons in 1903 (with 46,314 tons from Morgan county), and 68,400 tons in 1904 (with 52,492 tons from Morgan county). According to the United States Geological Survey's *Mineral Resources of the United States* for 1907, the production of cannel coal, including 4,650 tons of semi-cannel coal, was 77,733 tons.

## Metallic and Non-Metallic Minerals
Mineral compositions vary widely by region and element. The Carpathian system in Europe is noted as being richer in metallic ores than any other mountain system on the continent, containing large quantities of iron, copper, lead, zinc, gold, silver, coal, salt, and petroleum.

Certain chemical elements, such as calcium (atomic weight 40.0), do not occur in a free state in nature but are widely diffused in combination. Calcium is found in:
*   **Sulphates:** Selenite, gypsum, alabaster, and anhydrite.
*   **Carbonates:** Calcite, aragonite, marble, and chalk, as well as double carbonates like barytocalcite, bromlite, and dolomite.
*   **Other forms:** Apatite (fluophosphate) and fluorspar (fluoride).

In other regions, such as the Isle of Wight, pure sands are extracted for use in glass making.

## Geological Survey Documentation
Geological surveys provide critical data on the physical and economic resources of various territories. In the United States, the U.S. Geological Survey has published reports on the gold and silver veins of mining districts in Idaho, such as De Lamar and Silver City, as well as the water resources of the Snake River Plains.

In Great Britain, the Geological Survey of Great Britain has produced extensive memoirs. Alexander Henry Green, a geologist who joined the survey in 1861, surveyed the midland counties, including Yorkshire and Derbyshire. His work included memoirs on the Geology of Banbury (1864), Stockport (1866), North Derbyshire (1869), and the Yorkshire Coal-field (1878). Other survey documentation includes studies on the Jurassic rocks of Great Britain and the geology of the country around Southampton and the Isle of Wight.
