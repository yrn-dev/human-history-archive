# Brixham Cave and Kent's Cavern

Brixham Cave, also known as Windmill Hill Cavern, is a well-known ossiferous cave located near Brixham on the brow of a hill consisting of Devonian limestone. Discovered by chance in 1858, the cave had previously been hermetically sealed by a mass of limestone breccia. Following its discovery, it was excavated by Dr. Hugh Falconer with the assistance of a committee of geologists.

## Geological Structure and Formation
The cave consists of a succession of beds arranged in the following descending order:
1. A shingle layer of limestone, slate, and other local rocks, containing fragments of stalagmite, a few bones, and worked flints, varying in thickness from five to sixteen feet.
2. Red cave earth containing worked flints, bones, and angular limestone fragments, measuring 3 to 4 feet thick.
3. An old stalagmitic floor remaining *in situ*, approximately nine inches thick.
4. Black peaty soil with a maximum thickness of about one foot.
5. Angular debris fallen from above, varying from one to ten feet in thickness.
6. Stalagmite containing a few bones and reindeer antlers, varying from one to fifteen inches thick.

Geologists have noted patches of an old stalagmitic floor three to four feet above the current floor. Fragments of quartz and limestone attached to the underside of these ledges indicate that the shingle bed once extended to that height. It is theorized that a shock or jar, such as an earthquake, broke the stalagmite, causing the sand and pebbles of the shingle to sink deeper into the limestone fissures. This process increased the size of the cave, which was then partially filled by cave earth. Eventually, the cave was closed by the fall of angular fragments at the entrance, leaving it accessible only to a few burrowing animals.

## Fauna and Animal Remains
The fauna found in Brixham cavern closely resembles that of Kent's Hole. Excavations unearthed the bones of the elephant, rhinoceros, lion, bear, hyena, horse, and various small rodents and birds. A total of 1,621 bones were found, nearly all of which were gnawed and broken; of these, 691 belonged to small rodents and birds from more recent times. Additionally, 334 specimens of bears in all stages of growth, including sucking cubs, were discovered.

Geological evidence suggests the cave's formation occurred simultaneously with the excavation of the valley. Small streams flowing from the upper ramifications of the valley entered the cave's western opening, traversed limestone fissures, and escaped through lower openings in the chief valley, carrying in the rounded pebbles found in the shingle bed. Animals likely frequented the cave only during times of drought, which explains the limited quantity of animal remains found in the shingle. As the valley excavation proceeded and the stream's course was diverted and lowered, the cave became drier and more frequently used as a den by predatory animals.

## Human Presence and Implements
Evidence of human activity is present in the form of seventeen chipped flints. These implements are of a roughly-chipped type that resembles those of the Mousterian period. While human visits were occasional during the period when the cave served as an animal den, the flint implements are relatively more common than the animal remains found in the shingle.

## Comparison with Kent's Cavern
Kent's Cavern, or Kent's Hole, is the largest of the English bone caves and is located about a mile east of Torquay harbour. Like Brixham, it runs into a hill of Devonian limestone. Scientifically examined in 1825 by Rev. J. McEnery, the cave provided evidence of humans existing in Devon contemporaneously with animals that are now extinct or no longer indigenous. These findings were later verified by Godwin Austen and a Committee of the British Association under Wm. Pengelly between 1865 and 1880.

Kent's Cavern features four distinct strata:
1. A surface of dark earth containing Roman pottery, medieval remains, and evidence of use during the Neolithic, Bronze, and Iron Ages.
2. A stalagmite floor between 1 and 3 feet thick.
3. Red earth containing flint implements, an engraved antler, and bones of the lion, hyaena, mammoth, and rhinoceros. Above this and below the stalagmite is a black band (2 to 6 inches thick) containing charcoal, flint implements, and animal teeth and bones, some of which were perforated for use as ornament.
4. A hard breccia filling the bottom of the cave, up to 12 feet thick, containing flint implements (generally ruder than those in higher strata) and bear remains.

The most notable animal remains in Kent's Cavern are those of the Sabre-toothed tiger, *Machairodus latidens*. The 1858 exploration of the nearby Brixham cave served to prove the validity of McEnery's earlier discoveries regarding the presence of man.
