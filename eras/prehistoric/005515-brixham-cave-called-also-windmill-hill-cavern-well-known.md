# Brixham Cave

Brixham Cave, also referred to as Windmill Hill Cavern, is a well-known ossiferous cave located near Brixham. The cave is situated on the brow of a hill composed of Devonian limestone.

## Discovery and Excavation
The cavern was discovered by chance in 1858. Prior to its discovery, the cave had been hermetically sealed by a mass of limestone breccia. Following its discovery, it was excavated by Dr. Hugh Falconer, who was assisted by a committee of geologists.

## Stratigraphy
The cave contains a succession of beds, which, in descending order, are as follows:
1. A shingle layer consisting of fragments of stalagmite and pebbles of slate, limestone, and other local rocks. This layer varies in thickness from five to sixteen feet and contains worked flints and a few bones.
2. Red cave earth, three to four feet thick, containing worked flints, bones, and angular fragments of limestone.
3. An old stalagmitic floor remaining *in situ*, measuring approximately nine inches in thickness.
4. Black peaty soil with a maximum thickness of about one foot.
5. Angular debris that fell from above, varying in thickness between one and ten feet.
6. Stalagmite, one to fifteen inches thick, containing antlers of reindeer and a few bones.

Additionally, ledges or patches of an old stalagmitic floor are found three to four feet above the current floor. Fragments of quartz and limestone attached to the underside of these ledges indicate that the shingle bed once extended to that height, meaning the shingle originally stood several feet higher than its current position. It is supposed that the stalagmite was broken up by a jar or shock, such as an earthquake, causing the sand and pebbles of the shingle to sink deeper into limestone fissures. This increase in the cave's size was partially filled by cave earth. Eventually, the cave was closed by the fall of angular fragments at the entrance, making it inaccessible except to a few burrowing animals, whose remains are located above the newer, second stalagmite floor.

## Fauna and Human Presence
The fauna discovered in Brixham Cave closely resembles that of Kent's Hole. Unearthed remains include those of the lion, horse, hyena, elephant, rhinoceros, bear, as well as various small rodents and birds. A total of 1,621 bones were found, nearly all of which were gnawed and broken; 691 of these belonged to small rodents and birds of more recent times. Specifically, the remains of 334 bear specimens were discovered in all stages of growth, including sucking cubs.

Evidence of human presence is indicated by seventeen chipped flints. These implements are of a roughly-chipped type that resembles those of the Mousterian period.

## Geological and Environmental Theory
Geologists suggest that the formation of the cave occurred simultaneously with the excavation of the valley. It is theorized that small streams from the upper ramifications of the valley entered the cave's western opening, traversed limestone fissures, and escaped through lower openings into the chief valley. These streams are believed to have carried in the rounded pebbles found in the shingle bed.

The quantity of animal remains in the shingle is small, leading to the theory that animals only frequented the cave during times of drought. As the excavation of the valley continued, the course of the stream was diverted and the water level lowered, resulting in a drier cave. Consequently, the cavern became more frequently inhabited by predatory animals, serving essentially as an animal den. During this period, humans visited only occasionally, as evidenced by the rare occurrence of flint implements.
