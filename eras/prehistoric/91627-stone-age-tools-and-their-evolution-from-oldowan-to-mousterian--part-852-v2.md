# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. To understand the evolution of prehistoric technology, we must examine the "raw material economy": how early hominins identified, sourced, and transported stone across the landscape.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a shift in how hominins interacted with their environment. The ability to recognize high-quality lithic sources and transport them over long distances suggests a developing capacity for foresight and spatial mapping.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever cobbles were available in their immediate vicinity, often scavenging riverbeds for basalt or quartz. The "cost" of transporting heavy stones was high, and the distance between the source and the tool's use-site was generally minimal.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), we see a marked increase in selectivity. Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flaking required for the Levallois technique.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The Mousterian era reveals a sophisticated understanding of resource management. Archaeological evidence shows that Neanderthals often transported high-quality raw materials over dozens of kilometers. This implies one of two things: either a highly mobile lifestyle with planned seasonal migrations, or the existence of early social networks involving the exchange of materials between groups.

There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding the "curation" of tools. Some archaeologists argue that the transport of raw cores suggests a "curated" technology—where tools were maintained and sharpened over time—while others suggest it reflects a simple need for better materials in resource-poor areas. Regardless, the movement of stone indicates a mental shift from immediate survival to long-term planning.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, which is essential for creating sharp, predictable edges.
* **Lithic Sourcing:** The scientific process of tracing a stone tool back to its original geological quarry.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expenditure required to move heavy raw stone, which influenced the size and shape of early tool cores.
* **Material Selectivity:** The transition from using any available stone (Oldowan) to seeking specific, high-quality minerals (Mousterian).
* **Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Travel:** In some prehistoric sites, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers away from the nearest volcanic source, proving early hominins had vast knowledge of their geography.
* **The "Core" Concept:** By the Mousterian period, hominins didn't just make a tool; they prepared a "core" (a parent stone) that could produce multiple standardized tools.
* **Quartz Challenges:** While flint is ideal, many early hominins used quartz, which is much harder to control and often shattered unpredictably during knapping.
