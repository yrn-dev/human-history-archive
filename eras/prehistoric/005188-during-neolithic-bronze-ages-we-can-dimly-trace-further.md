# The Neolithic and Bronze Ages

The Neolithic and Bronze Ages represent critical stages in the development of human civilization, marking the transition from nomadic existence to settled cultivation and the eventual mastery of metallurgy. In Europe, these periods are part of a broader "Age of Cultivation" involving white men, which began approximately 10,000 to 12,000 years ago.

## Characteristics of the Neolithic Period
The Neolithic age is characterized by the beginning of cultivation. All Old World peoples who reached this stage grew and consumed wheat, whereas American Indians developed agriculture independently using maize (Indian corn). 

Archaeological evidence places the start of the Neolithic age in various regions at different times. In Crete, Sir Arthur Evans suggests it began more than 14,000 years ago, while deposits at Susa show remains that may be more than 20,000 years old. In Egypt, primitive hut-builders of a low Neolithic stage were succeeded around 5000 B.C. (or earlier) by a civilized Neolithic people who utilized wood and brick buildings and worked stone.

The psychological and social state of Neolithic man is described as being dominated by fear of tribal gods and a belief in sacrificial propitiation. This led to practices such as infanticide and human sacrifice during seedtime or at the burial of chieftains, where wives and slaves were killed. Men, women, and children were also sacrificed during times of adversity to appease the gods.

## Transition to the Bronze Age
The appearance of bronze did not immediately revolutionize human methods; for a significant period, bronze axes and other tools were cast in moulds that mimicked the stone implements they replaced. 

The materials for bronze—copper and tin—were found in various locations. Native copper is found in Cornwall, Hungary, and Italy. Tin was a foreign import in Egypt during the XVIIIth Dynasty and has been found in Swiss pile-dwelling deposits. However, distinguishing tin from antimony was difficult for ancients; some Cyprus bronze contains antimony, which the ancients mistakenly believed was tin.

Physical health appears to have improved during this transition, with evidence suggesting that people in the Bronze Age were longer-lived and healthier than those in the Neolithic age, and that the difference in stature between males and females was reduced.

## Regional Developments

### Britain
The peopling of Britain is a subject of geological debate, but it is believed to have been invaded by a succession of races. Following the Paleolithic men, further immigrations occurred during the Neolithic and Bronze Ages. Real historical knowledge begins with Celtic invasions: the Goidels arrived in the later part of the Bronze Age, followed by the Belgae and Brythons in the Iron Age. By the time of Julius Caesar, most inhabitants (except perhaps some far northern tribes) shared Celtic speech and customs.

### Egypt and Crete
The civilized Neolithic people of Egypt quickly transitioned into the Bronze Age, developing a system of picture writing and using papyrus reed strips for writing. In Crete, the island civilization was launched upon the sea as early as 4000 B.C. and is considered at least as old as the Egyptian civilization. Vigorous trade existed between Crete and Egypt, continuing until approximately 1000 B.C.

### Japan
Archaeology in Japan identifies three distinct cultures: the Neolithic culture of the Yemishi, an intermediate Bronze culture of an unidentified race, and the Iron culture of the Yamato. The intermediate Bronze culture is evidenced by pottery that was not turned on a wheel and featured subdued decoration. This pottery is found across the main island and in Yezo, though it is not conclusively associated with a specific race.

## The Shift to the Iron Age
The smelting of iron began in Asia Minor and later in Europe, perhaps as early as 3000 years ago. Iron was produced by blowing charcoal fires and wrought through heating and hammering. 

A distinction exists between "ornamental iron"—which was rare, possibly meteoric, and used for jewelry or magic in east Europe during the XVIIIth Dynasty—and "useful iron," which appeared in Greece much later from the North. While the introduction of iron caused a gradual revolution in implements and weaponry, it did not fundamentally change the general character of daily life for peasants in remote areas.
