# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. The evolution of stone tool technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and resource management.

For the early hominins of the Oldowan tradition, toolmaking was largely opportunistic. They utilized "river cobbles"—stones already rounded and weathered by water—found in their immediate vicinity. However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a dramatic shift in how prehistoric humans interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan phase (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), the "catchment area" for raw materials was small. Hominins typically gathered stones within a few hundred meters of their home base. The focus was on availability rather than quality; as long as the stone could produce a sharp edge through percussion, it was sufficient.

By the time of the Acheulean hand-axe (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), *Homo erectus* began demonstrating a preference for specific materials. Archaeologists have found evidence of "selective sourcing," where hominins bypassed readily available low-quality stone to seek out higher-grade flint, chert, or quartzite. This suggests a mental map of the environment and a conceptual understanding of material properties—knowing that a denser, more homogenous stone would allow for the symmetrical thinning required for a hand-axe.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Transition

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) introduced the Levallois technique, a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape. This precision required high-quality silica-rich stones. 

This necessity birthed a primitive "supply chain." Evidence suggests that Mousterian toolmakers transported high-quality raw materials over distances of several kilometers, and in some cases, dozens of kilometers. This indicates a shift from immediate survival to forward planning. The transport of "blanks" (roughly shaped stones) suggests that Neanderthals were managing their resources to ensure they had the right tools for specific tasks, regardless of where they wandered.

There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were transported via organized trade between groups or through individual foraging expeditions. While "trade" may be too modern a term, the presence of non-local stone in various sites suggests a sophisticated level of spatial awareness and social coordination.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Catchment:** Early tools were typically sourced from immediate riverbeds or surface deposits.
* **Material Preference:** Acheulean toolmakers prioritized cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for their predictable fracturing patterns.
* **Levallois Precision:** The Mousterian technique required high-quality stone to prevent the core from shattering prematurely.
* **Transport Distance:** Evidence shows raw materials being carried far beyond the immediate vicinity of habitation sites.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The shift in sourcing indicates an evolved ability to plan for future needs.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded after one use, Mousterian tools were often "curated," meaning they were resharpened and carried for long periods.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Prehistoric humans understood "conchoidal fracture," the way certain stones break in curved, glass-like shells, which is essential for creating a sharp edge.
* **Natural Selection:** The move toward better materials likely increased hunting efficiency, providing more calories and fueling further brain evolution.
