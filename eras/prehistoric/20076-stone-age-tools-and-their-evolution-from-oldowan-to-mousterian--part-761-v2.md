# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the procurement of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in "raw material economy."

For the early hominin, a stone tool was only as effective as the mineral it was carved from. The transition from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality flint and obsidian reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and planning.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally operated on a "convenience" model. They utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity, often river-worn basalt or quartz. The distance between the source of the stone and the site of use was typically minimal, suggesting a limited mental map of the surrounding landscape.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see the emergence of "selective procurement." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials located kilometers away. This indicates a capacity for forward planning: the toolmaker was not reacting to a current need, but anticipating a future one.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape—required high-quality, predictable stone like flint.

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported these materials over significant distances, sometimes exceeding 10 to 20 kilometers. This suggests the existence of "resource maps" passed down through generations. There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individuals traveled to known quarries, but both theories point to a complex social structure and a high degree of environmental mastery.

## Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily relied on local river cobbles; minimal transport of raw materials.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that could hold a symmetrical edge.
* **Mousterian:** High reliance on cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for precision flaking.
* **Transport:** The distance raw materials traveled increased significantly over time, indicating expanded home ranges.
* **Waste Analysis:** The presence of "debitage" (stone chips) at specific sites helps historians distinguish between workshops and usage sites.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Appeal:** In certain regions, prehistoric humans prized obsidian (volcanic glass) for its ability to produce an edge thinner and sharper than a modern surgical scalpel.
* **Curated Tools:** By the Mousterian era, tools were often "curated," meaning they were resharpened and reused multiple times rather than discarded after one use.
* **The Quartz Challenge:** While flint is ideal, many early hominins worked with quartz, which is much harder to control because it fractures unpredictably.
