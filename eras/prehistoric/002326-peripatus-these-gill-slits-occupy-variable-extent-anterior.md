# Peripatus

In the study of biological organization, *Peripatus* is noted for the presence of tracheae. This feature is considered as interesting a characteristic of its organization as the possession of gill-slits is to the organism *Balanoglossus*.

## Comparison of Respiratory Structures

The respiratory systems of various organisms demonstrate a wide range of structural adaptations. While *Peripatus* utilizes tracheae, *Balanoglossus* possesses numerous pairs of gill-slits. These gill-slits in *Balanoglossus* lead from the digestive canal through the body-wall to the exterior. They occupy a variable extent of the anterior portion of the trunk and begin immediately behind the collar-trunk septum.

The gill-slits of *Balanoglossus* are subdivided by tongue-bars that depend from the dorsal borders of the slits. These tongue-bars are the essential organs of the gill-slit in *Balanoglossus*, exceeding the septal bars in bulk and containing a large coelomic space. This differs from *Amphioxus*, where the septal bars are the primary structures and the tongue-bars are solid and subordinate in size, vascularity, and development.

## Evolution of Gill-Slits and Air Breathing

The history of respiratory organs shows a progression from water-breathing to air-breathing adaptations. Ancestral fish of the vertebrated line relied on gills, which were inadaptable for land use. Adaptation to air breathing occurred through two primary methods: the development of covers for old-fashioned gills to prevent evaporation, or the creation of internal breathing organs, such as tubes or lungs, moistened by watery secretions.

In the case of amphibians, such as newts and frogs, life begins in water where they breathe via gills. As they transition to land, the lung—developing as a baglike outgrowth from the throat similar to the swimming bladder of many fishes—takes over the respiratory function. Consequently, the gills dwindle and the gill slits disappear, with the exception of one outgrowth of a gill slit that becomes the ear-drum and the passage of the ear. In some later vertebrated animals, gills were eliminated entirely, and gill slits appear only during the embryonic phase.

## Functional Anatomy of Gill Clefts

A typical functional gill cleft is a vertical slit separated from its neighbors by a gill septum. Respiratory exchange occurs between the blood and water through a network of capillary blood-vessels in the lining of the cleft. To increase the respiratory surface area, outgrowths called gill lamellae often develop.

The structure of these lamellae varies across species:
* **Selachians:** Lamellae are strap-like structures attached along nearly their whole length to the gill septum. In young Selachians, these may be prolonged as filaments projecting to the exterior.
* **Holocephali and Sturgeon:** The outer portions of the gill septa have disappeared.
* **Higher Teleostomes:** The entire septum has disappeared except for a thick inner edge containing the skeletal arch, meaning the lamellae are attached only at their extreme inner end.

## Structural Variations in Teleostomes and Primitive Fish

In Teleostean fish, the partial atrophy of gill septa results in a single large opening on each side known as the opercular opening. This is covered by the operculum, an enlarged valvular flap originating from the hyoid arch. While there are usually five functional clefts in Teleostomi, this is a reduction from a larger ancestral number.

Evidence of this reduction is found in the embryos, where rudiments of a spiracular cleft are laid down in front of the first functional cleft. In primitive fishes, the extension of gill clefts backwards was much greater. For example, adult *Notidanus cinereus* possesses seven branchial clefts, and *Chlamydoselachus* possesses six. In embryonic Selachians, pouch-like outgrowths of entoderm may appear as rudimentary gill pouches that never fully develop.

## The Case of Balanoglossus

Originally discovered in 1825 by J. F. Eschscholtz and initially described as a holothurian (sea-cucumber), *Balanoglossus* was later placed in the class Enteropneusta after Kowalevsky discovered its respiratory gill-slits in 1865. Subsequent research by Bateson between 1883 and 1886 indicated that Enteropneusta have chordate affinities regarding their skeletal, nervous, coelomic, and respiratory systems.

In most *Balanoglossus* species, gill-slits do not open directly to the exterior but open into individual atrial chambers or gill-pouches. Each pouch then opens to the exterior via a minute gill-pore. These pores are located in a longitudinal series at the base of a shallow branchial groove on the dorsal aspect of the worm.
