# The Raw Material Logic: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the cognitive and logistical effort required to acquire the stone itself. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a matter of learning how to strike a rock, but of understanding the geological landscape.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian industries represents a profound leap in spatial awareness and planning. Early hominins did not simply use whatever stone lay at their feet; they developed a sophisticated "mental map" of high-quality lithic sources, often transporting materials over vast distances.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized river cobbles found in the immediate vicinity of their campsites. The "raw material radius" was small, suggesting a lifestyle tied closely to immediate environmental availability.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a shift toward selectivity. Neanderthals and early *Homo sapiens* began seeking specific types of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials were prized for their predictable conchoidal fracture patterns, which allowed for the precision required to create the Levallois flakes characteristic of the Mousterian toolkit.

## The Logistics of Transport and Curation

The Mousterian era introduces the concept of "curation." Rather than discarding a tool once it became dull, prehistoric humans began to transport "cores"—large blocks of high-quality stone—over dozens of kilometers. This indicates a capacity for future-oriented planning: the toolmaker was not solving a problem for *now*, but preparing for a problem they might encounter days later in a different territory.

There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding the "trade" of these materials. Some archaeologists argue that the presence of non-local obsidian in certain sites suggests early social networks and exchange between groups. Others maintain that these materials were simply collected during seasonal migrations. Regardless, the movement of stone proves that the evolution of the tool was inextricably linked to the evolution of the hominin mind's ability to perceive distance and value.

## Key Facts
* **Lithic Selectivity:** Higher-order toolkits required stones with specific molecular structures to ensure sharp, predictable edges.
* **Transport Distance:** While Oldowan materials were often sourced within a few hundred meters, Mousterian materials are frequently found 10–100 kilometers from their source.
* **The Levallois Technique:** A hallmark of the Mousterian period, this method required the toolmaker to shape the core specifically to produce a flake of a predetermined size.
* **Material Preference:** Flint and obsidian were the "gold standard" due to their ability to produce edges thinner than a surgical scalpel.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** When fractured, obsidian can produce an edge significantly sharper than the highest-quality modern steel scalpels.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Paleolithic humans likely identified stone sources by observing the way certain rocks weathered in riverbeds or outcrops.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians study "debitage" (the waste flakes left over from toolmaking) to determine if a tool was made on-site or brought in finished from elsewhere.
