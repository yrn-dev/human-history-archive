# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a matter of improving knapping techniques; it was a revolution in spatial awareness and resource management.

For the early hominins of the Oldowan tradition, toolmaking was largely opportunistic. They utilized "local" materials, often gathering river cobbles from the immediate vicinity of their campsites. However, as we transition toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian, we see a sophisticated shift toward selective sourcing.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the Oldowan period (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), the primary goal was utility. If a piece of basalt or quartz was available and could be flaked to create a sharp edge, it was used. There was little evidence of long-distance transport, suggesting a limited "mental map" of the landscape.

By the Acheulean era, associated with *Homo erectus*, a distinct preference emerged. Hominins began seeking out specific materials—such as fine-grained flint or obsidian—that allowed for the symmetry and precision required to create the iconic handaxe. This shift indicates a higher level of foresight; the toolmaker was no longer just reacting to what was underfoot but was planning for a future need.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) represents the pinnacle of Paleolithic raw material economy. The introduction of the Levallois technique—where a core is carefully prepared to strike off a predictable, pre-shaped flake—demanded high-quality stone. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers frequently transported high-grade materials over several kilometers, sometimes even tens of kilometers, from the original quarry to the habitation site. This implies a complex social structure involving the memory of resource locations and, potentially, the organized movement of groups to secure "industrial" sites.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among prehistoric archaeologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the immediate area) proves the existence of early trade networks between different hominin groups, or if it simply reflects the wide migratory ranges of the individuals themselves. While some argue for a primitive form of exchange, others maintain that these materials were simply carried during seasonal migrations.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan:** Characterized by opportunistic use of local river cobbles and basalt.
* **Acheulean:** Marked by the selective search for materials capable of supporting bifacial symmetry.
* **Mousterian:** Demonstrated the highest level of planning, with evidence of long-distance raw material transport.
* **Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated method of core preparation that required specific, high-quality stone types.
* **Resource Mapping:** The evolution of tools mirrors the expansion of the hominin "cognitive map" of their environment.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian breaks with a molecularly sharp edge, it was one of the most prized materials in the prehistoric world.
* **Curated Toolkits:** Later Paleolithic humans practiced "curation," meaning they sharpened and reused tools repeatedly to avoid having to travel back to a distant stone source.
* **Material Influence:** The specific type of stone available in a region often dictated the "style" of the tool, meaning some regional differences in toolkits were caused by geology rather than cultural choice.
