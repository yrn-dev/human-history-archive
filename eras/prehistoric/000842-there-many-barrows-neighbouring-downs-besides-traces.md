# Barrows and Ancient Burial Customs

Barrows are prehistoric burial mounds that have been found with almost universal prevalence across Europe, as well as in Asia Minor, India, China, and Japan. Similar customs of burying the dead in barrows existed in the new world, specifically in the great plains of North America, where mounds of enormous magnitude were constructed. These North American barrows occasionally show a remarkable similarity to those found in Great Britain.

## Types and Evolution of Barrows
The traditional forms of barrows include the long barrow and the round barrow (or chambered cairn). In Ireland, the long barrow is almost unknown, and the round barrow prevailed from the earliest Pagan period until Christianity was introduced. Irish barrows often appear in groups; an ancient Irish manuscript from approximately A.D. 1100, the *Leabhar na h-Uidhri*, enumerates eight such groups that may have served as royal cemeteries for tribal confederacies. A notable example is located on the banks of the Boyne above Drogheda, featuring New Grange, a mound of earth and stones over 70 feet high and 300 feet in diameter, surrounded by a circle of large standing stones. In the Loughcrew Hills of Co. Meath, there is a group of about thirty stone barrows or cairns, some of which feature slabs ornamented with rudely incised spirals and other devices.

The structure of the barrow evolved alongside changes in sepulchral customs. In the Stone Age, the chamber was viewed as a habitation for the deceased. However, the rise of cremation led to a modification where the chamber became a simple cist for an urn containing ashes. Consequently, Bronze Age barrows—where cremation was common—are generally smaller and less imposing than those of the Stone Age, though they are often rich in art workmanship and relics.

## Burial Practices and Rituals
Both inhumation and cremation were practiced in early times. In the south of England, cremation is rare in long barrows, whereas it is the rule in the north of Scotland and Yorkshire. In North American barrows, cremation appears more frequently than inhumation. In both cases, burials were often accompanied by ornaments, weapons, and implements made of bone and stone, as well as elaborately ornamented pottery.

In Egypt, Neolithic pastoral people buried their dead, but evidence suggests they cut up the bodies and ate portions of the flesh. This practice, described by Mr. Flinders Petrie as being "eaten with honour," may have been intended to retain the virtue or strength of the deceased. Similar customs have been found in the long barrows of western Europe prior to the spread of the Aryan peoples, and have persisted in negro Africa.

## Regional Examples and Related Sites
In the vicinity of Avebury, there are many barrows on the neighbouring downs, along with the huge mound of Silbury Hill and traces of a double oval of monoliths on Hackpen hill. Nearby, Waden Hill has been identified with Badon Hill, the traditional site of King Arthur's twelfth and final great battle in 520.

Other global monuments are considered lineally descended from the prehistoric chambered barrow, modified by the architectural advancements of their respective eras. These include the pyramids of Egypt, the Etruscan tombs at Volci and Caere, the mausolea of the Lydian kings, and the circular, chambered sepulchres of Mycenae.
