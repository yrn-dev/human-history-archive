# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shapes and techniques used to create Oldowan choppers or Mousterian scrapers—we must examine the cognitive and social implications of the materials themselves. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a progression of skill, but a revolution in geological prospecting and resource management.

The transition from the opportunistic use of local river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their environment and planned for the future.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally utilized whatever raw materials were immediately available within their immediate vicinity. If a riverbed provided basalt or quartz, that is what was used. The "cost" of transporting material was high, and the planning horizon was short.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian traditions, we see a marked increase in "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the predictable, razor-sharp edges necessary for the sophisticated Levallois technique.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The presence of non-local stone in a campsite is one of the strongest indicators of cognitive complexity. When archaeologists find obsidian or high-grade flint kilometers away from its natural source, it suggests one of two things: high mobility (the toolmaker carried the stone while migrating) or an early form of social exchange.

By the Mousterian era, associated with Neanderthals and early *Homo sapiens*, the transport of raw materials became a strategic necessity. The ability to carry "cores" (the parent block of stone) over long distances indicates "curation"—the act of preserving a resource for future use. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and an understanding of seasonal resource availability.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Chance

There remains a scholarly debate regarding the "intent" behind material transport. Some archaeologists argue that the movement of stone was a byproduct of general foraging movements. Others contend that the specific selection of high-quality materials from distant sources proves a deliberate "raw material economy," where certain stones were valued for their superior performance, marking the earliest precursors to economic behavior.

### Key Facts: Lithic Sourcing
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint) break, essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Curation:** The practice of transporting and saving high-quality stone cores for later use.
* **Provenance:** The original geographic source of the raw material used to make a tool.
* **Lithic Economy:** The study of how prehistoric humans acquired, transported, and discarded stone tools.
* **Material Bias:** The tendency for some stone types to survive in the archaeological record better than others.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian’s Edge:** Obsidian can be flaked to an edge significantly sharper than a modern surgical steel scalpel.
* **The "Curated" Core:** Some Mousterian cores show evidence of being carried for dozens of kilometers, suggesting they were prized possessions.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans had to understand the difference between "weathered" stone (which is brittle) and "fresh" stone to ensure their tools wouldn't shatter during knapping.
