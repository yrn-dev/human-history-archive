# The Veins of Antiquity: Bronze Age Trade Networks

## Overview
The Bronze Age (roughly 3300–1200 BCE) marked a pivotal shift in human organization, transitioning from localized Neolithic farming to complex, interconnected societies. This transformation was driven by a fundamental geological necessity: the production of bronze requires both copper and tin. While copper is relatively common, tin is rare, occurring in only a few deposits across Eurasia (such as Cornwall in Britain, the Erzgebirge in Central Europe, and parts of Central Asia).

To acquire these materials, prehistoric peoples established vast, prestige-driven trade networks. These were not "roads" in the modern sense, but fluid chains of exchange where goods moved through "down-the-line" trade—passing from one village to the next. These networks facilitated more than just the movement of ore; they were conduits for linguistic shifts, religious ideas, and metallurgical technology. The "Amber Road," for instance, linked the Baltic coast to the Mediterranean, exchanging northern resin for southern gold and bronze.

## Timeline of Connectivity

| Period | Approximate Dates | Key Development |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Early Bronze Age** | 3300 – 2000 BCE | Emergence of primary metallurgy in the Near East; initial long-distance copper trade. |
| **Middle Bronze Age** | 2000 – 1600 BCE | Expansion of the "Tin Routes"; increased connectivity between Central Europe and the Steppes. |
| **Late Bronze Age** | 1600 – 1200 BCE | Peak of "Palatial" economies (Mycenaean/Hittite); highly organized maritime trade in the Mediterranean. |

## Key Facts
*   **The Tin Dilemma:** Because tin was so scarce, its trade created the first truly international dependencies. Historians debate the exact source of tin for the early Mediterranean civilizations; while some point to Afghanistan (via Mesopotamia), others argue that Cornish tin from Britain reached the Aegean via river systems and coastal hopping.
*   **Prestige Goods:** Trade was not merely utilitarian. High-status items—such as Baltic amber, Lapis Lazuli from Afghanistan, and gold from the Ural Mountains—were traded among elites to signal power and divine connection.
*   **The Late Bronze Age Collapse:** Around 1200 BCE, these interconnected networks suffered a systemic failure. The collapse of major centers (like the Mycenaean palaces) suggests that the very interdependence that made the Bronze Age prosperous also made it fragile; a disruption in one part of the tin supply chain could destabilize an entire empire.

## Did You Know?
1. **Lapis Lazuli's Journey:** This deep-blue stone was mined almost exclusively in the Badakhshan region of modern Afghanistan, yet it has been found in the royal tombs of Ur (Iraq) and ancient Egypt, proving the reach of Asian trade routes.
2. **The "Amber Road":** Amber from the Baltic Sea was so prized in the Mediterranean that it is often referred to as "the gold of the North," moving thousands of miles across land and sea.
3. **Technological Diffusion:** The spread of the spoke-wheeled chariot is believed to have followed these trade routes, moving from the Eurasian steppes into the Near East and Europe.
