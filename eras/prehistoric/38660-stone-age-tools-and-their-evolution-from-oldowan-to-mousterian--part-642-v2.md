# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shift from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of tool technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The transition from the Oldowan to the Mousterian reflects a growing sophistication in "lithic procurement." Early toolmakers were opportunistic, while later ancestors were strategic planners.

## From Opportunism to Selection
During the Oldowan period (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), *Homo habilis* generally utilized whatever stones were immediately available in their vicinity, such as river cobbles. The "cost" of transporting heavy stones was high, and the cognitive map of the landscape was likely limited. Tools were often discarded where they were made, as the effort to carry them outweighed the benefit.

As we move into the Acheulean era, we see the emergence of selective sourcing. *Homo erectus* began to seek out specific materials—such as quartzite or flint—that offered more predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). This indicates a mental shift: the tool was no longer just a reaction to a need, but a planned object.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly organized. The Levallois technique, a hallmark of this era, required high-quality stone to produce a specific, predetermined flake. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers often transported high-quality flint over several kilometers from its original source. This suggests a complex social structure involving "curation"—the practice of maintaining and sharpening a tool over time rather than discarding it. There is ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different kinship groups or if individuals traveled vast distances to quarry sites, but the evidence clearly points to a sophisticated understanding of geological geography.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian or flint) break, allowing humans to create sharp, predictable edges.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for, selecting, and extracting raw stone for tool production.
* **Curation:** The act of transporting and maintaining a tool, indicating a shift from "expedient" tool use to long-term planning.
* **Transport Distance:** While Oldowan tools were often found within meters of their source, Mousterian materials are frequently found kilometers away from the quarry.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late Paleolithic sites, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers from the nearest volcanic source, suggesting early networks of exchange.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Levallois technique, the "core" stone was carefully shaped like a turtle shell to ensure the final flake was the perfect size and shape.
* **Material Preference:** Early humans didn't just want "hard" stones; they wanted stones with a specific crystalline structure that allowed for "knapping" without the stone shattering randomly.
