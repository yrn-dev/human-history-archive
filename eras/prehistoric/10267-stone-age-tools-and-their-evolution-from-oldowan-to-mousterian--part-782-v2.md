# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

The transition from opportunistic scavenging of materials to strategic quarrying reveals a profound shift in spatial memory and planning depth.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. The "raw material economy" was local; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used. There was little evidence of selective transport, suggesting a "find-and-use" mental model.

As we move into the Acheulean period, associated with *Homo erectus*, we see the emergence of selectivity. Hominins began to bypass inferior stones in favor of materials that produced more predictable fractures. This indicates a burgeoning understanding of geology and a willingness to invest energy in transporting high-quality stone over several kilometers to ensure the success of a handaxe.

## The Mousterian Logistics Network
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated primarily with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. The Levallois technique required specific types of flint or chert to produce the characteristic "prepared core." 

Archaeological evidence suggests that Neanderthals did not just wander until they found stone; they maintained "mental maps" of high-quality outcrops. In some European sites, raw materials have been found transported over 30 to 50 kilometers. This suggests a complex social structure where certain areas were designated as quarry sites, and materials were either carried by individuals or cached for future use.

There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether this transport was a result of nomadic wandering or a deliberate, seasonal return to known sources. However, the consistency of material choice strongly supports the theory of intentional procurement.

## Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily local, relying on opportunistic finds in riverine environments.
* **Acheulean Shift:** Increased selectivity for materials that allowed for symmetrical bifacial shaping.
* **Mousterian Range:** Evidence of raw material transport spanning dozens of kilometers.
* **Material Preference:** A strong preference for cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) due to their predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **Cognitive Leap:** The shift from local to distant sourcing indicates an evolution in long-term planning and spatial memory.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after one use, Mousterian tools were "curated," meaning they were resharpened and carried for long periods.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Prehistoric toolmakers could distinguish between different types of stone based on texture and weight before even striking the first blow.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians determine the distance stone traveled by analyzing "debitage" (waste flakes); a lack of waste flakes at a campsite suggests the tools were brought in finished from elsewhere.
