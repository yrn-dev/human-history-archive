# The Raw Material Revolution: Lithic Procurement and Site Selection

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive leap involved in *procurement*. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a fundamental shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and the geological resources within it.

For the early tool-maker, the world was not a map of territories, but a map of raw materials. The transition from opportunistic scavenging of stones to the strategic quarrying of high-quality minerals reveals a sophisticated evolution in spatial memory and planning.

## From Opportunism to Selection (Oldowan)
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. *Homo habilis* and their predecessors typically utilized river cobbles found in the immediate vicinity of their campsites. The "raw material radius" was small; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used. There was little evidence of transporting stone over long distances, suggesting a "find-and-use" mentality.

## The Logistics of Quality (Acheulean)
As we move into the Acheulean era (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), the behavioral pattern shifts. *Homo erectus* began to exhibit "selective procurement." Archaeologists have noted that Acheulean handaxes were often crafted from materials far superior to the local rubble. 

This indicates the emergence of mental mapping. Hominins were now remembering the locations of specific outcrops and traveling several kilometers to retrieve high-quality flint or quartzite. This transport suggests that the "ideal form" of the handaxe existed in the mind of the maker before the stone was even touched, necessitating a specific grade of material to achieve the desired symmetry.

## Strategic Quarrying and the Mousterian Shift
By the Mousterian period (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago), associated primarily with Neanderthals, the relationship with stone became industrial. The Levallois technique required a "prepared core," which demanded high-quality, predictable fracturing patterns found only in specific types of cryptocrystalline silicates.

Neanderthals didn't just visit outcrops; they engaged in systematic quarrying. There is scholarly debate regarding the extent of "trade" or social exchange of these materials between groups, but the presence of non-local stone in Mousterian sites suggests a complex network of resource management and perhaps the first instances of curated toolkits—carrying a "specialist" stone for a specific task over vast distances.

## Key Facts
* **Raw Material Radius:** The distance between the source of the stone and the site where the tool was used.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of searching for, selecting, and transporting stone for tool production.
* **Cryptocrystalline Silicates:** High-quality stones (like flint or chert) that break with a predictable "conchoidal" fracture.
* **Curated Toolkits:** The practice of maintaining and transporting tools over long distances rather than making them on the spot.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Expensive" Stone:** Some Mousterian sites contain materials transported from over 10 kilometers away, a significant distance for a prehistoric hominin.
* **Recycling:** To conserve high-quality stone, prehistoric humans often "re-sharpened" tools, leading to the shrinking of the tool's original size over time.
* **Geological Knowledge:** The shift to the Levallois technique proves that Neanderthals understood the internal physics of stone, knowing exactly how to strike a core to produce a flake of a predetermined size.
