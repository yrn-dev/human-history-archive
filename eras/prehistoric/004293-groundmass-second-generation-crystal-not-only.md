# Basalt and Related Igneous Rocks

Basalt is one of the oldest known rock names, believed to be derived from the Ethiopian word *basal*, meaning a stone that yields iron. According to Pliny, the first basalts were obtained in Ethiopia. In modern petrology, the term refers to a wide variety of basic igneous rocks that are dark in color, weather to brown, and are relatively rich in iron and magnesia.

## Physical Structure and Composition
Basaltic rocks frequently exhibit a porphyritic structure, characterized by larger crystals—known as phenocrysts—set within a finely crystalline groundmass. The most common porphyritic minerals are augite, which is pitch-black, and olivine, which appears green or yellowish and may weather into brown or green serpentine. Porphyritic plagioclase felspars are also very common; while they usually do not exceed a quarter of an inch in length, some can reach one or two inches. Fresh plagioclase felspars are dark grey with smooth, lustrous cleavage surfaces, though they become turbid and assume greenish or grey shades upon decomposition.

The groundmass, referred to as the second generation of crystal, contains ingredients that are smaller and less perfect than the phenocrysts. In many basalts, the matrix is formed by minute prisms of augite and small lath-shaped felspars that are densely crowded together. Olivine is rarely found as an ingredient of the groundmass.

## Varieties of Basaltic Rocks
Basaltic rocks vary significantly in their crystalline and glassy states:

*   **Tachylites:** Basalts that are glassy to a large measure.
*   **Vitreous Basalts:** In these rocks, the majority of the material is a dark brown, almost opaque glassy material. These may contain steam vesicles, perlitic cracks, spherulites, skeleton crystals of felspar or augite, and black grains of magnetite.
*   **Dolerites:** These are basaltic rocks that are nearly or quite holocrystalline and comparatively coarse-grained. Named by Hauy from the Greek *doleros* (deceptive), dolerites are often intrusive, occurring as sills and dikes, though they also form lava flows. Their essential minerals are plagioclase felspar, augite, and olivine, with common accessory ingredients including biotite, apatite, ilmenite, and hornblende.

## Dolerite Structures
Dolerites may be porphyritic or non-porphyritic. The majority are non-porphyritic and generally possess a coarser grain than the groundmass of porphyritic varieties. A common feature in dolerites is the ophitic structure, which occurs when the rock's felspar crystallizes before the augite. In this arrangement, the augite forms shapeless masses that envelop idiomorphic felspars. This structure is most common in olivine-dolerites, although olivine does not participate in the ophitic structure itself. Some porphyritic dolerites exhibit this structure, while others are subophitic or contain augite and felspar as distinct, perfectly shaped crystals.

## Geological Features and Terminology
Basaltic lavas are often pumiceous or spongy, particularly near the surface. Over time, secondary minerals such as zeolites, chlorite, and calcite may fill the resulting steam cavities. A distinguishing characteristic of this group is "basaltic jointing," where the rocks show prismatic or columnar jointing.

Historically, continental geologists believed that pre-Tertiary basalts were a distinct class from recent or Tertiary representatives. Consequently, names such as melaphyre, *diabas-mandel-stein*, diabase porphyrite, and anamesite were used to describe altered dolerites and basalts. While these terms are still used by some writers, they are no longer considered to differ essentially from well-preserved basalts. 

Additionally, the term *trap*—derived from a Swedish word for "a stair"—is used by travelers and geologists to describe the whole suite of basaltic rocks. This name refers to the step-like or terraced features created when superposed sheets of basalt weather. Examples include the "traps" of the Deccan and trap-dikes.

## Associated Minerals
Certain rare minerals occur within these formations. Greenockite, a rare cadmium sulphide (CdS) appearing as canary-yellow powder or honey-yellow hexagonal crystals, has been found in the amygdaloidal cavities of basaltic igneous rocks. Specifically, crystals were discovered in the dolerite quarry at Bowling in Dumbartonshire around 1810, as well as at the Boyleston quarry near Barrhead and during the cutting of the Bishopton tunnel in 1840.
