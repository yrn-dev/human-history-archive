# Geological History of Central Asia and Afghanistan

The geological structure of central Asia is characterized by a complex history of marine deposits, tectonic folding, and the movement of ancient land masses. A defining feature of the region is a broad belt of Mesozoic and Tertiary deposits located between the ancient land masses of China and India.

## Regional Stratigraphy and Marine Deposits
In Afghanistan, the Mesozoic beds are composed of both fluviatile and marine deposits. This region is part of a larger area where marine deposits of the Mesozoic age are well developed, indicating that the territory was beneath the sea for the majority of the Mesozoic era. 

The extent of these seas varied over time. During the later stages of the Cretaceous period, the sea reached a significant extension, covering much of western Asia. This coverage included Arabia, Persia, Baluchistan, Afghanistan, and the drainage area of the Aral Sea, as well as the basin of the Obi. These waters spread across the whole of western Asia and slightly encroached upon the Indian land. While the Eocene sea was also extensive, it is noted that a considerable portion of this same region was likely dry land during Jurassic times.

## Tectonic Structure and Folding
The geological layout of the region can be divided into distinct zones based on the behavior of the rock strata:

*   **The Folded Belt:** A broad belt exists where Mesozoic deposits and the lower divisions of the Tertiary system are thrown into folds. These folds extend in arcs from west to east and form the principal mountain ranges of central Asia. This zone includes Afghanistan, Persia, Baluchistan, the Himalayas, the Tian-shan, Asia Minor, and the Burmese ranges. While the zone is characterized as one of "crumpling," there are some tracts within these arcs that remain relatively undisturbed.
*   **The Southern Quiescent Region:** In the peninsula of India, as well as in Arabia and Syria, the Mesozoic and Tertiary beds lie almost horizontally upon the oldest rocks. This area is characterized by faulting or quiescence rather than folding.
*   **The Northern Stable Region:** North of the folded belt—including northern China, Mongolia, and much of Siberia—the area is generally free from important Mesozoic or Tertiary folding. In these regions, the configuration is largely due to faulting, creating trough-like depressions and intervening mountain ridges.

## Continental Evolution and Mountain Formation
The geological history of the region involves the interaction of two primary land masses: Angaraland to the north and Gondwanaland to the south (of which the Indian peninsula is a fragment). These two masses were separated by a central sea, referred to as the Tethys by Suess and the central Mediterranean sea by Neumayr.

The elevation of the Himalayas and other great central Asian mountain chains occurred after the Eocene period. This process resulted in the isolation of portions of the former central sea and eventually led to the union of the old continents of Gondwana and Angara. Evidence for the height of this elevation is found in the presence of nummulitic limestone at an altitude of 20,000 feet. Following this union, Gondwanaland did not persist; the section located between South Africa and India sank beneath the waves during Tertiary times.

## Comparison with Surrounding Areas
The presence of marine deposits varies significantly across Asia. While Afghanistan and the Himalayas show a nearly complete series of marine deposits from the Carboniferous to the Eocene, other areas differ:
*   **Mongolia and Northern China:** In the area north of the Tian-shan, there are no marine beds of Mesozoic or Tertiary age, though plant-bearing deposits of the Angara series are present.
*   **Southern China:** Except for areas near the Burmese and Tibetan borders, southern China lacks marine deposits of the Mesozoic or Tertiary age.
*   **India:** The peninsula of India has remained land since at least the Carboniferous period, evidenced by the absence of marine fossils (except near its borders) and the presence of terrestrial and freshwater deposits of the Gondwana series.
