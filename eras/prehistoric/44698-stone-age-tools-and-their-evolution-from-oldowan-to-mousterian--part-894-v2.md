# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in *sourcing*. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a change in shape, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed raw materials.

The transition from opportunistic scavenging of stones to the strategic procurement of high-quality lithics reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and foresight.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever river cobbles or volcanic rocks were immediately available in their vicinity. The "cost" of transporting material was high, and the willingness to do so was low; most tools were discarded exactly where the raw material was found.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, we see the emergence of "selective procurement." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—which offered more predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). This indicates a mental map of the environment and the ability to plan for future needs.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Leap
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become sophisticated. The implementation of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality stone to be successful.

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers transported high-grade lithics over several kilometers, and in some cases, tens of kilometers. This suggests a "curated" technology. Rather than making a tool and dropping it, hominins were now carrying "blanks" or prepared cores with them, treating the stone as a portable resource. This shift implies a higher level of social organization and a conceptual understanding of "value" and "distance."

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?
A point of ongoing debate among historians and archaeologists is whether the presence of non-local stone in a site indicates long-distance migration or the earliest forms of inter-group trade. While some argue that Neanderthals simply carried stones as they wandered, others suggest that specific high-quality quarries were known "hubs," implying a social network of exchange that predates settled civilization.

### Key Facts: Lithic Procurement
* **Oldowan:** Characterized by "expedient" technology; materials were sourced within meters of the tool's final use.
* **Acheulean:** Marked the beginning of selective sourcing, with a preference for materials that could support the symmetry of hand-axes.
* **Mousterian:** Introduced "curated" toolkits, where prepared cores were transported across the landscape.
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The key physical property of stones like flint and obsidian that allowed prehistoric humans to create sharp, predictable edges.
* **Transport Cost:** The energy expenditure required to move raw materials, which decreased as hominins became more efficient at tool design.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Obsession:** Because obsidian (volcanic glass) produces an edge thinner and sharper than a modern steel scalpel, it was one of the most highly prized materials in the prehistoric world.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Levallois technique, the "waste" was not the flake, but the core; the entire process was a sophisticated exercise in subtraction to achieve one perfect shape.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early hominins had to possess an intuitive understanding of geology to identify "nodules" of flint hidden inside unattractive limestone crusts.
