# Cretaceous and Tertiary Beds in Folded Mountain Systems

Across various global regions, Cretaceous and Tertiary geological beds have been subjected to intense tectonic forces, resulting in the formation of complex series of folds. These folded strata often constitute the primary structure of major mountain ranges, though their characteristics vary significantly by geography.

## Central Asia and Baluchistan
A broad belt extending from west to east across central Asia—including Persia, Afghanistan, Baluchistan, the Himalayas, the Tian-shan, and the Burmese ranges—is characterized by the folding of Mesozoic deposits and the lower divisions of the Tertiary system. While some tracts within this zone remain undisturbed, the region as a whole is described as one of crumpling.

In Baluchistan, the mountain ranges consist chiefly of Cretaceous and Tertiary beds. These are thrown into folds that run approximately parallel to the mountain ridges, forming an extensive system resembling a south-hanging festoon between Mount Ararat and Peshawar. A subsidiary festoon, comprising the Bugti Hills and the Suliman, is formed where the outer folds loop up at Sibi. While most deposits are marine, north-western Baluchistan features an extensive development of conglomerates and volcanic tuffs in the lower Tertiary and upper Cretaceous. Additionally, Jurassic rocks contribute to the hills of British Baluchistan, and Triassic beds, along with *Fusulina* limestone, are found on the south side of the upper Zhob.

## Asia Minor and the Pontian Arcs
In Asia Minor, the central Lycaonian plateau consists of nearly horizontal late Tertiary or Neogene freshwater limestones and marls. However, the surrounding mountain chains are intensely folded. The Taurus and Anti-Taurus ranges form a south-facing crescent; in the Taurus, Cretaceous and Eocene limestones are prominent. While the Anti-Taurus folding affects the Eocene, it does not extend to the Miocene.

To the north of the plateau, the East and West Pontian arcs exhibit further folding. In the east, a system running parallel to the Black Sea coast from Sinope to Batum involves the Oligocene in its folds, with Cretaceous serpentine and limestones playing a large part in the formation. West of Sinope, Cretaceous beds form a strip parallel to the shore.

## The Balkan Peninsula and the Carpathians
The Balkan Peninsula features two belts of folded beds: one forming the chain of the Balkans to the north and another forming the mountain ranges of the Ionian and Adriatic coasts to the west. In the western belt, the folds strike N.W.-S.E. or N.N.W.-S.S.E. and curve toward Crete in the south. This chain is composed chiefly of Cretaceous beds in the south, though Jurassic and Eocene rocks are also present. The involvement of Sarmatian beds indicates that this folding continued until Pliocene times.

In the Carpathians, the outer zone is remarkably uniform, consisting of a succession of Tertiary and Cretaceous shales and sandstones (Carpathian Sandstone). These are thrown into isoclinal folds that dip constantly to the south, a process that occurred during the Miocene period.

## Southeast Asia and Burma
In British Burma, the western mountain ranges feature beds thrown into a series of folds forming a gentle curve with a westward convexity. This system includes an axial zone of Lower Eocene and Cretaceous, flanked by Miocene and Upper Eocene beds. The identification of the Cretaceous system in this region relies on the discovery of a single Cenomanian ammonite.

In Assam, the newer beds do not show signs of the Burmese or Himalayan folding on the plateau, where they are nearly horizontal. However, along the southern margin, they are bent sharply downward in a simple monoclinal fold. Cretaceous beds here consist mainly of sandstones with coal seams, thinning toward the north.

## European Archipelagos and Continental Margins
In the Balearic Isles, an older series ranging from the Devonian to the Cretaceous is folded and faulted, forming the higher hills. The direction of these folds varies by island: west to east in Iviza, south-west to north-east in Majorca, and south to north in Minorca, creating an arc convex toward the south-east. The principal folding of these beds appears to have occurred during the Cretaceous period.

Conversely, in Belgium, the Cretaceous and Tertiary beds covering the greater part of the country are nearly undisturbed, lying upon the northern margin of the ancient Palaeozoic Hercynian chain.
