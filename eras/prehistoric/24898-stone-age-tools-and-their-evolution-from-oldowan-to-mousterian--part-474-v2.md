# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian flake—we must shift our gaze from the finished object to the origin of the stone. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a sophisticated evolution in geological prospecting and logistical planning.

The ability to identify, acquire, and transport high-quality raw materials reveals a great deal about the spatial awareness and social organization of prehistoric hominins.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest **Oldowan** traditions (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. The "raw material economy" was local; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used. There was little evidence of selective transport, suggesting a "find-and-use" mentality.

As we transition into the **Acheulean** (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), a shift occurs. The production of the iconic handaxe required specific types of stone—materials with predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). Archaeologists have discovered sites where the stone used for tools was sourced from quarries several kilometers away. This indicates a cognitive shift: the hominin was no longer just reacting to their environment but planning for future needs.

## The Mousterian and the Mastery of Levallois

By the **Mousterian** period (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became even more complex. The introduction of the **Levallois technique**—a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape—demanded high-quality flint or chert.

The logistical effort increased significantly. Neanderthals didn't just carry stones; they curated them. We see evidence of "core transport," where a large block of stone was carried over long distances and slowly reduced into multiple smaller tools. This suggests a sophisticated understanding of the landscape and a social structure capable of coordinating the movement of resources across territories.

## Scholarly Debate: The "Cost-Benefit" Analysis
There is ongoing debate among lithic specialists regarding the "cost" of transport. Some scholars argue that the distance traveled for high-quality stone proves a high level of cognitive mapping. Others suggest that these movements were simply the result of natural migratory patterns, and the stone was gathered incidentally along the way.

### Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Material Preference:** Quartzite, basalt, chert, and obsidian were the most prized materials due to their sharp edges.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan tools were typically sourced within 1–2 km; Mousterian materials were often transported over 10–20 km.
* **Curated Technology:** The shift from "expedient" tools (made and discarded quickly) to "curated" tools (maintained and carried).
* **Core Reduction:** The process of shaping a stone "blank" to maximize the number of usable flakes produced.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In later prehistoric periods, obsidian (volcanic glass) was so highly valued that it created some of the world's first long-distance trade networks.
* **The "Wrong" Stone:** Hominins occasionally used poor-quality stone when high-quality sources were unavailable, showing a pragmatic ability to adapt to geological constraints.
* **Natural Selection:** The preference for certain stones wasn't random; materials like flint allow for a "surgical" precision that basalt cannot provide.
