# History of Brittany

## Pre-Roman and Roman Periods
Exact knowledge of Brittany before the arrival of the Romans is unavailable. The primitive populations left behind megalithic monuments, including cromlechs, menhirs, and dolmens, which remain in great numbers. Some sources attribute primitive temples such as Carnac to the "dark whites" of the heliolithic culture rather than primitive Aryans.

In 56 B.C., the Veneti of Brittany attempted to throw off their yoke and took two of Crassus's officers as hostages. In response, Julius Caesar crossed the Loire and invaded Brittany. The Veneti possessed a powerful fleet of high, flat-bottomed boats described as "floating castles." To counter this, the Romans constructed a fleet in the estuary of the Loire under the command of Decimus Brutus. The decisive battle likely took place in the Gulf of Morbihan, where the Romans won by using poles with attached sickles to cut down the enemy's rigging. As punishment, Caesar executed the senate of the Veneti and sold the population into slavery. 

In 52 B.C., the inhabitants of Armorica joined the great Gaulish insurrection against Caesar, but they were finally subdued by him in 51 B.C. Following these events, Roman civilization was established in Brittany for several centuries.

## Early Middle Ages and the Plantagenets
Brittany (known in French as *Bretagne*) derived its name from Britain due to migrations that occurred during the 5th and 6th centuries A.D. Linguistically, Brittany is noted as a place where modern forms of the Celtic branch of the Indo-European family survive.

By the second half of the 10th century and into the 11th century, the counts of Rennes held predominance in the region. In 992, Geoffrey, the son of Conan, took the title of duke of Brittany. Later, Conan II was threatened by noble revolts and attacked by the duke of Normandy (William I of England). However, a successor named Alain Fergent defeated William in 1085, forcing a peace.

In the following century, the Plantagenets established themselves in Brittany. Conan IV appealed to Henry II of England for help against revolted Breton nobles; in exchange, Conan's daughter was married to Henry's son, Geoffrey. This made Henry II the master of Brittany, and Geoffrey was recognized as duke. This dynasty ended after Geoffrey's posthumous son, Arthur, was assassinated by John of England in 1203. Arthur's sister, Alix, succeeded him and married Pierre de Dreux in 1212, initiating a ducal dynasty of French origin that lasted until the end of the 15th century.

## The War of Succession and the Duchy
The ducal power eventually grew strong enough to curb the feudal nobles, and civilization progressed under French influence, leading to over a century of undisturbed peace. This peace ended in 1341 when the death of John III without a direct heir sparked a war of succession between the houses of Montfort and Blois. This conflict lasted until 1364 and was an incident of the Hundred Years' War, with the houses of Montfort and Blois supported by the kings of England and France, respectively.

During this period, the region was the scene of a savage civil war. English soldiers of fortune, such as Sir Robert Knolles, took advantage of the distraction to gain wealth and fame. Knolles participated in the struggle for the duchy between Charles of Blois and John of Montfort, distinguishing himself at the fight at Auray in September 1364, where Charles of Blois was killed and Du Guesclin was captured. Following this, John of Montfort was recognized as Duke John IV in 1364.

## Annexation to France
The reign of John IV was troubled, particularly by his struggle with Olivier de Clisson. His successor, John V (d. 1442), is noted for a pacific and able policy. Under John V and subsequent dukes—Arthur III, Peter II, and Francis I—ducal authority expanded significantly. The dukes established a standing army and levied hearth taxes known as *fouages*.

Francis II (1435–1488) engaged in conflicts against Louis XI during the War of the Public Weal and later fought Charles VIII in "The Mad War" (*La Guerre Folle*). After Francis II died, the king of France invaded Brittany and forced Francis's daughter, Anne of Brittany, to marry him in 1491, preparing the reunion of Brittany and France. Anne later married Louis XII after the death of Charles VIII.

The definitive annexation of the duchy was settled by a contract in 1532 through Francis I, who married Claude, the daughter of Anne and Louis XII. This contract guaranteed the maintenance of Brittany's liberties and privileges. Brittany retained its own estates until the Revolution, though the royal power frequently attempted to reduce provincial privileges, leading to vigorous resistance, particularly between 1760 and 1769 during the administration of the duc d'Aiguillon.
