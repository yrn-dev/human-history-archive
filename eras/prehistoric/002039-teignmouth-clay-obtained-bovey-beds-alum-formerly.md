# Bagshot Beds and Associated Clays

The Bagshot beds are a geological series consisting of clays and sands of shallow-water origin, encompassing both marine and fresh-water deposits. These beds are part of the upper Eocene formation and are primarily found in the Hampshire and London basins of England, as well as the Isle of Wight. The series derives its name from Bagshot Heath in Surrey.

## Stratigraphic Divisions
The Bagshot beds are generally categorized into three primary divisions:
*   **Upper Bagshot Beds:** This division includes the Barton sand and Barton clay. In the Isle of Wight, these beds reach a thickness of 140 to 200 feet.
*   **Middle Bagshot Beds:** This division consists of the Bracklesham beds. In Surrey and Berkshire, these beds range from 20 to 50 feet in thickness, while in Alum Bay they reach 100 feet. In Alum Bay, the lower portion of the Bracklesham beds contains lignite and is separated from the overlying Barton clay by a conglomerate bed of flint pebbles.
*   **Lower Bagshot Beds:** This division includes the Alum Bay beds, Bournemouth beds, and Bovey Tracey beds.

## Regional Distribution and Characteristics
In the London basin, the Lower Bagshot beds consist of loam and pale-yellow, current-bedded sand, featuring occasional beds of flint pebbles and layers of pipeclay. There is no sharp line of junction between these beds and the underlying London clay. These formations are visible at Highbeech, Billericay, and Brentwood in Essex, as well as at Harrow, Highgate, and Hampstead. In Surrey, heath-bearing Lower Bagshot beds cover tracts of London clay in areas such as Woking, Aldershot, and Weybridge. The "Ramsdell clay" located northwest of Basingstoke is also attributed to this formation.

In the Isle of Wight, the lower division is well exposed at White Cliff Bay (140 feet) and Alum Bay (660 feet). Here, the formation consists of clays with ferruginous sandstone and lignite layers, alongside sands in various shades, including crimson, brown, yellow, and white. Similar deposits are found near Studland, Corfe, Wareham, Poole, and Bournemouth.

While Eocene rocks in Britain are largely confined to the south-eastern portion of England—separated by the denuded anticline of the Weald—exceptions include the Bovey beds and the leaf-bearing beds of Mull and Antrim.

## Economic Uses and Resources
The clays associated with these formations have significant industrial value:
*   **Pottery:** The clays of the Bovey beds and the sands and clays of Bovey Tracey are of great value for the manufacture of pottery. "Teignmouth clay" is specifically obtained from the Bovey beds. Similarly, clays mined near Corfe and Wareham are shipped from Poole and are known as "Poole clays."
*   **Alum:** Alum was formerly extracted from the clays of Alum Bay. (Note: Alum has also been obtained from the Kimeridge Clay).
*   **Fuel:** Lignites found in these formations have been utilized as fuel at Bovey and near Corfe.

## Paleontology and Climate
The leaf-bearing clays of Bournemouth and Alum Bay have yielded a diverse series of plant remains. These include *Populus*, *Platanus*, *Sequoia*, *Eucalyptus*, *Caesalpinia*, *Osmunda*, *Polypodium*, *Aralia*, and *Nipadites*. 

Geologists have used these plant remains to suggest that the Eocene period began with a temperate climate in northern latitudes. Evidence from the Bournemouth beds, Alum Bay beds, and London Clay suggests that temperatures later became at least subtropical. However, the presence of temperate forms mixed with tropical species makes it difficult to determine the exact degree of warmth.
