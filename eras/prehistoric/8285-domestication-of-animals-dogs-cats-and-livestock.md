# The Dawn of Partnership: Prehistoric Animal Domestication

## Overview
The transition from nomadic hunter-gatherer societies to settled agricultural communities was catalyzed by the domestication of animals. This process—the genetic modification of a species through selective breeding—transformed human survival strategies. While the domestication of livestock provided a stable source of calories and labor, the domestication of canines and felines offered symbiotic advantages in protection, hunting, and pest control. Historians and archaeologists view these shifts not as sudden events, but as gradual co-evolutionary processes.

## Key Facts

### The Canine Exception
Dogs were the first species domesticated, predating agriculture. There is significant scholarly debate regarding the exact timeline and origin; some genetic evidence suggests a single origin in Eurasia, while other theories propose multiple domestication events across different wolf populations. The relationship likely began as "commensalism," where less aggressive wolves scavenged around human camps, eventually evolving into active hunting partners.

### The Neolithic Revolution and Livestock
The domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle occurred primarily during the Neolithic period. In the Fertile Crescent, humans shifted from hunting wild ungulates to managing herds. This provided a "walking larder" of protein and, crucially, secondary products like milk and wool. The domestication of cattle was particularly transformative, as oxen provided the traction power necessary to plow larger fields, exponentially increasing food production.

### The Self-Domesticated Cat
Unlike dogs or cattle, cats are often described as having "self-domesticated." As early farmers stored grain, they attracted rodents. Wildcats (*Felis lybica*) moved into human settlements to prey on these rodents. Humans tolerated the cats for their utility, leading to a symbiotic relationship. Archaeological evidence from Cyprus (c. 7500 BCE) shows a human buried with a cat, suggesting an emotional bond developed early in the process.

## Did You Know?
* **Genetic Shifts:** Domestication often leads to "domestication syndrome," which includes physical changes like floppier ears, shorter snouts, and varied coat colors.
* **The Aurochs:** Modern domestic cattle are descendants of the Aurochs, a massive wild ox that is now extinct.
* **The Dog's Role:** Early dogs didn't just help with hunting; they provided warmth during the Ice Age and acted as early warning systems against predators.
