# The Steppes of the Kura

The steppes of the lower Kura and Aras are located south of the Caucasus within the government of Baku, a region of Russian Transcaucasia. This government stretches along the west coast of the Caspian Sea, from 38deg 30' to 41deg 50' N. lat., and is bordered by the province of Daghestan and the government of Elisavetpol to the west, and by Persia to the south.

## Geography and Land Use
The government of Baku covers an area of 15,172 sq. m. Its geography includes the Kuba plain on the north-east slope of the Caucasus and the Apsheron peninsula, where the Caucasus range terminates at the Caspian. The region also encompasses a narrow coast-belt between the Caspian and the Anti-Caucasus, which partially surrounds the south-opening Kizil-agach Bay.

The land varies significantly in fertility and vegetation. Both slopes of the Caucasus are well irrigated and very fertile, featuring flourishing gardens, fine forests, and fields of cereals and rice. In contrast, the Apsheron peninsula is bare of vegetation and dry. The steppes of the Kura are also fertile; however, they require artificial irrigation to be productive, a necessity that is especially critical for the cultivation of cotton. Beyond these specific crops, the region sees extensive growth of the mulberry and the vine, alongside activities in cattle-breeding and general agriculture.

## The Caspian Sea and Fisheries
The Kura is one of the large rivers that flow into the Caspian Sea, along with the Terek, Ural, and Volga. The mouths of these rivers are particularly rich in fish, contributing to the Caspian's status as a highly stocked inland sea. The most economically valuable species found here are the lobster, salmon, herring, and four species of sturgeon, the latter of which provide isinglass and great quantities of caviare. The total annual catch for the sea is valued at an average of one million sterling.

## Petroleum Industry of the Apsheron Peninsula
While the Apsheron peninsula lacks vegetation, it contains famous petroleum wells. These wells reach depths between 700 and 1700 ft. to yield crude naphtha. This naphtha is distilled to produce kerosene or petroleum, while the heavier residue, known as *mazut*, is utilized as fuel—such as for locomotives on the Transcaspian railway—and as lubricating oil.

The production of crude naphtha in the region saw a massive increase between 1863 and 1904, rising from 5,500 tons to 9,833,600 tons, although a serious fire in 1905 injured business operations. The oil fields are concentrated around the town of Baku:
* **Balakhany-Sabunchi-Romany**: The largest field (6 sq. m.), located 8-1/2 m. north of the town.
* **Bibi-Eybat**: Located 3-1/2 m. south of the town.
* **The "black town" (Nobel's)**: Located 2 m. south-east.
* **The "white town" (Rothschild's)**: Located beyond the "black town."

For export, lighter oils are transported via pipes to Batum on the Black Sea. Heavier oils are moved by tank railway-cars to the ports of Poti, Novorossiysk, and Batum.

## Cultural Sites
Located 13 m. east of the town of Baku at Surakhani is a temple of the Parsee fire-worshippers, which is now disused.
