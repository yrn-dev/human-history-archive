# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian point—we must shift our gaze from the finished tool to the origin of the stone. The evolution of Paleolithic technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in logistics and geological knowledge.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in how early hominins perceived their landscape. To create a tool, one must first possess the foresight to identify a suitable raw material, transport it over distance, and manage a finite resource.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used river cobbles found in their immediate vicinity. The "cost" of the tool was low, as the material was available underfoot. However, this limited the precision of the tools; if the local basalt was too coarse, the resulting edge was blunt.

By the time of the Acheulean (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), we see the emergence of "selective procurement." Hominins began bypassing mediocre stones to seek out specific materials, such as quartzite or flint, which offered more predictable fracturing patterns. This suggests a mental map of the environment and a willingness to invest energy in transport for the sake of quality.

## The Mousterian and the Economy of the Flake

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) represents a pinnacle of raw material efficiency. Through the Levallois technique, a core was carefully prepared so that a single, predetermined flake could be struck off. 

This shift was driven by necessity. As hominins moved into diverse environments, high-quality stone was not always available. The ability to extract the maximum amount of cutting edge from a single piece of stone reduced the need for frequent trips to distant quarries. Scholars continue to debate whether this efficiency was a conscious "economic" choice or a byproduct of evolving cognitive abilities regarding spatial planning.

## Key Facts: The Logistics of Stone
* **Transport Distance:** Some Acheulean handaxes have been found kilometers away from their geological source, indicating planned movement.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint and chert) were prized for their "conchoidal" fracture, allowing for razor-sharp edges.
* **Core Reduction:** The transition from Oldowan to Mousterian is characterized by a decrease in waste and an increase in the ratio of usable edge to raw material.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early humans had to distinguish between stones that would shatter unpredictably and those that would flake cleanly.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Curated" Tool:** Some Mousterian tools show signs of extensive re-sharpening, suggesting that a high-quality tool was treated as a valuable possession rather than a disposable item.
* **Natural Selection of Stone:** Not all stone is created equal; the specific molecular structure of flint allows it to break in a predictable wave, which is why it dominated European prehistoric toolkits.
* **The Weight of Knowledge:** The shift toward the Levallois technique required the knapper to "see" the finished tool inside the raw stone before the first strike was ever made.
