# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithics

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a change in shape, but a revolution in spatial awareness and resource management.

For the early hominin, a stone tool was only as effective as the mineral it was crafted from. The transition from opportunistic scavenging of river cobbles to the strategic mining of high-quality raw materials reveals a profound shift in prehistoric intelligence and social organization.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever basalt or quartz was available in their immediate vicinity. The "transport distance" for these tools was minimal, suggesting a lifestyle tied closely to the immediate landscape.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, we see evidence of "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint or chert, which allow for more predictable fracturing (conchoidal fracture). This indicates that early humans possessed a "mental template" of what a high-quality material looked like before they even began knapping.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade
The Mousterian period (associated largely with Neanderthals) showcases a sophisticated understanding of geology. Archaeological sites reveal that raw materials were often transported over dozens, and sometimes hundreds, of kilometers from their original source.

This movement of stone implies two possibilities that historians and anthropologists continue to debate:
1. **Planned Mobility:** Hominins mapped their territories and returned to specific quarries seasonally.
2. **Early Exchange:** The presence of non-local stone suggests a rudimentary form of trade or social gifting between different kinship groups.

The shift from using "whatever is here" to "bringing the best material from there" marks the birth of prehistoric logistics. It suggests a capacity for future planning—the ability to envision a tool needed tomorrow and secure the materials for it today.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way materials like flint break, allowing toolmakers to create sharp, curved edges.
* **Transport Distance:** The measurement of how far a finished tool or raw nodule traveled from its geological source.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of identifying, extracting, and transporting stone for tool production.
* **Material Preference:** The shift from generic river cobbles (Oldowan) to specific high-grade minerals (Mousterian).
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The ability of prehistoric humans to remember the location of distant resource patches.

## Did You Know?
* **Curated Tools:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after use, Mousterian tools were often "curated," meaning they were resharpened and carried for long periods.
* **The Flint Advantage:** Flint is prized because it is chemically stable and creates an edge that can be thinner than a modern surgical scalpel.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use "trace element analysis" to match a stone tool found in a cave to a specific quarry miles away, proving prehistoric travel patterns.
