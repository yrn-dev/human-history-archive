# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical intelligence required to create them. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in the understanding of geology and spatial planning.

The ability to identify, extract, and transport high-quality raw materials reveals a complex relationship between prehistoric hominins and their landscape, shifting from opportunistic scavenging to strategic resource management.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was primarily opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, such as riverbeds. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was within a few hundred meters, it was used; if not, the tool-making event simply did not occur.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, a distinct shift in "raw material economy" emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—located kilometers away. This indicates a mental map of the environment and the ability to plan for future needs, a trait known as "curation."

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the approach to stone had become highly specialized. The introduction of the Levallois technique required a specific type of stone that could withstand precise pressure and predictable fracturing.

This necessity drove a sophisticated transport system. Archaeologists have found "caches" of raw nodules far from their natural geological sources, suggesting that hominins were not just wandering, but were intentionally stocking materials at strategic campsites. There is an ongoing scholarly debate regarding whether these materials were traded between different bands of hominins or if individual groups maintained exclusive "quarry" territories, but the evidence of long-distance transport is undeniable.

## The Cognitive Cost of Quality

The transition from Oldowan to Mousterian reflects a growing investment in "energy expenditure." To travel ten kilometers for a piece of high-grade flint rather than using a low-grade river stone requires a cognitive calculation: the energy spent traveling must be outweighed by the efficiency of the resulting tool. This transition marks the birth of prehistoric logistics.

### Key Facts: Lithic Sourcing
* **Oldowan Range:** Raw materials were typically sourced within 1–2 km of the site.
* **Acheulean Expansion:** Evidence shows materials being transported over 10–20 km.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) were preferred for their predictable "conchoidal" fracture patterns.
* **Curation:** The practice of keeping a tool or raw stone for long-term use rather than discarding it immediately.
* **Quarrying:** The shift from gathering surface stones to intentionally mining nodules from the earth.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late prehistoric contexts, obsidian has been found hundreds of kilometers from its volcanic source, suggesting early networks of exchange.
* **The "Core" Logic:** In the Levallois technique, the "core" was prepared so carefully that the final flake could be struck off with a single, precise blow.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians identify "knapping floors" by looking for "debitage"—the tiny, useless shards of stone left behind during the shaping process.
