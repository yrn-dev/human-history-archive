# The Arteries of Antiquity: Bronze Age Trade Networks

## Overview
The Bronze Age (roughly 3300–1200 BCE) marked a pivotal shift in human organization, transitioning from localized Neolithic farming to complex, interconnected societies. The catalyst for this change was metallurgy. Because bronze is an alloy of copper and tin—two metals rarely found in the same geographic location—the demand for these resources necessitated the creation of vast, long-distance trade networks.

These networks stretched from the tin mines of Cornwall in Britain and the Erzgebirge in Central Europe to the copper deposits of Cyprus and the Caucasus. In Asia, the interaction between the Indus Valley Civilization and Mesopotamia created a maritime and overland corridor that exchanged lapis lazuli, carnelian, and metals. These routes were not merely economic; they were conduits for "prestige goods," ideas, and technological innovations, effectively shrinking the prehistoric world.

## Timeline of Key Developments

| Period | Approximate Dates | Key Development |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Early Bronze Age** | 3300 – 2000 BCE | Emergence of primary smelting; establishment of early copper routes in the Near East. |
| **Middle Bronze Age** | 2000 – 1600 BCE | Expansion of the "Tin Road"; rise of the Unetice culture in Central Europe. |
| **Late Bronze Age** | 1600 – 1200 BCE | Peak of Mediterranean maritime trade (Mycenaean/Hittite); the "Bronze Age Collapse." |

## Key Facts

### The Tin Dilemma
Copper was relatively abundant, but tin was the "strategic mineral" of the prehistoric world. Historians and archaeologists continue to debate the exact primary sources of tin used in early Europe. While Cornwall is a confirmed source, some scholars argue that much of the early tin may have come from Central Asia (modern Afghanistan/Uzbekistan) via a series of "down-the-line" exchanges, where goods passed through many intermediaries before reaching their final destination.

### The Amber Road
Trade was not limited to metals. The "Amber Road" connected the Baltic coast to the Mediterranean. Baltic amber, prized for its beauty and perceived magical properties, has been found in Mycenaean graves in Greece, proving that trade networks spanned the entire European continent long before the rise of Rome.

### The Indus-Mesopotamia Connection
In Asia, the Meluhha trade (as referred to in Akkadian texts) linked the Indus Valley to Mesopotamia. Cuneiform tablets record the import of ivory, gold, and exotic woods, indicating a sophisticated maritime system utilizing the Persian Gulf.

## Did You Know?
* **The Uluburun Shipwreck:** This Late Bronze Age wreck found off the coast of Turkey contained copper ingots from Cyprus, tin from Central Asia, and glass from Egypt, serving as a "time capsule" of prehistoric globalization.
* **Prestige Goods:** Many traded items, such as gold jewelry or ornate daggers, were not for general use but were used by emerging elites to signal status and power.
* **The Collapse:** Around 1200 BCE, many of these interconnected networks collapsed simultaneously—an event known as the "Late Bronze Age Collapse"—possibly due to a combination of climate change, migrations (the "Sea Peoples"), and systemic fragility.
