# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. The evolution of tool technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian industries reflects a growing sophistication in "lithic procurement." Early toolmakers were limited by proximity, but as cognitive abilities evolved, so did their willingness to travel and trade for superior stone.

## From Opportunism to Selectivity

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized whatever cobbles were available in nearby riverbeds or surface deposits. The "cost" of transporting heavy stones over long distances was too high, meaning the tool's quality was dictated by the local geology.

By the time of the Acheulean (the era of the handaxe), we see the first signs of selectivity. Hominins began to bypass mediocre stones in favor of materials like quartzite or fine-grained basalt that could hold a sharper edge. This indicates a mental "blueprint" not just for the finished tool, but for the ideal material required to achieve it.

## The Mousterian and the Logistics of Quality

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) represents a paradigm shift in resource management. The introduction of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality, predictable stone such as flint or obsidian.

Archaeological evidence suggests that Mousterian toolmakers were no longer tethered to their immediate surroundings. We find "exotic" materials in cave sites that originated dozens, and sometimes over a hundred, kilometers away. This suggests two possibilities: either a highly developed understanding of regional geography or the existence of early social networks involving the exchange of materials between groups.

## The Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among prehistoric archaeologists is whether the presence of non-local stone proves "trade." Some scholars argue that these materials were simply carried by migrating groups over long periods. Others contend that the selective clustering of high-quality flint suggests a deliberate "quarrying" economy, where specific sites were revisited across generations.

### Key Facts: Lithic Procurement
* **Oldowan:** Primarily opportunistic; materials sourced within a few hundred meters of the site.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity; preference for materials that allowed for symmetrical shaping.
* **Mousterian:** High demand for cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for precision flaking.
* **Transport Costs:** The distance raw materials traveled is used by historians to estimate the home range of prehistoric groups.
* **Material Bias:** The archaeological record is skewed toward durable stones; organic tools (wood/bone) rarely survive.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian breaks with a molecularly sharp edge, it was one of the most prized materials in the prehistoric world, often transported further than any other stone.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** In the Mousterian era, hominins would often carry a single large "core" of high-quality stone and strike small flakes off it as needed, acting as a portable toolkit.
* **Geological Knowledge:** To find flint or chert, prehistoric humans had to recognize specific geological strata, showing an intuitive understanding of earth science long before the field existed.
