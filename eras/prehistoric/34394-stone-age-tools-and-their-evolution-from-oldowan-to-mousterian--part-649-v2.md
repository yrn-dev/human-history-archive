# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in *sourcing*. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a progression of shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in spatial memory and planning depth.

## From Opportunism to Curation
In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized river cobbles found in the immediate vicinity of their habitation sites. The "cost" of transport was low, and the tools were often discarded shortly after use.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean (approx. 1.7 million to 100,000 years ago), a pattern of "curation" emerges. *Homo erectus* began selecting specific types of stone—such as quartzite or flint—that offered more predictable fracturing patterns. Archaeologists have discovered tool assemblages containing materials sourced from dozens of kilometers away, suggesting that these hominins were not just reacting to their environment, but actively mapping it.

## The Levallois Leap and Raw Material Quality
By the time the Mousterian industry emerged (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality, homogenous stone to be successful. 

This necessity drove a "raw material economy." Neanderthals did not just pick up the nearest rock; they sought out specific outcrops and, in some cases, transported cores over vast distances to ensure they had the correct material for their specialized tools. This indicates a level of foresight and social coordination previously underestimated in prehistoric studies.

## The Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Availability
A continuing debate among lithic specialists concerns whether the transport of high-quality stone was a conscious choice of "superior" material or simply a byproduct of migratory patterns. Some scholars argue that the presence of exotic stones at a site is merely a sign that the hominins passed through a quarry on their way to a camp. Others contend that the selective discarding of low-quality local stone in favor of transported materials proves a clear cognitive preference for efficiency and durability.

### Key Facts: Lithic Sourcing
*   **Oldowan:** Characterized by "local sourcing," utilizing materials found within a few hundred meters of the site.
*   **Acheulean:** Marked by the first significant evidence of transporting raw materials over several kilometers.
*   **Mousterian:** Utilized the Levallois technique, which demanded higher-grade stone for precise flake control.
*   **Material Preference:** Flint, chert, obsidian, and quartzite were the most prized materials due to their conchoidal fracture properties.
*   **Cognitive Load:** The shift from opportunistic to curated sourcing indicates an evolution in long-term planning and spatial mapping.

### Did You Know?
*   **Obsidian Trade:** In later prehistoric periods, obsidian (volcanic glass) was so highly valued for its sharpness that it created some of the earliest known "trade networks" across islands and continents.
*   **The "Core" Strategy:** Rather than carrying finished tools, many prehistoric humans carried "cores"—large chunks of raw stone—allowing them to manufacture a fresh edge whenever needed.
*   **Experimental Archaeology:** Modern historians often attempt to recreate these tools to determine exactly how much effort and time were required to source and shape specific stones.
