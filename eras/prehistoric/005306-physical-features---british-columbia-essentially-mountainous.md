# British Columbia

British Columbia is the western province of the Dominion of Canada. Its boundaries are defined by the Pacific Ocean to the west, where it fronts for approximately 600 miles, and the coast strip of Alaska for an additional 400 miles. To the north, the boundary is 60° N, and to the south, it is formed by 49° N and the strait that separates Vancouver Island from the state of Washington. The eastern boundary is the continental watershed in the Rocky Mountains, though this boundary follows 120° W north to 60° N in its north-westerly course, which incorporates a portion of the Peace river country to the east of the mountains. The province includes Vancouver Island, the Queen Charlotte Islands, and various smaller islands off the western coast of Canada.

## Topography and Mountain Systems

The province is essentially a mountainous country, comprising nearly the entire width of the North American Cordilleran belt (or Cordillera). The landscape consists of a series of parallel mountain ranges separated by long, narrow valleys. There are two primary ruling mountain systems: the Rocky Mountains on the north-east side and the Coast Range on the south-west or Pacific side. Between these two systems lie subordinate ranges and the "Interior Plateau," which is an elevated tract of hilly country with summits of an accordant altitude located east of the Coast Range.

These ranges were produced by successive foldings of the earth's crust parallel to the Pacific Ocean, resulting in a common trend that runs south-east and north-west. Vancouver Island and the Queen Charlotte Islands are remnants of another mountain range that runs parallel to the coast but is now almost entirely submerged. Additionally, the Cascade Mountains extend into British Columbia from the United States; while they are characterized by volcanic activity and imposing peaks in Oregon and Washington, there are no notable elevations in the British Columbia portion. The name "Cascades" is also applied to the river-dissected central mountain plateau that crosses the province from the south-east to the north-west.

## Geology and Glaciation

The Rocky Mountains are primarily composed of palaeozoic sediments from the Cambrian to the Carboniferous periods, with subordinate infolded Cretaceous areas containing coal. The average height of the range along the United States boundary is 8,000 feet. The highest peak in the Canadian Rockies is Mount Robson at 13,700 feet, though the highest peak in the province is Mount Fairweather, located on the International Boundary, reaching 15,287 feet. Other significant peaks include Columbia (12,740 ft.), Forbes (12,075 ft.), Assiniboine (11,860 ft.), Bryce (11,686 ft.), Temple (11,626 ft.), and Lyell (11,463 ft.).

During an early stage of the Glacial period, the province was covered by the Cordilleran glacier. This glacier moved north-westwards and south-eastwards from a gathering-ground near the 57th parallel. Ice poured through passes in the coast ranges and, to a lesser extent, reached the edge of the great plains beyond the Rocky Mountains. A separate glacier occupied the great valley between Vancouver Island and the coast ranges, moving in both directions from a central point near Valdez Island. These glacial actions, along with preceding erosion, have significantly influenced the character and distribution of the province's gold-bearing alluviums.

## Hydrography

A remarkable valley of considerable geological antiquity exists on the south-western side of the Rocky Mountains. This valley, ranging from 1 to 6 miles in width and extending at least 800 miles, contains the upper courses of seven great rivers of the Pacific slope, including the Finlay, Fraser, Columbia, and Kootenay.

The Fraser River rises in the Rocky Mountains, flows north-west, turns south, and crosses the Coast Range via a deep canton-like valley before emptying into the Strait of Georgia south of Vancouver. The Columbia River also rises in the Rocky Mountains; it flows north for about 150 miles, bends abruptly south, and passes through the Arrow Lakes in the Kootenay country into the United States, eventually emptying into the Pacific at Astoria, Oregon. In the north-western region, the Skeena flows south-west into the Pacific, while the Stikine rises in the province and crosses the Alaskan coast strip before reaching the Pacific. The Liard rises in the same district and flows east into the Mackenzie, which empties into the Arctic Ocean. The headwaters of the Yukon are also located in the northern part of the province. These rivers are generally swift and interrupted by rapids, making them of indifferent value for commercial communication.

## Infrastructure and Transport

The Canadian Pacific railway's Pacific division enters the province through the Rocky Mountains in the east and runs approximately 500 miles to its terminus at Vancouver. Another branch leaves the main line at Medicine Hat, crosses the Rocky Mountains via the Crow's Nest Pass, and runs parallel to the United States boundary to Midway in the Boundary district, opening up productive mining districts and coal fields. On Vancouver Island, the Victoria & Sydney railway (16 m.) and the Esquimalt & Nanaimo railway (78 m.) connect coal fields with southern ports. The Great Northern railway also operates short lines in the southern province. As of 1905, the province had 1,627 miles of railway, with 1,187 miles owned or controlled by the Canadian Pacific railway.

The Rocky Mountains are traversed by several passes, including:
* Peace River Pass (2,000 ft.)
* Pine River Pass (2,850 ft.)
* Yellow Head Pass (3,733 ft.)
* Kicking Horse or Wapta Pass (5,300 ft.)
* Crow's Nest Pass (5,500 ft.)
* Athabasca Pass (6,025 ft.)
* Boundary or South Kootenay Pass (7,100 ft.)
