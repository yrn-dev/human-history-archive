# Plant Remains and Geological Formations of the Sands and Clays

## Plant Remains of Alum Bay and Bournemouth
The leaf-bearing clays found at Bournemouth and Alum Bay are noted for yielding a large and interesting series of plant remains. These include various species such as *Polypodium*, *Osmunda*, *Nipadites*, *Eucalyptus*, *Caesalpinia*, *Populus*, *Platanus*, *Sequoia*, and *Aralia*.

## The Bagshot Beds
The Bagshot beds are a series of clays and sands of shallow-water origin, comprising both marine and fresh-water deposits. Located in the Hampshire and London basins of England, they are named after Bagshot Heath in Surrey. These beds are divided into three general sections:

*   **Upper Bagshot Beds:** This division consists of the Barton clay and Barton sand.
*   **Middle Bagshot Beds:** This section comprises the Bracklesham beds.
*   **Lower Bagshot Beds:** This division includes the Bovey Tracey beds, the Alum Bay beds, and the Bournemouth beds.

In the Isle of Wight, the Upper Bagshot beds (Barton sand and Barton clay) reach thicknesses of 140 to 200 feet. The Bracklesham beds vary in thickness from 20 to 50 feet in Berkshire and Surrey, while in Alum Bay they reach 100 feet and contain beds of lignite in their lower portion. In Alum Bay, the Bracklesham beds are sharply separated from the Barton clay by a bed of conglomerate consisting of flint pebbles.

## The Barton Beds
The Barton beds are Upper Eocene softish brown and grey clays with layers of sand. They are found in the Hampshire Tertiary basin, with significant exposures in the Isle of Wight and the cliffs of Hordwell and Barton. The series is divided into the highly fossiliferous Barton Clay (162-255 feet) and the overlying Barton Sands (also called Headon Hill sands), which are 140-200 feet thick and contain few fossils. These beds are absent from the London basin. The term "Bartonien" is used to describe the continental equivalents of this series, such as the Sands of Beauchamp and the Limestone of Saint-Ouen.

## Industrial Uses of Clays and Sands
Various clays and sands from these formations are utilized for industrial purposes:

*   **Pottery:** Clays from the Bovey beds and the vicinity of Corfe and Wareham are used for pottery manufacture. Those from the latter area are shipped from Poole and are known as "Poole clays," while those from the Bovey beds are called "Teignmouth clay."
*   **Fuel and Minerals:** Lignites found at Bovey and near Corfe have been used as fuel. Alum was previously extracted from the clays of Alum Bay.
*   **Glass and Ore:** Sands from the Lower Greensand are used in glass manufacture and are dug at Godstone, Aylesford, Hartshill (near Aylesbury), and near Reigate. Ferruginous sand at Seend is worked as an iron ore.
*   **Construction:** Brick clays are widely distributed; their strength is generally linked to the percentage of hydrated aluminium silicates. The color of the resulting brick—ranging from light buff to chocolate—depends on the presence of oxide of iron (typically 2% to 10%).

## Mortar and Sand Quality
In the mixing of mortar, the type of sand used is critical. Pit sand is considered the best because it is angular and sharp, though it must be screened for gravel and washed to remove earth or clay. River sand is less effective because attrition rubs the particles smooth. Sea sand is avoided because it contains a saline taint that attracts moisture, keeping brickwork damp, and its particles are rounded by the movement of the sea. Substitutes for ordinary sand include fine stone grit, cinders, clinkers, old bricks, or thoroughly burnt ballast ground to a uniform size. Blue or black mortar, used for durable external pointing, utilizes smith's ashes or foundry sand.

## Other Regional Formations
In the Isle of Wight, other notable beds include the Atherfield Clay (20-90 feet), the Hythe Beds (80-300 feet), the Sandgate Beds (75-100 feet), and the Folkestone Beds (70-100 feet). In the London basin, the London Clay is a marine deposit 500 feet thick, consisting of brown or blue clay with sandy layers and septarian nodules. Its equivalent in the Hampshire area is known as the Bognor Clay. Additionally, the Oldhaven beds (or Blackheath pebbles) consist of well-rounded flint pebbles set in sand.
