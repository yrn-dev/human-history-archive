# The Silent Sentinels: Megalithic Cultures of the Prehistoric World

## Overview
Megalithic architecture—derived from the Greek *megas* (great) and *lithos* (stone)—represents one of the most ambitious engineering feats of prehistoric humanity. Emerging primarily during the Neolithic and Early Bronze Age, these monuments are found across Europe, North Africa, and parts of Asia. While Stonehenge in England is the most iconic, it is part of a global phenomenon of "monumentalism" where early agrarian societies invested immense communal labor into permanent stone structures.

Historians and archaeologists generally categorize these structures into three types: **menhirs** (single standing stones), **dolmens** (portal tombs), and **cromlechs** (stone circles). The primary purpose of these sites remains a subject of intense scholarly debate. While the alignment of many sites with solar and lunar events suggests an astronomical or calendrical function, others argue they served as territorial markers, ancestral shrines, or sites for complex funerary rites.

## Key Facts

### The Engineering of Stonehenge
Stonehenge was not built in a single event but evolved over 1,500 years. The earliest phase (c. 3000 BCE) was a simple earthwork enclosure. The iconic sarsen stones (sandstone) and smaller bluestones were added later. A point of significant historical interest is the origin of the bluestones; geological analysis confirms they originated from the Preseli Hills in Wales, over 140 miles away, raising questions about whether the stones were transported by land, sea, or if the people migrated with them.

### Global Parallels
The megalithic impulse was not limited to Britain. In France, the **Carnac stones** consist of thousands of aligned menhirs, suggesting a scale of organization that rivals any prehistoric society. In Malta, the **Ġgantija temples** predate the pyramids of Egypt, showcasing sophisticated dry-stone masonry used for religious purposes.

### The Debate on Purpose
The "Archaeoastronomy" school of thought, championed by researchers like Alexander Thom, argues that these sites were precise observatories. However, more recent interpretations suggest a "social" function: the act of building the monument was perhaps more important than the finished product, serving as a mechanism for social cohesion and the assertion of power by emerging elites.

## Did You Know?
* **The Heel Stone:** At Stonehenge, the Heel Stone is positioned so that on the summer solstice, the sun rises roughly behind it, casting a beam of light into the center of the circle.
* **Not Just Stone:** Many "stone" circles actually began as wooden henges; archaeologists have found "post-holes" indicating that timber circles often preceded the stone versions.
* **Weighty Logistics:** Some of the largest sarsen stones at Stonehenge weigh approximately 25 tons, requiring hundreds of people and complex sled-and-roller systems to move.
