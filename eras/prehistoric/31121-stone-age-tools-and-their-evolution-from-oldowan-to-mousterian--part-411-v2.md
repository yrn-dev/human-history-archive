# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithics

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the *logistics* behind the stone. The evolution of prehistoric toolkits was not merely a leap in cognitive ability or manual dexterity; it was a revolution in how early hominins interacted with their geological landscape.

The transition from the opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and planning.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized materials found in the immediate vicinity, such as river cobbles. The "cost" of transport was low, and the tools were often discarded shortly after use. The focus was on immediate utility rather than long-term curation.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, a pattern of "raw material selectivity" emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials—such as obsidian, flint, or fine-grained quartzite—located kilometers away. This suggests the development of a "mental map" of the landscape, where specific geological outcrops were remembered and revisited over generations.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly sophisticated. The implementation of the Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a predictable, pre-shaped flake—required a specific grade of stone to be successful. 

This necessity drove a primitive "economy of stone." We see evidence of curated toolkits: tools were no longer just made and dropped; they were sharpened, repurposed, and carried over long distances. This transport indicates a shift in cognitive processing known as "future-oriented planning." The tool-maker was no longer reacting to a present need but anticipating a future requirement.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?

A point of ongoing debate among paleoanthropologists is whether the presence of "exotic" stones (materials not native to the site) proves the existence of early trade networks between different hominin groups, or if it simply indicates the wide migratory range of a single group. While some argue that the movement of high-quality flint suggests a social exchange, others maintain that it is more likely a result of seasonal nomadic movements.

### Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, utilizing local riverbed cobbles.
* **Material Selectivity:** The preference for cryptocrystalline rocks (like chert) due to their predictable fracturing patterns.
* **Transport Distance:** Evidence shows Mousterian materials were sometimes transported over 10–20 kilometers.
* **Curation:** The practice of retouching a tool to extend its life, reducing the need for new raw materials.
* **The Levallois Requirement:** A high-precision technique that demanded specific stone quality to prevent the core from shattering.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Value:** Because obsidian fractures into an edge thinner than a steel scalpel, it was one of the most prized materials in the prehistoric world.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" (the parent stone) was often treated as a precious resource, carefully shaped to maximize the number of flakes produced.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early hominins essentially functioned as amateur geologists, identifying specific strata in the earth to find the best knapping stone.
