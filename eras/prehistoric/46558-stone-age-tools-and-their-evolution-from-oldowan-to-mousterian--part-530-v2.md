# The Invisible Hand: Raw Material Procurement and the Logistics of Lithics

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the shape of the Oldowan chopper or the symmetry of the Mousterian point—we must shift our gaze from the finished product to the origin. The evolution of Stone Age technology was not merely a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in logistics. The transition from Oldowan to Mousterian industries is, in many ways, a history of how prehistoric hominins perceived and navigated their landscape.

The ability to select, transport, and curate stone is as significant an evolutionary marker as the knapping technique itself. To understand the tool is to understand the journey the stone took before it ever met a hammerstone.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically utilized "river cobbles"—stones already rounded and weathered by water—found in the immediate vicinity of their habitation sites. The "transport distance" was minimal; if a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used.

As we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis* and early *Homo sapiens*), a profound shift occurred. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials located kilometers away. This indicates a sophisticated mental map of the environment and a capacity for forward planning. They were no longer just reacting to their surroundings; they were sourcing materials based on the specific mechanical properties required for the intended tool.

## The Economics of the Core

The Mousterian industry introduced the Levallois technique, a method of preparing a stone core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape. This required a much higher grade of raw material—often flint or obsidian—which is not available in every geological setting.

This created a "lithic economy." Hominins had to weigh the caloric cost of traveling to a distant quarry against the efficiency of a superior tool. Scholars continue to debate whether these materials were transported by individuals over long distances or traded between social groups. Regardless, the presence of "exotic" stones far from their geological source proves that the Mousterian mind operated on a timeline of days or weeks, rather than the immediate "now" of the Oldowan.

## Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, relying on local riverine deposits.
* **Material Preference:** Transitioned from general volcanic rocks to high-silica stones like flint, chert, and obsidian.
* **Transport Distance:** Increased from a few hundred meters in early eras to tens of kilometers in the Middle Paleolithic.
* **Curation:** Mousterian tools show evidence of "resharpening," indicating that high-quality stone was valued and preserved.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** Sourcing distant materials suggests the development of long-term memory and spatial navigation.

## Did You Know?
* **The Obsidian Trail:** Some Middle Paleolithic sites contain obsidian that traveled over 100 kilometers from its volcanic source, suggesting highly organized movement or early exchange networks.
* **Selective Vision:** Hominins didn't just look for "stone"; they looked for *conchoidal fracture* patterns, meaning they understood the physics of how glass-like stones break.
* **Waste Analysis:** Archaeologists study "debitage" (the waste flakes left behind) to determine if a tool was made on-site or brought in as a finished product.
