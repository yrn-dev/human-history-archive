# THE PREHISTORIC SOVEREIGNS

The prehistoric era of Japan is defined as the first ten and a half centuries covered by the nation's annals. This period begins with the accession of Emperor Jimmu in 660 B.C. and terminates with the accession of Richu. The primary sources for this era are the *Kojiki* (Records) and the *Nihon Shoki* (Written Chronicles of Japan), the latter of which was a revision of the *Kana Nihongi* completed in 720 by Prince Toneri and Ono Yasumaro.

## Nature of the Historical Records
The annals of the prehistoric sovereigns are characterized by a confused chronology. For example, the *Nihon Shoki* records that Emperor Chuai died at fifty-two, yet based on the timing of his child's birth, he would have been over one hundred years old. Similarly, Emperor Keiko is described as ascending the throne in his eighty-third year, yet he later took a consort who bore him thirteen children.

Despite these chronological discrepancies and the presence of marvelous legends—such as the claim that a child's birth was artificially delayed until an empress returned from a Korean expedition—the records are not viewed as deliberate fabrications. While the *Nihon Shoki* utilizes Chinese history to develop scholarly diction and ethical codes for Imperial figures, the *Kojiki* shows no attempt to minimize evil or magnify the good.

## Social Organization and Governance
In prehistoric Japan, lineage was the primary determinant of social and official status. Lands, honors, and offices were held by the lineal descendants of the original Yamato chiefs. A hierarchy of esteem existed where the sovereign sat at the apex, followed by the *o-muraji* and *o-omi*, then the *muraji* and *omi*, and finally the *tomo-no-miyatsuko* and *kuni-no-miyatsuko*.

The sovereign's role was defined by the distinction between *ushi-haku* (holding in intimate lordship) and *shirasu* (exercising public rights as head of a State). While the Emperor held intimate lordship over confiscated estates and naturalized or conquered folks, his general function regarding the land and people was to govern (*shirasu*). The prerogatives of the sovereign included:
1. Acting as the general head of all *uji* to conduct the worship of national deities.
2. Representing the *uji* in declaring war or making peace with foreign nations.
3. Adjudicating disputes between *uji*, nominating *uji no Kami*, and establishing or abolishing *uji*.

## Notable Prehistoric Sovereigns
Several sovereigns are highlighted in the annals for their specific contributions or characteristics:

**Emperor Keiko:** Described in the Records as being ten feet two inches high, Keiko was nominated as Prince Imperial over his elder brother because he desired "the empire" rather than "a bow and arrows." He is credited with the first systematization of providing for Imperial scions. While predecessors had given sons local titles and estates, Keiko required his sons (with three exceptions) to leave the capital to serve as district or provincial viceroys, designating them as *wake*, or branch families.

**Emperor Ojin:** The fifteenth sovereign, Ojin, reportedly ascended the throne at age seventy and reigned for forty years. He had many children (twenty) and consorts (three), including two of the Emperor's younger sisters. Comparison with Korean history suggests his reign may be antedated by 120 years.

## Position of Women and Social Norms
Evidence from the prehistoric age indicates that women held a high position and their rights were widely recognized. This is evidenced by the fact that a goddess held the first place in the Japanese pantheon and Empresses frequently occupied the throne. Women also served as chiefs of tribes and led armies on campaign. In domestic life, mothers often had complete charge of their children's upbringing and chose their names, and wives were known to turn their backs on faithless husbands. However, these social standards did not restrict extra-marital relations for men. Additionally, the institution of slavery existed among the Yamato.

## Transition to the Historic Era
The prehistoric era ended with the accession of Richu, after which the reigns of sovereigns ceased to extend to "incredible lengths." The five sovereigns following Richu inherited a period of fifty-nine years characterized by a firmly established throne, a well-furnished treasury, peace, and satisfactory foreign relations. However, these comfortable conditions led to a decline in discipline, resulting in fratricide among princes due to quarrels over women and the only instance in Japanese history where the life of an Emperor was sacrificed.
