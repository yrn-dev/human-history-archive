# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous entries in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution of toolkits was not merely a matter of manual dexterity, but a revolution in spatial awareness and resource management.

For the early hominin, a stone tool was only as effective as the mineral it was crafted from. The shift from opportunistic scavenging of local river cobbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality materials marks a critical turning point in prehistoric cognitive evolution.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan assemblages (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally utilized whatever materials were immediately available in their vicinity. If a riverbed provided basalt or quartz, that is what was used. The "transport distance" was minimal, suggesting a localized, immediate-need approach to survival.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian traditions, we see a marked increase in "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the predictable, razor-sharp edges necessary for the complex flaking seen in Neanderthal toolkits.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade
The Mousterian era (approx. 160,000 to 40,000 years ago) reveals a sophisticated understanding of geography. Archaeologists have discovered tool fragments composed of materials that originated dozens, and sometimes over a hundred, kilometers away from the site of discovery.

This suggests two possibilities that remain subjects of scholarly debate: either Neanderthals engaged in long-distance seasonal migrations, carrying "curated" cores with them, or they established primitive exchange networks with other groups. The act of transporting a heavy stone core over great distances implies *foresight*—the ability to plan for a future need rather than reacting to a present one.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break, creating a curved, glass-like surface essential for sharp edges.
* **Curated Tools:** Tools designed for long-term use and transport, as opposed to "expedient tools" made and discarded on the spot.
* **Raw Material Bias:** The tendency for the archaeological record to over-represent durable stones while losing evidence of tools made from wood or bone.
* **Lithic Procurement:** The process of identifying, extracting, and transporting stone from a source to a workshop.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Obsession:** Because obsidian is volcanic glass, it can be flaked to an edge thinner than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In Mousterian technology, the "core" was treated as a precious resource; toolmakers carefully prepared the stone's shape to maximize the number of flakes they could extract.
* **Geological Fingerprinting:** Modern historians use "X-ray fluorescence" to trace the chemical signature of a tool back to the exact quarry where the stone originated.
