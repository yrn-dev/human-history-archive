# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude choppers of the Oldowan to the sophisticated flakes of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and transport.

The shift from the Oldowan to the Mousterian represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding how early humans selected and moved stone provides a window into their spatial awareness and social organization.

## From Localism to Long-Distance Transport

In the Oldowan industry (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was characterized by extreme localism. *Homo habilis* typically utilized whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often within a few hundred meters of the site. The "cost" of transporting heavy raw materials outweighed the benefit of seeking a superior stone grade.

However, as we move into the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian periods, we see a marked increase in "exotic" materials. Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, or obsidian. The presence of these materials kilometers away from their natural source suggests a sophisticated mental map of the landscape and a willingness to invest energy in procurement for the sake of tool longevity and sharpness.

## The Cognitive Shift: Planning and Curation

The Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals) introduced the Levallois technique, which required a highly specific type of stone that could be predictably flaked. This necessity drove a shift toward "curation." Rather than discarding a tool once a few edges were dull, Mousterian tool-users often reshaped and recycled their implements.

This transition suggests a shift in temporal perception. An Oldowan tool was often a "disposable" item created for an immediate task. A Mousterian tool, sourced from a distant quarry and carefully maintained, represents a future-oriented strategy. The hominin was no longer reacting to the environment; they were preparing for it.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Drift

There remains a scholarly debate regarding whether the transport of high-quality stone was a conscious "trade" or "planned expedition" or simply a byproduct of wider migratory patterns. Some archaeologists argue that the movement of stone reflects early social networks and exchange between groups, while others maintain it was merely a result of hominins carrying "core" stones with them as they wandered.

### Key Facts
* **Oldowan Sourcing:** Primarily opportunistic, utilizing local river cobbles and basalt.
* **Material Preference:** High-quality flint and obsidian were prized for their predictable conchoidal fracture.
* **Transport Distance:** Increased from a few hundred meters (Oldowan) to several dozen kilometers (Mousterian).
* **The Levallois Influence:** The demand for specific stone properties drove the strategic selection of raw materials.
* **Curation:** The practice of sharpening and reusing tools to extend the life of transported stone.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian's Edge:** Obsidian can be knapped to an edge significantly sharper than a modern steel surgical scalpel.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans demonstrated an intuitive understanding of geology, identifying "seams" of flint buried beneath soil.
* **Weight Management:** To reduce transport costs, hominins often "pre-formed" cores at the quarry, removing excess waste before carrying the stone to their camp.
