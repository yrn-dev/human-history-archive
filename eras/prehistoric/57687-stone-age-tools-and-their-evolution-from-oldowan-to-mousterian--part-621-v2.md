# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how a chopper differs from a Levallois flake—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a fundamental shift in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

The transition from opportunistic scavenging of materials to strategic quarrying reveals a growing capacity for foresight and spatial mapping.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), *Homo habilis* generally utilized "river cobbles"—stones found in immediate proximity to the site of use. The selection process was rudimentary; as long as the stone was hard enough to fracture (such as basalt or quartzite), it was sufficient. The "transport distance" for these tools was typically negligible, suggesting a localized, immediate-need economy.

As we move into the Acheulean era, the behavior of *Homo erectus* shifted. We begin to see evidence of selective sourcing. Hominins were no longer just picking up the nearest rock; they were seeking specific materials that yielded sharper edges or more predictable flaking patterns. This implies a mental map of the environment, where the toolmaker remembered the location of a high-quality outcrop several kilometers away.

## The Mousterian Logistical Leap
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials became highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique required high-quality flint or chert to produce the precise, predetermined flakes that characterized the era. 

Archaeological evidence shows that Mousterian toolmakers frequently transported raw materials over significant distances—sometimes exceeding 10 to 20 kilometers. This suggests a "curated" technology. Rather than making a tool and discarding it, Neanderthals invested time in transporting a high-quality core, retouching it multiple times, and carrying it across the landscape. This transition from "expedient" tools to "curated" toolkits marks a pivotal evolution in cognitive planning and social organization.

## Scholarly Debate: Trade or Migration?
A point of ongoing scholarly debate centers on the presence of "exotic" stones found far from their geological source. Some archaeologists argue this is evidence of early trade networks between disparate hominin groups. Others suggest it is simply the result of high mobility, where groups carried their preferred stones with them as they migrated seasonally.

### Key Facts: Material Evolution
* **Oldowan:** Primarily utilized local river cobbles; minimal transport distance.
* **Acheulean:** Increased selectivity for materials like quartzite and flint; evidence of targeted sourcing.
* **Mousterian:** High reliance on cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) for precision flaking.
* **Curated Technology:** The shift from making tools on-the-spot to carrying a "toolkit" for future use.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The ability to recall and navigate to specific geological sites indicates advanced spatial memory.

### Did You Know?
* **The Flint Preference:** Flint was prized because it has a "conchoidal" fracture, meaning it breaks like glass, creating an edge thinner than a surgical scalpel.
* **Recycling Centers:** Some Mousterian sites show evidence of "tool recycling," where old, blunt tools were reshaped into new ones to save on the cost of transporting new stone.
* **Material Quality:** Not all stone is created equal; the transition to more complex tools was often limited by the geological availability of high-quality stone in a specific region.
