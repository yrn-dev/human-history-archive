# Coelom and Pore-canals

In certain biological organisms, the coelom (also referred to as the body-cavity or perivisceral cavity) exhibits a structural organization that corresponds with the tri-regional differentiation of the body's external configuration. This organization is characterized by the division of the coelom into three distinct portions, which are completely separated from one another by septa.

## Divisions of the Coelom
The three portions of the coelom are categorized as follows:
1. **Proboscis-coelom**: The first body-cavity.
2. **Collar-coelom**: The second body-cavity.
3. **Truncal coelom**: The third body-cavity.

This subdivision of the coelom is mirrored by the structure of the enteron (the digestive tube or alimentary canal), which also shows three main subsections: the proboscis-gut (stomochord), the collar-gut (consisting of the throat and buccal cavity), and the truncal gut, which extends from the collar to the vent.

## The Pore-canals
The first two divisions of the coelom—the proboscis and collar coeloms—communicate with the exterior via a pair of ciliated pore-canals. These canals are located at the posterior end of their respective segments.

### Proboscis-pores
The proboscis-pores exhibit high variability. In many instances, only one pore is present, located on the left side. In other cases, the pore-canals may unite to open through a common median orifice, or their communication with the proboscis-coelom may appear to be occluded. In some instances, these pore-canals may be entirely vestigial.

### Collar-pores
Unlike the proboscis-pores, the collar-pores are remarkable for their constancy. This stability is attributed to their adaptation for a specific function: the inhalation of water, which renders the collar turgid during progression.

### Truncal Coelom and Vestiges
While the truncal coelom is not typically characterized by these structures, there are reasons to suppose it once possessed pore-canals. Vestiges of such structures have been described only for one genus, *Spengelia*, where they are located near the anterior end of the truncal coelom.

## Excretory and Vascular Functions
It is likely that coelomic pore-canals originally served as excretory organs. However, in existing Enteropneusta, these canals have either become vestigial or acquired new functions. In these organisms, excretion is primarily handled by the glomerulus—a vascular complex projecting into the proboscis-coelom and positioned on either side of the anterior portion of the stomochord.

The vascular system of these organisms consists of channels and lacunae without endothelium, located within the mesenteries, the gut-wall, and the basement-membrane of the body-wall. Blood, a non-corpuscular fluid, is propelled forward by a contractile dorsal vessel and collected in the central blood-sinus. This sinus lies over the stomochord and is surrounded on three sides by the pericardium (a closed vesicle with contractile walls). The pulsation of the pericardial vesicle drives blood into the glomerulus, from which it moves through efferent vessels to join the ventral (sub-intestinal) vessel in the trunk.

## Comparative Coelomic Structures in Fishes
In Teleosts and other fishes, the coelom (specifically the splanchnocoel or general coelom) serves different functions. The ventral portion of the trunk mesoderm gives rise to the splanchnocoel. In most cases, except for Myxinoids, the anterior part of the splanchnocoel separates to become a pericardiac cavity. In adult Selachians, this separation is incomplete, and the two cavities communicate via a pericardio-peritoneal canal.

### Renal and Genital Systems
The kidney system in fishes, known as the archinephros or holonephros, consists of tubes leading from the coelom into a longitudinal duct. The coelomic end of these tubules often features an open ciliated funnel called a nephrostome, which allows coelomic fluid to flush the tubule. In the mesonephros, the portion of the coelom surrounding the glomerulus may be nipped off from the general coelom to form a Malpighian body, sometimes remaining connected by a narrow peritoneal canal known as a peritoneal funnel.

Regarding reproduction, male reproductive elements were primitively shed into the coelom and passed through nephridial tubules. In existing fishes, they are generally conveyed from the testis through vasa efferentia (covered-in canals) to the kidney tubules or Malpighian bodies. In females, some Teleosts (such as the smelt *Osmerus* and capelin *Mallotus*) shed eggs into the splanchnocoel via oviducts resembling Müllerian ducts. Additionally, some adult fishes possess abdominal pores that lead directly from the coelom to the exterior near the cloacal opening; these are considered recent developments unrelated to the genitourinary system.
