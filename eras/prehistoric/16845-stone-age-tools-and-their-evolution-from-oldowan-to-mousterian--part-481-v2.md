# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistical challenge of the raw materials themselves. A tool is only as effective as the stone from which it is knapped. For prehistoric hominins, the evolution of toolkits was not just a cognitive leap in design, but a revolution in geological prospecting and resource management.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian industries represents a transition from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding how early humans selected, transported, and curated their stone reveals a great deal about their social organization and mental mapping of the landscape.

## From Local Cobbles to Curated Cores

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmaking was primarily a local affair. Hominins typically utilized river cobbles or surface stones found in the immediate vicinity of their camp. If a suitable piece of basalt or quartz was available within a few hundred meters, it was used and discarded. There was little evidence of "planning" for the future; the tool was a response to an immediate need.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian (associated largely with *Homo neanderthalensis*), a pattern of "curation" emerges. Hominins began to bypass mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials like flint, chert, or obsidian, often transporting these materials over several kilometers. This suggests a sophisticated mental map of the environment and a capacity for delayed gratification—carrying a heavy core of stone today because they knew they would need a sharp edge tomorrow.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Revolution

The Mousterian industry introduced the Levallois technique, a method of preparing a core to strike off a flake of a predetermined size and shape. This technique demanded a higher grade of stone with predictable fracturing patterns (conchoidal fracture). 

Because high-quality flint is not evenly distributed across the landscape, Mousterian toolmakers developed complex procurement strategies. Some scholars debate whether this indicates the existence of early trade networks between different bands of hominins or simply a wider foraging range. Regardless, the presence of "exotic" stones—materials not native to the immediate site—proves that the evolution of the tool was inextricably linked to the evolution of mobility and territorial knowledge.

## Key Facts
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like flint or obsidian) break in smooth, curved shells, essential for creating sharp edges.
* **Opportunistic Sourcing:** The Oldowan practice of using whatever stone was closest to the point of need.
* **Curated Toolkits:** The practice of maintaining and transporting a "core" of stone to be used over a long period.
* **Levallois Technique:** A sophisticated Mousterian method requiring high-quality raw materials to produce standardized flakes.
* **Transport Costs:** The energy expenditure required to carry raw stone, which influenced the location of prehistoric campsites.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Obsession:** Because obsidian is volcanic glass and incredibly sharp, it is one of the most frequently transported materials in the prehistoric record, sometimes moving hundreds of kilometers.
* **Recycling:** In some Mousterian sites, evidence suggests that broken tools were "resharpened" or repurposed multiple times to maximize the utility of a rare piece of stone.
* **Geological Knowledge:** Early humans didn't have textbooks, but they developed an intuitive understanding of geology, knowing exactly which riverbeds or outcrops yielded the best knapping stone.
