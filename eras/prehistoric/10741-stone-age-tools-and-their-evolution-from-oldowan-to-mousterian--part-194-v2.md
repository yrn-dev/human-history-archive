# The Raw Material Economy: Lithic Procurement and Transport

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the simple flakes of the Oldowan to the sophisticated Levallois technique of the Mousterian—we have largely ignored the "where" and "how" of the stone itself. To understand the evolution of the Stone Age, we must look beyond the finished tool and examine the cognitive demands of raw material procurement.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in knapping skill; it was a revolution in spatial planning and economic foresight.

## From Opportunism to Strategic Sourcing

In the earliest Oldowan sites (roughly 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), *Homo habilis* and early *Homo erectus* generally practiced "opportunistic procurement." They utilized cobbles found in immediate proximity, often within a few hundred meters of the site. The cognitive load was low: find a suitable river stone, strike it, and use it.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian, a pattern of "selective procurement" emerges. Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of high-quality materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—located kilometers away. This suggests a mental map of the landscape and the ability to plan for future needs, a hallmark of increasing cognitive complexity.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly strategic. The Levallois technique required a specific type of stone with predictable fracturing patterns. If the local geology did not provide this, Neanderthals did not simply settle for inferior tools; they transported high-quality cores over significant distances.

This "curation" of tools represents a shift from a disposable economy to a maintained one. Instead of discarding a tool after one use, Mousterian tool-users frequently reshaped and retouched their implements, extending the life of the raw material. This indicates a sophisticated understanding of resource scarcity and a commitment to long-term utility.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Availability

There remains a spirited debate among archaeologists regarding the "cost-benefit" analysis of these early humans. Some scholars argue that the transport of high-quality stone was a primary driver of migration and social interaction. Others suggest that the apparent "selectivity" is an illusion created by the archaeological record, arguing that hominins used whatever was available, but only the high-quality tools survived the millennia of erosion.

### Key Facts: Lithic Procurement
* **Opportunistic Sourcing:** Typical of Oldowan tools, where materials were gathered from the immediate vicinity.
* **Selective Sourcing:** The practice of bypassing local stone for superior materials, common in later Acheulean and Mousterian eras.
* **Curation:** The process of retouching and maintaining a tool to extend its lifespan, reducing the need for new raw materials.
* **Transport Distance:** Evidence shows Mousterian materials were sometimes carried over 10–20 kilometers, indicating advanced spatial memory.
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline silicates (like flint) were prized for their predictable "conchoidal" fracture.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late prehistoric contexts, obsidian—a volcanic glass—was transported hundreds of miles, suggesting the earliest forms of trade networks.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" was treated as a precious resource to be managed, rather than just a block of stone to be broken.
* **Geological Literacy:** Early humans had to possess an intuitive understanding of geology to identify "nodules" of flint hidden inside limestone crusts.
