# Archaean

The term Archaean refers to a category of ancient crystalline and metamorphic rocks found in various regions across the globe, characterized by their antiquity and specific mineral compositions.

## Archaean Formations in British East Africa
In British East Africa, Archaean rocks are prevalent in the districts of Taru and Nandi, as well as throughout Ukamba. These formations are part of an ancient plateau that begins at Taru and extends to the borders of Kikuyu. This plateau consists of ancient crystalline rocks, upon which post-Jurassic to Recent volcanic rocks have accumulated to create the volcanic plateau of Central East Africa.

The predominant rock type in these Archaean districts is a coarse gneiss. This gneiss is associated with much intrusive granite and garnetiferous mica-schists. While beds of metamorphic limestone and hornblende schists are present, they are considered rare. Additionally, mylonites interbedded with cherty quartzites occur on the flanks of the Nandi hills, though the age of these specific formations is unknown. In general geological classifications for the region, the Archaean category includes both gneisses, schists, and granites, as well as the quartzites and schists of Nandi.

## Archaean Rocks in Other Regions
Evidence of Archaean or similarly categorized fundamental rocks appears in other geographical surveys:

*   **Ireland:** In the southern mass near Pettigo, certain rocks were once regarded as Archaean and fundamental. These areas contain gneiss in which residual "eyes" of hornblendic rocks, associated with the Dalradian series, remain floating.
*   **Norway:** The geologist Theodor Kjerulf (1825–1888) made numerous and important contributions to the geology of Norway. His work specifically addressed the structure and relations of the Palaeozoic and Archaean rocks, as well as glacial phenomena, particularly in the southern portion of the country. His findings were detailed in his 1879 work, *Udsigt over det sydlige Norges Geologi*.

## Related Geological Processes
The formation of ancient crystalline rocks often involves complex metamorphic processes. For instance, greywackes—impure, gritty rocks common in the Palaeozoic systems of Wales, the Lake district of England, and southern Scotland—can pass into sedimentary gneisses, chloride schists, and mica-schists through increasing metamorphism.

Furthermore, the creation of crystalline marbles and calc-silicate rocks involves the recrystallization of original limestone. Impurities such as clay (alumina), sand or siliceous deposits (silica), and limonite or hematite (iron) influence the final composition and color of the rock. When white minerals like tremolite, feldspar, or wollastonite predominate, the rocks resemble marble; however, they may appear green due to amphiboles and green augites, or brown if vesuvianite and garnet are present in quantity.
