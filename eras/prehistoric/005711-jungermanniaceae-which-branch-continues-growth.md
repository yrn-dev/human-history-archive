# Jungermanniaceae

The Jungermanniaceae are a large series of liverworts characterized by a significant variety in the organization of their sexual generation. This group is divided into two primary categories based on whether the formation of archegonia utilizes the apex or terminates the growth of the branch.

## Anacrogynous Jungermanniaceae
The Anacrogynous Jungermanniaceae represent the more primitive group. In these plants, the branch continues its growth after the formation of archegonia. Consequently, the archegonia, and subsequently the sporogonia, are positioned on the dorsal surface of the leafy plant or thallus.

This group exhibits a wide range of forms in its sexual plants. In Britain, this is illustrated by nine genera, including:
*   **Pellia**: A thalloid form.
*   **Sphaerocarpus**: One of the simplest liverworts, occurring rarely in stubble fields. Its small thallus features antheridia and archegonia on the upper surface of distinct individuals, each surrounded by a tubular involucre. Its sporogonium has a small foot, but the sterile cells among the spores do not develop into elaters.
*   **Riella**: A non-British genus that grows in mud in shallow water. It possesses an erect thallus with two rows of large scales below and a broad wing-like outgrowth from the dorsal surface. Like *Sphaerocarpus*, its capsule lacks provision for opening and its sterile cells do not become elaters.
*   **Aneura**: A genus where the thallus may show a division of labor between assimilating, stem-like, and root-like branches. Sexual organs are located on short lateral branches.
*   **Metzgeria**: Found on tree trunks and rocks, this genus has a narrow thallus with small sexual branches springing from the lower surface of the midrib. In both *Aneura* and *Metzgeria*, elaters are attached to a sterile group of cells projecting into the upper end of the capsule and remain connected to the valve tips during dehiscence.
*   **Pallavicinia**: This genus and its relatives feature a definite midrib and broad wings made of one layer of cells. They are notable for a water-conducting strand in the midrib consisting of pitted walls and elongated lignified cells.
*   **Blasia pusilla**: Commonly found by streams and ditches, this species serves as a transition to foliose types. Its thallus has thin marginal lobes of limited growth, comparable to the leaves of other anacrogynous forms. Its ventral surface contains concave scales and flat scales.

## Acrogynous Jungermanniaceae
The Acrogynous Jungermanniaceae are characterized by a plant that is throughout foliose. In this group, the archegonia occupy the ends of the branches or the main shoot. The antheridia are typically long-stalked and globular, and the capsule opens by splitting into four halves.

The majority of existing liverworts belong to this group. In Britain, there are numerous species across thirty-nine genera. These plants typically form green tufts, though some possess a reddish tint, and can be found on tree trunks, rocks, the ground, or on leaves in the damp tropics.

### Structure and Growth
The construction of Acrogynous Jungermanniaceae generally follows a similar plan. The stem typically grows via a pyramidal apical cell that cuts off three rows of segments. Each segment produces a leaf. The two lateral rows of leaves are usually larger and different in shape from the ventral row (amphigastria), which may be absent in some genera.

Leaves may be inserted transversely on the stem with equally developed lobes, or they may be inserted obliquely, where the anterior edge of a leaf lies over or under the edge of the leaf preceding it. The development of the two lobes is often unequal:
*   In **Scapania**, the upper lobe is smaller.
*   In **Radula**, **Poretta**, and the **Lejeuneae**, the lower lobe is smaller.

The folding of one lobe against another helps the plant retain water. Some genera, particularly epiphytic forms such as **Pleurozia**, **Lepidolaena**, and **Frullania**, have developed pitcher-like structures. In **Trichocolea**, the leaves are finely divided and, together with hair-like paraphyllia, create a loose weft around the stem.

The branches of these plants arise by the side of and below the leaves. Rhizoids emerge from the bases of the leaves or the lower surface of the stem.
