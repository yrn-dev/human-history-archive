# The Tian-shan Mountains and the Central Asian Divide

The Tian-shan, also known as the Celestial Mountains, constitute one of the most significant mountain systems in Central Asia. They form a critical part of a great central water-parting, or divide, that stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and the Tian-shan in the Pamir region, extending eventually to the Bering Strait in the extreme north-east.

## Geography and Dimensions
The Tian-shan range is situated between 40 and 43 degrees North latitude and between 75 and 95 degrees East longitude. The mountains are characterized by considerable elevation, with some summits reported to exceed 20,000 feet. These mountains serve as the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. 

The range is part of a broader mountainous belt that extends from the western end of the Yablonoi range on the 115th meridian to the extremity of the Pamir. This belt is broken into several branches and encloses various isolated drainage areas that lack outflow, leading to the formation of numerous lakes.

## Regional Relations and Extensions
The Tian-shan is linked to several other geographical features and ranges:
*   **The Altai:** The Tian-shan radiates north-eastward to join the Altai mountains. The Altai range, located near the 50th parallel, rises to 10,000 or 12,000 feet and separates the waters of western Siberia from those of north-west Mongolia, Kalka, and Dzungaria.
*   **The Ala-tau:** This range exists as a prolongation of the Tian-shan. It flanks the Syr on the north, rising to altitudes of 14,000 or 15,000 feet, and acts as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes (the latter of which has an elevation of approximately 5,000 feet).
*   **The Tarim Basin:** Located between the Tian-shan to the north and the Kuen Lun to the south is the Tarim Basin (roughly Eastern Turkestan). This area consists of rivers that do not reach the sea, instead ending in intermittent lakes and swamps.

## The Gobi Desert and Surrounding Plateaus
The Tian-shan forms the northern limit of the great Gobi desert of central Asia. This desert is bounded on the south by the northern face of the Tibetan plateau, on the north and north-west by the Yablonoi Mountains and the western extension of the Tian-shan, and on the east by the Khingan range. 

The Gobi is a vast, almost waterless interior space where local drainage is lost to swamps or deserts. While the central portions of the Gobi (near Lop Nor) may be below sea level, no part of the desert is believed to be less than 2,000 feet above sea level. In specific areas, such as the plains around Yarkand and Kashgar, elevations range from 4,000 to 6,000 feet.

## Geological Composition
The region north of the Tian-shan, encompassing much of Mongolia, consists largely of Archean rocks. This area is characterized by ancient folds and a series of depressions formed by faults, with the remaining strips standing as mountain ranges. There are no marine beds of Tertiary or Mesozoic age in this specific region, though plant-bearing deposits of the Angara series are present. In the western Tian-shan, however, marine Mesozoic deposits have been identified.

## Human Settlement and Transit
Due to the barren and thinly peopled nature of the region, human habitation is concentrated along the foot of the Tian-shan range, where the principal cultivated districts of central Asia are located. The few towns that have developed in the region are also situated here.

The mountains serve as a significant barrier, yet they facilitate historical movement. To the west of the Tarim Basin, the mountain barrier is described as high but not forbidding. Travelers from China can move westward through the Tarim valley to Kashgar, where roads converge, allowing passage over the mountains to Bokhara, Samarkand, and Kokand. This geography has made the region a natural meeting place for Mongolian and Aryan peoples.
