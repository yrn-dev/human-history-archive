# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely ignored the logistics of the materials themselves. To understand the evolution of the Stone Age, we must look beyond the finished tool and examine the "raw material economy": where the stone came from, why specific types were chosen, and what the distance of transport reveals about prehistoric cognition.

The shift from Oldowan to Mousterian technology was not merely a change in knapping technique, but a fundamental shift in how early hominins interacted with their landscape.

## From Opportunism to Selection

In the earliest Oldowan sites (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool production was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often sourced from nearby stream beds. The "cost" of transporting heavy raw materials was high, and the cognitive map required to locate specific stone types was limited.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian traditions, we see a marked increase in "selective sourcing." Hominins began to bypass readily available local stones in favor of high-quality materials—such as flint, chert, or obsidian—that provided a more predictable fracture pattern. This suggests a developed mental map of the environment and a capacity for forward planning.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Mind

By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the transport of raw materials had become a strategic operation. Archaeologists have discovered lithic assemblages where the stone was sourced from quarries dozens of kilometers away from the site of use.

This movement of material indicates a complex social structure. Whether the stone was carried by individuals over long distances or traded between groups remains a subject of scholarly debate. Some researchers argue that the presence of non-local stone proves the existence of early trade networks, while others suggest it reflects the migratory patterns of highly mobile hunter-gatherer bands. Regardless, the transition from "using what is underfoot" to "seeking the best material" marks a pivotal leap in hominin intelligence.

## Key Facts: Lithic Logistics
* **Raw Material Preference:** High-silica stones like flint and obsidian were preferred for their "conchoidal fracture," allowing for sharper, more precise edges.
* **Transport Distance:** Oldowan materials are typically found within a few hundred meters of their source; Mousterian materials are often found tens of kilometers away.
* **Curation:** Later Stone Age hominins practiced "curation," carefully retouching and sharpening a tool multiple times to extend its life rather than discarding it.
* **Cognitive Mapping:** The ability to locate specific geological deposits suggests an advanced spatial memory and environmental awareness.

## Did You Know?
* **The Obsidian Trail:** In some prehistoric regions, obsidian has been found hundreds of miles from the only volcanic source in the area, suggesting incredibly long-distance movement or exchange.
* **Waste Analysis:** Historians study "debitage" (the waste flakes left over from tool making) to determine if a tool was made on-site or brought in finished.
* **The "Core" Strategy:** In Mousterian Levallois technique, the stone core was carefully shaped *before* the final flake was struck, meaning the hominin had to visualize the finished tool inside the raw stone.
