# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Early Lithic Industries

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of stone tools—how an Oldowan chopper differs from a Mousterian point—we must examine the cognitive and logistical leap involved in the *acquisition* of the stone itself. The evolution of tool-making was not merely a change in shaping techniques, but a revolution in how prehistoric hominins perceived their landscape and managed resources.

The transition from opportunistic scavenging of pebbles to the strategic quarrying of high-quality stone reveals a profound shift in spatial awareness and planning depth.

## From Opportunism to Selection
In the earliest Oldowan industries (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), tool-making was largely opportunistic. Hominins typically used whatever river cobbles were immediately available. However, archaeological evidence shows that even these early ancestors possessed a basic understanding of fracture mechanics, preferring cryptocrystalline rocks like quartz or basalt that broke predictably.

As we move toward the Acheulean tradition, we see the emergence of "raw material selectivity." Hominins began bypassing mediocre local stones in favor of superior materials located kilometers away. This suggests the development of a "mental map," where the tool-maker remembered the location of a specific outcrop and planned a journey to retrieve materials for future use.

## The Logistics of the Mousterian Shift
By the time of the Mousterian industry (associated largely with Neanderthals), the relationship with raw materials had become highly sophisticated. The Levallois technique—a method of preparing a core to strike off a specific, predetermined flake—required high-quality flint or chert to be successful. 

This necessity drove a "transport economy." We find Mousterian tools made from materials that originated dozens of kilometers from the site of discovery. This implies not only long-distance movement but potentially the early stages of social exchange or trade between groups. The ability to transport "blanks" (rough pieces of stone) to be refined later at a home base demonstrates a level of foresight and delayed gratification that separates the later Paleolithic from its predecessors.

## Scholarly Debate: Intentionality vs. Availability
A point of ongoing debate among archaeologists is whether the transport of high-quality stone was a conscious "economic" choice or simply a byproduct of larger migratory patterns. Some scholars argue that hominins moved with the herds and happened to pick up stone along the way, while others point to the selective abandonment of inferior stone as proof of a deliberate procurement strategy.

### Key Facts
* **Material Preference:** Cryptocrystalline stones (like flint, chert, and obsidian) were prized for their predictable conchoidal fractures.
* **Transport Distance:** While Oldowan materials were often sourced within meters, Mousterian materials are frequently found 20–50km from their source.
* **The Levallois Leap:** The Mousterian Levallois technique required a specific grade of stone to ensure the "prepared core" didn't shatter prematurely.
* **Mental Mapping:** The shift in sourcing indicates an evolution in the hippocampus and the ability to plan for future needs.

### Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Trade:** In some late Paleolithic sites, obsidian (volcanic glass) has been found far from any volcanic source, suggesting early networks of human interaction.
* **Curated Tools:** Unlike Oldowan tools, which were often discarded immediately after one use, Mousterian tools show signs of "re-sharpening," indicating they were valued possessions.
* **The "Core" Concept:** In the Mousterian era, the "core" (the parent stone) was often carried for miles, serving as a portable toolkit from which multiple blades could be struck.
