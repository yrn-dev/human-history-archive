# Mongolia North of the Tian-shan

## Geological Composition
The region of Mongolia situated north of the Tian-shan mountains is characterized by an extensive area consisting primarily of Archean rocks. This area is located south and east of the Palaeozoic plateau. A defining geological feature of this region is the total absence of marine beds dating from the Mesozoic or Tertiary ages. Instead, the area contains plant-bearing deposits that are identified as belonging to the Angara series.

## Structural Configuration
The structural folds found in this region are of an ancient date. The landscape is primarily shaped by faulting, which has created a series of depressions. The strips of land between these depressions, which did not sink to the same extent, now form the region's mountain ranges. 

This area is part of a broader zone—including the greater part of Siberia, Mongolia, and northern China—that is generally free from any significant folding of Mesozoic or Tertiary age. While mountain ranges exist in this zone, they are often formed of folded beds of an ancient date (mostly Palaeozoic or Archean), and the direction of the chains frequently differs from the direction of the folds, suggesting that their elevation was caused by factors other than folding.

## Geography and Elevation
The northern parts of Mongolia are situated at elevations between 4,000 and 6,000 feet. In the desert region between the Chinese frontier and Kiakhta, no portion of the route falls below 3,000 feet. 

The region is bounded to the south by the Tian-shan (or Celestial Mountains), which serve as the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. The Tian-shan range lies between 40° and 43° N. latitude and 75° and 95° E. longitude, with some summits reported to exceed 20,000 feet in altitude. To the north-west, the Altai range (near the 50th parallel) rises to 10,000 or 12,000 feet; this range separates the waters of western Siberia's great rivers from those that collect in Dzungaria, Kalka, and the lakes of north-west Mongolia.

## Regional Context and Hydrography
Mongolia north of the Tian-shan is part of a vast northern land mass that has remained land since the close of the Palaeozoic era. This is evidenced by the lack of marine deposits later than the Carboniferous period across a broad area stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the south of China.

The region is influenced by a great central water-parting or divide that extends from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and Tian-shan mountains toward the Bering Strait. A portion of this divide, including the Altai and Khangai ranges, reaches great heights, though other sections remain below 5,000 feet. South of this divide, the land drops toward the central depression of the Gobi, a nearly waterless interior space where local drainage is lost to swamps or deserts.
