# The Arctic Ocean and Its Surrounding Regions

The Arctic Ocean forms the northern boundary of Asia, with a coastline situated between 70 and 75 degrees North, placing it within the Arctic circle. Its extreme northern point is Cape Sivero-Vostochnyi (also known as Cape Chelyuskin) at 78 degrees North.

## Geological History and Land Masses
Geological evidence suggests that a broad area stretching from the Arctic Ocean as far as the south of China remained land from the close of the Palaeozoic era. This conclusion is based on the absence of marine deposits later than the Carboniferous period, except in the extreme north, and the presence of terrestrial and freshwater deposits of Mesozoic age. The limits of this land mass are indicated by marine Mesozoic deposits in Tibet to the south, the western Tian-shan to the west, and Japan to the east. Triassic deposits in the Verkhoyansk Range indicate that this specific land mass did not extend to the Bering Sea.

## Regional Geography and Administration
Siberia is the portion of Asia located between the Arctic Ocean and the mountainous belt that bounds Turkestan, Mongolia, and Manchuria to the north. This region is characterized by an immense high and broken plateau that narrows and loses altitude as it advances north-east. The landscape includes high plains (1,500 to 2,000 feet high) and lowlands, with a surface that is often peaty or swampy. Vegetation consists of pine forests on mountain-sides and shrubs or grasses on the plains. The population is scanty, consisting of nomadic Mongols and Russian towns.

In East Siberia, the Maritime Province (Primorskaya Oblast) is a Russian province consisting of a strip of territory along the Pacific coast from Korea to the Arctic Ocean. This province includes the peninsula of Kamchatka, several small islands, and part of Sakhalin. Its western boundary reaches the Arctic Ocean at Chaun Bay (70° N.) after crossing the Stanovoi watershed and barren tundras. The total area of the Maritime Province is 715,735 square miles.

In northern Russia, the Kola peninsula lies between the White Sea to the south and the Arctic Ocean to the north. The Arctic coast, known as the Murman coast, is 260 miles long and remains ice-free year-round due to the North Atlantic drift. This rocky granite coast rises to 650 feet and includes Kola Bay, where the naval harbor of Alexandrovsk was founded in 1895. The peninsula's interior is a plateau of approximately 1,000 feet elevation with mountains exceeding 3,000 feet.

## Hydrography and River Systems
The northern division of Asia includes drainage falling into the Mediterranean, the Aralo-Caspian depression, and the Arctic Sea, covering an area of approximately 6,394,500 square miles. The Arctic river basins alone account for 4,367,000 square miles. The Ob is the largest Asiatic river basin, exceeding 1,000,000 square miles. Other major rivers traversing Siberia include the Yenisei and the Lena.

In the North-West Territories of Canada, the Mackenzie River discharges the waters of the Great Slave Lake into the Arctic Ocean. Discovered and navigated by Sir Alexander Mackenzie in 1789, the river has a total length of 2,350 miles including its tributary, the Peace. The Mackenzie enters the Arctic Ocean near 68° 50´ W. and 135° W. through a flat delta. It is navigable from June 10 to October 20.

## Marine and Biological Characteristics
The Barents Sea is the portion of the Arctic Ocean demarcated by the islands of Spitsbergen, Franz Josef Land, Novaya Zemlya, and the north coast of Europe. It extends from approximately 67 to 80 degrees North and 20 to 60 degrees East.

From a biological perspective, the world's shore-fishes are divided into regions, one of which is the Arctic region. The fish-fauna of the Arctic is chiefly characterized by Gadids, Zoarcids, Cyclopterids, and Cottids. There is a well-established circumpolar distribution of shore fishes between the Arctic and Antarctic regions, although the theory of bipolarity is not supported by the study of these fishes.

## Historical and Paleogeographic Theories
Some historical theories suggest that in the distant past, a great sea extended across the Volga region of south-eastern Russia into Turkestan, encompassing the modern Aral, Caspian, and Black Seas. It is proposed that this sea may have sent arms to the Arctic Ocean, acting as a barrier between the people of north-eastern Asia and the Aryan speakers, who resided in a region bounded by the Volga, Don, Dnieper, and Danube rivers.
