# Palaeozoic Era

The Palaeozoic era is one of the three great eras of geological time, alongside the Mesozoic and the Cainozoic (also written as Cenozoic). It is characterized by significant geological formations and the development of ancient land masses and mountain chains across the globe.

## Geological Structures and Mountain Chains
In Europe, the "Hercynian chain" is composed entirely of Palaeozoic rocks. This ancient mountain chain, which has been worn down to a low level, has remnants that rise to the surface in the Ardennes and extend eastward into Germany, forming the Taunus, Hunsruck, Westerwald, and Eifel. In the Ardennes, these rocks belong primarily to the Devonian System, though Cambrian beds rise through the Devonian strata to form masses such as Stavelot and Rocroi. The final elevation of this chain occurred toward the close of the Carboniferous period, and the latest beds involved in its folds belong to the Coal Measures.

In South America, the eastern ranges of the Bolivian Andes are formed of Palaeozoic rocks featuring other intrusions and granitic ones. These beds have yielded fossils from the Carboniferous, Devonian, Ordovician, and Cambrian ages. In the north-eastern ranges of Bolivia, the Carboniferous and Devonian are extensively developed, while the southern eastern Andes are primarily composed of Cambrian and Ordovician beds.

## Asian Land Masses and Marine Deposits
Evidence indicates that large portions of northern and eastern Asia have remained land since the Palaeozoic era. This northern land mass, named Angaraland, begins in the area between the Lena and the Yenisei. In this region, Cambrian and Ordovician beds—which mostly remain flat and undisturbed—overlie folded Archean rocks. These are topped by the Angara series, which consists of plant-bearing freshwater deposits of sandstone and conglomerate, including workable coal seams of probably Jurassic or Permian age.

A broad area stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the south of China shows no marine deposits later than the Carboniferous, except in the extreme north. The Triassic deposits of the Verkhoyansk Range demonstrate that this land mass did not extend to the Bering Sea. The limits of this land in other directions are indicated by marine Mesozoic deposits found in Tibet to the south, the western Tian-shan to the west, and Japan to the east.

In the Chinese provinces of Shensi and Shansi, the geological succession includes nearly horizontal strata of Lower Palaeozoic age overlying a floor of folded Archean rocks. While marine beds from the Carboniferous period are present, no marine deposits are known from the Upper Carboniferous onward.

## Gondwanaland and the Southern Regions
The peninsula of India has been land since at least the Carboniferous period. This is evidenced by the presence of the Gondwana series—terrestrial and freshwater deposits representing the geological scale from the top of the Jurassic to the top of the Carboniferous—and a general absence of marine fossils except near the borders. This region was part of a great land mass known as Gondwanaland, which likely extended across the Indian Ocean and was once united with southern Africa.

In the south, specifically in Arabia, Syria, and the peninsula of India, the Upper Palaeozoic beds, along with the Tertiary and Mesozoic, lie almost horizontally upon the oldest rocks, which are the only ones folded in these areas.

## Regional Variations and Stratigraphy
In Northern Bulgaria, the earlier Palaeozoic systems are missing, but the Carboniferous appears in the western Balkans with a continental facies (Kulm), where sandstone and argillite beds contain anthracitiferous coal.

In Siberia and northern China, the region is generally free from important folding of Tertiary or Mesozoic age. Many mountain ranges in this area owe their elevation to faulting rather than folding, as the folds are mostly of Palaeozoic or Archaean date. In some parts of northern China and Siberia, Cambrian beds remain as horizontal as they were when first laid down. However, in the Taimyr peninsula mountains and the Verkhoyansk range in the extreme north, there are indications of a zone of folding from the Mesozoic or a later date.
