# The Raw Material Economy: Sourcing and Transport in Paleolithic Toolmaking

While previous installments in this series have focused on the *morphology* of tools—the transition from the crude Oldowan choppers to the sophisticated Mousterian flakes—we have largely overlooked the logistics of the materials themselves. The evolution of stone tools was not merely a cognitive leap in shaping stone, but a revolution in how early hominins interacted with their landscape and managed resources.

The transition from the Lower to the Middle Paleolithic represents a shift from "opportunistic" sourcing to "strategic" procurement. Understanding where the stone came from provides a window into the spatial awareness and social organization of our ancestors.

## From Local Opportunism to Selective Sourcing

In the Oldowan tradition (approx. 2.6 to 1.7 million years ago), toolmakers generally utilized whatever cobbles were available in the immediate vicinity, often scavenging materials from riverbeds. The "cost" of transporting heavy raw materials was high, and the cognitive map required for sourcing was limited to the immediate home range.

However, as we move toward the Acheulean and eventually the Mousterian industries, a pattern of selectivity emerges. Hominins began to bypass readily available low-quality stones in favor of high-quality cryptocrystalline silicates, such as flint, chert, and obsidian. These materials possess a conchoidal fracture pattern, allowing for the precise, predictable flaking necessary for the Levallois technique characteristic of the Mousterian period.

## The Logistics of Transport and Trade

The presence of non-local stone at archaeological sites is one of the most significant indicators of evolving hominin behavior. In many Middle Paleolithic sites, raw materials have been found that originated dozens, and sometimes over a hundred, kilometers away.

This suggests two possibilities that remain subjects of scholarly debate: either early humans were migrating over vast distances while carrying their "toolkits" with them, or they had established rudimentary social networks for the exchange of high-quality stone. The latter implies a level of social complexity and communication previously thought to belong to much later eras. The effort required to transport these materials indicates that the *utility* of a superior edge outweighed the *energy cost* of the journey.

## Key Facts: Material Logistics
* **Conchoidal Fracture:** The specific way certain stones (like obsidian) break, which is essential for creating the sharp edges of Mousterian tools.
* **Raw Material Provenance:** The study of the geographic origin of stone tools, used by historians to track prehistoric movement.
* **Curated vs. Expedient:** "Expidient" tools are made and discarded quickly from local stone; "curated" tools are crafted from high-quality stone and carried over long distances.
* **The Levallois Shift:** The Mousterian period saw a shift toward preparing a "core" to strike off a specific shape, requiring a higher grade of raw material than Oldowan tools.

## Did You Know?
* **Obsidian Tracking:** Because obsidian has a unique chemical "fingerprint," modern geologists can trace a single Mousterian blade back to the specific volcanic flow where it originated.
* **Recycling Centers:** Some Paleolithic sites show evidence of "tool recycling," where old, blunt tools were re-sharpened or broken down to create smaller, new flakes.
* **Weight Management:** To reduce transport weight, hominins often "rough out" a tool at the source, removing excess bulk before carrying the preform to their final destination.
