# The House of Bohemund

The history of the Bohemunds is inextricably linked to the principality of Antioch and, from the time of Bohemund IV, the county of Tripoli. The dynasty began with Bohemund I, a central figure of the First Crusade and a prince of Otranto.

## Bohemund I and the First Crusade
Bohemund I (c. 1058–1111), born Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, the *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*, and his first wife. Bohemund served under his father during the attack on the East Roman empire from 1080 to 1085. During Guiscard's absence between 1082 and 1084, Bohemund commanded the Normans and penetrated into Thessaly as far as Larissa, where he was repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early conflict with Alexius significantly influenced Bohemund's later career and the trajectory of the First Crusade.

Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a war broke out between Bohemund and his younger half-brother, Roger, who had succeeded to the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This conflict was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions. In 1096, while attacking Amalfi, Bohemund was inspired by the passing crusaders. He viewed the First Crusade as an opportunity to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* and sought to establish an eastern principality for himself.

Leading a Norman army, Bohemund crossed the Adriatic and arrived at Constantinople in April 1097, where he did homage to Emperor Alexius. From Constantinople to Antioch, Bohemund acted as the real leader of the First Crusade, guiding the host across Asia Minor.

## Bohemund II and Bohemund III
Bohemund II (1108–1131) was the son of Bohemund I and Constance of France. Born in the year of his father's defeat at Durazzo, he arrived in Antioch from Apulia in 1126. In 1127, he married Alice, the younger daughter of Baldwin II. After participating in an attack on Damascus with Baldwin II, Bohemund II was defeated and killed in 1131 by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo on his northern frontier.

Bohemund III succeeded his mother, Constance (daughter of Bohemund II), in the principality of Antioch in 1163. In 1164, while serving as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem during Amalric I's expedition to Egypt, he was defeated and captured by Nureddin at Harenc. He was ransomed by his brother-in-law, Emperor Manuel, and returned to Antioch with a Greek patriarch. In 1180, Bohemund III was excommunicated after deserting his second wife, Orguilleuse, for Sibylla. His final years were dominated by a succession dispute over Antioch between his grandson, Raymund Rhupen, and his younger son, Bohemund IV. Bohemund III died in possession of his principality in 1201.

## Bohemund IV and Bohemund V
Bohemund IV, the younger son of Bohemund III and Orguilleuse, became count of Tripoli in 1187 and succeeded his father in Antioch in 1201. His succession was contested for many years by Raymund Rhupen, supported by Leo of Armenia. Although Leo captured Antioch in 1216 and installed Raymund Rhupen, Bohemund IV regained control before Leo's death in 1220. 

Bohemund IV was a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, which led to his excommunication in 1230, though he was released from this by Gregory IX before his death in 1233. He was supported by the Templars and the Greek inhabitants of Antioch, to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207.

Bohemund V succeeded his father in 1233 as prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. He maintained an alliance with the Templars but faced hostility from Armenia until 1251, when peace was achieved through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund V also connected Antioch with Cyprus through his 1225 marriage to Alice, the widow of Hugh I. He died in 1251, having resided primarily in Tripoli.

## Bohemund VI and Bohemund VII
Bohemund VI (born 1237), son of Bohemund V and Luciana, succeeded his father in 1251 and was knighted by St Louis in 1252. He established himself in Antioch in 1252 and recognized his nephew, Hugh II, as king of Jerusalem in 1257. To counter the Egyptian sultan, Bohemund VI allied himself with the Mongols. However, he lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268. By the time of his death in 1275, he was only the count of Tripoli.

He was succeeded by his son, Bohemund VII, who reigned as count of Tripoli from 1275. His short reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue, and the county of Tripoli became extinct shortly thereafter when the city was captured.
