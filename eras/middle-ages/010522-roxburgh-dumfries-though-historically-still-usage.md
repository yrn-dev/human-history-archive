# Roxburgh and Dumfries

Roxburgh and Dumfries are identified as Scottish Border counties. The "Borders" refers to the territory on both sides of the boundary line between Scotland and England. While the term has geographical, historical, and literary meanings, it is most frequently applied to the Scottish side.

## Geography of the Borders
The boundary line between England and Scotland begins on the coast of Berwickshire, 3 miles North by West of Berwick. It runs West and South to the Tweed near Paxton, following the river to a point beyond Carham. From there, it moves South-South-East to the Cheviot Hills, which serve as the watershed and boundary for 35 miles. The line is then formed by several streams: the Kershope, Liddel, Bells Burn, and Esk. After one mile on the Esk, the boundary cuts west to the Sark, following it to the mouth at the head of the Solway Firth. 

The total length of this boundary is 108 miles, though the direct distance from the Solway to the North Sea is 70 miles. To the east, a small 8 square mile district north of the Tweed forms the "liberties" or "bounds" of Berwick. To the west, between the Sark and the Esk (up to the Liddel junction), existed a "No man's land" known as the Debatable Land. This area was a haunt for brigands and outlaws and a source of contention between the two nations until its boundaries were adjusted in 1552.

The Scottish Border counties include Berwick, Roxburgh, and Dumfries. Additionally, the shires of Peebles and Selkirk have historically been classed as Border shires by usage. The region on the Scottish side is watered by the Sark, Esk, Liddel, Kershope, Jed, Kale, Leet, Whiteadder, and the Tweed.

## Dumfries: History and Development
Dumfries was once the site of a camp for the Selgovian Britons, though its history is largely unknown until after the Romans withdrew. William the Lion (who died in 1214) established it as a royal burgh, although the oldest existing charter dates to 1395, granted by Robert II.

The town was involved in the struggles for Scottish independence. It initially supported the claims to the throne of the Red Comyn and John Baliol. Devorgilla, the mother of John Baliol and daughter of Alan, lord of Galloway, contributed to the town's prosperity by constructing the stone bridge over the Nith. In 1300, Edward I lodged in the Franciscan monastery during the siege of Carlaverock Castle. This same monastery was the site of the murder of Comyn on February 10, 1306.

From that period until nearly the end of the 16th century, the burgh suffered frequent raids from English freebooters and partisans of turbulent chiefs, including the Johnstones, Maxwells, and Douglases. Despite this, Scottish sovereigns visited the town, including Mary, James IV, and James V. On August 3, 1617, James VI was entertained in the town and presented the seven incorporated trades with a silver gun to encourage musketry practice. This led to an annual competition—with a major festival every seven years—that continued until 1831.

The Union with England was unpopular in Dumfries. On November 20, 1706, Cameronians burned the articles of the Union at the Market Cross while the inhabitants cheered, and the provost voted against the measure in the Scottish parliament. The town remained apathetic during the uprisings of 1715 and 1745, although Prince Charles Edward did occupy the town.

## Administration and Economy of Dumfries
Dumfries serves as the county town of its shire and forms a sheriffdom along with the shires of Wigtown and Kirkcudbright. A resident sheriff-substitute sits at Dumfries, as well as at Lockerbie, Langholm, and Annan. Other royal burghs in the county include Sanquhar, Lochmaben, and Annan.

As a parliamentary burgh, Dumfries includes Maxwelltown (which otherwise belongs to Kirkcudbrightshire). Together with Sanquhar, Lochmaben, Annan, and Kirkcudbright, it forms the "Five Carlins" of Burns's Election Ballads, returning one member to Parliament.

The local economy is supported by the timber trade, nursery gardening, and the manufacture of leather, baskets, clogs, hosiery, and tweeds, as well as iron implements and machinery. The town hosts weekly markets for sheep and cattle, a weekly pork market from November for five months, and horse markets five times a year.

## Regional Infrastructure and Demographics
In the county of Dumfries, the population was 74,245 in 1891 and 72,571 in 1901. The chief towns include Dumfries (pop. 14,444 in 1901), Annan (4,309), Langholm (3,142), Lockerbie (2,358), and Moffat (2,153). Education is managed under school-board jurisdiction, with academies located in Annan, Moffat, and Dumfries.

Communications are provided by several railways:
* **Glasgow & South-Western railway:** Runs from Glasgow to Carlisle through lower Annandale and Nithsdale.
* **Caledonian railway:** Runs through Annandale with branches to Moffat (from Beattock), Dumfries (from Lockerbie), and Bowness (from Kirtlebridge).
* **North British railway:** Connects Langholm to Riddings Junction in Cumberland, providing access to Edinburgh via the Waverley route and to Carlisle.

In the neighboring Kirkcudbrightshire, the population was 39,985 in 1891 and 39,383 in 1901. Its chief towns include Maxwelltown (5,796), Dalbeattie (3,469), Castle Douglas (3,018), Kirkcudbright (2,386), Gatehouse of Fleet (1,013), and Creetown (991).
