# Introduction of Christianity in Bohemia

## Early Resistance and Adoption
The history of Bohemia becomes less obscure following the introduction of Christianity. The initial attempts to bring the faith to the region originated from Germany. These early efforts were largely unsuccessful because the Bohemians possessed an innate distrust of the Germans, which made them unfavourable to a creed that reached them from the realm of their western neighbours.

The situation changed when Christianity approached from Moravia. In Moravia, the doctrine had been taught by Methodius and Cyrillus, who were Greek monks from Thessalonica. The adoption of the faith accelerated around the year 873, when the Bohemian prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius.

## Early Rulers and Expansion
One of the most famous rulers of this era was Wenceslas, known as the Holy. He was an ally of Germany, but in 935 he was murdered by his brother, Boleslav. Wenceslas was later canonized by the Church of Rome. The murder of Wenceslas led to a war with Germany, a conflict in which Boleslav, who succeeded his brother, was generally successful.

Under the reigns of Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, the frontiers of Bohemia expanded. Boleslav II established rule over Moravia, Bohemia, a large portion of Silesia, and the part of Poland that is currently the Austrian province of Galicia. However, this empire did not last long. Boleslav III, the son of Boleslav II, lost all foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great, the king of Poland. Boleslav III's reign was characterized by constant civil war due to dissensions with his brothers, Ulrick and Jaromir. Although Boleslav III managed to expel his brothers, his cruelty led the Bohemians to dethrone him. They chose the Polish prince Vladivoj—son of the Bohemian princess Dubravka and brother of Boleslav the Great—as their ruler. To strengthen his position, Vladivoj sought aid from Germany and agreed to continue paying tribute.

## Religious Reform and Intellectual Development
By the 14th century, Bohemia had reached a high intellectual level, producing writers on philosophical and religious subjects who utilized the national language. Thomas of Stitny (c. 1331-1401) was a prominent figure of this period; his works *Besedni reci* ("learned entertainments") and *O obecnych vecech Krestanskych* (on general Christian matters) contain ideas similar to those of Wycliffe. Stitny and other contemporaries are regarded as the forerunners of Huss.

Huss wrote in both Latin and Bohemian, contributing significantly to the development of the native language. His important Bohemian works include the *Postilla* (a collection of sermons), treatises on simony (*O svatokupectvi*) and the true road to salvation (*O poznani cesty prave k spaseni*), as well as a collection of letters written while in prison. In 1412, he wrote the *Vyklad viry, desatera Boziho prikazani, a patere*, an exposition of the Lord's Prayer, the ten commandments, and the creed.

## The Reformation and Confessional Conflict
Following the start of the Reformation in Germany, Bohemian reformers evolved. The Bohemian Brethren, supported by powerful nobles, sympathized strongly with German Protestants, while some advanced Utraquists held views similar to German Lutherans.

In 1575, King Maximilian returned to Bohemia after the estates refused subsidies to an absentee monarch. The Bohemians demanded the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," a summary of Luther's teachings. They also demanded the right for the estates to appoint members of the consistory, the ecclesiastical body ruling the Utraquist church. When Maximilian refused to recognize the Augsburg confession, the advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian brotherhood created the *Confessio Bohemica*. While mostly identical to the Augsburg confession, the *Confessio Bohemica* differed regarding the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, aligning instead with the views of Calvin due to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren. Maximilian gave verbal approval to the *Confessio* but refused to make it public or include it in the kingdom's charters. He did, however, exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the jurisdiction of the consistory.

## Habsburg Rule and the Re-establishment of Catholicism
During the reign of Rudolph, a successor to Maximilian, religious controversy was met with apathy and indifference. However, the political and religious landscape shifted violently during the Thirty Years' War. Following the battle of the White Hill, Ferdinand implemented a system of wholesale land confiscation from those involved in the national movement. Most of the ancient Bohemian nobility were exiled.

Those who refused to recognize the creed of the Roman church were expelled, and Catholicism was entirely re-established through the use of terrible cruelty. In 1627, Ferdinand published a decree suppressing the ancient free constitution of Bohemia and proclaiming the Bohemian crown as hereditary within the house of Habsburg. This new constitution added the clergy as a primary estate, consisting of the Roman archbishop of Prague and ecclesiastics with landed estates. The diet lost its legislative power, and the German language was granted equal right with Bohemian in law-courts and government offices.
