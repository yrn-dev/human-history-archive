# The Treaty of Brétigny and the Ransom of John II

## The Treaty of Brétigny
The Treaty of Brétigny was a celebrated agreement concluded on May 8, 1360, in the French town of Brétigny, located in the commune of Sours within the arrondissement and canton of Chartres (dept. Eure-et-Loir). The treaty was established between Edward III of England and John II of France, who was surnamed "the Good." 

The negotiations for the treaty began in early April and lasted for more than a month. The process was made difficult by the exactions of the English, who sought to retain as many of the advantages as possible that had been claimed in the Treaty of London. The final agreement was ratified and sworn to by both kings and their eldest sons on October 24, 1360, at Calais. This ratification included the signing of renunciatory clauses, in which both kings abandoned their rights over the territories they had yielded to one another, as well as special conditions relating to each important article of the treaty.

## Territorial Transfers
Under the terms of the treaty, Edward III of England obtained a significant number of territories. These included:
* Guienne and Gascony
* Poitou, Saintonge, and Aunis
* Agenais, Périgord, Limousin, Quercy, and Bigorre
* The countship of Gaure
* Angoumois and Rouergue
* Montreuil-sur-mer and Ponthieu
* Calais and Sangatte
* Ham and the countship of Guines

In exchange, the king of England relinquished the countships of Anjou and Maine, the duchies of Normandy and Touraine, and the suzerainty of Flanders and Brittany.

## The Ransom of John II
As part of the agreement, John II was required to pay three million gold crowns for his ransom. To guarantee this payment, John the Good provided several hostages:
* Two of his own sons
* Various nobles and princes
* Four inhabitants of Paris
* Two citizens from each of the nineteen principal towns of France

## Related Royal Actions of John II
During the mid-14th century, King John II was involved in several other territorial and political arrangements. In 1356, he raised Castres to a countship. In 1360, he erected the *Terre d'Auvergne* into a duchy in the peerage of France (*duche-pairie*) for his son, John. 

Additionally, John II's second marriage was to Jeanne of Auvergne and Boulogne. Following the death of their son, Duke Philip de Rouvres, in 1361, the duchy of Burgundy returned to the French crown. In 1363, John granted this duchy and its hereditary rights to his son Philip, surnamed "the Bold," establishing the second Capet house of Burgundy.
