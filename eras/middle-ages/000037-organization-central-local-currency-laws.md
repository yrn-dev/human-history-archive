# ORGANIZATION, CENTRAL AND LOCAL; CURRENCY AND THE LAWS OF THE TOKUGAWA BAKAFU

## The Tokugawa Bakufu
The Tokugawa Bakufu represents a specific period of Japanese history characterized by a structured system of central and local organization. The era is divided into three distinct phases: the First Period, spanning from the first shogun, Ieyasu, to the fourth, Ietsuna (1603-1680); the Middle Period, from the fifth shogun, Tsunayoshi, to the tenth, Ieharu (1680-1786); and the Late Period, under the eleventh shogun, Ienari (1786-1838). The administration of this era included specific frameworks for currency and law, as well as the distribution of fiefs to feudatories.

## Comparative Systems of Government and Law
Different historical contexts demonstrate varying methods of central and local organization. In the case of the Sarre Basin, the territory was governed by a five-member commission appointed by the League of Nations to secure the welfare of the population and ensure French freedom in operating mines. This commission held all government powers previously belonging to Prussia, Bavaria, and the German Empire, including the administration of public services and railroads. While local courts continued to operate, they remained subject to the commission. Legislation was based on existing German law, though the commission could make modifications after consulting a local representative assembly. This assembly was also required to approve any new taxes, which were limited to local purposes.

In Japan, later reforms between 1906 and 1908 involved a complete remodeling of the Central Government. These changes included the differentiation of the executive from the Court, and the separation of the judiciary from the administrative branch. This period saw the organization of law-courts featuring a majority of Japanese jurists and the enactment of a new penal code.

## Currency Standards and Economic Laws
Currency systems have historically fluctuated between different metallic standards and paper-based systems. In the early 19th century, the United States (1792) and France (1803) legally established a double standard, while England adopted the gold standard by law in 1816. Silver served as the currency for other European nations. However, gold discoveries in Australia (1851) and California (1848) led France to displace silver with gold. In the United States, a law from 1834 over-rated gold (1:16) compared to the French ratio (1:15 1/2), which resulted in the expulsion of most silver from circulation. By 1867, an international conference in Paris unanimously accepted gold as the standard for a proposed international system coordinated with the Latin Union, which had been established in 1865.

In economics, "Gresham's Law"—a term suggested by H. D. Macleod in 1857—describes the principle that "bad money drives out good." This occurs when debased or under-weight coins circulate alongside full-weight coins of the same metal, or when inconvertible paper money is used alongside metallic currency. In such instances, the worst form of currency regulates the value of the entire system and drives other forms out of circulation.

## Paper Currency and Banking
The use of paper currency has varied by region and government control. In certain Chinese cities, such as Fuchau, private and local promissory notes were used as currency, almost entirely displacing bullion. These banking-houses operated without government control, allowing any company or individual with sufficient credit to issue bills ranging from 100 cash to 1,000 dollars.

The Imperial Government later attempted to resume a government-issued paper currency system due to an exhausted Treasury. This effort met with poor success. For example, in 1858 at Fuchau, the dollar was valued between 18,000 and 20,000 cash in Government Bills. In Peking, Dr. Rennie noted the dollar's value at 15,000 and later 25,000 paper cash. The Regent Sushun issued a vast number of notes through his own banks but failed to redeem them, leading to the failure of the banks and significant commotion in the city. Following the death of Emperor Hien Fung, the Empress ordered the execution of Sushun on November 8, 1861, an event cheered by defrauded creditors.

## Infrastructure and Fiscal Administration
Modernized administrative organization often involves the integration of taxation and public works. Reforms implemented between 1906 and 1908 included the elaboration of taxation systems, the re-assessment of taxable property, and the organization of staff to manage annual budgets. These efforts supported the floating of public loans for productive enterprises and the establishment of various commercial and agricultural banks. 

Concurrent with these fiscal changes was the development of local and national infrastructure, including:
* The provision of roads, railways, telegraphs, and posts.
* The construction of waterworks, hospitals, medical schools, and sanitary works.
* The lighting and buoying of coasts.
* The creation of industrial enterprises in coal mining, forestry, brick making, and printing.
* The establishment of an educational system and industrial training schools.
