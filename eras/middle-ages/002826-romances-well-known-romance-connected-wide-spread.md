# Romances

Romances encompass a diverse array of literary forms, ranging from ancient Greek imitations and medieval chivalric narratives to early modern prose fiction. These works often blend historical figures with imaginative elements to amplify ideals or explore romantic adventures.

## Medieval and Chivalric Romances
In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, romantic narrative poetry flourished in England, France, and Germany. These tales, such as those concerning Arthur, Charlemagne, and the Niebelungen, served as illustrations of the chivalrous ideal. To the audiences of the time, these narratives were not viewed as fiction but as history, though they often differed significantly from historical reality. For example, the Charlemagne of romance shares little more than a name with the historical figure. Some works, such as the *Chronicle of Turpin*, were accepted as genuine history and even received papal authority.

The *geste* of Doon de Mayence represents a specific cycle of Charlemagne romances focusing on feudal revolts. While some elements of the story—such as Doon's wars in Saxony—may be based on historical events, other portions, including the account of his romantic childhood, are considered pure fiction from the 13th century. The heroes of this cycle include Ogier the Dane, Huon of Bordeaux, and the four sons of Aymon. These characters are often linked to Doon through imaginary genealogical ties.

## Greek and Byzantine Romances
Ancient Greek romances served as models for later voluminous poems in the 12th century, a period of cultural revival under the Comneni. These imitations include:
* **Theodorus Prodromus**: The story of Rodanthe and Dosicles (written in duodecasyllable metre).
* **Nicetas Eugenianus**: The story of Drusilla and Charicles (an imitation of Prodromus).
* **Constantine Manasses**: The love story of Aristander and Callithea (written in "political" verse and preserved only in fragments).
* **Eustathius (or Eumathius) Macrembolita**: The prose story of Hysmine and Hysminias.

## Evolution of Prose Romance
The 13th century saw the rise of prose romancers who adapted old metrical stories. According to Sir Walter Scott, authors such as Rustician of Pisa and Robert de Borron often took the subjects of old minstrels and recomposed them with additional adventures and characters, which frequently obliterated the original authentic traditions.

In Britain, the Arthurian prose romance *Morte d'Arthur* by Caxton was a notable example. Over time, various other forms of prose fiction emerged, including:
* **Arcadian or pastoral heroic romances**: Such as the "wearisome" Arcadian style.
* **Utopian and philosophical romances**: Including *Oceana* and *The Man in the Moone*.
* **Modernized classic heroism**: Represented by *The Grand Cyrus*.
* **Other types**: Romances of roguery, religious allegories (such as Bunyan's *Life and Death of Mr Badman*), and novels of French or Italian gallantry.

In Holland, the French school of Honore d'Urfe and Scudery influenced local writers. Johan van Heemskerk published the first original Dutch romance, *Batavian Arcadia*, in 1637, which was later imitated by Lambertus Bos and Hendrik Zoeteboom. Nikolaes Heinsius the younger wrote the more original *Mirandor* in 1675.

## Mythology and Tradition
Romance is also tied to cultural mythology. In Arthurian romances, Avalon is depicted as the abode of heroes where King Arthur was taken after his final battle. Originally appearing in Welsh mythology as the kingdom of the dead or an earthly paradise (Ynys yr Afallon, the "Isle of Apples"), it has been associated with locations such as Glastonbury Tor.

In Irish tradition, the "Old Celtic Romances" include the story of the four children of Lir. According to tradition, these children were changed into swans by their step-mother and remained enchanted until they were released by St Columba. This romance is linked to a widespread Irish belief that killing a swan brings ill-fortune.

## Historical Contexts
The "romantic" nature of certain historical periods is often highlighted by their adherence to knightly gallantry. The Third Crusade (1189), involving Frederick Barbarossa, the King of France, and Richard I of England, is described as the most courtly and romantic of all crusades, as the ideal of gallantry mitigated religious bitterness between the Christians and Saladin.
