# Possible, Impossible, Being, and Nothing: Logical Discussions

The logical discussion of existence, possibility, and the nature of being has been a central preoccupation of philosophy, evolving from early ontological speculations to the formal structures of logic and epistemology. These discussions often center on the tension between sensible phenomena and abstract rational truth.

## Ontological Theories of Being and Nothing
Early Greek philosophical inquiry focused primarily on attempts to explain existence. Parmenidês proposed a doctrine of the "One and All," arguing that the real, ultra-phenomenal substance was One. In this view, truth and knowledge were entirely removed from the senses and divested of sensible properties, conceived instead as an *Ens Rationis* and discussed using the most general language. Parmenidês denied the existence of real change in color or place.

This ontological theory was vehemently controverted by those who argued that the real was not One, but Many—divisible, movable, and changeable. In response, Parmenidês' pupil, Zeno, attacked the theory of the Many. Zeno maintained that motion and change of place were impossible and self-contradictory, utilizing logical difficulties derived from the infinite divisibility of matter to prove that the testimony of the senses was unworthy of trust. Similarly, Melissus argued in a similar vein to Zeno, attempting to demonstrate Parmenidês' doctrine by deducing impossible inferences from the opposite hypothesis.

## Epistemology and the Theory of Being
In the broader study of philosophy, a distinction is often drawn between epistemology and metaphysics. Epistemology is the department of thought concerned with the nature and origin of knowledge, specifically the possibility of knowledge in the abstract (*sub specie aeternitatis*). Metaphysics, by contrast, considers the nature of reality.

If philosophy is divided into the theory of knowing (Epistemology) and the theory of being (Ontology), the two are nearly impossible to separate entirely due to their close connection. Furthermore, the relationship between the theory of knowledge and logic is extremely tight; some thinkers identify the two, while others view epistemology as a subdivision of logic. Others restrict logic to "formal logic," defined as the science of the laws of thought.

## Formal Logic and the Syllogism
The application of logic to determine what is possible or necessary often employs the syllogism. A syllogism is a logical structure used to reach a conclusion based on premises. For example, the recluses of Port Royal utilized a syllogism to argue against the thinking capacity of animals: "No matter thinks; every soul of beast is matter: therefore no soul of beast thinks."

In formal logic, an "enthymeme" is a syllogistic argument that is incompletely stated, where one of the premises is omitted because it is obvious or naturally present to the mind. While Aristotle originally applied the term to syllogisms based on signs or probabilities, later usage—such as that found in the *Port Royal Logic*—refers to the deliberate suppression of a premise for dramatic or rhetorical effect.

Modern developments in logic continued with figures such as De Morgan, who published *Formal Logic, or the Calculus of Inference, Necessary and Probable* in 1847. De Morgan developed a new logic of relations and an onymatic system of logical expression, expanding the doctrine of the syllogism and the numerical definite syllogism.

## Medieval Scholasticism and the Role of Aristotle
During the growth of universities in Bologna, Oxford, and Paris, philosophical discussion focused heavily on logical questions. This discourse was based primarily on the logic of Aristotle, which became more widely known through Latin translations of an Arabic edition annotated by Averroes. 

The "schoolmen" of the medieval period engaged in a severely technical form of discussion. This technicality was necessitated by the presence of ignorant and intolerant church dignitaries who monitored for heresy, forcing thinkers to hint at ideas they dared not state openly. Despite being decried by some as futile, these discussions were a necessary struggle to correct inherent defects of the human mind.

## Perceptions of the Impossible
Logical discussions of the impossible often contrast human reason with divine power or physical reality. For instance, while it is considered possible for statues to sweat or make moaning sounds due to the decaying of wood and stone or the tearing of particles, it is regarded as altogether impossible for inanimate things to produce articulate human speech, as speech requires a body fitted with the organs of speech.

However, from a religious perspective, the ways of God are not as the ways of humans. Because God resembles mankind in nothing—neither in power, nature, movement, nor learning—it is not considered wonderful if He performs acts that seem impossible to human observers.
