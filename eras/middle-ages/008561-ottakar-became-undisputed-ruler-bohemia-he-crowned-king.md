# Premysl Ottakar and the Bohemian Monarchy

## Accession of Premysl Ottakar I
During a period characterized by constant internal struggles in Bohemia, the power of the sovereign declined while the nobility gained new privileges, as competitors for the crown relied heavily on noble support. This era also saw an increase in German influence. In 1197, Premysl Ottakar became the undisputed ruler of Bohemia. He was crowned as king in 1198, establishing a royal title for Bohemian sovereigns that continued uninterruptedly from that date. During his reign, King Ottakar I established a mint and a mining-office in the old mining town of Iglau.

## The Reign of Premysl Ottakar II
The lineage continued through Wenceslas I (1230–1253), who succeeded his father without opposition, though his final years were marked by internal discord. His son, Premysl Ottakar II, initially ruled Moravia under his father's sovereignty and served for a time as the leader of the malcontents, though he and his father reconciled before the latter's death.

Premysl Ottakar II is regarded as one of the greatest kings of Bohemia. Before ascending the Bohemian throne in 1253, he had already obtained possession of the archduchies of Austria. In 1251, the estates of Austria, weary of disorder and lacking central authority, had chosen him as their duke at Truebensee. This appointment was supported by the pope. Ottakar strengthened this position by marrying Margaret, the sister of Duke Frederick II, and promising assistance to William of Holland in exchange for his investiture.

## Territorial Expansion and Military Power
Ottakar II significantly expanded his dominions. Around the time he became king of Bohemia, the nobility of Styria recognized him as their ruler. Although King Bela IV of Hungary initially conquered Styria and signed a treaty with Ottakar in 1254 confirming his possession, the Styrians soon resented Hungarian rule. Ottakar utilized this resentment to interfere in the duchy, leading to war. Following a decisive victory over King Bela in the great battle of Kressenbrunn in 1260, a treaty in March 1261 recognized Ottakar as the duke of Styria.

In 1269, Ottakar inherited the duchy of Carinthia upon the death of Duke Ulrich III. At the height of his power, his possessions stretched from the Giant Mountains in Bohemia to the Adriatic, encompassing almost all areas west of the Leitha that would later comprise the Habsburg empire, as well as Istria and parts of northern Italy. Because of his immense wealth, contemporaries called him "the man of gold," and because of his military strength, they called him "the man of iron."

## Internal Policy and Later Years
Ottakar II sought to balance the overwhelming power of the Bohemian nobility by encouraging the immigration of Germans into his territories, hoping that German townsmen would serve as a counterpoise. In the duchies, he worked to restore order, benefit town inhabitants, and introduce German colonists into eastern districts.

As his power grew, Ottakar began to aspire to the German throne. While it is considered very probable that the German crown was offered to him, he refused it; factors contributing to this refusal included his Slavic nationality. In 1273, Rudolph, count of Habsburg, was elected king of the Romans instead.

## Succession and the End of the Dynasty
Following the death of Premysl Ottakar II, his son Wenceslas II ascended the throne at age seven. During his minority, Otto of Brandenburg, Ottakar's nephew, governed as guardian. Otto's unpopular rule led to an insurrection and a period of complete anarchy. Order was eventually restored through the intervention of Rudolph of Habsburg, and Wenceslas became the nominal ruler at age twelve.

Actual power was initially held by Zavis of Falkenstein, a great Bohemian noble who had married the king's mother, Kunegunda. Wenceslas ordered the beheading of Zavis in 1290, after which the nineteen-year-old king began governing Bohemia himself. His short reign was a period of great happiness; Poland accepted his rule, and the Hungarian crown was offered to him.

Wenceslas II died suddenly in 1305 while preparing to invade Austria during a war with Albert, archduke of Austria. He was succeeded by his sixteen-year-old son, Wenceslas III, who ruled for only one year. Wenceslas III was murdered by unknown assassins in 1306 while planning a warlike expedition against Poland. His death marked the end of the Premyslide dynasty's rule over Bohemia.
