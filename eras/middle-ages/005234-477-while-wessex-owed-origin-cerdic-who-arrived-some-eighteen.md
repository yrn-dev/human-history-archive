# The Kingdom of Wessex

## Origins and Foundation
The kingdom of Wessex was founded by the Saxons, according to the historian Bede. The archive contains conflicting accounts regarding its precise beginning. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle attributes the origin of Wessex to Cerdic, who arrived approximately eighteen years after the landing of Ella (or Ælle) in Sussex in 477. However, other records suggest the kingdom originated with the settlement of Cerdic and his followers in Hampshire in 495. Some sources caution that the dates provided by the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle may lack value and that the story of Cerdic's arrival is open to suspicion.

The establishment of the West Saxon monarchy is described as a laborous process spanning three martial generations. Cerdic, identified as one of the bravest children of Woden, spent his life conquering the Isle of Wight and Hampshire, though his progress was hindered by a loss at the battle of Mount Badon. His son, Kenric, expanded the territory into Wiltshire, where he besieged Salisbury and defeated a relief army. The power of the kingdom was further established by Cerdic's grandson, Ceaulin, who achieved two decisive victories, reduced the cities of Gloucester, Bath, and Cirencester, and killed three British kings, eventually extending his reach to the banks of the Severn. Additionally, some antiquarians suggest that in 508, Cerdic fought a battle against Natanleod, resulting in the death of Natanleod and five thousand of his subjects.

## Territorial Expansion and Administration
Beyond the initial conquests in Hampshire and Wiltshire, the tide of colonization spread over the district of Dorsetshire before the start of the 8th century, moving beyond the Kennet and Frome valleys. The shire of Dorset is explicitly named in the Saxon Chronicle in 845.

The social organization of Wessex differed significantly from that of Kent. In Wessex, the noble classes were categorized as *gesithcund* (originally *eorlcund*). The *wergeld* (compensation) for a *ceorl*, or ordinary freeman, was lower in Wessex than it was for the same class in Kent.

## Religious and Cultural Development
In 705, the West Saxon see was transferred to Sherborne. This move was followed by the foundation of numerous religious houses, which contributed to the industrial and social development of the region. Several West Saxon kings resided in Dorsetshire; specifically, Æthelred was buried at Wimborne, while Æthelbert and Æthelbald were buried at Sherborne.

Culturally, the reign of Alfred marked an effective beginning for English prose. While Latin remained the preferred language for literary works of pretension, kings of Wessex, following the example of the kings of Kent, published their laws in the native tongue.

## Conflict with the Danes and the Conquest
Wessex faced prolonged conflict with Danish invaders. In 787, the Danes landed at Portland, and in 833, they arrived at Charmouth with thirty-five ships to fight Ecgbert. In 845, the men of Dorsetshire, led by ealdorman Osric, routed the Danes at the mouth of the Parret. Later, in 876, the invaders captured Wareham, but they were driven out the following year by Alfred, during which time 120 Danish ships were wrecked at Swanage. Plundering expeditions continued for two centuries, including a visit by Canute to the mouth of the Frome in 1015. During Canute's reign, Wareham served as the shire town and a thriving seaport.

## The Reign of Egbert and Later History
Egbert, an exile from Wessex, was sheltered and assisted by Charlemagne, which enabled him to establish himself as King in Wessex in 802. Egbert subdued the Britons in Cornwall and, through a series of wars, became the first King of all England by 828.

At the time of the Norman Conquest, Dorset was part of the earldom of Harold. The region's resistance to the Conqueror resulted in a "merciless harrying" that devastated Shaftesbury, Wareham, and Dorchester, and completely ruined Bridport.
