# Breviary

The term "breviary" (from the Latin *breviarium*, meaning epitome or abridgment) refers to two distinct types of historical documents: the liturgical book used for the daily services of the Roman Catholic Church and a specific collection of Roman law compiled for the Visigoths.

## The Liturgical Breviary
The Breviary is the book containing the offices for the canonical hours, which constitute the daily service of the Roman Catholic Church. It includes hymns and lessons for every day of the year. While it is more comprehensive than the Anglican Book of Common Prayer in its daily lessons and hymns, it is less comprehensive because it excludes the Eucharistic office (found in the Missal) and special offices for ordination, burial, marriage, and baptism (found in the Pontifical or Ritual).

### Evolution and Structure
In early Christian worship, the Bible provided all necessary materials, including the psalms and lessons. The evolution of the Breviary began when the Psalter was separated into a choir-book. Initially, the leader of the choir or the bishop chose psalms as appropriate. By the 4th century, psalms were grouped together, a process encouraged by monks who recited all 150 psalms daily. To manage the time required, monks divided the day into hours and allotted a portion of the Psalter to each. In the 6th century, St Benedict drew up an arrangement based on an older, less skillful Roman division.

Over time, additions such as collects, responses, and antiphons were added for those unable to improvise metrical compositions. By the 12th century, liturgical author Jean Beleth listed several necessary books for the canonical office, including the *Psalterium*, *Collectarium*, *Antiphonarium*, *Homiliarius* (Gospel homilies), *Sermologus* (sermons), the Old and New Testaments, the *Legendarius* (saints), and the *Passionarius* (martyrs).

The modern Roman Breviary is typically printed in four seasonal volumes (winter, spring, summer, autumn). Each part comprises:
1. The Psalter
2. *Proprium de Tempore* (special office of the season)
3. *Proprium Sanctorum* (special offices of saints)
4. *Commune Sanctorum* (general offices for saints)
5. Extra Services

The Psalter serves as the backbone of the book. Its current arrangement dates to the 8th century: Psalms 1–108 (with omissions) are recited at Matins; omissions are said at Compline, Prime, and Lauds; Psalms 109–147 (excluding 117, 118, and 142) are said at Vespers; and Psalms 148–150 are used at Lauds. The text used is the "Gallican" version, which was Jerome's second revision (A.D. 392) of the *Itala* version.

### Usage and Regulation
Every member of a religious order and every clerk in orders is required to read the daily allotted services aloud. In large churches, these are grouped: Matins and Lauds (approx. 7:30 A.M.); Prime, Terce, Sext, and None (approx. 10 A.M.); and Vespers and Compline (4 P.M.). This process can take between four and eight hours daily.

Until the Council of Trent, bishops had the power to regulate the Breviary of their own diocese, and monastic communities maintained their own versions. Pope Pius V (1566–1572) made the Roman Breviary obligatory everywhere, except for those that could prove 200 years of existence. The Roman version eventually superseded almost all local "uses," though dioceses may still add offices for specially venerated saints.

## Revisions and Regional Variants
The Roman Breviary has seen several revisions:
* **1536:** Francis Quignonez produced a remarkable revision. Although not accepted by Rome, it served as the model for the Church of England's 1549 reform.
* **1588:** Sixtus V introduced the revised Vulgate text.
* **1602:** Clement VIII revised the rubrics via Bellarmine and Baronius.
* **1623–1644:** Urban VIII altered the text of the hymns.

In France, a 17th and 18th-century movement led by Jean de Launoy and Louis Sébastien le Nain de Tillemont sought to remove false lives of saints. The Port Royal school further influenced this by requiring all responses and antiphons to be exact words of Scripture. These reformed French Breviaries, such as those by Archbishop François de Harlay (1680) and Archbishop Charles Gaspard Guillaume de Vintimille (1736), were eventually suppressed during the pontificate of Pius IX by an Ultramontane movement led by Dom Gueranger and Louis François Veuillot. The last diocese to surrender was Orleans in 1875.

Other notable versions include the Sarum (Salisbury) Breviary, which was widely used and favored in Scotland as a protest against the jurisdiction of the church of York. A specific Scottish form of the Sarum Office, the Aberdeen Breviary, was revised by Bishop William Elphinstone and printed in 1509–1510.

## Breviary of Alaric
The *Breviarium Alaricanum* is a collection of Roman law compiled in A.D. 506 by order of Alaric II, king of the Visigoths. It includes the Institutes of Gaius, sixteen books of the Theodosian code, the Gregorian code (thirteen titles), the Hermogenian code (two titles), and fragments of the *Responsa Papiniani* and *Sententiae Receptae* of Julius Paulus.

Unlike the code of Justinian, the Breviary of Alaric includes both juridical treatises (*jura*) and imperial constitutions (*leges*). Because a royal rescript directed that copies certified by Anianus, the king's referendary, be received exclusively as law, some writers call it the Breviary of Anianus. It was known among Visigoths as "Lex Romana" or "Lex Theodosii"; the title "Breviarium" was only introduced in the 16th century to distinguish it from a 9th-century recast used by Romans in Lombardy.
