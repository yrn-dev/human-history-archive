# Collects

Collects are short prayers used within the Roman Catholic Church's daily service, known as the canonical hours. They are positioned at the close of the office and serve to sum up the supplications of the congregation.

## Origins and Evolution
The practice of using collects arose from a primitive tradition performed by the local president, or bishop. Evidence of this early practice is found in the letters of Cyprian and Clement of Rome, as well as in the *Didache* (the Teaching of the Apostles). 

In the early stages of Christian worship, which followed Jewish custom, the Bible provided all necessary materials, including the psalms and lessons. The evolution of the Breviary began with the separation of the Psalter into a choir-book. Initially, the leader of the choir or the bishop chose psalms based on what he deemed appropriate. By the 4th century, psalms began to be grouped together, a process accelerated by monastic practices. Monks recited the 150 psalms daily, eventually spreading this task over a week by dividing the day into hours and allotting a portion of the Psalter to each. St Benedict established such an arrangement in the 6th century, likely based on an older, less skillful Roman division.

As the church order crystallized, improvisation in prayer was largely replaced by set forms. Collects, along with responses, antiphons, and other short prayers, were added to the psalter choir-books to assist those who were not skilled in metrical compositions or improvisation.

## Function and Content
Collects are one of the constituent elements of the hours of the office, alongside hymns, lessons, antiphons, responsories, versicles, little chapters, and psalms. In the context of the Breviary, collects are used to summarize the dominant idea of the specific festival with which they are associated.

## Liturgical Collections and Guides
The development of set prayer forms led to the creation of collections that eventually became Orationals and Sacramentaries. The collects found in the Breviary are drawn largely from other Sacramentaries, such as the Gelasian. 

The 12th-century liturgical author Jean Beleth listed the *Collectarium* as one of the necessary books for the correct conduct of the canonical office, alongside the *Psalterium*, the Old and New Testaments, the *Antiphonarium*, the *Homiliarius*, the *Sermologus*, the *Passionarius* (concerning martyrs), and the *Legendarius* (concerning saints), as well as the works of the Fathers.

To assist in the proper reading of the office and to help harmonize the *Proprium Sanctorum* and the *Proprium de Tempore*, the church utilizes thirty-seven chapters of general rubrics. Further guidance is provided by the *Ordo Recitandi Divini Officii*, a type of Catholic Churchman's Almanack published by various dioceses and countries, which provides minute daily directions for reading.
