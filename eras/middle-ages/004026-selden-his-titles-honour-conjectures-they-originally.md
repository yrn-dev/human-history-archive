# Selden's Titles of Honour

John Selden's work, *Titles of Honour* (specifically the 1672 edition), provides extensive conjectures and analysis regarding the origins, usage, and evolution of various titles of nobility, judicial offices, and chivalric ranks. His writings address a wide array of designations, ranging from the judicial Barons of the Exchequer to the military rank of knight banneret and the imperial title of despot.

## Barons of the Exchequer
In analyzing the Barons of the Exchequer—who were formerly a group of six judges consisting of a chief baron and five puisne barons responsible for justice in revenue causes between the king and his subjects—Selden conjectures that these individuals were originally chosen from among the barons of the kingdom. This origin, according to Selden, explains the use of the name "baron" for these judicial officers.

## Knights Banneret and Baronets
Selden provides detailed observations on the rank of knight banneret, a feudal designation for nobles who held the right to lead vassals into battle under their own banner. He notes that in English custom, a knight banneret could only be created in the field, and only when the king was present or the royal standard was displayed. The ceremony involved the solemn delivery of a banner charged with the recipient's arms, or the cutting of a pennon's end to transform it into a square banner. Selden traces the creation of these bannerets to the era of Edward I, noting that bannerets received wages based on the number of esquires and knights bachelors who served under them.

Regarding the relationship between "banneret" and "baronet," Selden observes that "old stories" frequently use the term *baronetti* where *bannereti* was intended. He also notes that in France, the title of banneret had become hereditary. However, Selden is careful to state that the title of banneret has no relation to the later title of baronet created by King James I. He further indicates, drawing from parliament rolls, that the term banneret was occasionally used in England as an equivalent to "baron."

## The Title of Despot
Selden's work also touches upon the title of "despot." In the Roman empire of the East, this was used as a title of address for the emperor. Alexius I (1081-1118) extended the title to his brothers, sons, and sons-in-law. Selden notes that the title was not confined solely to the heir-apparent by Alexius II.

## Other Titles and Ceremonies
Selden's research extends to various other honors and legal titles:

*   **The Title of "Honourable":** In his 1672 work, Selden does not include "honourable" among the courtesy titles granted to the children of peers.
*   **Knighthood Ceremonies:** Selden, along with other sources such as Favyn and Menestrier, notes that while details of knighthood ceremonies varied by time and place, the core military and ecclesiastical elements remained largely the same. Selden specifically quotes the forms for the blessing or consecration of new knights found in the *Pontificale Romanum* and the English Common Prayer Book used before the Reformation.
*   **Legal Works:** Selden held an early 14th-century manuscript entitled *Summa de legibus Anglie que vocatur Bretone*. He expressed the opinion that this work, known as *Britton*, derived its title from Henry de Bracton (the last of the chief justiciaries) and served as a royal abridgment of Bracton's work on English laws and customs, supplemented with later statutes.
