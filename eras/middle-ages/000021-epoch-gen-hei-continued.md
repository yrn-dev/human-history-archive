# THE EPOCH OF THE GEN AND THE HEI (CONTINUED)

The Epoch of the Gen and the Hei refers to a period of significant turbulence in Japanese history defined by the struggle between two great families: the Minamoto (pronounced "Gen") and the Taira (pronounced "Hei"). These two clans are frequently referred to together as the "Gen-Pei."

## The Taira (Hei) Clan
The Taira family is descended from Prince Katsurabara and served as generals of the Imperial guards. Known by the Chinese name "Heike," the family rose to prominence during the time of Sadamori, who quelled the insurrection of Masakado. The Taira established various branches, including the Ise-Heishi (descended from Sadamori's son, Korehira), the Shiro-uji (descended from Koremochi and Shigemori), and the Soma family, who succeeded to the domains of Masakado. Other branches included the Daijo-uji of Hitachi, the Nishina-uji, the Iwaki-uji, and the Chiba-no-suke and Miura-no-suke of Musashi, Kazusa, and Shimosa.

During the Heiji tumult, the Taira crushed the Minamoto and secured the most important offices. However, they eventually entered into a quarrel with the Minamoto, leading to a period of struggle. Notable figures of the clan included Tadamori and Atsumori (1169–84), the latter of whom was killed at Ichi-no-tani.

## The Minamoto (Gen) Clan
The Minamoto recognized the line of Tsunemoto, the commander-in-chief of local governments, as their principal family line. From Tsunemoto descended several illustrious chiefs, including Mitsunaka, Yorimitsu, Yorinobu, and Yoriyoshi. The lineage continued through Yoshiiye to Tameyoshi, and subsequently to Yoshitomo.

Yoshitomo's sons included Yoshinaka of Kiso, Yoritomo, Noriyori, and Yoshitsune. Following a great struggle in 1160, Yoritomo—then fourteen years old—was captured by Taira Munekiyo and taken to Kyoto for execution. Yoritomo avoided death by winning the sympathy of Munekiyo through his calm demeanor and his stated desire to live so that he alone might pray for the memories of his father and elder brothers. Munekiyo, with the help of Kiyomori's step-mother, succeeded in saving the boy.

## Military Feudalism and Social Change
The Gen-Hei epoch marked a shift in the nature of rewards for successful soldiers, as the manor became the principal prize. This led to an increasing greed for domains. When Yoritomo assumed the roles of *so-jito* (general steward) and *so-tsuihoshi* (commander-in-chief) of the country, he appointed his meritorious vassals as *jito* and *shugo* in each province. This transition shifted local authority from the Throne to military families.

Military feudalism was practically established after the Shokyu struggle, when the *shugo* and *jito* took actual possession of the estates they had previously administered. While the Hojo initially brought this system into esteem through astute and just administration, the later Ashikaga regime saw the pursuit of landed possession become the sole aim of existence. This resulted in a decline of fealty and paternal relations, as individualism became paramount and social obligations were ignored.

## Impact on Society and Religion
The Gen-Hei struggle plunged the empire into a state of turbulence that caused pitiable misery for the lower orders. This era of conflict coincided with a discrepancy between religious precept and practice. Despite the "True Word" creed and other doctrines emphasizing the sacredness of life, preachers and inmates of great monasteries frequently resorted to violence and bloodshed to defend temporal privileges or pursue ambitious designs.

Additionally, the military era saw a decline in the influence of ethical primers that had previously been part of a literary education. The period was characterized by a lack of loyalty among both masters and servants; for example, Matsunaga Hisahide poisoned his liege lord to bring down the Miyoshi family and slew the shogun Yoshiteru to overthrow the Ashikaga.
