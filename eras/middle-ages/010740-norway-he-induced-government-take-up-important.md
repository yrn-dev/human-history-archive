# Norway

## Early Governance and Monarchy
The history of Norway's government is characterized by various claims of hereditary right and the influence of foreign powers. King Canute the Great, who ruled England and Denmark, asserted a hereditary right to all of Norway. While some people had previously supported King Olaf Haraldson, others felt they had lost their self-government due to his power and sought the friendship of Canute.

Another instance of governance involved Svein, the son of King Canute and Alfifa. After being appointed to govern Jomsborg in Vindland, Svein was instructed by his father to proceed to Norway to take charge of the kingdom and assume the title of king. Accompanied by his mother and Earl Harald, Svein was received as king at every Law-thing in the country, including Viken and the Throndhjem country.

Internal power struggles also shaped the region's administration. Gunhild, the King-mother, and her sons frequently consulted on the government of the country. Gunhild encouraged her sons to secure their father's heritage in the Throndhjem country, arguing that it was improper to allow an earl to rule such a large kingdom while they focused on viking cruises.

## Religious Development and the Episcopate
Christianity was introduced to Norway around the year 1000 and following years by Leif Ericsson, acting at the instance of King Olaf Trygvasson. Later, during the era of Harald's sons, Cardinal Nikolas was sent from Rome by the pope. The cardinal improved many customs of the Northmen and established that the archbishop's seat should be located in Christ church in Nidaros, where King Olaf the Saint reposes. Prior to this, Norway had only common bishops.

In more recent history, Norway maintained a Lutheran state church. In this system, the king is recognized as the *summus episcopus*, or the supreme authority in ecclesiastical matters, though this power is somewhat limited by the parliament. The king manages this authority through a minister who oversees education and religion. The rite of ordination remains in the hands of the bishops.

## Territorial Extensions and Colonies
Norway's crown once extended to Greenland. The Greenlanders, who had a republican organization, were induced to swear allegiance to the king of Norway between 1247 and 1261. Greenland remained under the Norwegian crown until 1814, when the union between Denmark and Norway was dissolved. Because Greenland, Iceland, and the Faeroes were not mentioned during this dissolution, they were kept by the Danish king.

The Norse colonies in Greenland consisted of the *Oster Bygd* (eastern settlement) and *Vester Bygd* (western settlement). At their peak, these settlements were estimated to have between 3,333 and 10,000 inhabitants. The colonies featured twelve churches, one nunnery, and one monastery in the Osterbygd, and four churches in the Vesterbygd. Communication with Norway eventually ceased after the middle of the 14th century, partly because the king of Norway established a monopoly on shipping and trade, sending only one ship to Greenland at long intervals.

## Folklore and Cultural Preservation
In the 19th century, Peter Christen Asbjornsen (1812-1885) and Jorgen Engebretsen Moe (1813-1882) collaborated to collect Norwegian folklore. Asbjornsen began collecting fairy stories and legends in 1832, later traveling throughout Norway on foot. Moe, a student of theology, also collected stories while wandering through remote mountain districts.

The two friends began working in concert in 1834. Between 1842 and 1844, they published the first two volumes of *Norwegian Popular Stories (Norske Folkeeventyr)*, which gained recognition across Europe as a contribution to literature and comparative mythology. Sir George Dasent translated many of these stories into English in 1859. Additionally, Asbjornsen published a separate collection of Norwegian fairy tales (*huldreeventyr og folkesagn*) in 1845.

## Industry and Geography
The town of Kongsberg, located in Buskerud *amt* (county) on the Laagen, is a mining town whose industry is tied to government silver-mines. These mines were first discovered by a peasant in 1623. During the 18th century, Kongsberg was more significant than it is in the modern era, possessing double its 1900 population of 5,585. In addition to the silver-mines, the town houses a mint, smelting-works, and a Government weapon factory.
