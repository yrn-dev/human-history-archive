# Boiling to Death

## Overview and Legal History
Boiling to death was a form of punishment that was once common on the European continent and in England. In England, the only extant legislative notice regarding this practice is an act passed in 1531 during the reign of Henry VIII. This specific statute provided that individuals convicted of poisoning should be boiled to death. While the 1531 act is the only remaining legislative record, the punishment is frequently mentioned in earlier contexts as a penalty for coining. The practice was eventually abolished in England when the act was repealed in 1547 during the reign of Edward VI.

## Notable Cases and Execution Methods
Records from the *Chronicles of the Grey Friars* (published by the Camden Society in 1852) describe the method of execution used for poisoning at Smithfield in 1522. In this instance, the condemned man was fastened to a chain and lowered into boiling water repeatedly until death occurred.

The preamble of the 1531 statute of Henry VIII, which classified poisoning as treason, cites the case of Richard Roose (also referred to as Coke). Roose, a cook, poisoned food intended for the poor of the parish of Lambeth and the household of the bishop of Rochester, resulting in the deaths of a man and a woman. Found guilty of treason, Roose was sentenced to be boiled to death without benefit of clergy and was executed publicly at Smithfield.

Other documented cases from the same period include:
*   **King's Lynn (1531):** A maid-servant was boiled for the poisoning of her mistress.
*   **Smithfield (1542):** Margaret Davy, a servant, was boiled for poisoning her employer.
