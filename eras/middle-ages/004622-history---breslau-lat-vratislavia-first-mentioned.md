# Breslau

Breslau, also known by the Latin name *Vratislavia*, is a city first mentioned in the year A.D. 1000 by the chronicler Thietmar, the bishop of Merseburg. It is believed to have been founded several years prior to this mention.

## Early History and Medieval Development
Early in the 11th century, Breslau was established as the seat of a bishop. After a period of being part of Poland, the city became the capital of an independent duchy in 1163. In 1241, the city was destroyed by the Mongols, though it recovered its prosperity shortly thereafter, aided by a large influx of German colonists. By 1290, the bishop of the city obtained the title of a prince of the Empire.

In 1195, Jaroslaw, the son of Boleslaus I. of Lower Silesia, inherited the duchy of Neisse. Jaroslaw became the bishop of Breslau in 1198 and, upon his death in 1201, bequeathed the duchy to his successors in the see. To this day, the bishop of Breslau retains the title of prince bishop and continues to hold the Austrian part of Neisse.

## Shifts in Sovereignty
The city's political control shifted several times between the 14th and 16th centuries. Following the death of Henry VI, the last duke of Breslau, in 1335, the city was purchased by John, king of Bohemia. His successors maintained control until approximately 1460. During the Bohemian period, the kings granted various privileges to Breslau, which allowed the city to expand its commerce. This increase in wealth led the citizens to adopt a more independent stance.

Due to a dislike of the Hussites, Breslau sought the protection of Pope Pius II in 1463. A few years later, the city came under the rule of the Hungarian king, Matthias Corvinus. After Corvinus died in 1490, the city returned to Bohemian subjecthood. In 1526, when Ferdinand (later emperor) was chosen as king of Bohemia, Breslau and the rest of Silesia passed to the Habsburgs.

## Prussian and French Conflicts
Breslau remained largely undisturbed during the Thirty Years' War and the Reformation. However, in 1741, the city was forced to acknowledge the authority of Frederick the Great. The Austrians recovered the city in 1757, but Frederick regained it later that same year following his victory at Leuthen. Since then, the city has belonged to Prussia, with two brief exceptions: the French held the city for a few days in 1807 after the battle of Jena, and again in 1813 after the battle of Bautzen.

In 1807, the French dismantled the city's fortifications. King Frederick William III later gave these sites to the civic authorities, who converted them into promenades. In March 1813, King Frederick William III issued two stirring appeals to the Prussians from Breslau: *An mein Volk* and *An mein Kriegesheer*. The city served as the center for Prussian preparations for the campaign that concluded at Leipzig. Later, in 1866, following the Prussian victory at Sadowa, William I made a complimentary and triumphant entry into the city.

## Academic and Intellectual Activity
Breslau has served as a center for higher education and scholarship. Julius Furst (1805–1873), a German Orientalist, studied oriental literature in Breslau, among other cities. The city has also been a site of academic publication; works published in Breslau include J. Beloch's *Campanien* (1890), A. Wagner's *Die unteritalischen Normannen und das Papsttum 1086-1150* (1885), and Bauch's biography of Epaminondas (1834).
