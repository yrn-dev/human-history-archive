# Manuscripts and Autographs in Historical Collections

The study of manuscripts (MSS.) provides critical evidence for the history of art, textual transmission, and the lives of historical figures. Collections such as those in the British Museum, the Cottonian library, and various European archives preserve a range of documents from the early middle ages through the Renaissance.

## Autograph Manuscripts
Autographs of famous men from the early middle ages are rare and often difficult to identify. For instance, a charter at Canterbury claiming to be written by Dunstan is likely a copy, as a duplicate with the same claim exists in the British Museum. However, several confirmed autograph manuscripts do exist, including the chronicles of Sigebert of Gembloux, Robert de Monte, and Ordericus Vitalis. 

The Cottonian MSS. contain undoubtedly autograph writings of Matthew of Paris, who served as the English chronicler during the reign of Henry III. Other examples of autograph documents include the writings of the historian Joinville in French archives and documents in the hand of William of Wykeham held in the British Museum. By the 15th century, the Paston Letters—the correspondence of the Norfolk family of Paston—provide numerous autographs of historical personages.

## Byzantine and Early Christian Manuscripts
Early Byzantine manuscript work is scarce, but surviving fragments illustrate a high level of ornamentation. Two fragmentary leaves of the Eusebian canons (Brit. Mus., Add. MS. 5111), likely executed in the Eastern capital in the 6th century, feature bright colors and classical style designs on entirely gilt vellum. Other significant specimens from the late 6th century include the Gospels of Rossano in southern Italy and the Genesis of the Imperial Library of Vienna; both utilize silver-written texts on purple vellum, though their paintings lack gold.

The Greek MS. of Genesis, once part of the Cottonian collection, was characterized by classical style miniatures with gold shading, though it was almost entirely destroyed by fire. Another notable example is the MS. of Dioscorides at Vienna, executed in 472, which maintains a classical sentiment in its paintings. The *Natural History* of Dioscorides is specifically noted for the accuracy of its botanical drawings, a level of observation that disappeared for a millennium.

## Development of Insular and European Styles
In Britain, the Celtic style of ornamentation spread from Ireland to Scotland—specifically St Columba's foundation at Iona—and then southward. By the early 8th century, this style was practiced at Canterbury, as seen in a British Museum psalter (Cott. MS. Vespasian A. 1). This same manuscript shows the influence of Rome's classical style, likely introduced by St Augustine and subsequent missionaries to centers like Winchester and Canterbury, as well as northern monasteries in York and those of Wearmouth and Jarrow.

The school of Lindisfarne, founded by Aidan (a monk of Iona) in the kingdom of Northumbria, produced finely ornamented Celtic MSS. The most perfect example is the *Lindisfarne Gospels* (also known as the *Durham Book* or *St Cuthbert's Gospels*), held in the Cottonian collection. While primarily Celtic, it uses sparing amounts of gold and features figures of the four evangelists copied from Byzantine models. This Neapolitan influence is attributed to the visit of Adrian and Theodore, archbishop of Canterbury, to Lindisfarne.

Another significant work, the *Utrecht Psalter* (formerly in the Cottonian Library), dates to approximately 800 and was likely executed in north or north-east France. It is characterized by minute pen-sketches and serves as a link to late classical art from two or three centuries prior.

## Italian Illumination and Later Works
Italian illumination grew rapidly, reaching a peak of splendor in the 15th century. Early evidence of Italian skill appears in the middle of the 12th century, such as two masterly paintings of the Virgin in the *Winchester Psalter* (Cottonian MS. Nero C. iv.). By the 14th century, particularly in Florence, illumination expanded with energy. Examples include the Royal MS. 6, E. ix. (an address to Robert of Anjou, 1334-1342) and the Add. MS. 27,428 of legends of the saints (c. 1370). Over time, this art extended to large folio choral books and law books, such as the Decretals.

## Textual Transmission and Distribution
The study of Greek uncials from the 4th century has historically been central to textual evidence, though some critics argue that versions and quotations from the 2nd and 3rd centuries provide more important, if less complete, evidence.

Regarding the distribution of specific works, the *Travels* of Marco Polo exist in approximately eighty registered manuscripts in various languages. A large portion of these are Pipino’s condensed Latin Translation, released around 1320. In comparison, Dante’s great Poem is reckoned to have nearly 500 MSS., while the *Tresor* of Brunetto Latini has some fifty, and the *Travels* of Friar Odoric have at least forty-two.
