# Lord Berkeley

The name Berkeley is associated with various figures of historical significance across different eras, including members of the English nobility and high-ranking naval officers.

## Early Nobility and the Manor of Redcliffe
In the early history of Bristol, the manor of Bedminster was divided. The eastern half was held by the Templars, having been gifted by Earl Robert of Gloucester. The western half, known as Redcliffe, was sold by the same earl to Robert FitzHardinge, who later became Lord Berkeley. While the Templars acquiesced to the struggle of Bristol to absorb the manor without much difficulty, the owners of Redcliffe resisted the union for nearly one hundred years. This resistance was supported by the fact that the owners of Redcliffe possessed their own quay, market, fair, and manorial courts.

## Lord Thomas de Berkeley
Lord Thomas de Berkeley (1245–1321) operated within a feudal system where the person of a woman was often inseparable from her fief. In this system, parents or guardians frequently handed over lands and women together as business bargains, rather than following the Church's theoretical insistence on the freedom of Christian marriage. Lord Thomas de Berkeley utilized the practice of buying and selling wardships and marriages as a regular and considerable source of income.

## George Berkeley, 1st Earl Berkeley
George Berkeley, the 1st Earl Berkeley (1628–1698), resided at Cranford House in Middlesex. He served as a patron to Fuller, who acted as his chaplain and was granted the Cranford rectory in 1658. In 1659, Fuller inscribed a work titled *The Appeal of Injured Innocence* (a reply to Heylyn's *Examen Historicum*) to the Earl.

## Sir Berkeley Milne
Sir Berkeley Milne was a naval officer and Commander-in-Chief, Mediterranean, during the onset of the First World War in August 1914. He was tasked by the Admiralty with the objective of shadowing the German vessel *Goeben*. On August 3, he received instructions to maintain a watch on the mouth of the Adriatic but to prioritize the *Goeben*, following and shadowing her wherever she went in preparation for a probable declaration of war.

By August 4, Milne reported that the *Indefatigable* and *Indomitable* were shadowing the *Goeben* and the *Breslau*. The Admiralty instructed him to hold the vessel and prevent her by force from interfering with French transports. However, a delay occurred in communicating the news that the *Goeben* was at Messina; this information did not reach London until 6 p.m. on August 5. Although the Admiralty eventually gave Sir Berkeley Milne permission to chase the vessel through the Straits, it was realized too late that the *Goeben* was escaping unblocked to the Southward.

Following these events, the Board of Admiralty conducted a careful examination of Milne's conduct and dispositions regarding the *Goeben* and *Breslau*. The First Sea Lord opined that Admiral Milne had taken the best measures possible with the force at his disposal and that his dispositions were proper. The Board of Admiralty officially approved the measures taken by Sir Berkeley Milne in all respects, noting that they successfully prevented the Germans from interrupting French troops crossing from Africa.
