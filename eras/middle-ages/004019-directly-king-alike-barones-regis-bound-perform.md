# Baron

The term "baron" has a varied history across Europe, evolving from a general description of a man to a specific rank of nobility and a defined legal status within feudal systems.

## Origins and Development in England
Introduced into England during the Conquest, the word "baron" was originally of uncertain origin and denoted "the man"—specifically one who had performed homage to a great lord or the king. In the early feudal structure, all individuals who held land "in chief" (directly) from the king were considered *barones regis*. These individuals were bound to perform a stipulated service and were, at least in theory, members of the king's council.

The terminology was also used by great nobles, including earls, to refer to their own tenants as "barons." This was particularly evident in palatinates. In the palatinate of Chester, the earl's barons were a recognized body, exemplified by the Venables family ("barons of Kinderton"), who existed until 1679. Similarly, in the palatinate of Durham, the bishop had barons, such as the Hiltons of Hilton Castle, who used the style "Barons of Hilton" until 1746.

The term was applied more loosely in other contexts. Henry II referred to "mes baruns de Lundres," and charters regarding the election of a mayor mentioned the "barons of our city of London." In London, aldermen were loosely deemed equivalent to barons and were assessed for the poll-tax as such under Richard II. In Ireland, the title was common due to the palatine nature of great lordships, including the barons of Slane, Naas, and Galtrim.

## Feudal Obligations and Hierarchy
Under the system established by the Conqueror, lay lands in England were divided among followers to be held by the service of a fixed number of knights. This system was also adopted in Ireland under Henry II. The tenants-in-chief, or barons, were required to provide knights for the host. The quota was based on the *constabularia* (a unit of ten knights); principal barons provided one or more of these units, while lesser barons might be called upon for five knights. Barons fulfilled this obligation either by hiring knights or through subenfeoffment, assigning portions of their fief to under-tenants.

Over time, a distinction emerged between "greater" and "lesser" barons. By the reign of Henry II, the *Dialogus de Scaccario* distinguished between greater and lesser baronies. By 1164, the practice of sending special summons to the council was reserved for greater barons, while the Magna Carta (1215) stipulated that lesser barons be summoned only through sheriffs. This distinction eventually restricted the privileges of peerage to the greater barons.

## The Title in Continental Europe
On the European continent, the title *baro* originally meant "man" in Salic and other "barbarian" laws. It was used in the Assize of Jerusalem to mean "husband." Gradually, the term evolved to mean a "strong or powerful man" and eventually a "magnate."

In 12th-century France, *barones* referred specifically to lords possessing an important fief held of one overlord alone, subject to primogeniture. By the 13th century, the Register of King Philip Augustus placed the *barones regis Francie* as vassals of the second rank, positioned next to dukes and counts holding in chief. By the end of that century, the title implied the bearer held his principal fief directly from the crown, making the status more important than that of a count, as many counts were only mediate vassals. Consequently, kings granting an apanage to sons or brothers used the phrase *in comitatum et baroniam*.

However, by the 14th century, the feudal hierarchy became stereotyped, and barons were ranked below both counts and viscounts, regardless of their actual power. Until the 17th century, the title required the holder to possess a territorial barony. Louis XIV later "cheapened" the title by creating numerous barons via royal letters.

## Peerage and Ceremonial Status
In England, barons created by patent were solemnly invested by the king, who enrobed the peer in a scarlet "robe of estate." This ceremony continued until the reign of James I, after which the delivery of letters patent was deemed sufficient. These patents defined the limits of inheritance, typically to the grantee and heirs male of his body, though exceptions existed for collateral male relatives, heirs-male of a daughter, or heirs-male of a sister. In one instance in 1821, the barony of Rayleigh was bestowed upon a lady with remainder to the heirs-male of her body.

The ceremonial dress of a baron is similar to that of an earl, but with only two rows of spots on each shoulder and two guards of white fur on parliamentary robes. King Charles II granted barons a coronet featuring six large pearls. A baron's style is "Right Honourable," and they are addressed by the sovereign as "Right Trusty and Well-beloved." Their children are entitled to the prefix "The Honourable."

## Other Historical Occurrences
The term appears in various other historical contexts:
* **Sicily:** A contemporary historian noted that the title of Roger (reigning 1127–1135) was founded on merit, power, the ancient royalty of Sicily and Palermo, and the consent of the barons.
* **Corsica:** Feudal barons in the south resisted Genoese governors. In the late 14th century, the barons of Cape Corso appealed to Genoa against Arrigo, count of La Rocca.
* **Romania:** The barons of Romania once borrowed 13,134 pieces of gold using the holy crown as credit. When they failed to perform the contract, the relic was lodged at Venice.
