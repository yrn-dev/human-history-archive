# Legends of the Saints in the Scottish Dialect of the Fourteenth Century

*Legends of the Saints in the Scottish Dialect of the Fourteenth Century* is a three-volume work published in 1896 by the Scottish Text Society. Edited by W. M. Metcalfe, the publication presents a long Scots poem consisting of over 33,000 lines. The text focuses on the legends of saints as they were recounted in other legendaries and the *Legenda Aurea*.

## Discovery and Content
The poem was discovered in the university library of Cambridge by Henry Bradshaw. The work is characterized by its specific regional focus; it excludes the lives of English saints while inserting the lives of St Ninian and St Machar, the latter being the patron saint of Aberdeen. 

The text provides evidence regarding the "district" of its author, which is identified as the north-east of Scotland. This is evidenced in the fortieth legend (concerning St Ninian), where the author tells a "lytil tale" about a man named John Balormy, described as a man of "ful gud fame" who was born in Elgin, Moray. The author notes his personal familiarity with this individual, stating, "for I kend hyme weile mony day."

## Authorship Debates
The authorship of the *Legends of the Saints* has been a subject of scholarly disagreement. C. Horstmann upholds the claim that the work was authored by Barbour, printing the text in his two-volume *Barbours des schottischen Nationaldichters Legendensammlung nebst den Fragmenten seines Trojanerkrieges* (1881-1882). Horstmann also included the legend of St Machor in his *Altenglische Legenden. Neue Folge* (1881).

Conversely, W. M. Metcalfe disputes the claim that Barbour is the author. While the ascription to Barbour was initially considered plausible due to similarities in style, dialect, and verse-length compared to Barbour's accepted works, later criticism has tended toward a negative judgment. These opposing views are based primarily on the analysis of vocabulary, assonance, and rhymes. Despite these debates, the available data has not provided a definitive answer as to whether the north-east Scots author is indeed Barbour.

## Scholarly Context and Bibliography
The study of this text is situated within a broader body of philological and historical research. Scholars such as Skeat and Koeppel have engaged with the material, and P. Buss addressed the question of Barbour's authorship in *Sind die von Horstmann herausgegebenen schottischen Legenden ein Werk Barberes?* (1886). Additionally, Metcalfe utilized the work to provide evidence regarding Gospel-stories.
