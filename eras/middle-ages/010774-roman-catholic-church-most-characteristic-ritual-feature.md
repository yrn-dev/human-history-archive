# Ritual Features of the Roman Catholic Church

The Roman Catholic Church employs a variety of ritual practices and liturgical objects that carry specific symbolic meanings, ranging from the celebration of the Mass to the observance of specific festivals.

## The Festival of Ascension
In the Roman Catholic Church, the most characteristic ritual feature of the festival of Ascension is the solemn extinction of the paschal candle. This occurs after the Gospel at high mass. The paschal candle is lit at every mass for the forty days following Easter to symbolize the presence of Christ with his disciples; consequently, its extinction symbolizes his parting from them.

This specific custom dates back to 1263 and was originally limited to the Franciscans. It was later prescribed for the universal church by the Congregation of Rites on May 19, 1697. Other rituals associated with this feast have since become obsolete, such as the blessing of new beans following the Commemoration of the Dead in the canon of the mass. During the middle ages, some churches practiced a ritual where an image of Christ was raised from the altar through a hole in the roof, followed immediately by the throwing down of a burning straw figure representing Satan.

## Liturgical Objects of the Mass
The Roman Catholic Church utilizes specific linen cloths during the service of the Mass. One such object is the corporal, a small square linen cloth placed on the altar beneath the paten and the chalice. The corporal represents the winding-sheet used by Joseph of Arimathea to wrap the body of the dead Christ. 

The corporal must be blessed by a priest with special faculties or by a bishop, using ritual prayers that invoke a divine blessing so the linen is worthy to enwrap and cover the blood and body of the Lord. While the corporal was originally large enough to cover the entire altar surface—a practice still observed by the Carthusians—the chalice is now covered by a separate small square of linen stiffened with cardboard known as the pall. When these items are not in use, the pall and corporal are carried in a square silken pocket called a burse.

## The Latin Rite and Global Influence
The Latin ritual and rite have been extended beyond Europe. In the late 13th century, John of Monte Corvino, who later became the Archbishop of Peking (Cambaluc), converted King George to the Catholic faith. King George, formerly of the Nestorian sect, took the Lesser Orders and attended mass in his royal robes. He constructed a magnificent church in honor of the Pope, the Holy Trinity, and God, naming it "the Roman Church." During his lifetime, Monte Corvino celebrated mass in this church according to the Latin rite and had been in treaty with the king to translate the entire Latin ritual so it could be sung throughout the king's territory.

## Architectural and Historical Context
Historically, the Roman Catholic Church's basilicas differ from Eastern basilicas primarily in the use of galleries. While galleries are a characteristic feature of Eastern churches—intended to provide privacy for women—they are very rare in the West. Examples of galleries in the West occur in some few instances in Ravenna, though their antiquity in Rome is questioned.

In various regions, the presence of the Roman Catholic Church is noted through its architecture and demographics. In Danzig, there are seven Roman Catholic churches. In Belfast, the Roman Catholic community is one of the three chief religious divisions, though they are outnumbered by Protestant Episcopalians and Presbyterians. An example of Catholic architecture in Belfast is St Peter's Roman Catholic chapel, which features a Florentine spire.
