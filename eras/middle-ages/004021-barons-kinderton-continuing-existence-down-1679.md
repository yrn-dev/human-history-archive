# Barons of Kinderton

The barons of Kinderton were a recognized body of tenants within the earldom of Chester, which possessed a palatine character. This group was represented by the Venables family, who continued in existence as the barons of Kinderton until 1679.

## The Concept of the Baron in England
The term "baron" was introduced into England following the Conquest. Its origin is uncertain, but it was used to denote "the man"—specifically one who had performed "homage"—of a great lord or the king. In theory, all individuals who held land "in chief" (directly) from the king were considered *barones regis*. These individuals were members of the king's council and were required to perform a stipulated service.

While the term was used for those holding directly from the king, great nobles—regardless of whether they held the rank of earl—also referred to their own tenants as "barons." This distinguished them from lesser magnates, who referred to their tenants simply as "men" (*homines*).

## Palatine Barons and Other Titles
The use of the title "baron" for tenants of great lords was particularly prevalent in earldoms of a palatine character. In addition to the Venables family in Chester, the palatinate of Durham featured barons under the bishop, including the Hiltons of Hilton Castle, who were styled "Barons of Hilton" until the line became extinct in 1746.

Beyond these palatine structures, the title was occasionally accorded to other families on somewhat uncertain grounds and independently of peerage dignity. Examples include the Cornwalls ("barons of Burford"), the "barons of Stafford," and the "barons of Greystock."

Similar palatine characteristics in Ireland also made the title common within great lordships, as seen with the barons of the Naas, the barons of Slane, and the barons of Galtrim.

## Urban and Civic Usage
The term "baron" was also applied in civic contexts, specifically within the city of London. Historical documents reflect this usage:
* Fantosme records Henry II referring to "mes baruns de Lundres."
* A charter from John granting permission to elect a mayor mentions "our barons of our city of London."
* Other London documents refer to "the greater barons of the city."

In these instances, the aldermen appeared to be loosely considered equivalent to barons. This classification was formal enough that, under Richard II, aldermen were actually assessed for the poll-tax as barons.
