# The Hussite Movements and Conflicts in Bohemia

The Hussite movements emerged from a period of incipient religious conflict in Bohemia during the reign of King Wenceslas. This unrest was preceded by a weakening of the Church of Rome's influence, driven by attacks on clerical immorality from pious priests such as Waldhauser and Milic. The situation was further exacerbated by the church schism, where rival pontiffs engaged in medieval theological strife, alienating the Bohemian population.

## The Cause of Huss and the Outbreak of War
The majority of the Bohemian nation espoused the cause of Huss. While King Wenceslas was characterized by irresolution, Queen Sophia openly favored Huss, who served as her confessor for a time. Huss traveled to the council of Constance under a letter of safe conduct provided by Wenceslas's brother, Sigismund, king of the Romans. However, the council declared him a heretic, and he was burnt on July 6, 1415.

This execution triggered an immediate civil war in Bohemia. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. In this document, they defended Huss as a "good, just and catholic man" and denounced those who claimed heresy existed in Bohemia as "liars, vile traitors and calumniators." The nobles declared their intent to defend the law of Jesus Christ and its preachers "at the cost of their blood," effectively declaring war against the Roman church.

## Internal Divisions and the Hussite Wars
The Hussite movement was not monolithic, eventually splitting into different factions. The "army of the towns" was formed by the Taborites, while the "army of the nobles" consisted of more aristocratic Hussites. These two groups clashed at Lipan, near Kolin, on May 30, 1434. The Taborites were defeated, and their leaders, including the two Prokops, perished.

Other conflicts involved the city of Kuttenberg, which was a center for fanatically Catholic German miners. In the autumn of 1410, these miners carried out reprisals for Hussite outrages in Prague, burning, beheading, or throwing Hussites into disused mine shafts; it is said 1,600 people perished. Later, in 1420, Emperor Sigismund used Kuttenberg as a base to attack the Taborites, but the city was taken by Zizka. In 1421, the Taborites stormed, sacked, and burned the town of Komotau.

## The Crusades and the Compacts
Attempts were made by Germans to subdue Bohemia by armed force. In 1431, a crusade consisting of approximately 90,000 infantry and 40,000 horsemen attacked from the west. After storming the town of Most and committing atrocities, the crusaders reached the neighborhood of Domazlice (Taus). On August 14, 1431, the Hussites under Prokop the Great approached. The sound of their war-wagons and the chanting of "All ye warriors of God" caused the German camp to fall into utter confusion, leading to a rapid retreat.

Following the failure of military conquest, negotiations began at Basel. The Hussites, led by Prokop the Great, eventually reached a reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund. This resulted in the "Compacts," which granted Bohemians the right to preach the gospel freely and receive communion in both kinds, while also limiting the clergy's ability to acquire worldly goods. On July 5, 1436, a treaty was signed at Iglau acknowledging Emperor Sigismund as the king of Bohemia.

## Later Political and Religious Struggles
The struggle for the Bohemian throne continued into the late 15th century. George of Podebrad sought to found a national dynasty but eventually offered the succession to Vladislav of Poland to gain aid against King Matthias of Hungary. Vladislav was elected at Kutna Hora on May 27, 1471, a victory for the national party and a reflection of strong anti-clerical sentiment, as Vladislav promised to maintain the Compacts.

Although Vladislav remained faithful to the promise of maintaining the Compacts and did not prevent communion in both kinds, his overall policy was reactionary. He appointed government officials in Prague from the section of the Utraquist party closest to Rome and oversaw the severe persecution of the Bohemian Brethren, who were extreme Hussites. This led to serious riots in Prague, where advanced Hussites stormed the city's three town halls. In response to this reaction, nobles of the same faith formed a league for their own protection. Peace was temporarily restored following a meeting of all estates at Kutna Hora in 1485.
