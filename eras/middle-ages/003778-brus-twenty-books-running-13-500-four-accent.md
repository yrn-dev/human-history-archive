# The Brus

*The Brus* is a narrative poem written in couplets, consisting of twenty books and totaling over 13,500 four-accent lines. The work serves a purpose that is partly patriotic and partly historical.

## Content and Themes
The poem begins with a description of the state of Scotland following the death of Alexander III in 1286 and concludes in 1332 with the burial of the Bruce's heart and the death of Douglas. The central episode of the narrative is the battle of Bannockburn.

The poem presents freedom as a "noble thing" that should be won and sought regardless of the cost. While the work is patriotic, its sentiment is expressed in more general terms than those found in later Scots literature. The king is depicted as a hero of the chivalric type, a common trope in contemporary romance. Conversely, those who oppose freedom are portrayed in dark colors, as required by poetic propriety and history. Despite this, the work avoids the complacency associated with a merely provincial habit of mind. The poem is noted for its vigour and passages of high merit, specifically the frequently quoted section that begins, "A! fredome is a noble thing."

## Authorship and Textual History
The authorship of *The Brus* is attributed to John Barbour. While no one has doubted his authorship, some have argued that the existing text is an edited copy. This potential editor may have been John Ramsay, a scribe from Perth who wrote the two extant texts. These texts are preserved in the library of St John's College, Cambridge, and the Advocates' library in Edinburgh.

Historically, the poem is considered more trustworthy than contemporary verse-chronicles and is especially more reliable than the *Wallace*, which was written in the following century, although *The Brus* does contain some factual errors, most notably the confusion of three different Bruces into the single person of the hero. Extensive portions of the poem were later incorporated into the *Chronicle* by Wyntoun.

## Publication History
The first extant printed edition of *The Brus* was published by Charteris in Edinburgh in 1571. This was followed by a second edition published by Hart in Edinburgh in 1616. Modern editions include:
* J. Pinkerton (1790), a three-volume set printed from the Advocates' Library text.
* Jamieson (1820).
* Cosmo Innes (1856), published by the Spalding Club.
* W. W. Skeat (1870-1889), published by the Early English Text Society and later reprinted by the Scottish Text Society between 1893 and 1895.

## About the Author
John Barbour was a Scottish poet born approximately in 1316, possibly in Aberdeenshire. His career included several official and academic roles:
* **Education:** In 1357, a letter of safe-conduct allowed him to study at Oxford. In 1368, another letter granted him permission to travel to France, likely for further study at the university of Paris.
* **Official Roles:** He is described as the archdeacon of Aberdeen in a 1357 letter. In 1372, he served as one of the auditors of exchequer, and in 1373, he was a clerk of audit in the king's household.
* **Composition of *The Brus*:** Barbour composed *The Brus* in 1375, at which time he stated his age was 60. For this work, he received a gift of ten pounds in 1377 and a life-pension of twenty shillings in 1378. He later received an additional ten pounds for his pension and a renewal of his exchequer auditorship.

The final years of Barbour's life are documented by his signature as a witness to various deeds in the "Register of Aberdeen" as late as 1392. According to the obit-book of the cathedral of Aberdeen, he died on March 13, 1395, a date supported by state records showing his life-pension ceased after that time.
