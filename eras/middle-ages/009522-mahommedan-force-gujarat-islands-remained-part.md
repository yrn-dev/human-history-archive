# Mahommedan Conquest and Rule in Gujarat and Bombay

## Early Invasions and Establishment of Power
The establishment of Mussulman domination in western and southern India began with significant military incursions into Gujarat. In 1023, Mahmud of Ghazni invaded the region with a large army, during which he destroyed the national Hindu idol of Somnath and seized an immense booty. Mahommed Ghori also conducted invasions of Gujarat, leaving a garrison in the capital. However, the Mahommedan sovereigns of Delhi did not attempt to conquer the south until after their power was firmly established in northern India.

In 1294, Emperor Ala-ud-din first invaded the Deccan, and by 1297, he had conquered Gujarat. The reach of Mahommedan arms expanded further in 1312, achieving triumph throughout the Mahratta country, followed seven years later by the fall of the whole of Malabar to the invaders.

## The Kingdom of Gujarat and the Islands of Bombay
The islands of Bombay, originally consisting of seven islands known as the *Heptanesia* of Ptolemy, were historically part of the dominions of various dynasties in western India, including the Mauryas, Satavahanas, Rashtrakutas, and Chalukyas. During the Maurya and Chalukya periods (450-750), the principal location in Bombay harbour was the city of Puri on Elephanta Island. Later, King Bhima—likely of the house of the Yadavas of Deogiri—founded the first town on Bombay Island, Mahikavati (Mahim), following Ala-ud-din Khilji's 1294 raid into the Deccan.

Mahikavati remained under Hindu rule until 1348, when it was captured by a Mahommedan force from Gujarat. Following this capture, the islands became part of the province, and later the kingdom, of Gujarat.

## Provincial Revolts and the Mogul Empire
By the middle of the 14th century, the weakness of the sovereigns in Delhi led provincial governors to revolt and establish independent kingdoms. This period saw the creation of the Bahmani kingdom in the Deccan, which included a portion of the Bombay presidency, as well as the establishment of a new kingdom in Gujarat.

The Mogul empire eventually reasserted control over these regions. In 1572, Akbar overthrew the kingdom of Gujarat and annexed it to the empire in 1573. This conquest included the ancient city of Broach (also known as Bharuch), which had been part of the Mahommedan state of Gujarat. Following its annexation, Broach was governed by a Nawab. Akbar further expanded his control by reconquering Ahmednagar in 1600 and Khandesh in 1599.

## Portuguese Acquisition and Decline
The Portuguese arrived at Calicut in 1498 and gradually established a presence on the coast after their victory over the Egyptian fleet at Diu. They captured Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511, and Ormuz in 1515. Taking advantage of the decay of the kingdom of Gujarat, the Portuguese occupied several key areas: Chaul in 1531, Bassein and its dependencies—including Bombay—in 1534, Diu in 1535, and Daman in 1559. The islands of Bombay had been ceded to the Portuguese by Sultan Bahadur in 1534. The Portuguese power was eventually undermined by the inherent vices of their intolerant system before the arrival of English and Dutch rivals.

## Regional Geography and Demographics
Gujarat is characterized by rich, highly cultivated alluvial plains watered by the Nerbudda and Tapti rivers. Its western boundary is formed by the Runn or Lake of Cutch, a salt marsh that can convert the territory of Cutch into an island during the rains. Geologically, the region south of Gujarat is largely covered by the horizontal lava flows of the Deccan Trap series.

Demographically, Hinduism is the predominant religion in Gujarat, although the province retains the influence of various petty Mahommedan kingdoms. In terms of language, Gujarati and Hindustani are spoken in Gujarat, with Gujarati containing several survivals of an old language spoken by earlier Aryan inhabitants.

## Later History and Social Unrest
In the latter part of the 17th century, the Mahrattas rose to power, and nearly every part of the Bombay presidency fell under their sway. In Broach, the Mahrattas became masters in 1685, holding it in subordination to the peshwa until 1772.

In more recent history, the region faced severe plague and famine. Between September 1896 and October 1902, over 531,000 deaths occurred due to bubonic plague in Bombay and the surrounding country. Social unrest accompanied these crises; for instance, in March 1898, a riot initiated by Mahommedan weavers broke out in Bombay, resulting in fatal injuries to several Europeans. Additionally, the region suffered from famine, including a failure of the monsoon in Gujarat in 1899.
