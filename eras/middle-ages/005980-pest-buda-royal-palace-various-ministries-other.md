# Budapest

Budapest is the capital and largest town of the kingdom of Hungary and the second largest town of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy. Situated on both banks of the Danube, the city is located where the river begins its southward course and where the Bakony Mountains meet the Carpathians. Strategically, it dominates the approach from the west to the great Hungarian plain. The city is characterized by a sharp contrast between its two banks: Pest lies on a flat sandy plain, while Buda is built upon a series of steep hills, commanded by the Blocksberg (770 ft. high) and a fortress.

## History and Development
The history of Budapest is the combined history of two sister towns. On the right bank, the Romans founded a colony called Aquincum in the 2nd century A.D. on the site of a former Celtic settlement. This Roman presence lasted until A.D. 376, after which the area was invaded by Slavs, Avars, Ostrogoths, and Huns. The Magyars arrived at the end of the 10th century, preserving the names Buda and Pest. While the origin of Pest is obscure, its name likely derives from the old Slavonic *pestj* (meaning stove).

Pest was initially a German settlement, described by a 13th-century chronicler as "Villa Teutonica ditissima." Christianity was introduced here in the early 11th century. After being destroyed by Tatars in 1241, Pest was made a royal free city by Bela IV in 1244. However, it was soon eclipsed by Buda, where King Bela IV built a palace and fortress in 1247. Buda became the capital of Hungary in 1361.

The city faced significant upheavals during the Turkish occupation; Pest was pillaged in 1526, and from 1541 to 1686, Buda served as the seat of a Turkish pasha. Following the departure of the Turks, Pest was largely in ruins, though it revived due to its favorable location and the renewal of privileges, becoming the seat of the highest Hungarian officials in 1723. Maria Theresa and Joseph II further increased its importance, but the 19th century saw Pest's rapid growth, eventually outstripping Buda. In 1872, the two towns were united into a single municipality.

## Municipal Structure and Geography
Budapest covers an area of 78 sq. m. and is divided into ten municipal districts. The right bank (Buda) comprises Vár (Festung), Viziváros (Wasserstadt), and Ó-Buda (Alt-Ofen). The left bank (Pest) consists of Belváros (Inner City), Lipótváros (Leopoldstadt), Terézváros (Theresienstadt), Erzsébetváros (Elisabethstadt), Józsefváros (Josephstadt), Ferenczváros (Franzstadt), and Köbánya (Steinbruch).

The two banks are connected by six bridges. These include two railway bridges, the Margaret bridge (with a junction to Margaret island), the Franz Joseph bridge, and two suspension bridges. The Ketten-Brücke, built between 1842 and 1849 by Adam Clark and the brothers Tiernay, is 410 yards long and ornamented with four colossal stone lions. It connects to a 383-yard tunnel constructed by Adam Clark in 1854. The second suspension bridge, the Schwurplatz bridge, was completed in 1903.

## Administration, Culture, and Education
Buda serves as the official center, housing the royal palace, various ministries, and government offices. Pest is the center for industry, commerce, and intellectual and nationalistic life. The royal palace in Buda, erected by Maria Theresa between 1748 and 1771, houses the regalia of Hungary, including the crown of St Stephen, the orb, sceptre, sword, and coronation robes.

Budapest is the intellectual capital of Hungary. Its university, established in 1635 at Tyrnau before moving to Buda (1777) and then Pest (1783), had 4,983 students and nearly 200 professors in 1900. Other institutions include a polytechnic, a veterinary academy, a rabbinical institute, and military academies. The academy of sciences, founded in 1825, encourages the study of sciences and the Hungarian language. The national museum, founded in 1807, contains a library with over 20,000 manuscripts.

## Population and Demographics
The city experienced extraordinary growth in the 19th century, surpassed in rate of increase only by Berlin. In 1799, the joint population was 54,179; by 1900, the civil population reached 716,476. Including a garrison of 15,846 men, the total population was 732,322. 

The population is ethnically diverse, consisting of 578,458 Magyars, 104,520 Germans, and 25,168 Slovaks, along with Greeks, Armenians, Russians, Rumanians, Servians, Croatians, and Gypsies. Religiously, the population includes 445,023 Roman Catholics, 168,985 Jews, 67,319 Helvetic Protestants, 38,811 Augsburg Protestants, 5,806 Greek Catholics, and 4,422 Greek Orthodox.

## Notable Buildings and Landmarks
The oldest church is the Matthias church in Buda, which served as a mosque during the Turkish occupation and was the site of Franz Joseph's coronation in 1867. In Pest, the Leopoldstadt Basilica is the most magnificent church, featuring a dome 315 ft. high. The city also possesses two Moorish-style synagogues (built 1861 and 1872) and a third larger one begun in 1901. A small octagonal Turkish mosque remains in Buda, preserved by the treaty of Karlowitz (1699).

Public spaces include the Városliget, a 286-acre park containing the zoological garden and the agricultural and ethnographical museums. Margaret island, an English-style park owned by archduke Joseph, contains the ruins of a convent built by St Margaret, daughter of Bela IV. Other sites include the ruins of Aquincum, the Rákos field where diets were held from the 10th to 14th centuries, and the royal castle of Gödöllö.
