# Religious Images of Christ in Early and Medieval Christianity

The use of images representing Christ evolved significantly from the primitive Christian Church through the Middle Ages, moving from initial condemnation and scarcity to widespread use and subsequent theological conflict.

## Early Christian Attitudes and Constraints
In the primitive Christian Church, there was a unanimous condemnation of heathen image-worship. Early converts, often characterized by poverty and obscurity, had meeting-houses with few works of art. This lack of visible aids to devotion was reinforced by the church's sacramental use of Christ's name as a name of power, a belief in his continued real but unseen presence, and the expectation that his second advent was imminent. Consequently, critics such as Celsus and Min. Fel. accused Christians and the Bani Israel of having no temples, altars, or known images—a charge that Origen gloried in.

Despite this general trend, some Gnostic sects, such as the followers of Marcellina, utilized the fine arts. They possessed painted images and those made from different materials, claiming a likeness of Christ had been created by Pilate during Jesus' life among men. These Gnostics crowned these images and displayed them alongside images of philosophers like Plato, Aristotle, and Pythagoras.

## Development and Resistance (4th–8th Centuries)
From the 4th century onward, the use of fine arts in the church increased, though often without formal ecclesiastical sanction. Paulinus of Nola noted that Bible histories, martyrdoms, and symbols of the Trinity were freely represented pictorially. However, this trend faced strong opposition. Augustine expressed disapproval of those who sought Christ and his apostles on painted walls rather than in written words. 

Eusebius of Caesarea maintained that it was unlawful to possess images pretending to represent the Saviour in either his human or divine nature; he reportedly removed pictures of Christ and Paul from a lady friend to avoid the reproach of idolatry. Similarly, Epiphanius tore down a curtain featuring the image of Christ or another saint in a church at Anablatha, using it instead to bury a pauper. Nilus, a defender of Chrysostom, permitted the symbol of the cross and pictorial histories of the Old and New Testaments but deprecated other pictures, including those of martyrs.

## Iconoclasm and the Second Nicene Council
A movement known as the "iconoclasts proper" eventually emerged, rejecting all religious art. They believed fleeting matter could not embody the heavenly energies of Christ and the saints. They specifically viewed statues of Christ—particularly those depicting him hanging on the cross—with horror and indignation. Because of this movement, none of the graven images of Christ common before the outbreak of iconoclasm survive. While the synod of 692 allowed the crucifix, Greek churches discarded it after the 8th century.

Later, the restoration of monks under Irene led to the exposure of a thousand images for public veneration. The second Nicene council, ratified by 350 bishops, unanimously pronounced that the worship of images was agreeable to reason, Scripture, the councils, and the fathers of the church. However, the council hesitated on whether the adoration of the figure of Christ was direct or relative, and whether it was entitled to the same mode of adoration as the Godhead.

## Medieval Customs and the Image of Christ
In the Middle Ages, images of Christ were integrated into specific liturgical rituals. During the festival of the Ascension—celebrated forty days after Easter—some churches practiced a dramatic ritual where an image of Christ was raised from the altar through a hole in the roof. Immediately following this ascent, a burning straw figure representing Satan was thrown down through the same hole.

Other traditions involved the veneration of specific relics or images. The image of Edessa, described as a linen cloth bearing the impression of Christ's face, was revered as a palladium. According to ecclesiastical accounts, this image was exposed on the ramparts of Edessa to help deliver the city from the arms of Chosroes Nushirvan.
