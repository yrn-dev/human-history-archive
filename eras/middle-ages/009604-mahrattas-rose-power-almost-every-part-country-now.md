# The Mahrattas

The Mahrattas are a Hindu people and the dominating race in their region next to the Europeans. They are native to the Deccan, where they constitute 30% of the population. As of 1901, the Mahrattas numbered 3,650,000, a figure composed of 1,400,000 Mahrattas not otherwise specified, 1,900,000 Kunbis, and 350,000 Konkanis. While the Kunbis are the great agricultural caste of Western India and are ethnically indistinguishable from the Mahrattas, the term "Mahrattas" is sometimes specifically reserved for those claiming a higher rank as descendants of the soldiers of Sivaji.

## Rise to Power and Territorial Expansion

During the latter part of the 17th century, the Mahrattas rose into power. Their ascent coincided with a period of decay for the Great Mongol Empire of Baber, Akbar, and Aurangzeb. Following the death of Aurangzeb, the Great Mogul at Delhi maintained only nominal authority outside the immediate vicinity of the capital. In the southwest, the Mahrattas rose against Islam and restored Brahminism as the ruling religion.

For a period, the Mahrattas extended their power over the entire southern triangle of India. Almost every part of the region now comprising the presidency of Bombay fell under their sway. Their influence eventually expanded into Bundelkhand. In 1732, during a Mahommedan invasion, Chhatar Sal—who had successfully resisted the proselytizing efforts of Aurangzeb—obtained the assistance of the Mahratta Peshwa. Chhatar Sal adopted the Peshwa as his son and granted him one-third of his dominions. The Mahrattas continued to extend their influence over Bundelkhand until 1792, when the Peshwa was acknowledged as the lord paramount of that country.

## Conflicts and Regional Rivalries

The Mahrattas were frequently engaged in military conflicts with other regional and imperial powers. In the north, they entered into a long-standing feud with the Rohilla Pathans in Bijnor. This conflict began when the Mahrattas invaded Bijnor at the instigation of enemies of the Rohilla chief Najib-ud-daula. Following the death of Najib in 1770, the Mahrattas defeated his son, Zabita Khan, and overran all of Rohilkhand. They remained in the area until 1772, when the nawab of Oudh entered into a treaty with the Rohillas to expel them in exchange for payment.

Additionally, the Mahrattas were rivals of the Mogul empire. The English settlement at Bombay found itself isolated between these two competing powers. During the administration of Richard Keigwin (who served as governor of Bombay after a mutiny in 1683), the English obtained notable concessions from the Mahrattas.

## Relations with the British

The first collision between the English and the Mahratta power occurred in 1774. This conflict resulted in the treaty of Salbai in 1782, which saw the cession of Salsette to the British and the handing over of Broach to Sindhia. A second Mahratta war ended in 1803; by this time, Surat had already been annexed in 1800, and the East India Company received the districts of Kaira and Broach.

## Decline and Fragmentation

The Mahratta power eventually declined. For a period, they fought with Afghans for the rule of North India. Subsequently, the unified Mahratta power broke up into a series of separate principalities, including Baroda, Gwalior, and Indore.
