# RECOVERY OF ADMINISTRATIVE AUTHORITY BY THE THRONE

The recovery of administrative authority by the Japanese throne involved various attempts to shift power away from influential families and military governments, ranging from the introduction of "camera government" to diplomatic efforts in the modern era.

## The Insei System and the Fujiwara
During the Heian epoch, the Fujiwara family employed a strategy of seating minors on the throne and replacing them once they reached the age of discretion. This system was presented as a principle that sovereign power should not be exercised exclusively. 

Emperor Go-Sanjo challenged this arrangement, arguing that such a system was unnatural and impaired Imperial authority. He contended that reducing the occupant of the throne to a "puppet" who sat with "folded hands" was unacceptable, as no father would be content to be divested of practical interest in his family's affairs. To render the regent a superfluity, Go-Sanjo proposed the implementation of "camera government" (Insei), which allowed a sovereign to continue taking an active part in the administration of State affairs even after abdicating. However, Go-Sanjo was unable to put this project into practice, as he abdicated due to ill health in 1073 and died the following year.

## The Influence of the Bakufu
The relationship between the Throne and the Bakufu underwent significant changes over time. Following the Shokyu war, the Insei (camera system) of administration at the Court was destroyed. This shifted the balance of power, transforming the Bakufu's authority in Kyoto from a largely nominal status into a "supreme reality."

While the Imperial city of Kyoto maintained its outward appearance of importance—keeping its old codes of etiquette, administrative machinery, and government functions—the substance of authority passed to Kamakura. Kamakura became the true administrative metropolis of the empire, and no question was finally decided without reference to it. To enforce this, Kamakura appointed two special officials known as "inquisitors" (tandai), such as Yasutoki and Tokifusa, who presided over administrative machinery at the northern and southern suburbs of Kyoto (the two Rokuhara). This machinery was organized similarly to the Kamakura polity, consisting of a Monju-dokoro, a Man-dokoro, and a Samurai-dokoro.

Later regulations further restricted the Throne's independence. Rules stipulated that all reports to the Emperor must be submitted via an administrator (bugyo), a regent, or the denso (an official appointed by the Bakufu). Anyone attempting to address the Throne directly faced exile regardless of rank. Because the Bakufu controlled the appointment of ministers and regents, and the bugyo were nominees of Yedo, the Throne became approachable only through the Bakufu.

## Diplomatic Efforts for Recovery
In a later period, Sir Harry Parkes, a representative of Great Britain, sought to accelerate the Emperor's recovery of administrative power. Parkes believed that the ratification of treaties by the sovereign was essential for their validity. He reasoned that by investing this ratification with maximum formality, the process of the Emperor recovering administrative power could be hastened. To achieve this, Parkes planned to travel to Hyogo with a powerful naval squadron to seek the ratification of the treaty, the opening of the ports of Osaka and Hyogo, and the reduction of the import tariff from an average of fifteen per cent, ad valorem, to five per cent.

## Related Administrative Contexts
The history of the throne's authority is also marked by periods of internal struggle and structural organization. For example, Emperor Heijo (the fifty-first sovereign) attempted to simplify administrative procedures and curtail unneeded outlays upon ascending the throne. However, his efforts were wrecked by his own physical weakness and the influence of Kusu, the daughter of Fujiwara Tanetsugu. Kusu's brother, Nakanari, used his relationship with her to usurp almost Imperial authority.

Earlier, under the Daiho code, the central government was organized into eight departments, two boards, and one office. The Jingi-kwan (Board of Religion) stood at the head of all others. This placement recognized the divine origin of the Imperial family, based on the tenet that a throne founded on divine authority—rather than human military prowess—is perennial and secure against being overthrown.
