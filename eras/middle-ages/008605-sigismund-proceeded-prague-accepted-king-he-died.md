# Sigismund of Hungary and Bohemia

## Accession and Conflict in Bohemia
Sigismund, the king of the Romans and brother of King Wenceslas, claimed the Bohemian crown as Wenceslas's successor. His arrival in Bohemia was preceded by renewed rioting in Prague and other Bohemian cities following the death of the previous king, which resulted in the expulsion of many Germans who were mostly adherents of the Church of Rome. Early in the year following these riots, Sigismund arrived at Kutna Hora (Kuttenberg).

On March 1, 1420, Pope Martin V proclaimed a crusade against Bohemia. Sigismund led a large army composed of Bohemians and men from various other countries. On June 30, 1420, this army occupied the castle of Prague. The crusaders viewed the city as a heretical community because its inhabitants accepted other evangelical truths and used the sacred chalice.

## Military Campaigns and Failures
Sigismund's attempts to conquer Prague were unsuccessful. His forces were repulsed at the Vitkov (now Zizkov) hill by the Taborites, who were led by Zizka. Following this defeat, Sigismund decided to abandon the siege of Prague. He further attempted to relieve the Vysehrad fortress garrison on the outskirts of the city, but he was entirely defeated at the battle of the Vysehrad on November 1, 1420.

During this period, Sigismund used the town of Kuttenberg as a base for his unsuccessful attacks on the Taborites. The city was eventually taken by Zizka. Following a temporary reconciliation between the warring parties, imperial troops burned Kuttenberg in 1422 to prevent the Taborites from regaining control of the city.

## Relationship with the Hussite Movement
Prior to his military campaigns, Sigismund had a role in the events leading to the Hussite wars. He had provided a letter of safe conduct to Huss, who proceeded to the council of Constance. Despite this, Huss was declared a heretic and was burnt on July 6, 1415. This execution triggered a civil war in Bohemia, as the majority of the population greatly revered Huss. In response, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague on September 2, 1415, and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*, defending Huss as a "good, just and catholic man" and declaring war against the Roman church.

## Final Years and Succession
Sigismund eventually proceeded to Prague and was accepted as king after the Compacts had been formally recognized at Iglau in Moravia. He died in 1437. 

Upon his death, he was succeeded by his son-in-law, Albert of Austria, whom the estates chose as their king. Albert had married Sigismund's daughter, Elizabeth. Through this marriage, the county of Luxemburg—which had been under the rule of Sigismund's descendants—passed to the house of Habsburg.
