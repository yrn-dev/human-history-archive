# Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) was a prominent 13th-century scholastic doctor and theologian. He is ranked among the great men of his era, alongside figures such as Albertus Magnus and Bonaventura. In his own time, Aquinas enjoyed an infinitely wider renown than contemporaries like Roger Bacon.

## Academic and Religious Affiliation
Aquinas was a leader in the activity of the Dominican Order within the universities. The Dominicans, following the policy of St. Dominic, established houses in major university towns including Paris, Bologna, Oxford, Palencia, Padua, Cologne, Vienna, Prague, and Salamanca. Aquinas, together with Albert the Great, served as a leading figure in this academic expansion. The principles and methods he elaborated continue to be maintained by the Dominican school of theology.

## Philosophical and Theological Contributions
Aquinas sought to reconstruct Catholicism using a sounder system of reasoning. His intellectual approach is described as constructive, contrasting with the destructive genius of critics like Duns Scotus. 

In the realm of philosophy, Aquinas was an eclectic regarding the matter of Universals. One of his central tenets was the belief that revelation and reason serve as two independent sources of knowledge. This position led him to offer an ontological proof for the existence of God.

Theologically, the system developed by Aquinas approximates to pantheism. He was also a strenuous opponent of the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception. Regarding the nature of the human will, Aquinas maintained that the will was dependent upon the understanding.

## Comparisons and Criticisms
The work of Aquinas was later subject to the criticism of successors such as Duns Scotus and Occam. Duns Scotus challenged several of Aquinas's core positions:
* **Knowledge and Proof:** Contrary to Aquinas, Scotus argued that no true knowledge of anything knowable exists apart from theology based on revelation, and he therefore rejected Aquinas's ontological proof as worthless, claiming the existence of God could not be proved by reasoning.
* **The Will:** Scotus opposed Aquinas by maintaining that the will was independent of the understanding and that the freedom of the will was absolute.
* **Divine Grace:** These differing views on the will resulted in a subsequent difference between the Thomists and Scotus regarding the operation of divine grace.
