# Monza, the Stowe Missal, and the Lindau Gospels

## Illuminated Manuscripts and Precious Bindings
From the 7th century through the 16th century, illuminated manuscripts were held in the highest esteem, featuring both exquisite calligraphy and miniature painting. The high quality of the illuminations found inside these texts often inspired equally magnificent exterior coverings. These bindings frequently utilized splendid work in metals, incorporating enamels, jewels, and carved ivory.

Among the notable examples of such precious bindings are the *Gospels of Theodolinda* at Monza, which date from the 7th century, and the *Lindau Gospels*, which are currently located in America. Other significant examples include the *Gospels of Charlemagne*, held in the Victoria and Albert Museum at South Kensington, and the 14th-century Limoges enamel bindings found in the British Museum. While similar intrinsically precious bindings may have existed in England, most have disappeared, likely having been melted down by Edward VI or Henry VIII. An exception is *St Cuthbert's Gospels* at Stonyhurst, which features a red leather binding with a repousse design and likely dates to the 7th or 8th century.

## The Stowe Missal and Irish Book Ornamentation
The *Stowe Missal* is associated with the Irish cumdach, a type of precious covering. The ornamentation of Irish manuscripts, which reached full development as early as the 7th century, was a native school of art that later exerted a profound influence on the art of the continent and Britain. This style is characterized as a mechanical art of extreme precision and patient elaboration.

Key features of this ornamentation include:
*   **Design Elements:** The use of intricate knots, spirals, zig-zag ornaments, and delicate interwoven patterns. These are combined with interlaced ribbons and entangled zoomorphic creatures.
*   **Technical Execution:** Designs are often worked in red dots and arranged with mathematical accuracy.
*   **Materials:** Artists used thick pigments in harmonious colors to create an appearance similar to enamel; notably, gold was never used in these designs.

The Irish artists likely modeled these book designs after classical jewelry, mosaics, enamels, and early metal work. The *Book of Kells*, dating from the latter part of the 7th century and preserved in Trinity College, Dublin, is cited as the finest example of this early Celtic ornamentation.

## Figure Drawing in Irish Manuscripts
Despite the excellence of their ornamental work, Irish artists are noted for a complete failure in figure drawing. In early copies of the Gospels, the figures intended to represent the evangelists are described as rude, with limbs that are contorted or composed of extraordinary convolutions and interlacings. 

This approach was likely a result of tradition; rather than attempting to represent the human form accurately, the artists treated these figures as details to be integrated into the general ornamental scheme of the page. Consequently, the human figure became a subject for the artist to exercise ingenuity by knotting the forms into fantastic shapes.

## Monza and Technical Innovation
Beyond the ecclesiastical history of the *Gospels of Theodolinda*, the city of Monza is noted in the archive for later technical contributions. In 1839, C. A. Boracchi of Monza developed a mechanism for the tuning of kettledrums. This was part of a broader international effort in the 19th century to invent mechanisms that allowed performers to tune the drum to a fixed note using a single movement, rather than the time-consuming process of adjusting screw-heads.
