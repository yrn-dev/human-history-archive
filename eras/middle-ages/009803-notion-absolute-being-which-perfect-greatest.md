# Notions of the Absolute and Perfection

The concept of an absolute being or substance, characterized by perfection and infinity, appears across various philosophical and theological frameworks. These perspectives range from metaphysical postulates and mystical speculations to rationalist arguments regarding the nature of existence and the divine.

## Metaphysical and Moral Dimensions of the Absolute
In certain philosophical investigations, the "universal substance" is referred to as the absolute. At a primary stage of analysis, this absolute is not endowed with the attributes of a personal Deity, serving instead as a metaphysical postulate. For this empty notion to become significant or a "living power," it must be connected to human experience and thought, specifically through the realization of the significance of creation, the value of the good and the beautiful, and the worth of personal holiness. To describe the universal substance as the perfect realization of the holy, the beautiful, and the good—and to grant it moral attributes beyond being a metaphysical ground—is meaningful only to those who recognize real values within those expressions.

## The Infinite as a Positive Idea
René Descartes addressed the relationship between the infinite and the finite, countering critics who argued that the idea of God as an infinite Being is merely a negative concept derived from the finite. Descartes maintained that the infinite is the positive idea and the presupposition of the finite; the finite is understood only through the limitation of the infinite. 

According to Descartes, the self-consciousness of a finite being is inextricably linked to the consciousness of the infinite. He argued that the notion of the infinite is prior to the notion of the finite. This is evidenced by the fact that a being cannot recognize its own defects or know that it is not "altogether perfect" without possessing an idea of a being more perfect than itself. Consequently, the idea of God is not simply one among many, but is necessary for existence as a thinking being.

## Mystical and Platonizing Speculations
Bonaventura presented a mystical and Platonizing mode of speculation, contrasting with the Aristotelian scholasticism of his contemporaries. He prioritized the "living power of the heart" and the affections over purely intellectual elements. Bonaventura adopted a Platonic theory suggesting that ideas exist as thoughts of the divine mind rather than within the nature of things, and that actual things were formed according to these divine thoughts.

In this system, the supreme end of life is an ecstatic union with God, achieved through contemplation of the intellect and intense absorbing love. While this union is a hope for the future and cannot be fully reached in this life, the soul may prepare for it through prayer, meditation, and the exercise of virtues to receive divine illumination.

## Perfection in Nature and Spirit
Different thinkers have applied the concept of perfection to the transition of the soul and the nature of the divine:

*   **Spinoza:** He defined joy as the "transition from less to greater perfection" and pain as the "transition from greater to less perfection." He suggested that progress toward perfection increases one's participation in the divine nature. Spinoza condemned hatred, ambition, and rivalry as resulting from an over-estimate of finite things, asserting that the highest good is that which is enjoyed more fully as the number of participants increases.
*   **Ancient and Modern Spirit:** While modern philosophy developed the idea of "pure and absolute spirit," ancient views of incorporeal essence—ascribed to the Deity, celestial beings, and human souls—did not necessarily exclude the notion of extended space, often imagining a subtle nature composed of aether, fire, or air.

## Perfection in Governance and Art
The notion of perfection also extends to secular structures and creative achievements:

*   **Monarchical Government:** It has been observed that monarchical government has made significant advances toward perfection in modern times, evolving into a government of laws rather than men. Such systems are characterized by stability, the security of property, and the flourishing of arts.
*   **Musical Composition:** In the works of Beethoven, the fugue was used to achieve a "perfect" unity of form and texture. This allowed the mind to concentrate entirely on the texture, though this was achieved through a struggle and the use of bold, logical strokes of harmony.
