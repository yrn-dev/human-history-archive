# The Battle Between Chinghis Kaan and Prester John

The conflict between Chinghis Kaan, the first Lord of the Tartars, and Prester John was a monumental military engagement resulting in the total conquest of Prester John's kingdom. The struggle was precipitated by a diplomatic insult regarding marriage and claims of feudal servitude.

## Origins of the Conflict
In the year of Christ's Incarnation 1187, the Tartars chose Chinghis Kaan as their King. Described as a man of great valour, ability, and eloquence, Chinghis Kaan successfully united all the Tartars in the world under his sovereignty. After conquering eight provinces—during which he gained the devotion of the conquered by protecting them and their property—he sought to expand his dominion further.

In the year 1200, Chinghis Kaan sent an embassy to Prester John to request his daughter's hand in marriage. Prester John reacted with anger, viewing the request as an act of impudence. He informed the envoys that Chinghis Kaan was his "liegeman and serf," stating that he would rather set his daughter on fire than marry her to him. Prester John further characterized Chinghis Kaan as a "rebel and traitor" who deserved death.

## Mobilization and Preparation
Upon receiving this "brutal message," Chinghis Kaan was seized with rage and vowed to take a revenge for the insult that would be the most dearly paid for in the world. He mustered a host of such magnitude that it had never before been seen or heard of.

Prester John, despite receiving tidings of the approaching multitude, initially treated the threat as a "jest and a trifle," claiming that the Tartars were "no soldiers." However, he eventually marshalled his own forces, which consisted of many different nations in a multitude described as a "world's wonder," with the intent to capture and execute Chinghis Kaan.

## The Plain of Tanduc and Divination
The two armies eventually converged on a vast and beautiful plain called Tanduc, which belonged to Prester John. Chinghis Kaan pitched his camp there with a multitude of people too great to be numbered. Prester John arrived and pitched his own camp 20 miles away from that of Chinghis Kaan. Both armies remained at rest for two days to ensure they were heartier and fresher for the coming combat.

During this period, Chinghis Kaan consulted his astrologers, both Saracens and Christians, to determine the outcome of the battle. While the Saracens could not provide a true answer, the Christian astrologers performed a divination using a cane split lengthwise. One piece was named for Chinghis Kaan and the other for Prester John. After reading a Psalm from the Psalter and performing incantations, the piece representing Chinghis Kaan moved without being touched and climbed on top of the piece representing Prester John. This was interpreted as a sign of victory for the Kaan, leading him to treat Christians with great respect thereafter.

## The Battle and Aftermath
Following the two days of rest, the two hosts engaged in a "desperate combat" described as the greatest battle ever seen. Although the number of slain on both sides was very great, Chinghis Kaan emerged victorious. Prester John was slain during the fighting.

Following the death of Prester John, his kingdom passed day by day into the hands of Chinghis Kaan until the entire territory was conquered. In later years, the province of Tenduc, located toward the east and containing numerous towns and villages, remained under the rule of a king named George. George, a descendant of Prester John, held the land under the Great Kaan. It became a custom for the kings of Prester John's lineage to marry princesses or daughters of the Great Kaan.
