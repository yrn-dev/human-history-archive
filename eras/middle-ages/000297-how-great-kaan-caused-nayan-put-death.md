# The Execution of Nayan

The execution of Nayan, a Tartar Chief and uncle to the Emperor Cublay Kaan, followed a period of rebellion and a subsequent military defeat. Nayan, who was the Lord of four great provinces—Chorcha, Cauly, Barscol, and Sikintinju—had sought to wrest the empire from his nephew. Despite being a liegeman to the Great Kaan, Nayan attempted to coordinate a dual attack on the sovereign by forming an alliance with another Tartar Prince, Caidu.

## The Defeat of Nayan
The conflict culminated in a fierce battle in a remote wilderness near the river Liao. The Great Kaan personally led his forces, a decision driven by his view that Nayan's rebellion was too serious and perilous to be handled by his sons or barons. Through strict secrecy and the use of scouts, the Kaan's army surprised Nayan, who was asleep at the time of the arrival.

The engagement involved a massive force of no fewer than 760,000 horsemen, in addition to numerous foot soldiers. The battle lasted from morning until noon, ending in a complete rout of Nayan's forces. Nayan, who was a baptized Christian and bore the cross on his banner, was taken prisoner along with his barons and forced to surrender all his arms.

## The Method of Execution
Upon learning that Nayan had been captured, the Great Kaan ordered that he be put to death immediately and in secret. This secrecy was intended to prevent any attempts to obtain pity or pardon for Nayan, given that he was of the Kaan's own flesh and blood.

The Great Kaan employed a specific method of execution to avoid spilling the blood of the Line Imperial upon the ground or exposing it before the Sun and the eye of Heaven. Nayan was wrapped in a carpet and tossed to and fro mercilessly until he died.

## Aftermath and Political Consequences
Following the victory and the death of Nayan, the people and Barons of the four provinces previously under Nayan's lordship renewed their fealty to the Great Kaan. The news of Nayan's fate caused great trouble for the Tartar Lord Caydu; while Caydu remained ready for war, he was noted to be in great fear of being handled in the same manner as Nayan. After the campaign, the Great Kaan returned to his capital city of Cambaluc to take his ease and hold festivities.

## Religious Discourse Following the Execution
The defeat of Nayan sparked a dispute among the various groups present, including Saracens, Idolaters, and Jews. These groups mocked the Christians, arguing that the cross on Nayan's standard had provided no help to him. 

The Great Kaan intervened by sharply rebuking those who mocked the Christians. He declared that the cross had indeed "done right well" by not aiding Nayan, as Nayan was a disloyal and traitorous rebel who deserved his fate. The Kaan asserted that the cross did not help those engaged in wrong-doing or disloyalty against their Lord.
