# Texier and Pullan's Byzantine Architecture and Couchaud's Choix d'eglises

The study of Byzantine architecture and the early Christian basilican form is supported by several key historical and archaeological texts. Among the primary authorities cited in the archive for the description and visual documentation of these structures are the works of Texier and Pullan, specifically *Byzantine Architecture* (published in London, 1864), and the works of Couchaud, including *Choix d'eglises byzantines* and *Églises byzantines en Grèce*.

## Scope and Application of the Texts
Texier and Pullan's *Byzantine Architecture* and Couchaud's *Choix d'eglises byzantines* serve as essential references for the views and descriptions of various basilican churches located in the East. These texts provide detailed accounts of significant religious structures, including:
* The church of the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai, which was built by Justinian.
* The church of Dana, located between Antioch and Bir, dating to the same period as the monastery of St Catherine.
* St Philip at Athens.
* Bosra in Arabia.
* Xanthus in Lycia.
* The church of St Demetrius at Thessalonica, described as "very noble."

Additionally, the work of Texier and Pullan is cited as a source for the study of antiquities in Macedonia.

## Context of Byzantine Architecture
The architectural style documented in these works emerged from the "orientalization of Roman art" and later influenced the formation of Mahommedan or Saracenic styles. According to the scholar Choisy, Byzantine architecture represents an original type of architecture that arose when the Orient returned to its own freedom of exploration following the fall of Rome, coinciding with the emergence of Christian civilization.

This style was closely identified with the outward expression of the Christian church. The transition from classical art to the Byzantine style is marked by the moral and intellectual criticism applied to old materials. The art reached a point of maturity under Justinian (527-565) and experienced a second culmination between the 10th and 12th centuries.

## Related Architectural Features and Works
The archive notes specific architectural elements common to the period studied by these authors. One such feature is the "dosseret" (or impost block), a French term for the cubical stone block placed above the capitals in a Byzantine church. This block was used to support arches and vaults where the springing area was significantly larger than the column supporting it.

Beyond the basilican forms found in the East, the archive mentions other regional examples of early Christian and Byzantine influence:
* **North Africa:** S. Gsell's *Les Monuments antiques de l'Algerie* identifies more than 130 examples of early Christian churches in the Roman province of North Africa.
* **Europe:** While strictly early Christian basilicas are absent in Spain, France, and Germany, the archive notes the "Basse Oeuvre" at Beauvais (possibly Carolingian) and an unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great at Michelstadt in the Odenwald. The 11th-century basilica of St Mauritius near Hildesheim also represents a later iteration of the form.
