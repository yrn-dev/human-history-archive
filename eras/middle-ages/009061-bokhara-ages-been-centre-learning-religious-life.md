# Bokhara

Bokhara, known to the ancients as Sogdiana, is a city in central Asia that has served for ages as a primary center of religious life and learning. Historically, it was too far east to fall under Roman dominion, though it was deeply affected by the various revolutions of Asia.

## Early History and Governance
The foundation of the capital is attributed to the Persian hero Efrasiab. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, the region became part of the Seleucidae empire. Later, the nomad Yue-chi moved into the valley of the Oxus from the east, establishing a settled power in Sogdiana and Bactria; the dominions of King Kadphises I, who is believed to have ascended the throne around A.D. 45, extended from Bokhara to the Indus.

The district was subsequently reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand. Islam was introduced shortly after the Arab conquest of Persia (640-642) and quickly became the dominant faith. During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the region—then called Sughd—was one of the most flourishing districts of central Asia. While Samarkand generally served as the seat of government, Bokhara earned a high reputation as a seat of learning and religion. The region was also known as Ma wara 'l Nahr, or Transoxiana.

## Conquests and Political Shifts
The city's history is marked by successive conquests. At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah of the Seljuk dynasty subdued the country watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus. In 1216, Bokhara was subdued by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm, but this was overturned in 1220 by Jenghiz Khan. Although the conqueror's fury wasted the country, prosperity partially returned under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent. His descendants held power until 1369, when Tamerlane established his capital at Samarkand.

In more recent history, the city has been ruled by an autocratic emir whose power is limited only by the traditional custom of the Mussulmans, known as the *sheriat*. The emir maintains an army of approximately 11,000 men but exists as a vassal under Russian control. A nearby Russian town called *New Bokhara*, or *Kagan*, located 8 miles from the city near the railway station on territory ceded by the emir, is growing rapidly with a population of 2,000.

## Center of Erudition and Religion
Bokhara has long been viewed as the center of Mussulman erudition in central Asia. In the 9th century, learned men in Bokhara corresponded with scholars in Bagdad, Cairo, and Cordoba. The city was the birthplace of Avicenna (Ibnsinā), the "Prince of Physicians" (980-1037), and Bukhari (Mahommed ibn Ismail al-Bukhari, 810-872). Bukhari, an authority on traditions (*hadith*), traveled the Moslem world to collect and test traditions before returning to Bokhara to compile his *Sahih*, a collection of 7,275 tested traditions. This work is treated almost like the Koran in Islam.

The city's intellectual life was further shaped by mysticism from Persia during the middle ages. When 13th-century Mongol invasions devastated other Moslem cities like Samarkand, Bokhara remained independent and continued as a chief seat of Islamitic learning. Education is widespread; primary schools exist in the capital, other cities, and even villages, while *madrasas* (theological seminaries) for higher study are plentiful. About one-fourth of the population is said to be literate.

## Economy and Society
Bokhara is the most important trading town and the principal book-market of central Asia. Bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan visit the city to purchase rare books, as treasures of literature remain concealed in private libraries despite the loss of many *madrasa* libraries to confiscation or fire. The city bazaars trade in carpets, metal wares (particularly copper), silk stuffs, and Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins. Additionally, high-quality *Lájwurd* (lazuli) is sold in Bokhara, with the best quality priced at 30 to 60 tillas per pood.

The population is estimated between 50,000 and 100,000, including a large and ancient colony of Israelites. Socially, *mullahs* (priests) hold significant influence, and the population is noted for beliefs in omens, spirits, the evil eye, and witchcraft. Women occupy a low social position, although slavery has been abolished due to Russian influence.
