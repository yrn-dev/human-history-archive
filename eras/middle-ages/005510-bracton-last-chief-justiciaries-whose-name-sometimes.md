# Henry de Bracton

Henry de Bracton (also referred to as Henry of Bratton) was a prominent legal figure and one of the justices to King Henry III for many years. He died in 1268 and is described as the last of the chief justiciaries.

## The Treatise of Bracton
Bracton is the author of a large and noble book on the customs and laws of England. The work was produced during a great constructive era and was modeled after the treatise of Azo of Bologna, who was regarded by one Englishman as the "master of all the masters of the laws." From Azo's work, Bracton adopted many generalities of jurisprudence and maxims considered to have natural and universal validity.

The core of Bracton's work, however, focused on the practice of an English court. Bracton utilized parchment rolls that had kept diligent records of the court's operations for half a century, citing numerous decisions as precedents. He paid particular attention to the judgments of two deceased judges: William Raleigh and Martin Pateshull. To facilitate this work, Bracton compiled a large Note Book, which was discovered in the British Museum in 1884 by Professor Vinogradoff.

## Influence on English Common Law
Bracton's approach demonstrated an early tendency for English "common law" to evolve into "case law." During this period, English lawyers adopted the term "common law" from the canonists to distinguish the general law of the land from royal prerogatives, local customs, and other special or exceptional matters. In the legal understanding of the time, all expressly enacted laws—including statutes and ordinances—were excluded from the notion of "common law," as such enactments were still rarities.

While the Great Charter of 1215 was technically a grant of "liberties and privileges" similar to grants given to favored towns or individuals, its solemnity and importance led later ages to view it as the first article of a statute book. Other enacted laws of the era included the provisions issued at Marlborough (following the Barons' War) and those issued at Merton in 1236.

## Relationship to Later Legal Works
Bracton's treatise served as a primary source for subsequent legal texts. Two comprehensive text-books from the reign of King Edward I—known as *Fleta* and *Britton*—both quarried their materials from Bracton's work.

The work titled *Britton* is the earliest summary of English law written in the French tongue and purports to have been written by command of King Edward I. The authorship of *Britton* has been disputed; some attributed it to John le Breton, bishop of Hereford. However, this theory is complicated by the fact that the bishop died in 1275, while *Britton* references statutes passed later, specifically the statute *Quia emptores terrarum* of 1290. Selden proposed that *Britton* was actually a royal abridgment of Bracton's great work, supplemented with subsequent statutes, and that the title derived from Henry de Bracton (whose name appears in the fine Rolls as "Bretton" or "Bratton"). Despite this theory, the two works differ in arrangement, and only a small portion of Bracton's original work is incorporated into *Britton*.
