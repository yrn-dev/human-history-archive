# The Scottish Borders and the Cheviot Region

The term "Borders" refers to the territory situated on both sides of the boundary line between Scotland and England. While the name has geographical, historical, and literary meanings, it is most frequently applied to the Scottish side. The boundary line extends 108 miles from the coast of Berwickshire to the mouth of the river Sark at the head of the Solway Firth.

## Geography and Boundaries
The boundary begins 3 miles North by West of Berwick, running to the Tweed near Paxton and following the river to a point beyond Carham. From there, it strikes South-South-East to the Cheviot Hills, where the watershed serves as the boundary for 35 miles. The line is then formed by a series of streams, including the Kershope, Bells Burn, Liddel, and Esk. After a one-mile stretch along the Esk, it cuts west to the Sark.

The Scottish Border shires include Berwick, Roxburgh, and Dumfries, though Selkirk and Peebles are also historically and customarily classed as Border shires. The region is watered by several rivers, including the Tweed, Leet, Whiteadder, Kale, Jed, Kershope, Liddel, Esk, and Sark. Physically, the Scottish side differs from the southern side, which consists largely of rugged glens and bleak moorland.

## Early History and Roman Influence
In Roman times, the region was occupied by the Otadini (according to Ptolemy). The Roman road of Watling Street crossed the Cheviots at Brownhartlaw (1664 ft.), near the camp of *Ad Fines*, which was used to keep the Gadeni and Otadeni of the north and the Brigantes of the south in check. Another road, the Wheel Causeway, entered Scotland near the Liddel and North Tyne headwaters.

Roman control of the area was volatile. While the Wall of Antoninus Pius was drawn from sea to sea around A.D. 140, the land north of Cheviot appears to have been lost between A.D. 180 and 185. Emperor Septimius Severus later conducted a punitive expedition against northern tribes around A.D. 208, but ultimately fixed the substantive frontier at Hadrian's Wall.

From the 7th century onward, the region—including Roxburghshire, Berwickshire, and likely upper Tweeddale—formed part of the kingdoms of Northumbria and Bernicia. The Northumbrian king Æthelfrith is credited with conquering much of this territory, replacing natives with English settlers.

## Conflict and Lawlessness
For approximately 1,500 years, the region experienced frequent strife. Following the deaths of Margaret the Maid of Norway (1290) and Alexander III (1286), the country entered wars of independence and succession. Until the union of the crowns in 1603, the borders were frequently disturbed by battles and raids. Notable conflicts occurred at Flodden (1513), Otterburn (1388), Halidon Hill (1333), and Ancrum Moor (1544).

Lawlessness was further fueled by family feuds involving the Grahams, Eliots, Maxwells, Johnstones, Armstrongs, and other families. Significant encounters included the combat at Dryfe Sands (1593), the Raid of Reidswire (1575), and the clash at Arkenholme (14holm) in 1455. English expeditions in 1544 and 1545 were particularly destructive, resulting in the obliteration of Roxburgh and the destruction of four Scottish border abbeys.

## Administration and Defense
To regulate border lawlessness, the frontier was divided into the West, Middle, and East Marches. Each was managed by a Scots warden and an English warden. These officials were tasked with enforcing the law, protecting property, and preventing encroachment. The position was lucrative, as wardens received forage for their retinues and a portion of forfeits and fines.

Defensive structures were common on both sides. Castles were built to protect the kingdoms and overawe "mosstroopers" and freebooters, who remained a menace until the 17th century. A chain of peel towers, often the homes of marauding chiefs, crossed the country and served as beacon stations.

Following the union of the crowns in 1603, James I sought to erase the memory of past bitterness. He forbade the word "Borders," suggesting "Middle Shires" instead, and ordered the reduction of garrisons and the dismantling of frontier fortresses. While many English strongholds like Alnwick and Chillingham were modernized, most Scottish fortresses were demolished.

## Religious and Cultural Landmarks
The 12th century saw the establishment of several significant religious houses. On the Scottish side, the abbeys of Melrose, Kelso, Dryburgh, and Jedburgh were founded. On the English side, the cathedral of Carlisle and the abbeys of Alnwick and Hexham, along with the priory church of Lindisfarne, were established.

## Economy and Industry
The region's climate is mild, with an average annual rainfall of 53 inches and a mean yearly temperature of 48 deg. F. Historically, the area has focused on livestock. By the mid-19th century, 20,000 head of heavy cattle were sent annually to English markets, with shorthorns and Ayrshire dairy cattle replacing the earlier Galloways. Sheep breeding is extensive, with Cheviots used in higher hilly country and half-bred lambs (a cross of Leicesters or other long-woolled rams and Cheviots) on richer pastures.

Other industries include horse-breeding and salmon fisheries in the Solway Firth and the rivers Annan and Nith. Local manufactures include breweries and distilleries in Sanquhar and Annan, and Langholm is noted for its tweeds.
