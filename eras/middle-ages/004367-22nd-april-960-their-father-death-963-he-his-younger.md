# Basil II

## Early Life and Accession
Basil II was the son of Romanus II and Theophano, and the great-great-grandson of Basil I. Born approximately in 958, he was crowned as the Roman emperor in the East on April 22, 960. Following the death of his father in 963, Basil and his younger brother, Constantine, served as nominal emperors. During this period, actual power was held by their stepfather, Nicephorus Phocas, and subsequently by John Tzimisces.

## Joint Reign and Governance
Upon the death of John Tzimisces on January 10, 976, Basil and Constantine assumed sovereignty without a colleague. Despite this joint reign, Constantine exercised no power and focused primarily on pleasure. This arrangement adhered to the Byzantine principle that only one individual governed when two or more *basileis* were co-regnant.

Basil is described as a superb horseman and a brave soldier who eventually proved himself to be an able general and a strong ruler. However, he did not immediately demonstrate the full extent of his energy. Initially, the administration was managed by Basileios, a wily and gifted eunuch and president of the senate who was an illegitimate son of Romanus I. Basileios hoped to use the young emperors as puppets; during this time, Basil remained unobtrusive, observing and learning military science and the details of administrative business.

## Internal Conflicts and Rebellions
The throne faced a serious threat from Bardas Sclerus, an ambitious general from the powerful landed aristocracy of Asia Minor. After being deposed from his post as general in the East by the eunuch Basileios, Sclerus seized control of the Asiatic provinces and threatened Constantinople. To counter this, the emperor recalled Bardas Phocas, another general who had previously revolted and been interned in a monastery. Although Phocas was defeated in two battles, he was victorious in a third, which resulted in the suppression of the revolt in 979.

Bardas Phocas continued to serve as the general in the East until 987. At that time, he rebelled and was proclaimed emperor by his troops. It appears that the minister Basileios was privy to this rebellion, which was driven by dissatisfaction regarding the energy displayed by the emperor.
