# AVENZOAR

## Biography and Professional Standing
Avenzoar, also known as Abumeron (Ab[=u] Merw[=a]n 'Abdal-Malik ibn Zuhr), was an Arabian physician who flourished at the beginning of the 12th century. Born in Seville, he practiced his profession there and earned a great reputation. Avenzoar came from a lineage of celebrated physicians spanning several generations. This tradition of medical excellence continued with his son, who was regarded by Arabians as being even more eminent in the profession than Avenzoar himself.

Avenzoar was a contemporary of Averroes. According to the accounts of Leo Africanus, Averroes learned physic from Avenzoar and attended his lectures.

## Medical Philosophy and Methodology
In terms of his medical approach, Avenzoar was aligned with the Rational School, or the Dogmatists, rather than the Empirics. He was a great admirer of Galen. In his written works, he expressed emphatic protests against the use of superstitious remedies provided by astrologers as well as against quackery.

Avenzoar's perspective on the medical literature of his time was critical. While the *Canon* of Avicenna served as a guide for medical study in European universities from the 12th to the 17th century, Avenzoar held the view that the *Canon* was useful only as waste paper.

## Clinical Contributions and Observations
Avenzoar demonstrated significant knowledge of anatomy through his descriptions of medical conditions. He provided a remarkable description of an abscess and inflammation of the mediastinum occurring in his own person, detailing how to diagnose this condition to distinguish it from common pleuritis, as well as from dropsy and abscess of the pericardium.

He also developed ingenious treatments for cases of palsy or obstruction of the gullet. His proposed methods included:
* The use of nutritive enemata, which he strongly recommended.
* The introduction of food into the stomach via a silver tube, a method he spoke of more favorably than others.
* Placing the patient in a tepid bath of nutritious liquids to support strength through cutaneous imbibition, though he did not recommend this specific approach.

Furthermore, Avenzoar's writings indicate that during his time, the roles of the apothecary, surgeon, and physician were already viewed as distinct professions.

## Written Works
Avenzoar authored a book titled *The Method of Preparing Medicines and Diet*. This work underwent several translations to reach a wider audience: it was translated into Hebrew in 1280 and subsequently translated into Latin by Paravicius. The Latin version was first printed in Venice in 1490 and has since been published in several editions.
