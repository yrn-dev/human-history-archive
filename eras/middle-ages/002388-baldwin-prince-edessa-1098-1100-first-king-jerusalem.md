# Baldwin I

Baldwin I was the first king of Jerusalem (1100–1118) and previously served as the prince of Edessa from 1098 to 1100. He was the brother of Godfrey of Bouillon. Originally a clerk in orders who held several prebends, Baldwin joined the first crusade in 1096.

## Rise to Power in Edessa
During the first crusade, Baldwin accompanied his brother Godfrey as far as Heraclea in Asia Minor. While at Heraclea, Baldwin followed Tancred into Cilicia, driven by political motives and jealousy. In September 1097, Baldwin wrested Tarsus from Tancred and established his own garrison there. After rejoining the main army at Marash, he was invited by an Armenian named Pakrad to move east toward the Euphrates, where he occupied Tell-bashir. 

Following a subsequent invitation from Thoros of Edessa, Baldwin arrived in Edessa first as a protector. He became Thoros's successor in March 1098 after Thoros was assassinated. For the next two years, Baldwin ruled Edessa, marrying an Armenian wife and serving as a general intermediary between the Armenians and the crusaders. During this period, he successfully defended his position against a conspiracy of his own subjects in 1098 and against surrounding Mahommedan powers, from whom he won Seruj (Sarorgia) and Samosata.

## Accession to the Throne of Jerusalem
Baldwin visited Jerusalem in late 1099 with Bohemund I, though he returned to Edessa in January 1100. Following the death of his brother Godfrey, Baldwin was summoned to Jerusalem by a party seeking a successor. This summons was the result of a lay reaction against the theocratic pretensions of Dagobert, who had been counting on Norman support. 

Baldwin was crowned the first king of Jerusalem on Christmas Day, 1100, by the patriarch. However, his early reign was marked by a struggle between church and state. In the spring of 1101, Baldwin had Dagobert suspended by a papal legate. Later that year, the two clashed over the patriarch's required contribution to the defense of the Holy Land. This conflict concluded in 1102 with the deposition of Dagobert and the triumph of Baldwin.

## Expansion and Governance
Having secured the supremacy of lay power, Baldwin worked to transform straggling territories into a compact kingdom. He achieved this through alliances with Italian trading towns, most notably Genoa. In exchange for quarters in conquered towns, these allies provided the skill and instruments necessary for siege warfare. Through these means, Baldwin captured Caesarea and Arsuf in 1101, Acre in 1104, and Sidon and Beirut in 1110, the latter with assistance from the Norwegians and Venetians.

Baldwin also defended the kingdom against Egyptian attacks in 1102, 1103, and 1105. In the final years of his reign (1115–1118), he pushed southward into Egypt, reaching as far as the Red Sea and establishing an outpost at Monreal. In the north, Baldwin acted as a practical, though nominally, suzerain over the three Christian principalities of Edessa, Antioch, and Tripoli. Between 1109 and 1110, he composed dissensions among the princes of these regions and helped them maintain their positions against the amirs of Mosul, specifically Aksunk-ur and Maudud.

## Death and Succession
Baldwin I died in 1118 following an expedition to Egypt in which he captured Farama. According to the chronicler Fulcher, Baldwin died after eating many fish during this expedition. 

Upon receiving news of Baldwin I's death, his nephew, Baldwin II (formerly Baldwin de Burg), traveled to Jerusalem. Baldwin II had previously served as the count of Edessa, having been summoned to that position by Baldwin I. Through the influence of Patriarch Arnulf, Baldwin II was made king.

## Historical Sources
The primary authority for the career of Baldwin I is the *Historia Hierosolymitana* (also known as *Gesta Francorum Jerusalem expugnantium*), written by Fulcher of Chartres. Fulcher was a French priest and chronicler who served as Baldwin's chaplain, living with him first at Edessa and later in Jerusalem. Fulcher's account is considered one of the most trustworthy sources of the first crusade as he only detailed events he personally witnessed. Other sketches of Baldwin's reign are found in the works of R. Roehricht and a monograph by Wolff.
