# Buckinghamshire and the Danish Invasions

## Early History and Saxon Settlement
The region that became Buckinghamshire was first reached by the West Saxons in 571. This expansion occurred as they secured a series of victories while pushing northward along the Thames valley. Over time, settlements were grouped into kingdoms, and Buckinghamshire was incorporated into Mercia during the consolidation of that kingdom under Offa.

## Danish Influence and the Danelaw
Buckinghamshire's political status shifted when the kingdom of Mercia submitted to the Northmen, at which point the district became part of the Danelaw. Throughout the 10th century, the area suffered frequently from the ravages of the Danes. Evidence of the struggles against these invaders remains in the landscape, where numerous earthworks and barrows mark the scenes of conflict. These relics are particularly abundant in the vale of Aylesbury, which was likely one of the best protected and richest Saxon settlements during this period.

## The Chiltern District
In contrast to the vale of Aylesbury, the Chiltern district was described as an impassable forest. This area was infested by wild beasts and hordes of robbers. To combat these depredations, Leofstan, the 12th abbot of St Albans, cut down large tracts of wood during the reign of Edward the Confessor. Leofstan granted the manor of Hamstead in Hertfordshire to two fellow-soldiers and a valiant knight on the condition that they check the robbers' activities. This necessity led to the early appointment of a steward of the Chiltern Hundreds; although the need for the office eventually vanished, it continued as a sinecure. The district was not finally disforested until the reign of James I.

## Post-Conquest and Later History
At the time of the Norman invasion, Buckinghamshire was likely part of the earldom of Leofwine, son of Godwin. Because the county supported Leofwine at the battle of Hastings, it was punished with sweeping confiscations following the Conquest. Due to its proximity to London, the county was involved in many major national events in subsequent centuries.

During the conflict between King John and his barons, William Mauduit defended Hanslape Castle against the king until it was captured and demolished by Falkes de Bréauté in 1216. The county also suffered severely from the Black Death, with districts such as Winslow becoming almost entirely depopulated. 

During the civil war, Buckinghamshire was among the first counties to join a mutual defence association supporting the parliament, maintaining garrisons at Brill and Aylesbury. Royalist troops briefly garrisoned Newport Pagnell, and in 1644, the king established his headquarters at Buckingham.
