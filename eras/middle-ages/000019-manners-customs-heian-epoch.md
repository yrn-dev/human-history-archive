# MANNERS AND CUSTOMS OF THE HEIAN EPOCH

The Heian epoch was a period of 398 years, beginning in 794 with the transfer of the Imperial palace from Nara to Kyoto and ending in 1192 with the establishment of an administrative capital at Kamakura. The city of Kyoto was given the name "Heian-jo," meaning "Castle of Peace," shortly after it became the residence of the Mikado. The epoch began under the rule of Emperor Kwammu, who had been selected for the throne by his father, Konin, at the suggestion of Fujiwara Momokawa.

## Court Amusements and Literature
Pastimes within Heian Court society were characterized by extravagance and elaboration. There was a significant passion for literature, which led to the extreme development of the couplet-tournament, known as *uta awase*. This activity, which had some vogue during the Nara epoch, became a "furore" at Court. During the first half of the Heian epoch, Emperors such as Emperor Koko hosted splendid verse-making parties in palaces that were richly decorated, frequently with beautiful flowers. In these early parties, the ladies and gentlemen of the Court remained separated, sitting on opposite sides of the room.

In the later portion of the Heian epoch, competitive amusements shifted toward the composition of love letters. While these letters followed implicit canons of elegant phraseology, their actual contents were often frankly indecent. Other recorded pastimes of the era included football and the playing of battledore and shuttlecock.

## Music and Dance
The growth of luxury and extravagant entertainment during the Heian epoch resulted in a remarkable development of dance and music. The variety of musical instruments increased greatly, with as many as twenty different kinds in use. These included the six-stringed harp (or wagon) as well as more complex lutes or harps featuring twenty-five or thirteen strings.

The epoch saw a proliferation of dance, with varieties numbering three or four times the amount of musical instruments. Most of these dances were pantomimic in style. According to usual classifications, the origins of these dances were foreign, specifically Chinese, Korean, and Hindu, though they existed alongside adaptations of foreign styles and older native styles.

## Domestic Life and Pets
The nobility of the Heian epoch utilized a specific style of dwelling house known as *shindenzukubi*. A notable custom of the period was the keeping of animals as pets, particularly dogs and cats. These pets were often given official titles and human names. 

The treatment of pets could be exceptionally lavish; for example, kittens born at the palace toward the end of the tenth century were treated with a level of consideration comparable to that shown to Imperial infants. Courtiers would send ceremonial presents to the cat-mother following childbirth, and a lady-in-waiting would be appointed as a guardian for the kittens. When these pets died, they were given elaborate funerals.

## Social Turbulence and Religious Conflict
Despite the "Peace" implied by the name of the capital, the era experienced instances of turbulence. This instability was reflected in the "reckless prodigality" of Shirakawa in the furnishing and building of temples. Conflict was common among cenobites; for instance, Todai-ji and Kimbusen were involved in quarrels with Kofuku-ji. Some priests abandoned their cassocks and stoles to engage in temporary brigandage.

The military family of the Taira, specifically the leader Tadamori and his son Kiyomori, dealt strongly with the Shinto communities of Gion and Hiyoshi and forcibly removed Kofuku-ji priests from the streets of Kyoto. These actions made the Taira family objects of execration at Enryaku-ji and Kofuku-ji. The Court struggled to maintain peace between these factions. It is noted that Shirakawa Ho-o identified three things that defied his control: the *yama-hoshi*, the fall of the dice, and the waters of the Kamo River.
