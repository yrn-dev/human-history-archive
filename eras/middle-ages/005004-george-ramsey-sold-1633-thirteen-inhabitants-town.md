# Sir George Ramsey and the Manor of Bridlington

## History of Bridlington
Bridlington (also referred to as Brellington or Burlington) was established as a borough prior to the Norman Conquest, as evidenced by the Domesday survey's mention of four burgesses. During the reign of Edward the Confessor, the town was part of the possessions of Earl Morcar and held a value of £32. However, following the ravages of the Normans, the town's value decreased to 8s. by the time of the Domesday survey, at which point it had passed into the hands of the king due to the forfeiture of Earl Morcar.

William II later granted Bridlington to Gilbert de Gaunt. Gilbert's son and heir, Walter, founded the priory and provided it with the manor of Bridlington and additional lands. The town's importance grew steadily from this period. The gift made by Walter de Gaunt was confirmed by Henry I and several subsequent kings; notably, King Stephen granted the town the right to have a port. In 1200, King John granted the prior of Bridlington an annual fair on the morrow, feast, and vigil of the Assumption of the Virgin Mary, as well as a weekly market held on Saturdays. Later, in 1446, Henry VI granted the prior three additional yearly fairs on the day, vigil, and morrow of the Nativity of the Virgin Mary, and the Translation and Deposition of St John, a former prior of Bridlington.

## Crown Ownership and the Ramsey Family
In 1546, Henry IV granted the convent and prior exemption from subsidies, tenths, and fifteenths in exchange for prayers for himself and his queen during every mass sung at the high altar. Following the Dissolution, the manor of Bridlington remained the property of the crown until 1624. In that year, Charles I granted the manor to Sir John Ramsey. Upon Sir John's death, the manor passed to his brother and heir, Sir George Ramsey.

## The Sale of 1633 and Local Governance
In 1633, Sir George Ramsey sold the manor to thirteen inhabitants of the town. This sale was conducted on behalf of all the tenants of the manor. These thirteen lords were supported by an additional twelve inhabitants who were chosen by the freeholders. 

The governance of the manor established a system where a chief lord was chosen annually. In instances where the number of lords was reduced to six, seven new lords were selected from the group of assistants. This system of governance remained in effect. The fact that thirteen inhabitants were permitted to hold the manor suggests that Bridlington possessed some form of incorporation during the 17th century, although the town was not officially created a municipal borough via an incorporation charter until 1899.
