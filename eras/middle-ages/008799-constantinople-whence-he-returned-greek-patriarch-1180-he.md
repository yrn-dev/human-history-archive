# Constantinople

Constantinople served as the capital of the Greek-speaking Eastern Empire and functioned as a primary center of ecclesiastical and political authority. Throughout its history, the city was the seat of a dominant emperor and a high-bishop, known as the patriarch, who exercised significant influence over the Church.

## Ecclesiastical Structure and Schisms
In the early history of the Christian church, ecclesiastical authority in the empire of Constantinople was shared among the patriarchs of Jerusalem, Alexandria, Antioch, and Constantinople. While the Bishop of Rome was recognized as the first among these patriarchs, the patriarch of Constantinople remained a dominant figure within the Eastern Empire. However, the Greek prelate was often viewed as a domestic slave to the emperor, moving between the throne and the convent at the emperor's will.

A formal severance occurred in 1054 between the Latin-speaking Western church and the Greek-speaking "Orthodox" church. This split was ostensibly caused by the Latin addition of the word "*Filioque*" ("and from the son") to the creed, which originally stated that the "Holy Ghost proceeded from the Father." This theological disagreement, along with differences in habits—such as the *fakiolion* or cardinal's hat—imbittered the schism.

## Political and Religious Transitions
The city's religious landscape shifted significantly following the taking of Constantinople. After this event, the Greek church experienced periods of domestic quarrels and slavery. One patriarch who succeeded Gennadius is recorded to have thrown himself into a well in despair. Gennadius himself, originally named George Scholarius, was a figure of perceived duplicity, having defended the union at Florence while later attacking it furiously in Constantinople.

During the period of the Empire des François, the city saw the appointment of Latin patriarchs. Due to a national monopoly sought by Venetians, it was required that canons of St. Sophia be Venetians who had lived in Venice for ten years; consequently, only the first and last of the six Latin patriarchs of Constantinople were Venetians.

## The Ottoman Period
Under Ottoman rule, the city's demographics continued to evolve. In 1586, records indicate that Christians and others were more numerous in the capital than Moslems. The city also became the site of the *Turbé*, the sepulchral monument of Abu Ayub.

In the 17th century, Cyrillus Lucaris (1572–1637), a native of Crete, served as the patriarch of Constantinople starting in 1621. Lucaris sought to reform the Orthodox Eastern Church along Calvinistic lines, sending young Greek theologians to universities in England, Holland, and Switzerland. He published a *Confessio* in 1629 that attempted to accommodate Calvinistic doctrine with the creeds of the Orthodox Church. Lucaris also established a Greek printing-press in Constantinople, which he used to issue a work condemning the faith of Mahomet. This act led to the suppression of the press after he was denounced to the Turks by the Jesuits. Lucaris was eventually killed by the Janissaries in June 1637 on the orders of Sultan Murad, who wished to avoid trouble before the Persian War after Lucaris was accused of attempting to stir up the Cossacks.

## External Relations and Influence
Constantinople maintained complex relations with surrounding powers and leaders. In 1164, Bohemund III, the prince of Antioch, visited Constantinople after being ransomed by his brother-in-law, Emperor Manuel; he returned from the city accompanied by a Greek patriarch. 

The city also remained a point of contention in ecclesiastical jurisdiction disputes, such as those between the patriarchs of Rome and Constantinople regarding provinces of the Greek idiom and empire assigned to the Bulgarian kingdom. Additionally, the city was a site of imperial trials; for example, Severus was summoned to Constantinople for a trial where, according to some accounts, it was intended that his tongue be cut out.
