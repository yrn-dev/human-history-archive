# Official Residences, Endowments, and Legal Codes

## Official Residences and Administrative Centers
In various historical contexts, the residence of high-ranking officials served as a center of authority. In the city of Khiva, the capital of the khanate of Khiva in Western Asia, the citadel serves as the residence for both the khan and the higher officials. In the context of international diplomacy, the concept of "exterritoriality" and inviolability applies to the official staff of diplomatic missions. For non-official staff members who are citizens of the country where the mission resides, these immunities are limited specifically to the area within the official residence.

## Land Ownership and State Obligations
Historical records indicate evolving systems of land tenure and state demands. Originally, the god of a city was considered the owner of the land, with citizens acting as tenants. Over time, the king and the god ceased to disturb these tenancies, accepting fixed dues in the form of money, service, stock, or *naturalia*. While the Code recognized complete private ownership of land—extending this right to merchants, votaries, and potentially resident aliens—all land was sold subject to fixed charges.

The king possessed the authority to issue charters to free land from these charges, a method frequently used to reward those who served the state well. The state imposed several obligations on landholders:
* **Military Service:** Certain areas, termed "a bow" as early as the 8th century B.C., were required to provide a bowman and a linked pikeman (who served as the shield-bearer for both), including their campaign supplies. Later, some areas were required to provide a horseman. While the land had to provide a man annually, an individual was only bound to serve a limited number of times (possibly six). Service was typically performed by serfs and slaves, though the *muskinu* and *amelu* went to war.
* **Labor and Maintenance:** The state demanded corvee labor, though religious officials and shepherds in charge of flocks were exempt. Riparian owners faced special liabilities to repair quays, bridges, and canals.
* **Dues:** The state claimed proportions of stock and crops. Additionally, cities levied water rates, highway rates, ferry dues, customs, and octroi duties.

## The Daiho Code and Japanese Administration
In Japan, the Daiho legislators of the early eighth century established a comprehensive legal framework consisting of a penal law (*ritsu*), a code (*ryo*), operative regulations (*shiki*), and official rules (*kyaku*). The scope of the code was extensive, covering thirty sections including:
* **Officialdom:** Official titles, ranks, salaries, and the duties of officials, including those in the households of the Empress, the Heir Apparent, and officers of high rank.
* **State and Society:** Taxation, the land, public works, the army, frontier defenses, and the family.
* **Court and Ceremony:** Ceremonies, official costumes, the descent of the crown, and the mode of addressing persons of rank.
* **Public Order:** Jails, the arrest of criminals, and "watch and ward and markets."

The potency of the Daiho code was tied to the centralization of administrative authority; it lost binding force during periods when feudalism prevailed. Some sections, such as the Land Code (*Den-ryo*), fell into disuse quickly, while the hereditary nobles' influence led to the neglect of the Code of Official Ranks and Titles and the Military Code (*Gumbo-ryo*).

## Legal Rank and Penalties
Under the Japanese legal system, the rank of a culprit influenced the mitigation of penalties. There were eight major crimes (*hachi-gyaku*) viewed as offenses against the family, the Court, and the State. These were ordered by gravity as follows:
1. High treason (against the State)
2. High treason (against the Crown)
3. Treason
4. Fratricide, parricide, etc.
5. Offences against humanity
6. Lèse majesté
7. Unfilial conduct
8. Crimes against society

To lighten punishments based on public services, official position, or rank, six mitigations (*roku-gi*) were enacted. Conversely, the law took no cognizance of slaves, as they were viewed as the proprietor's property and chattels.

## Administrative Evolution and Guidance
During the reign of Temmu, the obligations of decorum were elaborated upon through a law of ninety-two articles governing Court ceremonials. This included explicit instructions on the salutation and demeanour of each grade of official, notably banning the custom of kneeling or moving on hands and knees in the presence of superiors; all salutations were required to be performed standing.

By 819, under the orders of Emperor Saga, a committee presided over by the dainagon Fujiwara Fuyutsugu issued ten volumes of rules and forty volumes of regulations. These served as administrative guides, collecting rescripts, decrees, and instructions from high officials since 701. Despite these guides, metropolitan officials and provincial governors—often chosen for aristocratic influence or literary ability—were frequently incapable of managing lawless conditions in their districts.
