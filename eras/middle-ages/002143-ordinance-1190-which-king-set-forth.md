# The Ordinance of 1190 and Related Historical Events

## The French Administrative Ordinance of 1190
In 1190, the King of France issued an ordinance to arrange for the administration of his kingdom while he was away on a crusade. This ordinance established a general system of governance featuring local bailiffs (*bailli*). These officials, who were functionally identical to seneschals (the terminology varying by district), served as the king's primary representatives in the provinces.

The royal bailiffs or seneschals likely originated from seigniorial bailiffs of great fiefs that had been annexed and reunited with the crown. Initially, their primary role was the surveillance of royal provosts (*prevots*), who had previously held sole administration over various parts of the domain. The bailiffs concentrated the produce of the provostships in their own hands and were responsible for organizing and leading men who owed military service to the king under feudal rules.

Over time, the judicial functions of the bailiffs expanded. They held periodical assizes in the principal centers of their districts. Following the institution of the right of appeal, bailiffs heard appeals from sentences issued by seigniorial justices and inferior royal judges. Additionally, cases involving nobles as defendants and other royal cases were reserved specifically for them. Consequently, the *bailliage* or *senechaussee* became as significant judicially as it was administratively.

## Other Events of 1190
The year 1190 is associated with several other distinct historical developments across different regions:

**Japan:** On November 2, 1190, Yoritomo departed from Kamakura, arriving in Kyoto on December 5. His journey was an Imperial progress characterized by a leisurely pace, including a stop in Owari to worship at his father's tomb. While the cloistered Emperor received him with consideration and held long conversations with him, Yoritomo did not achieve his primary purpose. He was granted the appointments of general of the Right division of the guards and *gon-dainagon*, but he resigned these posts within a few days, viewing them as limited and formal, preferring instead to be nominated *so-tsuihoshi*.

**Serbia:** In 1190, King Stephen Nemanya founded a diminutive church of white marble at the monastery of Studenitsa. Located 24 miles south-west of Kralyevo in the south-western mountains overlooking a tributary of the Ibar, the monastery consists of a belfry and timber and plaster buildings. King Stephen Nemanya later became a monk and was canonized as St Simeon.

**England:** Around the year 1190, John de Hauteville (or Hanville), a monk of St. Alban's, wrote a poetical work titled *Architrenius*.

## Comparative Use of Ordinances in History
The provided archives contain various examples of "ordinances" used as instruments of state power and law across different civilizations:

**Ancient Persia:** King Darius utilized royal ordinances to maintain control over his satraps. In one instance, Bagæus used ordinances bearing the seal of Darius to destroy the rebellious satrap Orœtês of Phrygia, Lydia, and Ionia. After presenting less important ordinances to gain the trust of the Persian guards, Bagæus had a secretary read an ordinance forbidding the Persians to serve Orœtês, followed by a final warrant commanding them to kill him.

**Ancient Athens:** Drako set forth in writing ordinances that the eupatrid archons had previously enforced without written records. While later viewed by Aristotle as intolerably rigorous, these ordinances introduced mitigating distinctions regarding homicide based on circumstances. For example, accidental homicide was tried at the palladium, while justified homicide (such as self-defense) was tried at the Delphinium.

**Tudor England:** In the 16th century, the translation of the Bible by Thomas Matthew (likely an alias for John Rogers) was "set forth with the kinges most gracyous lycence." This was done to ensure the book could be sold and read without danger from any previous proclamation or ordinance granted to the contrary.
