## The Bohun Family and the Great Charter
The Bohun family was a significant force in English history during the 13th and 14th centuries, originating from a village in the Cotentin between Coutances and the estuary of the Vire. The family's prominence began with Humphrey III, a steward in the royal household during the latter years of Henry I. Humphrey III served Henry II loyally, fighting against East Anglian rebels at Farnham in 1173.

His grandson, Henry, obtained the earldom of Hereford from John in 1199, aided by his mother Margaret's connection to the royal house of Scotland. Henry de Bohun became a foremost Marcher baron and was one of the twenty-five barons elected to enforce the terms of the Great Charter. During the subsequent civil war, Henry fought for Louis and was captured at the battle of Lincoln in 1217.

Henry's son, Humphrey V, initially returned to loyalty and inherited the earldom of Essex from his uncle, William de Mandeville, before 1239. However, by 1258, Humphrey V shifted his allegiance to the baronial cause, serving as a nominee of the opposition on a committee of twenty-four appointed to reform administration during the Oxford parliament. He only returned to royal allegiance following the alliance between Montfort and Llewelyn of North Wales.

## Defiance and Sovereignty in Norway and Orkney
In the chronicles of Norway, the relationship between kings and earls is often marked by tension and defiance. Sigurd Syr once cautioned King Olaf that "great people" were historically accustomed to bidding defiance to their sovereigns, warning that the king's wilfulness might lead to betrayal.

In another instance, Earl Einar and the people of Orkney were condemned by the king to pay a fine of sixty marks of gold. To resolve this, the earl offered to pay the entire fine if the bondes surrendered their udal lands to him. This arrangement remained in place until Sigurd, son of Hlodver, restored the udal rights.

Political friction is also noted in the case of Earl Ragnvald. The Swedish king expressed severe disapproval of Ragnvald for entering into a peaceful truce and friendship with Olaf the Thick, characterizing this action as treason. The Swedish king suggested that Ragnvald deserved to be driven out of the kingdom and attributed the earl's imprudent actions to the influence of his wife, Ingebjorg.

## Conflict in Ireland
In the 16th century, Gerald, the earl of Desmond, engaged in a series of conflicts involving the English crown and other nobles. After succeeding to the earldom in 1558, Desmond established relations with Shane O'Neill and Gerald Fitzgerald, the 11th earl of Kildare. Despite royal awards intended to regulate disputes, Geraldine outlaws continued to plunder.

Desmond's relationship with the crown was strained by his insolent conduct before the privy council in May 1562, which led to a brief imprisonment in the Tower of London. Upon his return to Ireland in 1564, he engaged in raids in Thomond and attempted to enforce feudal rights in Waterford against Sir Maurice Fitzgerald of Decies. This led to open war and a battle at Affane on the Blackwater, where Desmond was defeated and captured.

Later, James Fitzmaurice Fitzgerald, a cousin of the earl, sought to usurp the earldom by acclaiming himself captain of Desmond in defiance of Sidney. Fitzmaurice attempted to gain foreign assistance by giving the movement an ultra-Catholic character, allying with Connor O'Brien, the earl of Thomond, and John Burke.
