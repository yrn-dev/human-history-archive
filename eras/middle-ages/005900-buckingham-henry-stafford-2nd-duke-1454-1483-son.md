# BUCKINGHAM, HENRY STAFFORD, 2ND DUKE OF

Henry Stafford, 2nd Duke of Buckingham (1454–1483), was a prominent English nobleman whose royal lineage and extensive inheritance made him a figure of significance to King Edward IV. He was the son of Margaret, daughter of Edmund Beaufort, 2nd Duke of Somerset (grandson of John of Gaunt), and Humphrey Stafford, who was killed in 1455 at the first battle of St Albans. Through his father, Henry was the grandson of Humphrey, the 1st Duke of Buckingham, who was killed at Northampton in 1460. Both his father and grandfather fought for Lancaster.

The 1st Duke had held the title of earl of Buckingham through his mother, Anne, who was the daughter of Thomas, Duke of Gloucester (the youngest son of Edward III) and the sister of Humphrey, who bore the title of earl of Buckingham only. Anne had married Edmund Stafford, Earl of Stafford, and upon her death in 1438, the title passed to her son Humphrey Stafford, who was created duke of Buckingham in 1444.

## Early Life and Marriage
Henry was recognized as duke in 1465. In 1466, he married Catherine Woodville, the sister of the queen. Upon reaching manhood in 1474, he was appointed a knight of the Garter. In 1478, he served as high steward during the trial of George, Duke of Clarence. Aside from these roles, he did not hold positions of great importance during this period, and it appeared that his marriage to the queen's sister secured his fidelity.

## Service Under Richard III
Following the death of Edward IV, Buckingham became one of the first individuals influenced by Richard, Duke of Gloucester. Buckingham assisted Richard in obtaining possession of the young king, Edward V. As a reward, he was granted several offices, including chamberlain and justiciar of South and North Wales, as well as constable of all the royal castles in the Welsh Marches and the principality.

Buckingham played a prominent role in the proceedings that led to the deposition of Edward V. On June 24, 1483, he delivered an eloquent speech at the Guildhall, urging the citizens to accept Richard as king, noting that Richard was "marvellously well spoken" and "neither unlearned." During Richard's coronation, Buckingham served as chamberlain and was subsequently confirmed in his Welsh powers and appointed constable of England.

## Rebellion and Downfall
Despite the rewards bestowed upon him, Buckingham withdrew from the court to Brecon in early August 1483. This withdrawal may have been motivated by a desire for greater rewards or ambitions to establish his own claims to the crown. He eventually led an insurrection, which included the support of Thomas Grey, 1st Marquess of Dorset. This rebellion failed, and the archive's records indicate that Francis Lovell, Viscount Lovell, assisted Richard III in suppressing the uprising.

## Legacy and Succession
Henry Stafford was succeeded as 3rd Duke by his eldest son, Edward (1478–1521), after an attainder was removed in 1485. Henry's second son became the Earl of Wiltshire. The 3rd Duke served as lord high constable at the start of Henry VIII's reign but was executed for treason in 1521 following his opposition to Wolsey. With the 3rd Duke's attainder, the title was forfeited. 

The dukedom of Buckingham remained in the Stafford family until the execution of the 3rd Duke. While the title was later recreated for other families, such as the Villiers and the Grenvilles, the Stafford line continued through the 3rd Duke's son, Henry (1501–1563), who was restored in blood as Baron Stafford in 1547.
