# The Decay of the Abbasid Caliphate

The Abbasid dynasty rose to power in 749 through a carefully prepared revolution that overthrew the Omayyads. The first Abbasid Caliph, Abul Abbas, established his rule by massacring the male members of the Omayyad line and destroying their tombs. While the early period of the dynasty included figures such as Haroun-al-Raschid (786-809), who presided over an outwardly prosperous empire, the caliphate eventually entered a period of significant political and territorial decline.

## Political Fragmentation and the Rise of the Turkish Guard
The downfall of the Abbasid dynasty and its capital, Bagdad, began with the rise of the Turkish body-guard under Mo'tassim, the successor to Mamun. Mo'tassim founded the city of Samarra, and for fifty-eight years, the court and caliph deserted Bagdad. In 865, the Caliph Mosta'in attempted to flee the tyranny of the Turkish guard by returning to Bagdad, but the city was besieged and taken.

From this point until their final collapse, the Abbasid caliphs became mere puppets. Real temporal power was held successively by the Turkish guard, the Buyids, and the Seljuks. In Bagdad, the Caliph became a creature of his Turkish palace guards, with a Turk serving as a "mayor of the palace" and acting as the actual ruler.

## Territorial Loss and Religious Division
As the central authority of the Abbasids decayed, the Moslem Empire ceased to be a single entity. The orthodox Sunnite Abbasid rule shrunken until it occupied only what was once Babylonia. The empire became religiously and politically divided:
* **The West:** Egypt became a separate power under the Fatimite Caliphate (established 969), led by a Shiite Caliph claiming descent from Ali and Fatima.
* **The East:** A Shiite kingdom emerged in Persia.
* **Other Regions:** Spain remained under a separate Omayyad Caliph, and North Africa became effectively independent, though it remained nominally subject to the Abbasids.

## The Seljuk Intervention and the Battle of Manzikert
In the eleventh century, the Seljuk Turks, who were orthodox Sunnites, entered the decaying fragments of the empire. They conquered the Shiite rulers in Persia, Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, establishing themselves as the protectors of the Bagdad Caliph and assuming the temporal powers previously held by the Turkish "mayor of the palace."

The Seljuks expanded rapidly, conquering Armenia from the Greeks and moving into Asia Minor. Under Alp Arslan, the Seljuks utterly smashed the Byzantine army at the battle of Manzikert (or Melasgird) in 1071. This victory created the impression that Islam, which had appeared to be in deep decay, had risen again, while the Byzantine Empire seemed to be on the brink of dissolution. The Seljuks subsequently established themselves at Iconium (Konia) and took possession of the fortress of Nicæa.

## The Decline of Major Cities
The decay of the caliphate had a direct impact on the urban centers of the region:
* **Bagdad:** Although it remained the residence of the caliphs—who continued to serve as the religious heads of Islam—and remained a city of the first rank for a time, it suffered a rapid decline in its final century. By the close of the 12th century, travelers described the western city as almost in ruins and the eastern half as containing large uninhabited spaces.
* **Basra:** The importance of Basra declined alongside the power of the Abbasid caliphate. Its canals were neglected and communication with the Persian Gulf was severed, leading to the eventual abandonment of the site.

## The End of the Abbasid Caliphate
The extinction of the Abbasid caliphate of Bagdad occurred in 1258 when the city was captured by the Mongols under Hulagu, the grandson of Jenghiz Khan. With this event, Bagdad ceased to be a city of the first rank and lost its status as the religious center of Islam.

The remnants of the dynasty persisted in a diminished state; the last Abbasid Caliph lived in Egypt under the protection of the Mameluke Sultan. This final degenerate Abbasid eventually sold the title of Caliph, along with the sacred banner and other relics of the Prophet, to the Ottoman Sultan Selim (1512-1520). Consequently, the Ottoman Sultan became the Caliph of all Islam.
