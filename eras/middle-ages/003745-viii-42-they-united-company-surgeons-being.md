# Surgery and the Barber-Surgeon Profession

## The Evolution of the Barber-Surgeon
Historically, the craft of the barber—defined as one who shaves or trims beards and acts as a hairdresser—was considered a profession because it was conjoined with the art of surgery. In England, barbers first received incorporation in 1461 under Edward IV. Later, by 32 Henry VIII. c. 42, barbers were united with the company of surgeons. This union established a legal division of labor: barbers were required to limit their practice to "minor operations," specifically the drawing of teeth and blood-letting, while surgeons were forbidden from engaging in "barbery or shaving."

The barber's shop served as a social hub and a focus for news, often providing musical entertainment for waiting customers via a viol, lute, or similar instrument. The traditional sign of the barber—a striped pole with a suspended basin—carried symbolic meaning related to these surgical roots. The basin represented the vessel used to collect blood, and the fillet around the pole represented the ribbon used for bandaging the arm during the process of bleeding.

The union of these two crafts eventually ended. In France, barber-surgeons were incorporated as a distinct body during the reign of Louis XIV, separating them from the *perruquiers*. In England, the separation into distinct corporations occurred in 1745 via 18 George II. c. 15.

## Military Surgery and Field Operations
In military contexts, such as those within the German armies in the West, medical service staff are tasked with supervising hygiene, analyzing drinking water, and overseeing food supplies to prevent the spread of epidemics. Sick soldiers are initially cared for in "local sick-rooms" before being transferred to the Army Medical Service.

At the fighting line, army surgeons establish regimental dressing stations. While some surgeons accompany bearer patrols onto the battlefield, they generally do not enter the actual fire zone except in exceptional cases. Each infantry division maintains at least one medical service company, which consists of nine surgeons and a variety of apothecaries, sick attendants, and bearers. These companies establish principal dressing stations at the rear and utilize ambulance wagons—including two medical-store wagons equipped with dressing materials, stretchers, and medicaments, as well as food-supply and baggage wagons—to transport the dangerously wounded from advanced halting-places.

Modern field surgery, as noted by Professor Hildebrand of Göttingen, operates on the cardinal principle of avoiding amputation unless it is a case of utmost urgency. This marks a shift from previous eras where limbs were amputated in far greater numbers; current practice emphasizes saving whatever is possible.

## Surgical Techniques and Innovations
The treatment of fractures, specifically the *fracture of the patella* (knee-cap), has seen significant evolution. Traditional methods involved awaiting results while fixing the limb in plaster of Paris splints or on a back-splint. While many recovered, some were left with enfeebled limbs because the fragments remained wide apart.

In 1883, Lister introduced a method of opening the knee-joint under conditions of cleanliness and care to secure the broken fragments in close apposition using a stout wire suture. This allowed the fragments to unite solidly by bone. Although some surgeons feared the risk of septic micro-organisms invading the joint, Lister's operation is regarded as providing perfect results with comparatively small risk when performed by skillful surgeons. Alternative "compromise" methods include Professor A.E.J. Barker's technique of passing a strong silver wire vertically around the fragments with a large needle without opening the joint, or using metal or silk sutures circumferentially. Lister also emphasized that the careful selection of patients is itself an antiseptic measure.

## Notable Figures in Surgery
Sir William MacCormac (1836–1901) was a prominent Irish surgeon who studied in Paris, Dublin, and Belfast. He served as the surgeon to the General Hospital in Belfast before moving to London in 1861. MacCormac gained expertise in gun-shot wounds through his service as surgeon-in-chief to the Anglo-American Ambulance during the Franco-German War of 1870 (where he was present at Sedan) and during the Turco-Servian War of 1876.

His career included serving as an assistant-surgeon and consulting surgeon at St Thomas's Hospital in London for twenty years. He held several prestigious positions, including president of the College of Surgeons (elected 1896 and re-elected through 1900), Hunterian Orator in 1899, and surgeon-in-ordinary to the prince of Wales. He also served as consulting surgeon to the forces in Natal and Cape Colony from November 1899 to April 1900. He was appointed honorary serjeant-surgeon to the king in 1901 before his death in December of that year.
