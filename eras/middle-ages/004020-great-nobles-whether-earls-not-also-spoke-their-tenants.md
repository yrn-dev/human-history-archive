# The Terminology of Tenancy and Nobility in Medieval England

## The Use of the Term Baron
Following the Conquest, the word "baron" was introduced into England. Its origin is uncertain, but it was used to denote "the man"—specifically one who had performed homage—of a great lord, and most notably of the king. Those who held land "in chief," meaning they held it directly from the king, were referred to as *barones regis*. In theory, these individuals were members of the king's council and were required to perform a stipulated service.

The application of the term extended beyond those holding directly from the crown. Great nobles, regardless of whether they held the rank of earl, also referred to their own tenants as "barons." This distinguished them from lesser magnates, who instead referred to their tenants as "men" (*homines*).

## Palatine Lordships and Local Usage
The use of "baron" to describe tenants was particularly prominent in earldoms of a palatine character. In the palatinate of Chester, the earl's barons constituted a well-recognized body; for example, the Venables family served as "barons of Kinderton" until 1679. Similarly, in the palatinate of Durham, the bishop maintained his own barons, including the Hiltons of Hilton Castle, who used the style "Barons of Hilton" until the family became extinct in 1746.

Other families were accorded the title on somewhat uncertain grounds and independently of peerage dignity, such as the "barons of Stafford," the "barons of Greystock," and the Cornwalls, known as "barons of Burford."

## Urban and Regional Applications
The term "baron" was also applied within city contexts. Documents from London show that the city's inhabitants were sometimes referred to as such; for instance, a charter from King John mentions "our barons of our city of London," and another document refers to "the greater barons of the city." Henry II is recorded by Fantosme as speaking of "mes baruns de Lundres." Furthermore, under Richard II, aldermen were loosely considered equivalent to barons and were assessed for the poll-tax as such.

In Ireland, the title was not uncommon due to the palatine nature of the great lordships, as seen with the barons of the Naas, the barons of Slane, and the barons of Galtrim.

## Under-tenants and the Domesday Survey
The Domesday Survey provided a record of landholding and descent that was of primary importance to genealogists and topographers. By rearranging original returns on a feudal basis, William the Conqueror and his officers could easily identify the extent of a baron's possessions and determine who the "under-tenants" were and how far the baron had enfeoffed them.

While under-tenants were the "men" of their respective lords, William the Conqueror resolved that they must swear allegiance directly to him for military reasons. The Domesday records typically list only the Christian names of these under-tenants, the majority of whom bear foreign names. In Derbyshire, for example, Elfin was an under-tenant of Henry de Ferrers; unlike many Englishmen, Elfin retained considerable property after the Conquest and became the ancestor of the Brailsford family.

## Military Tenants and the Evolution of "Knight"
Under the Anglo-Saxon regime, magnates were surrounded by retainers and dependants, including some called *cnihtas*. Domesday evidence indicates a large class of landholders during the reign of Edward the Confessor who had commended themselves to lords in a manner that may have already been feudal.

Following the Norman Conquest, the term *knight* (likely a continuation of *cniht*) was first applied to the military tenants of barons, bishops, and earls. Although these tenants held land from mesne lords, they owed their services to the king. Initially, the term "knight" defined social status rather than the specific nature of service. In contrast, the Normans used the term *chevaliers* to define the nature of the service, while ignoring social status. Early translations of the honorary *chevalier* used the word "rider"; for example, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle notes that in 1085, William the Conqueror "dubbade his sunu Henric to ridere."
