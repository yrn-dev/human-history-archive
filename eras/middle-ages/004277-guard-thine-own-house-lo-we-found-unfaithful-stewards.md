# Guard Thine own house; for lo we are found unfaithful stewards

The phrase "Guard Thine own house; for lo we are found unfaithful stewards" appears in texts relating to the fall of Jerusalem. It is recorded as a plea from priests who, according to the account, took the keys of the sanctuary and cast them into the height of heaven, returning them to the Lord. This specific event is recounted as a historical fact in rabbinical literature, including the *Pesikta Rabbati*, *Ta'anith*, *Yalqu[t.] Shim'oni* on Is. xxi, and the *Aboth* of Rabbi Nathan.

## Religious and Temple Contexts
In the accounts of the Hebrew people, the concept of the temple as a divine house is central. In one record, a king addresses God, stating that while the heavens and their immensity are a small habitation for the Deity, the temple is even more so. The king beseeches God to keep the temple as His own house and possession, protecting it from destruction by enemies. However, this plea is coupled with the acknowledgment that if the people are found to have sinned, they may be afflicted by God with plagues, such as pestilence or dearth.

## The Role of the Steward in Various Societies
The term "steward" appears across different historical and legal contexts, denoting various levels of responsibility, from domestic management to judicial authority.

### Domestic and Estate Stewardship
In the history of the Israelites, stewards managed the estates of nobility. For example, Ziba served as the steward for Mephibosheth, the grandson of Saul. In this instance, the steward was accused of being unfaithful; Mephibosheth claimed that Ziba had calumniated him with invented lies and had failed to prepare for his flight during a change in the king's affairs, treating him as a slave.

### Japanese Land-Stewards (Jito)
In Japan, the appointment of land-stewards (*jito*) was used to remedy administrative issues. Initially, land-stewards were responsible for collecting taxes from private manors, deducting their own salaries, and maintaining peace and order. These officials, along with high constables, were responsible to Kamakura and existed beyond the jurisdiction of the Imperial Court. 

Under Yoritomo, these retainers did not own the estates but exercised general supervision and tax collection. Following the Shokyu struggle, the Bakufu confiscated approximately three thousand manors from hostile courtiers. These were distributed among the Hojo, the Minamoto, and their partisans. This created a new class of manor-holders known as *shimpo-jito* (newly appointed land-stewards), who were both the owners and the stewards of the land, distinguishing them from the *hompo-jito* (originally appointed).

### English Parliamentary Stewards
In the English legal system, the role of the steward was utilized during the trials of peers. Until 1477, it was common practice to appoint a steward to execute judgment upon a peer during bills of attainder. Under the Stuarts, this practice returned for proceedings founded on indictment.

The functions of the steward varied based on the timing of the trial:
* **During Parliamentary Recess:** In the steward's court, the steward acted as the judge. He convened the prisoner and the peers, determined all questions of law by his sole authority, but did not vote on the final verdict of guilty or not guilty.
* **During Parliamentary Session:** The steward served only as the chairman of the court and voted along with the other lords, as all peers acted as both triers and judges.

The steward of the court was addressed as "his grace" and carried a rod of office, which was broken to dissolve the commission.

## Related Historical Structures
While stewardship focused on management, other historical structures emphasized ownership and opulence. In Rome, during the Theodosian age, wealthy citizens resided in stately mansions—some numbering 1,780—that were described as being equal to cities. These palaces included their own markets, temples, baths, and hippodromes. The wealth of these senators was immense, with some receiving annual incomes of four thousand pounds of gold from estates that extended beyond the Ionian and Aegean Seas.
