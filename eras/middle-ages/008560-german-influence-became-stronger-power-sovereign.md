# German Influence and the Power of the Sovereign

The dynamics of sovereign power and the reach of German influence have varied significantly across different regions and eras, ranging from the consolidation of imperial authority in Central Europe to the decline of German hegemony in the Low Countries.

## German Influence in Bohemia and Central Europe
In Bohemia, internal struggles led to a dual outcome: the influence of Germans grew stronger, while the power of the sovereign declined. This decline occurred because competitors for the crown were forced to rely on the nobility, who in turn secured new privileges. Despite this trend, certain sovereigns expanded their reach. Premysl Ottakar II, one of Bohemia's greatest kings, extended his dominions to include the archduchies of Austria, as well as Carinthia, Istria, and parts of northern Italy, following a decisive victory over King Bela of Hungary at the battle of Kressenbrunn in 1260.

Ottakar, known as "the man of iron" for his military power and "the man of gold" for his wealth, encouraged the immigration of Germans into his lands. This was a political decision intended to create a counterpoise to the overwhelming power held by the Bohemian nobility.

In later centuries, German influence continued to play a role in the region's governance. In 1872, a government with a pronounced German tendency took office in Vienna. This followed a failed attempt in 1871 by Austrian prime minister Count Karl Hohenwart to reconcile Bohemia with the Habsburg empire through a modernized version of the ancient constitution.

## The Decline of German Influence in Lotharingia and Flanders
In contrast to its strength in Bohemia, German influence practically disappeared in Lotharingia. This decline was accelerated by the "Struggle for the Investitures," which destroyed the political and religious power of the German imperial and feudal church. Consequently, the bishops of the Empire ceased to be political servants of the Emperor, and the Germanization of Lotharingia broke down. Local princes, who preferred their own national Carolingian dynasty over foreign German emperors, threw off the feudal yoke and divided the province among themselves. This shift allowed the Duke of Brabant to extend his political influence over the region.

In Flanders, the influence of Germany was similarly eclipsed. Literary and artistic movements in Flanders were French rather than German; for example, the artistic capital of Tournai was a Romance bishopric with French teachers. By the twelfth century, the expansion of Flanders was checked by the strengthening of the French monarchy. Following the battle of Bouvines on July 27, 1214, where Philip August defeated a coalition including Emperor Otto of Brunswick, France established political hegemony in Europe and the subjection of Flanders.

## The Evolution of Sovereign Power in Other Contexts
The nature of sovereign power varied across different empires and periods:

*   **Germany:** In the late 19th century, the German sovereign sought to consolidate Germany and establish it as a "world power." He used his influence to reorganize the Turkish Army along German lines and promoted the Pan-German movement, which viewed the Flemish Belgians, Dutch, Scandinavians, and German Swiss as part of a great German brotherhood. He also used his authority to protect the distinctive German blackletter type against the Roman type used in western Europe.
*   **Japan:** In Japanese history, sovereign power was sometimes diverted toward cultural patronage. The Taiko used a considerable portion of his sovereign power to popularize art, bestowing allowances and distinctions on artisans. This patronage transformed the town of Fushimi into the empire's art capital. Conversely, other periods saw the Imperial house incur disgrace, such as when the influence of Fujiwara no Nakamaro grew daily through his relationship with the Empress.
*   **Belgium:** In the early 17th century, under the rule of Albert and Isabella, sovereign power became even stronger than it had been before the 16th-century crisis. This period of peace and reconstruction saw the sovereign order the publication of old customs and the creation of the *Édit perpétuel* to reform civil and criminal law.
