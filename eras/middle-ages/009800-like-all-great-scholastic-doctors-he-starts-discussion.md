# Scholastic Doctors

The scholastic doctors were prominent theologians and philosophers of the medieval period, primarily active within the universities of Europe. Their work was characterized by a systematic approach to theology and philosophy, often focusing on the reconciliation of faith and reason.

## Institutional Influence and Education
The Dominican order played a significant role in the development of scholasticism by establishing houses in major university towns, including Paris, Bologna, Palencia, and Oxford. The order eventually extended its influence to chairs in Prague, Vienna, Cologne, Padua, and Salamanca. Leading figures such as Thomas Aquinas and Albert the Great were central to this activity. The Dominican school of theology continued to maintain the principles and methods developed by St. Thomas.

Other figures associated with scholasticism include Guillaume Durand (known as *Doctor Resolutissimus*), a French theologian who taught in Paris before becoming a theological adviser to Pope John XXII in Avignon and serving as bishop of Meaux, Le Puy, and Limoux.

## Core Philosophical and Theological Debates
A fundamental starting point for the great scholastic doctors was the discussion of the relations between faith and reason. In this framework, all sciences were viewed as handmaids to theology. While reason could discover some moral truths that served as the groundwork for the Christian system, other truths could only be apprehended through divine illumination.

Scholastic doctors engaged in weighty decisions regarding several "disputed questions," including:
*   **Universals:** Bonaventura, known as the Seraphic Doctor, considered universals to be ideal forms pre-existing in the divine mind, which served as the patterns for shaping things.
*   **Matter:** Bonaventura held that matter is pure potentiality that receives determinateness and individual being from the formative power of God.
*   **The Intellectus Agens:** Bonaventura maintained that the *intellectus agens* does not have a separate existence.
*   **Divine Attributes:** The nature and meaning of divine attributes were subjects of careful discussion.

Some doctors diverged from established realism. Guillaume Durand, for instance, broke with the realism of Thomas Aquinas and anticipated the *terminism* of William of Occam, abandoning the attempt to prove that dogmas could be demonstrated through reason.

## Divergent Perspectives and Criticisms
Not all thinkers adhered to the scholastic system. Roger Bacon withdrew from scholastic routine to pursue experimental research and languages. Bacon criticized the professors of his time, noting that they relied on authority or custom rather than Aristotelian experiment and often used erroneous versions of the Scriptures or the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard. He specifically targeted Alexander of Hales and another anonymous professor in his *Opus Minus* and *Opus Tertium*.

Similarly, Ramon Lull (the *doctor illuminatus*) broke away from the scholastic system. While his incoherent formulae were later disdained by Bacon, his dialectic was admired by Leibniz and Giordano Bruno. Lull's doctrines were formally condemned by a papal bull in 1376 at the request of Nicolas Emeric, though this was later annulled by Martin V in 1417.

Bonaventura also presented a contrast to Thomas Aquinas and Roger Bacon. While Aquinas represented Aristotelian scholasticism in its most perfect form, Bonaventura embraced a mystical and Platonizing mode of speculation influenced by St. Augustine, Bernard of Clairvaux, and the Alexandrian school. He rejected the authority of Aristotle, attributing to his influence the heretical tendencies of the age, and vigorously opposed doctrines such as the eternity of the world.

## Casuistry and Practical Application
The scholastic doctors also applied their reasoning to practical and legalistic questions, a practice known as casuistry. This included grave discussions on the "matter" of baptism, specifically whether a soul could be made regenerate using substances other than water, such as wine, rose-water, or milk.

## Historical Development
The transition toward Scholasticism was aided by earlier figures such as Gregory, the Fourth Doctor of the Latin Church. Gregory served as a link between the Latin Fathers (such as Augustine, Ambrose, and Tertullian) and later medieval Catholicism. By providing detailed expositions on purgatory, the Eucharistic Sacrifice, angelology, demonology, and the efficacy of relics, Gregory helped construct the system that would later be completed in Scholasticism.
