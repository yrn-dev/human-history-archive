# History in the Middle Ages

The Middle Ages represent a transformative period across various global regions, characterized by the emergence of distinct national identities, the shifting of political boundaries, and the evolution of urban centers.

## European Political Developments
In the region that now constitutes the grand-duchy of Baden, the Middle Ages were marked by the rule of various counts, most notably the counts and dukes of Zaehringen. The separate history of Baden is considered to have begun in 1112 when Hermann—the son of Hermann, margrave of Verona, and grandson of Bertold, duke of Carinthia and count of Zaehringen—inherited family estates and adopted the title of margrave of Baden. This specific title was used rather than the more common "count" due to the family's connection to the margraviate of Verona. 

Over the following century, the territories were expanded by two subsequent Hermanns (son and grandson). Around 1200, these lands were divided into the lines of Baden-Baden and Baden-Hochberg. The Baden-Hochberg line further split approximately a century later into the branches of Baden-Hochberg and Baden-Sausenberg. By 1391, Margrave Bernard I, a renowned soldier, succeeded in uniting several divisions of the Baden-Baden possessions. He further expanded the territory by acquiring other districts, including Baden-Hochberg, after its ruling family died out in 1418.

In the Balkans, a vilayet that now exists was part of the Servian Empire during the Middle Ages; its northern districts continue to be known to the Serbs as Old Servia (*Stara Srbiya*). A significant event in this region's history occurred in 1389 at the plain of Kossovo (Kossovopolje, or "Field of Blackbirds"), a long valley west of Prishtina watered by the Sibnitza. The resulting battle of Kossovo led to the destruction of Servian power by the Turks.

## Belgian National Identity
The Middle Ages served as the era in which the common civilization and characteristics of the Belgian nation were born. This period saw a strong factor of national union between Flanders and Lotharingia, driven by a shared culture, religion, artistic and economic aims, and political institutions. The Belgian people developed a distinctive unity characterized by a deeply religious spirit and a common desire for freedom and independence. This included a jealous regard for popular rights as a guarantee of that independence. Throughout their history, the Belgians cast off the yoke of princes—such as William I of Holland, Joseph II, and Philip II—who attempted to impose their own religious beliefs or attack the people's privileges and liberties.

## Middle Eastern and Asian Urban Centers
In the region of Kermán, the city known today as Kermán was called Bardshír during the Middle Ages. Bardshír served as the capital and the primary location for striking coins. There is some historical confusion regarding the names of the province and its towns; for instance, a coin from A.H. 462 belonging to Qara Arslán Beg, King of Kermán, has been read by Mr. Stanley Lane Poole as "Yazdashír" instead of "Bardshír," though some suggest this may be a clerical error as the words are written alike without diacritical points. Additionally, the town of Sírján (or Shírján) was once more important than Bardshír and is spoken of as the capital of Sardsír, Bardshír, and Kermán. During this era, the Nestorian bishop of the Kermán province resided at Hormúz, under the authority of the Metropolitan of Fars.

In other districts, such as those in British India, little is known of the history prior to the Mahommedan invasion of A.D. 1033. While Masaud was defeated and slain by the nobles of Bahraich in 1033, Mahommedan authority was not fully established over the country until the middle of the 13th century.

## Cultural and Intellectual Transitions
The transition from the Middle Ages to the Renaissance marked a profound change in the European mind, affecting art, thought, and temper. In England, the "break" with the middle ages is considered more significant than later shifts, such as the one occurring around 1660. Despite this break, certain medieval filaments persisted into the Renaissance. These included the characteristic medieval form of allegory, 14th-century metres, and specific literary topics such as the *Falls of Princes* and the tales of Troilus, Arthur, and Geoffrey of Monmouth. Examples of these survivals or transformations can be found in the works of Spenser and Shakespeare.
