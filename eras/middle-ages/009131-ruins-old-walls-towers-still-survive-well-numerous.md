# Ruins of Walls and Towers in Historical Sites

Across various regions of the world, the remnants of ancient walls and towers serve as physical records of former urban centers, military strongholds, and religious institutions. These ruins vary from the remnants of early capitals and Roman colonies to medieval fortifications and ecclesiastical structures.

## Ancient and Medieval Urban Ruins

In Russia, the ruined town of Bolgari, located in the government of Kazan approximately 4 miles from the left bank of the Volga, is generally considered the capital of the Bulgarians from the 5th to the 15th century. The site retains ruins of old towers and walls. Additionally, the area contains numerous burial-mounds, or *kurgans*, featuring inscriptions in Turkic, Armenian (dated 557, 984, and 986), and Arabic (1222-1341). These tombs contained utensils, implements, weapons, and copper and silver coins with Arabic and Kufic inscriptions. The city was destroyed by the Mongols in 1238 and later by Tamerlane in the early 14th century, eventually serving as the capital for the Khans of the Golden Horde before becoming part of the principality of Kazan and Russia.

In Palestine, Caesarea Palaestina, built by Herod between 25 and 13 B.C., contains the ruins of a boundary wall and a castle. The medieval town's walls occupy only one-tenth of the area of the original Roman city. Other surviving structures include a theatre, a temple dedicated to Caesar, a hippodrome, two aqueducts, and a massive mole 200 feet wide.

The ruins of Dara are characterized by walls and towers constructed of large hewn stone, which can be traced across the valley and over low rocky hills. The circumference of these fortifications is estimated to be nearly two and a half miles. Within these walls, remains include a palace or church measuring 100 paces by 60 paces, with the gate still standing and a significant portion of the wall remaining.

## European Fortifications and City Walls

In the Netherlands, the seaports of Enkhuizen and Hoorn retain fragments of their former defenses. Enkhuizen has ruined gateways from its old city walls, including the Dromedary tower-gateway (1540), which contains several rooms and formerly served as a prison. In Hoorn, while walks and gardens have replaced the old city walls, a few gateways and towers remain. These include the East gate (no later than 1578) and a Gothic bastion tower built in 1532.

In England, the town of Kenilworth is noted for the ruins of its old castle, whose walls originally enclosed 7 acres. Extant structures include the gatehouse, the great hall built by John of Gaunt, and Caesar's tower, which features walls 16 feet thick.

Regarding the evolution of fortifications, there is a transitional type of coast castle erected by Henry VIII around 1540, such as those at Sandown, Deal, and Walmer. In other contexts, medieval walls and towers eventually served as a pure *enceinte de surete* by the 17th century. Some old castles have been merged into town fortifications, while others have become peaceful mansions or ruins.

## Ecclesiastical and Other Ruins

In Scotland, the ruined abbey at Kelso, founded in 1128 by David I, features a great central tower and remains of the south transept walls. Other remnants include the north and west sides of the north transept, a fragment of the richly moulded doorway on the west front, and a remnant of the chancel. The abbey was reduced to ruins in 1545 by the earl of Hertford.

In Turkey, the city of Konia saw its walls and palace gradually dismantled for building materials after the decay of the Seljuk state. By 1882, only a small part of the walls remained, stripped of outer stones. By 1906, the walls had been wholly removed, though an "unsightly fragment" of a palace-tower persisted.
