# Public Penance and Reconciliation in the Early Church

## Practices of Public Penance
In the early Church, the process of reconciliation for a sinner involved the custom of public penance. To be reconciled, the sinner was required to appear before the congregation covered in ashes and clad in sackcloth. This practice was documented by Tertullian in *De Pudicitia*. Over time, this ceremony evolved; the phrase *dies cinerum* appears in the earliest extant copies of the Gregorian Sacramentary, suggesting the custom was established by the 8th century. By 996 or 997, the Anglo-Saxon homilist Aelfric noted that the practice was in common use, though it was not authoritatively prescribed until a decree from the synod of Beneventum in 1091.

## Confession and Satisfaction
In the earliest stages of church life, Christians who fell into sin were required to make a public confession before the congregation. During this process, they had to declare their sorrow and vow to perform specific acts. These acts served as evidence of the sincerity of their repentance. 

As the custom shifted from public confession to private confession before the clergy, the responsibility for imposing these "satisfactions" fell to the confessor. To ensure uniformity in treating repentant sinners, books were developed that listed various sins alongside their corresponding suitable satisfactions. For heinous sins, the imposed satisfactions were severe and could last for many years.

## Evolution of Indulgences
By approximately the 7th century, a custom emerged to relax or commute these imposed satisfactions. For example, a penance requiring several years of fasting could be commuted into a money-fine, the giving of alms, or the recitation of a specific number of prayers. In the case of money-fines, the practice often followed the analogy of the Wergeld found in German tribal codes.

This evolution led to the origin of Indulgences. The practice generally functioned such that an individual who visited a designated church on a specific day and contributed to its funds would have their penance shortened by one-half, one-third, or one-seventh. These Indulgences were viewed as mitigations of the satisfactions imposed by the church, which served as tests of fitness for pardon, outward signs of inward sorrow, and necessary precedents for absolution.

## Restoration and Exclusion
The church viewed baptism as the act that justified the believer and abolished past sins. However, sinning after baptism excluded the individual from the sacraments and divine grace, returning them to the status of a catechumen. From the 2nd century onward, the church debated how, or if, such persons could be restored.

A rite known as *exhomologesis* was developed for this purpose. This rite functioned as a repetition of baptism, though the water of the font was omitted. Before re-entering the church, the lapsed person had to undergo a term of repentance characterized by austerities more strict than those of a modern Trappist monk. In the early church, this restoration could only be granted once. A second lapse resulted in perpetual exclusion from the sacraments and the means of salvation. This severity led some individuals, including Constantine, to remain catechumens throughout their lives and receive baptism only on their deathbeds.

## Canonical Jurisprudence and Ecclesiastical Justice
The discipline of penance eventually became a system of canonical jurisprudence. This system defined the rules of evidence, degrees of guilt, the measure of punishment, and the duties of public or private confession. The bishop acted as the perpetual censor of the morals of his people.

While bishops often avoided arraigning emperors due to loyalty or fear, they frequently used ecclesiastical justice against subordinate tyrants. For example, St. Athanasius excommunicated a minister of Egypt, and Bishop Synesius of Ptolemais inflicted the "last sentence of ecclesiastical justice" upon the president Andronicus and his associates. Such impenitent sinners were deprived of the hope of Paradise, the participation in the sacraments, and the privileges of being Christians.

## Later Developments and Reforms
By the beginning of the 11th century, the church held significant power over the consciences of men, though this moral prestige eventually declined. One contributing factor was the accumulation of wealth; penitent sinners were often exhorted to leave lands to the church, resulting in the church owning as much as a fourth of the land in some European countries.

Following the Reformation, the Anglican Church maintained a special service known as the Commination service, which involves the solemn reading of "the general sentences of God's cursing against sinners" drawn from other places of Scripture and the 27th chapter of Deuteronomy. While Henry VIII (1538) and Edward VI (1550) enjoined the ceremonial of ashes, it had fallen into complete disuse by the start of the 17th century.
