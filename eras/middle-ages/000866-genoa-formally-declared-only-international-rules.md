# Genoa

## Political Structure and Governance
Genoa was established as a town state, existing as an independent or quasi-independent aristocratic republic. Its governance featured the institution of the doge, which dates from 1339. Initially, the doge was elected by popular suffrage without restriction and held the office for life. This system was altered in 1528 through reforms enacted by Andrea Doria, which reduced the term of office to two years. Following these reforms, plebeians were declared ineligible for the position. The appointment of the doge was instead entrusted to the members of the little and great councils, utilizing a complex machinery of appointment. The office of the doge in Genoa eventually came to an end during the Napoleonic Wars.

## Maritime Conflict and Territorial Expansion
Genoa was involved in a long-standing dispute with the republic of Pisa for the supremacy of the seas. This conflict extended to the island of Corsica. While the Pisans had taken solemn possession of the island and utilized it as a storehouse for their fleet, the Genoese intrigued at Rome to reverse the papal gift of the island to their rivals. In 1138, Pope Innocent II attempted a compromise by dividing the ecclesiastical jurisdiction of Corsica between the archbishops of Genoa and Pisa. This arrangement provided the Genoese with significant influence on the island. In 1195, the Genoese gained a physical footing in Corsica by capturing Bonifacio, which had served as a nest for pirates preying on the commerce of both republics.

## Relations with Venice and Naval Warfare
Genoa and Venice were competing maritime powers. In October 1298, 6,000 or 7,000 Venetians were taken as prisoners and thrown into the prisons of Genoa. Among these prisoners was Marco Polo, who was carried to Genoa following the action at Curzola. Polo's eventual release was part of a treaty ratified by the Doge of Venice on July 1, with formalities continuing through July 18. The terms of this treaty were reciprocal and equal, and it included the mutual release of prisoners. Despite the success of Genoa in this conflict, it was not followed by a systematic attack on Venetian commerce.

## Imprisonment and Literary Contributions
The prisons of Genoa held various captives, including Pisan prisoners from Meloria. It is conjectured that Rusticiano of Pisa was among the remnant of Pisan prisoners in the Genoese gaol. According to some accounts, a Genoese gentleman showed kindness to Marco Polo during his imprisonment and assisted him in reducing his travels to writing; however, some sources suggest this figure is a distorted image of Rusticiano. While patriotic Genoese writers have attempted to identify this figure as a countryman of their own, other records identify him as "Messire Rustacians de Pise."
