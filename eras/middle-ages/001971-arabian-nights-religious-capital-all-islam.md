# Arabian Nights and the Splendour of Bagdad

The *Arabian Nights* is a collection of narratives that reflects the immense splendour and imagination associated with the historical peak of the Arab world. These stories serve as a literary window into the wealth and cultural supremacy of the era, particularly regarding the city of Bagdad.

## Bagdad: The Religious and Political Capital
Founded in A.D. 762 by the caliph Mansur of the Abbasid dynasty, Bagdad was established on the west side of the Tigris, just north of the Isa canal. The original "round city" was designed in concentric circles with a diameter of one mile, featuring four gates oriented toward the four points of the compass. At its center stood the palace of the caliph and the mosque.

The city expanded rapidly, leading to the immediate development of the suburb of Rusafa on the eastern bank. Following the siege and capture of the round city by Mamun in 814, Rusafa became the capital's most important sector. Bagdad reached its zenith of prosperity from its foundation until the death of Mamun (the successor of Harun) in 833. During this peak, the walled city spanned five miles across both sides of the river and is said to have housed a population of 2,000,000 people.

In terms of commerce and wealth, Bagdad far surpassed Cordova, though it shared global supremacy with that city in the fields of science, art, and literature. At this time, Bagdad served as the political capital for the greater part of Islam and the religious capital for all of Islam.

## Religious and Cultural Diversity
During the period of the eastern caliphate, the city was a center of diverse religious significance. The Jews were treated with relative favour, and the seat of the *resh galutha* (or *exilarch*) was moved from Pumbedita in Babylonia to Bagdad. This transition established Bagdad as the capital of oriental Judaism, a role in which the Jewish population continued to play a significant part.

## Decline of the Abbasid Capital
The downfall of Bagdad began with the rise of the Turkish body-guard under Mo'tassim, the successor of Mamun. Mo'tassim founded the city of Samarra, and for fifty-eight years, the court and caliph deserted Bagdad. Although Mosta'in attempted to return to Bagdad in A.D. 865 to escape the tyranny of the Turkish guard, the city was besieged and taken.

From that point until the final collapse of the dynasty, the Abbasid caliphs remained the religious heads of Islam and resided in Bagdad, but they functioned as mere puppets. The actual power was held successively by the Seljuks, the Buyids, and the Turkish guard. Despite this political decline, Bagdad remained one of the world's richest and most splendid cities for much of this period, a fact evidenced by the number of great shrines dating from that era.

A more rapid decline occurred in the final century of the caliphates. By the close of the 12th century, travellers described the western city as being almost in ruins, with large uninhabited spaces in the eastern half. The city's status as the religious center of Islam officially ended in 1258 when it was captured by Hulagu (the grandson of Jenghiz Khan) and the Abbasid caliphate was extinguished. Despite this, the city retained a lingering glamour, continuing to be referred to as the "glorious city" in Turkish official documents.
