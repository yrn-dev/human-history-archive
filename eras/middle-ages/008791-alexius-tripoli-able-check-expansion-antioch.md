# The Principality of Antioch and the County of Tripoli

The history of the Principality of Antioch and the County of Tripoli is closely linked to the lineage of the Bohemunds, beginning with the First Crusade and extending through several centuries of political instability, dynastic disputes, and external conflicts.

## Bohemund I and the Foundation of Antioch
Bohemund I (c. A.D. 1058–1111), born Marc and the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, was the prince of Otranto and later the prince of Antioch. His early career was marked by hostility toward the East Roman Emperor Alexius Comnenus; between 1080 and 1085, Bohemund served under his father in an attack on the East Roman empire, penetrating as far as Larissa in Thessaly before being repulsed by Alexius.

Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a conflict arose between Bohemund and his younger brother, Duke Roger, over the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This war was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions. In 1096, Bohemund joined the First Crusade, potentially seeing it as an opportunity to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* and carve out an eastern principality. Upon arriving in Constantinople in April 1097, he performed homage to Emperor Alexius. While Bohemund may have negotiated for a principality at Antioch, the archive indicates he received little encouragement. Nevertheless, Bohemund became the real leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch, guiding the host across Asia Minor.

## The Succession of Bohemund III and IV
By the mid-12th century, the principality was held by Bohemund III, who succeeded his mother, Constance, in 1163. His reign was characterized by conflict and captivity; in August 1164, while serving as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem, he was defeated and captured by Nureddin at Harenc. He was later ransomed by Emperor Manuel and returned to Antioch with a Greek patriarch.

The later years of Bohemund III's life were dominated by a succession dispute between his younger son, Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV), and his grandson, Raymund Rhupen. This conflict was championed by Leo of Armenia, who had previously captured Bohemund III at Gastin in 1194 in an attempt to force the cession of Antioch. Bohemund IV, who had become count of Tripoli in 1187, vigorously pursued his claim and evicted his father from Antioch around 1199, though he was temporarily ousted by Leo. Bohemund III died in possession of the principality in 1201, and Bohemund IV subsequently reigned over both Antioch and Tripoli.

## Governance and Conflict under Bohemund IV and V
Bohemund IV's reign was marked by tension with the Hospitallers and support from the Templars and the Greek inhabitants of Antioch. In 1207, he granted the Greeks their own patriarch. His reign saw further instability when Leo of Armenia captured Antioch in 1216 and established Raymund Rhupen as prince, though Bohemund IV regained control before Leo's death in 1220. Bohemund IV was excommunicated in 1230 due to his enmity with the Hospitallers, though he was released from this status by Gregory IX before his death in 1233.

He was succeeded by Bohemund V, who was both prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. Bohemund V resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving the governance of Antioch to a commune and its bailiff. He maintained an alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia. This tension was not resolved until 1251 through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund V also connected the history of Antioch to Cyprus through his 1225 marriage to Alice, the widow of Hugh I.

## The Decline of the Bohemund Dynasty
Bohemund VI (born 1237) succeeded his father in 1251. In 1252, he established himself in Antioch and left Tripoli to its own administration. He allied with the Mongols to oppose the Egyptian sultan, but in 1268, he lost Antioch to Bibars. By the time of his death in 1275, he held only the title of count of Tripoli.

The county of Tripoli passed to his son, Bohemund VII. His reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars established in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue, and since Tripoli was captured within two years of his passing, the county of Tripoli is considered extinct with him.
