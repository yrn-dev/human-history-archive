# Charles Martel

Charles Martel was a prominent Frankish leader who served as the Mayor of the Palace. He exercised practical rule over the Frankish kingdom, which at the time was nominally led by a degenerate descendant of Clovis. By the early 8th century, Martel had established himself as the practical overlord of Europe north of the Alps, with a domain extending from the Pyrenees to Hungary. In this capacity, he ruled over a diverse population of subordinate lords who spoke High and Low German, as well as French-Latin.

## Military Conflicts and the Battle of Poitiers

Charles Martel is most noted for his military engagements against Moslem forces. In 711, a Moslem army had invaded Spain from Africa, and by 715, the domains of the Caliph Walid I stretched from China to the Pyrenees. When Moslem forces crossed the Pyrenees in 720, they encountered the Frankish kingdom under Martel's rule. This conflict culminated in 732 at the Battle of Poitiers, where Charles Martel decisively defeated the Moslems.

Beyond his conflicts with the Moslems, Martel was involved in regional warfare within Northern Europe. During the time that the missionary Saint Boniface first attempted to evangelize Frisia in 716, Charles Martel was at war with Radbod, the king of the Frisians.

## Relations with the Papacy and Rome

The relationship between Charles Martel and the city of Rome was characterized by a mutual need for protection and legitimacy. Because the reign of the Greek emperors was suspended, the senate and people of Rome invested Charles Martel and his descendants with the honors of "patrician of Rome." As a symbol of this sovereignty and the duty to defend the church and city, Roman ambassadors presented these patricians with a holy banner and the keys to the shrine of St. Peter.

However, the physical distance between the Franks and Rome, combined with the presence of the Lombard kingdom, meant that the patriciate often represented an alliance or a title rather than immediate control. For example, Pope Gregory III later invoked the aid of Charles Martel to support his position against the Lombards, though this request was in vain.

## Influence on the Church and Missions

Charles Martel provided essential political protection for the spread of Christianity in heathen Germany. Saint Boniface, the apostle of Germany, confessed that his great career was made possible by Charles's protection. This support allowed Boniface to conduct a systematic crusade that involved founding monasteries and churches, overturning idols, and baptizing populations. 

In 722, Pope Gregory II consecrated Boniface as a bishop and provided him with letters of recommendation to Charles Martel. These instructions charged Boniface not only with the conversion of heathens but also with the suppression of heresy.

## Succession and Legacy

Charles Martel divided his power between two sons. One son resigned his position to enter a monastery, leaving his brother, Pepin, as the sole ruler. Pepin eventually extinguished the last descendants of Clovis to take the kingly title for himself. To justify this transition, Pepin consulted the Pope on whether the true king should be the man who held the power or the man who wore the crown; the Pope decided in favor of the Mayor of the Palace. 

Pepin was subsequently anointed and crowned king in 751 at a gathering of Frankish nobles in the Merovingian capital of Soissons. This lineage continued with Pepin's son, Charlemagne, who further consolidated the Franco-Germany realm and eventually revived the title of Latin Emperor, conquering North Italy and becoming master of Rome.
