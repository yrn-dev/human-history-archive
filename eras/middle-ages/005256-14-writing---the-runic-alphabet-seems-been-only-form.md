# Writing

## Runic Writing and the Anglo-Saxons
The Runic alphabet served as the sole form of writing known to the Anglo-Saxons prior to the invasion of Britain and until the adoption of Christianity. In its earliest form, as seen in Scandinavian countries and Schleswig, the alphabet consisted of twenty-four letters; all of these are present in English abecedaria. In practical application, two letters became obsolete, while others were added over time, some of which were specific to certain regions of England and others found on the continent.

Originally, Runes were used for writing on wooden boards, though no such boards have survived. The surviving inscriptions are found on metal objects, such as brooches and swords, and on memorial stones common in northern England. Notable examples include the cross at Ruthwell in Dumfriesshire and the column at Bewcastle in Cumberland, the latter believed to commemorate Alhfrith, son of Oswio, who died around 670. Runic legends continued to appear on coins until at least the middle of the 8th century, and some memorial stones likely date to the 9th century.

## Transition to the Roman Alphabet
The introduction of Christianity brought the Roman alphabet to the Anglo-Saxons. This new system was quickly applied to the native language, as seen in the laws of Æthelberht. However, the Runic alphabet did not vanish immediately. The characters used in early manuscripts are believed to be of Celtic origin, likely introduced by Welsh influences or Irish missionaries who evangelized northern England.

The Roman alphabet used in Britain was a variety shared by the Irish and Welsh, featuring several letters with forms or values different from those on the continent. Specifically, the letters *q* and *z* were not used; *q* was represented by *cw*, and *k* served as a rare alternative to *c*. The letter *u* or *v* functioned only as a vowel, while the consonantal power of *v* was represented by *f*. Additionally, the letters *c* and *g* originally possessed only hard or guttural powers, similar to neighboring Celtic languages.

To accommodate sounds for which the Roman alphabet had no character—specifically the consonantal values of *th* and *w*—the Anglo-Saxons initially used *th*, *dh* (a contraction for [g][g] or [g]h), and *v* or *u*. Eventually, before the end of the 7th century, the alphabet was enlarged by reviving two Runic letters, known as *thorn* and *wen*, to represent these sounds.

## Theories on Runic Origins
Scholars have proposed various theories regarding the antiquity and origin of Runic characters. One theory, held by Celsius, suggests they are simply Roman letters with curves replaced by straight lines to facilitate engraving. Another theory by Schlozer posits that the sixteen letters are a post-Christian corruption of the Roman alphabet introduced by the Alemanni. Frederick Schlegel suggested that the characters were left on the Northern and Mediterranean coasts by Phoenicians and used by priestly castes for magic. A third theory suggests the Runes are the original characters of the Indo-Teutonic tribes, brought from the East and preserved across different races of that stock.

The oldest Runic inscriptions are thought to date to the 3rd century. The earliest writer to mention them is Venantius Fortunatus, who lived toward the end of the 6th century. Furthermore, the Anglo-Saxon words for "book" (*boc*, *bece*, or *beoce*) are linked to the word for "beech-tree," likely because ancient Runic tablets were made from thin boards of beech-wood.

## Other Global Writing Systems
### East Asian Systems
Chinese writing is a complex system of sign-writing and ideograms. Because the spoken language varies widely by province, a "standard Chinese" bookish idiom is used by educated people. The complexity of this system created a special ruling class of mandarins. While there have been attempts to introduce alphabetical systems—some influenced by Sanskrit during the early days of Buddhism—the traditional system remains demanding.

Japanese writing is derived from Chinese, utilizing both Chinese ideograms and a syllabary. Korean developed a true alphabet from these same Chinese origins.

### Indian and Mediterranean Systems
In northern India, the Nagari character served as the basis for several alphabets. The Bengali alphabet, dating from the 11th century A.D., is a cursive script. The Assamese alphabet is identical to Bengali but includes an additional character for the sound of *w*. In Orissa, writing was traditionally scratched with an iron stylus onto talipot palm-leaves; to avoid splitting the leaf, the alphabet adopted a characteristic curved appearance.

In Greece, writing was well established by the early 7th century B.C. The oldest known stage of the Greek alphabet is found in inscriptions from Crete, Melos, and Thera, dated to the 40th Olympiad (620 B.C.).
