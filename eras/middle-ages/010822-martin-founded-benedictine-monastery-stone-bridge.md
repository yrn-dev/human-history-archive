# The Benedictines

The Benedictines, also known as the Black Monks, are a monastic order of monks who live according to the Rule of St Benedict of Nursia. The order originated in Subiaco in the Abruzzi, where St Benedict established twelve monasteries. He later migrated to Monte Cassino, establishing a monastery that served as the center for the spread of his institute and Rule. Before his death, St Benedict also founded a monastery at Terracina.

## Expansion and Influence in Europe
The knowledge of the Benedictine Rule began to spread beyond St Benedict's own foundations through the third abbot of Monte Cassino. In 596, St Augustine and his companions traveled from a monastery on the Caelian Hill—which was fundamentally based on the Benedictine Rule—to England. Consequently, England became the first country outside of Italy where Benedictine life was firmly planted. During the 7th century, the order was introduced in Gaul, and by the 8th century, it reached Germanic lands via England.

By the 10th or 11th century, Benedictine houses were numerous throughout Latin Christendom, with the exception of Ireland. In these regions, the Benedictines acted as centers of civilizing influence, providing object-lessons in farming, agriculture, organized work, trades, arts, and well-ordered living.

## Education and Preservation of Learning
Benedictine monasteries played a critical role in the cultivation of letters and education. In the early middle ages, these monasteries were the only places in western Europe providing the security and rest necessary for the cultivation of letters. Monks transcribed and preserved ecclesiastical and classical writings of Latin antiquity. The Venerable Bede is cited as a typical representative of this scholarly tradition.

Regarding education, boys were taught in Benedictine houses from the beginning, though they were initially destined for monastic life. During the Carolingian revival, schools were commonly attached to monasteries to educate boys intended to work and live in the world. Education has since remained one of the principal recognized works of the Benedictines.

## Governance and Organizational Structure
For the first four or five centuries, Benedictine monasteries operated as independent, autonomous families with no organic bond between them, subject only to the bishop of the diocese. While the influence of Cluny led many houses to adopt its customs and practices, the majority of Black Monk abbeys remained in "primeval isolation" until the end of the 12th century.

This structure changed in 1215 at the fourth Lateran council, which decreed that Benedictine houses in each ecclesiastical province be federated for discipline and mutual help. This required abbots to meet every third year in a provincial chapter or synod to pass binding laws and appoint visitors to report on the spiritual and temporal condition of the monasteries.

The modern Benedictine polity preserves the principle of autonomy. Each congregation is self-governing and independent, with no central authority or superior-general. Each congregation has a president with limited powers. While Leo XIII established an international Benedictine College in Rome and gave its abbot the title of "Abbot Primate," this figure is merely *primus inter pares* (first among equals) and holds no superiority over other congregations or abbots.

## Regional History and Notable Foundations
### England
Benedictine foundations were particularly numerous in Kent due to its fertility and beauty. Early foundations included the abbey of St Peter and St Paul (St Augustine's) and the priory of Christ's Church at Canterbury. Other early Benedictine sites included the priory of St Martin at Dover (696) and nunneries at Folkestone, Lyminge, Reculver, and Minster-in-Thanet. By the time of the Dissolution, there were nearly 300 Black Benedictine houses in England. The English line was perpetuated after the Reformation through Sigebert Buckley, who in 1607 affiliated two English priests to the English congregation.

### Germany and Other Regions
In Aschaffenburg, originally a Roman settlement, Bonifacius founded a Benedictine monastery and erected a chapel to St Martin. In other parts of Europe, such as Belgium, the Benedictine rule suffered during the 9th-century Norman invasions, leading to a decline in discipline and the dependence of monasteries on powerful laymen.

## Challenges and Revivals
The Benedictines faced significant upheaval during the Reformation and religious wars in northern Europe. Under the rule of Joseph II of Austria and during the French Revolution, nearly every Benedictine monastery in Europe was suppressed; by the early 19th century, reportedly only thirty survived.

A series of revivals occurred in the latter half of the 19th century, notably in Bavaria under Louis I. New creations since 1830 include the French congregation (inaugurated by Dom Gueranger in 1833), the German congregation of Beuron (1863), and two congregations in the United States. In Italy, the original monasteries of Subiaco and Monte Cassino remain relatively flourishing.
