# The Hussite Wars

The Hussite wars were a series of religious conflicts in Bohemia that began with an insurrection in 1419. These wars marked the breaking-up of Christendom and were preceded by a period of intense theological strife following the death of John Huss and the subsequent burning of his colleague, Jerome of Prague.

## The Crusades and Early Conflict
In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull calling for a crusade to destroy the Hussites, Wycliffites, and all other heretics in Bohemia. This invitation attracted various soldiers of fortune and "drifting blackguardism" from across Europe. Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites operated on extreme democratic lines and were fueled by national enthusiasm. Despite several attempts by crusaders to take Prague, they failed and were forced to retreat. A second crusade in 1421, as well as two subsequent crusades, also ended in failure.

The town of Kuttenberg played a significant role during this period. A center of silver mining, it was one of the most important cities in Bohemia alongside Prague. However, unlike the Czech and utraquist Prague, Kuttenberg's mining population was primarily German and fanatically Catholic. In 1410, Wenceslaus IV had signed the decree of Kuttenberg there, granting the Bohemian nation three votes in Prague University faculty elections against one for the other three nations. In the autumn of 1410, the town saw horrible atrocities when Catholic miners, acting in reprisal for Hussite outrages in Prague, killed approximately 1,600 people—including clergy and magistrates from the town of Kaurim—by beheading, burning, or throwing them into disused mine shafts. In 1420, Emperor Sigismund used Kuttenberg as a base for an unsuccessful attack on the Taborites. Zizka eventually took the city, but imperial troops burned it in 1422 to prevent it from returning to the Taborites.

## Internal Dissensions and the Council of Basel
Following the failure of the first four crusades, the Hussites suffered from internal dissensions. A fifth crusade crossed the frontier in 1431 under Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg. Around this time, it became evident that conquering Bohemia by force was impossible, leading to the decision to convene a council at Basel to examine Hussite demands. While negotiations began in 1431—with the Hussites represented by an embassy led by Prokop the Great—the Germans, influenced by Sigismund, attempted one last armed subjugation. The Bohemians defeated the Germans at Domazlice on August 1, 1431.

The internal split among the Hussites was largely driven by the extreme section's drift toward a primitive communism, which alarmed influential and wealthy Czech noblemen. This divide manifested as a political conflict between the "army of the nobles" (the aristocratic Hussites) and the "army of the towns" (the Taborites). These forces clashed at Lipan, near Kolin, on May 30, 1434. The Taborites were defeated, and their leaders, including the two Prokops, perished.

## Reconciliation and the Compacts
The victory of the moderate party enabled a reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund. The Bohemians recognized Sigismund as their sovereign and reached an agreement in 1436 with the Council of Basle. This agreement, formulated as the "Compacts," allowed the Bohemian church to maintain certain distinctions from general Catholic practice until the sixteenth-century German Reformation. These concessions included the right to preach the gospel freely, the right of communion in both kinds, and limitations on the clergy's ability to acquire worldly goods.

## Literature and Peter Chelcicky
The Hussite movement was largely democratic, leading writers to use the national language rather than Latin. Much of this literature was later destroyed by Jesuits after the battle of the White Hill in 1620, particularly the writings of the Taborites. Preserved works include the *Postilla* by Archbishop John of Rokycan and texts such as *Lives of the Priests of Tabor* and *Of the Great Torment of the Holy Church*, both of which were hostile to the Taborite community.

Peter Chelcicky (born in the late 14th century, died 1460) was an independent religious writer of this era. Described as the "Tolstoy of the 15th," Chelcicky was a strenuous enemy of the Church of Rome but did not join any Hussite party. His philosophy was defined by a horror of bloodshed and a determination to accept all decrees of worldly authorities, even those that were unjust. His masterpiece is the *Sit viry* (the net of faith); he also wrote a *Postilla* and polemical letters to Bishop Nicolas of Pelhrimov and Archbishop John of Rokycan.
