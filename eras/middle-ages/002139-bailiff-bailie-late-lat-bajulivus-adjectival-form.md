# BAILIFF and BAILIE

## Etymology and General Definition
The terms bailiff and bailie are derived from the Late Latin *bajulivus*, which is the adjectival form of *bajulus*, meaning a custodian or governor. A bailiff is defined as a legal officer who is committed with a degree of jurisdiction, care, or authority. Because there are various kinds of bailiffs, their specific duties and offices vary greatly.

## The High-Bailiff and Court Bailiffs
Under the County Courts Act 1888, the legal framework for certain bailiffs is established as follows:

**Appointment and Authority**
The act provides for the appointment of one or more high-bailiffs. These officials are appointed by the judge and may be removed by the lord-chancellor. The high-bailiff has the power to appoint and dismiss, at his own pleasure, a sufficient number of fit and able persons to serve as bailiffs to assist him.

**Duties and Responsibilities**
The primary duty of the high-bailiff is the execution of all writs, precepts, warrants, and summonses issued by the court. The high-bailiff bears responsibility for all defaults and acts committed by himself and the bailiffs he appoints, a responsibility analogous to that of a county sheriff. According to section 49 of the act, bailiffs are answerable for any neglect, omission, or connivance regarding the levy of such executions.

**Legal Protections and Limitations**
Section 54 of the act stipulates that no action may be brought against a bailiff acting under a court order without six days' notice. Additionally, when a bailiff is given a warrant to provide possession of a tenement, he is justified in entering the named premises to give possession, provided the entry occurs between 4 P.M. and 9 A.M. Furthermore, the Law of Distress Amendment Act 1888 mandates that no individual may act as a bailiff to levy distress for rent unless they have been authorized to do so by a county-court judge.

## The Bailie in Scotland
In Scotland, the term bailiff takes the form of "bailie." In this context, a bailie is a magistrate or superior officer of a municipal corporation.

**Powers and Functions**
Bailies are invested with administrative and judicial powers within the burgh for which they are appointed. Their duties include:
* Serving as magistrates in police courts, where they are typically assisted by a paid legal adviser known as an "assessor."
* Acting as a licensing court in larger burghs.

**Appointment and Tenure**
A bailie is always appointed from within the council. While a bailie is considered a person of superior dignity in the council—making the role analogous to an English alderman—there is a key distinction: unlike an alderman, a bailie continues to represent the ward for which he was elected after his selection. The term of office for a bailie is the same as that of an ordinary councillor, which is no more than three years.

## Specialized Historical Roles
The archive notes two specific historical applications of the term bailie:

* **Bailie to give sasine:** This was the individual who appeared on behalf of the superior during the ceremony of giving sasine. This specific ceremony was abolished in 1845.
* **Bailie of Holyrood (or Bailie of the Abbey):** This official held jurisdiction over all civil debts that were contracted within the precincts of the sanctuary.
