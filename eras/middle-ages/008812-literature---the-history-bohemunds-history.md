# The Bohemunds

The history of the Bohemunds is inextricably linked to the history of the principality of Antioch and, following the reign of Bohemund IV, the county of Tripoli. The dynasty's trajectory began with the First Crusade and concluded with the extinction of the county of Tripoli in the late 13th century.

## Bohemund I and the Foundation of Antioch
Bohemund I (c. A.D. 1058–1111), originally named Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, the *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*, and his first wife. Between 1080 and 1085, he served his father during an attack on the East Roman empire, commanding the Normans from 1082 to 1084. During this time, he penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly before being repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early conflict significantly influenced Bohemund's future career and the course of the First Crusade.

Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a war broke out between Bohemund and his younger half-brother, Roger, who had succeeded to the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. The conflict was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions. In 1096, while attacking Amalfi, Bohemund was inspired by the passing crusaders. Seeking to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* and carve out an eastern principality, he led a Norman army across the Adriatic to Constantinople. Upon his arrival in April 1097, he did homage to Emperor Alexius. Bohemund served as the real leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch, successfully guiding the host across Asia Minor.

## Successors and the Principality of Antioch
Bohemund II (1108–1131), the son of Bohemund I and Constance of France, was born in the year of his father's defeat at Durazzo. In 1126, he arrived in Antioch from Apulia; the city had been under the regency of Baldwin II since 1119. Bohemund II married Alice, the younger daughter of Baldwin, in 1127. After participating in an attack on Damascus that same year and experiencing conflict with Joscelin of Edessa, he was defeated and killed in 1131 by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo on his northern frontier.

The succession later passed to Bohemund III, the son of Constance (daughter of Bohemund II) and Raymund of Antioch. Bohemund III succeeded his mother in 1163. In 1164, while serving as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem during Amalric I's expedition to Egypt, he was captured by Nureddin at Harenc. He was ransomed by Emperor Manuel and returned to Antioch with a Greek patriarch. Bohemund III's later years were marked by personal and political turmoil; he was excommunicated in 1180 after deserting his second wife, Orguilleuse, for Sibylla. He spent his final years embroiled in a succession dispute over whether the principality should pass to his grandson, Raymund Rhupen, or his younger son, Bohemund IV. Despite being evicted from Antioch by Bohemund IV around 1199, he was restored by Leo of Armenia and died in possession of the principality in 1201.

## The Union of Antioch and Tripoli
Bohemund IV, the younger son of Bohemund III and Orguilleuse, had become count of Tripoli in 1187. He succeeded his father in Antioch in 1201, excluding Raymund Rhupen. This sparked a long dispute supported by Leo of Armenia, which required the attention of Innocent III. Bohemund IV was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and the Templars. Conversely, Leo of Armenia and the Hospitallers supported Raymund Rhupen. Although Leo captured Antioch in 1216, Bohemund IV regained it before Leo's death in 1220. Bohemund IV remained a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, leading to his excommunication in 1230, though he was released from this by Gregory IX shortly before his death in 1233.

Bohemund V succeeded his father in 1233 as prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. He married Alice, the widow of Hugh I of Cyprus, in 1225, connecting the history of Antioch with Cyprus. His reign was characterized by an alliance with the Templars and hostility toward Armenia. This tension persisted until 1251, when peace was achieved through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of the future Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund V resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving Antioch to be governed by a commune and its bailiff.

## Final Rulers and Extinction
Bohemund VI (born 1237), son of Bohemund V and Luciana, succeeded his father in 1251. He was knighted by St Louis in 1252 and established himself in Antioch, leaving Tripoli to its own administration. In 1257, he secured the recognition of his nephew, Hugh II, as king of Jerusalem. Bohemund VI allied with the Mongols to oppose the Egyptian sultan, but he lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268. By the time of his death in 1275, he held only the title of count of Tripoli.

The dynasty ended with Bohemund VII, son of Bohemund VI and Sibylla. He succeeded to the county of Tripoli in 1275 with his mother serving as regent. His reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue, and with the capture of Tripoli within two years of his passing, the county of Tripoli became extinct.
