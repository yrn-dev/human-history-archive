# The Postal System of the Great Kaan

The Great Kaan established an extensive and highly disciplined communication network consisting of posts and runners designed to transmit information and goods rapidly across his lands and provinces. This system utilized both foot-runners and mounted couriers to ensure the Emperor remained informed of events across his vast empire.

## The Foot-Runner Network
To facilitate the rapid movement of despatches, the Great Kaan ordered the establishment of small forts at intervals of three miles between post-houses. Each of these forts consists of approximately forty houses, which serve as the residences for the Emperor's foot-runners.

These runners are equipped with wide belts adorned with bells. As they run the three-mile distance between posts, the jingling of these bells alerts those ahead of their approach. Upon reaching a post, a runner is met by another similarly equipped man who is ready to take his place. This relief runner instantly takes charge of the message or item and receives a slip of paper from a clerk who is always present at the station. This process of changing runners every three miles allows the Emperor to receive news from places ten days' journey away in a single day and night, or news from a hundred days away in ten days and nights.

The efficiency of this system is further illustrated by the transport of seasonal fruit; fruit gathered in the morning in Cambaluc can reach the Great Kaan at Chandu—a distance of ten days' journey—by the evening of the following day.

## Administration and Discipline
The postal system is strictly monitored to ensure reliability. Clerks at each post are required to note the exact time of every courier's arrival and departure. Additionally, officers are appointed to conduct monthly visitations of all posts to identify and punish any runners who have been slack in their duties. In recognition of their service, the Emperor pays these men and exempts them from all tribute.

## Mounted Couriers and Express Tablets
For urgent communications, the Kaan employs mounted couriers who are highly prized for their endurance. To withstand the rigors of their travel, these men bind their heads, chests, and stomachs tightly with strong bands. 

Couriers on urgent express missions carry a gerfalcon tablet. This tablet serves as a sign of their official status and grants them extraordinary powers; if a courier's horse breaks down or if they encounter any other mishap, they are empowered to compel any person they meet on the road to dismount and surrender their horse. Because of the authority of the tablet, no one dares refuse this demand, ensuring the courier always has a fresh horse to continue the journey.

## Logistics and Maintenance
The maintenance of the postal horses is largely funded through local obligations rather than the Emperor's treasury. Every hamlet, village, or city located near a post-station is required to provide a fixed number of horses to the post. In uninhabited areas, however, the horses are provided at the Emperor's own expense.

To ensure the horses remain healthy and capable, cities do not keep their entire quota—for example, 400 horses—at the station at all times. Instead, they rotate the stock, keeping 200 horses at the station while the other 200 are kept at grass, alternating their turns. Furthermore, in regions where runners and horse-posts must cross lakes or rivers, neighboring cities are mandated to keep three or four boats in constant readiness to facilitate the crossing.
