# Bokhara

Bokhara, also known as Bokkara-i-Sherif, is the capital of the state of Bokhara and serves as the most important trading town and the principal book-market of central Asia. Located on the left bank of the Zarafshan and the irrigation canal of Shahri-rud within a fertile plain, the city is situated at 39 deg. 47' N. lat. and 64 deg. 27' E. long. It is 8 miles from the Bokhara station of the Transcaspian railway and 162 miles by rail west of Samarkand.

## History
Known to the ancients as Sogdiana, the region was never under Roman dominion but was deeply affected by Asian revolutions. The foundation of the capital is attributed to the Persian hero Efrasiab. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana was part of the Seleucidae empire. Later, the nomad Yue-chi moved into the Oxus valley; by approximately A.D. 45, the dominions of their king, Kadphises I, extended from Bokhara to the Indus.

The Sassanian dynasty of Persia later reconquered the district. Islam became the dominant faith shortly after the Arab conquest of Persia (640-642). During early Mahommedan rule, the region—then called Sughd or Ma wara 'l Nahr (Transoxiana)—was one of central Asia's most flourishing districts. While Samarkand was generally the seat of government, Bokhara earned a high reputation as a seat of learning and religion.

In the late 11th century, Malik Shah of the Seljuk dynasty subdued the country. In 1216, Mahommed Shah Khwarizm subdued Bokhara, but Jenghiz Khan wrested control in 1220, destroying the city. It was subsequently rebuilt and saw a recovery of prosperity under the humane Ogdai Khan. Mongol possession continued until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand. In the 13th century, while Mongol invasions laid waste to other Moslem cities like Samarkand, Bokhara remained independent and continued as a chief seat of Islamitic learning.

## Education and Literature
Bokhara has long been regarded as the center of Mussulman erudition in central Asia. Approximately one-fourth of the population is said to be literate. Education is widespread, with numerous primary schools in the capital, other cities, and even villages. Higher education is provided by *madrasas* (theological seminaries or colleges), which are comparatively plentiful. In the capital alone, 140 *madrasas* are said to educate nearly 10,000 pupils.

The city's history as a center of learning is reflected in its libraries. Although many rich *madrasa* libraries were lost to conflagrations, confiscated by emirs, or scattered, treasures of literature remain concealed in private libraries. Consequently, bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan still travel to Bokhara to purchase rare books.

## City Structure and Economy
The city is enclosed by a stone wall 8 meters long and 28 feet high, featuring semicircular towers and eleven gates. The current city was begun in A.D. 830 on the site of an older settlement. Its architecture includes 360 mosques, most notably the 16th-century Mir-Arab with its lecture halls and the emir's chief mosque, the Mejid-kalyan (or Kok-humbez). Beside the latter stands a 203-foot brick minaret, used until 1871 to execute state criminals by throwing them from the top.

The principal square is the Raghistan, which borders the citadel. The citadel is built on an artificial eminence 45 feet high, surrounded by a 1-meter wall, and houses the prison, water-cisterns, the palace of the emir, and the residences of chief functionaries. Most city houses are one-storeyed with flat roofs, constructed from unburned bricks.

Economically, Bokhara is a major trading hub. Its bazaars sell carpets, Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins, metal wares (particularly copper), and silk stuffs. Near the railway station lies "New Bokhara" (or Kagan), a Russian town of 2,000 people growing on territory ceded by the emir.

## Society and Governance
The population of Bokhara is estimated between 50,000 and 100,000, including a large and ancient colony of Jews. Socially, women occupy a low position, though Russia prompted the abolition of slavery. The population is described as very superstitious, with beliefs in omens, spirits, the evil eye, and witchcraft. *Mullahs* (priests) wield significant influence.

The emir of Bokhara is an autocratic ruler whose power is limited only by *sheriat* (traditional Mussulman custom). He maintains an army of roughly 11,000 men. However, the emir is a vassal of the Russian Empire and remains subject to Russian control.
