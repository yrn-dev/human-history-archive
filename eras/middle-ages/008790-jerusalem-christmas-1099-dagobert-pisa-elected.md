# Jerusalem and the Early Kingdom

## The Capture of Jerusalem
In June 1099, the forces of the First Crusade invested the city of Jerusalem. After a siege lasting one month, the city was stormed. The resulting slaughter was described as terrible, with blood filling the streets to the point that men on horseback were splashed by it. By the nightfall of July 15, the Crusaders had overcome all opposition and fought their way into the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, where they knelt in prayer, weary and blood-stained. Godfrey of Bouillon is credited with the capture of the city in 1099.

## Political Transitions and the First King
Following the capture of the city, a period of political instability occurred regarding the leadership of Jerusalem. While some traditions attribute the refusal of the crown to Robert of Normandy or Raymond of Toulouse, other accounts mention the ambition of the count of St. Giles.

Baldwin I, the brother of Godfrey of Bouillon, had previously served as the prince of Edessa from 1098 to 1100. Baldwin had originally been a clerk in orders and held several prebends before joining the First Crusade in 1096. After ruling in Edessa—where he acted as an intermediary between the crusaders and the Armenians and won Samosata and Seruj—he visited Jerusalem at the end of 1099 alongside Bohemund I.

Upon the death of Godfrey, Baldwin was summoned to Jerusalem by a specific party to succeed his brother. This summons was driven by a lay reaction against the theocratic pretensions of Dagobert, who had been counting on Norman support. Leveraging this reaction, Baldwin became the first king of Jerusalem and was crowned on Christmas Day, 1100, by the patriarch.

## Conflict Between Church and State
The coronation of Baldwin I did not resolve the tensions between the secular and ecclesiastical authorities. A struggle between church and state persisted, primarily between King Baldwin and Dagobert. In the spring of 1101, Baldwin utilized a papal legate to have Dagobert suspended. 

Later in 1101, the two men disagreed over the amount the patriarch should contribute toward the defense of the Holy Land. This ongoing conflict eventually concluded in 1102 with the deposition of Dagobert and the triumph of Baldwin.

## Leadership of the First Crusade
The First Crusade was characterized by Norman leadership and spirit. Bohemund I, the eldest son of Robert Guiscard and prince of Otranto and Antioch, is regarded as the leader of the crusade. Bohemund had previously commanded Normans in attacks on the East Roman empire and had a long-standing hostility toward Alexius Comnenus. 

After arriving in Constantinople in April 1097 and doing homage to the emperor, Bohemund became the real leader of the crusade. He led the forces across Asia Minor and through the siege of Antioch, which lasted one year, before the army eventually moved toward Jerusalem.
