# Arthur III and the Ducal Authority of Brittany

## Development of Ducal Power
In the history of Brittany, the authority of the dukes underwent a period of significant expansion. Following the reign of John V (who died in 1442) and continuing through the reigns of his successors—Francis I, Peter II, and Arthur III—the ducal authority developed in a remarkable manner. During this era, the dukes strengthened their administrative and military control by forming a standing army and successfully levying hearth taxes, known as *fouages*, throughout the region.

## The House of Montfort and Successors
The path to this strengthened authority followed a period of instability. In 1341, the death of John III without a direct heir triggered a war of succession between the houses of Montfort and Blois. This conflict lasted until 1364 and functioned as an incident of the Hundred Years' War, with the houses of Blois and Montfort receiving support from the kings of France and England, respectively. 

In 1364, John of Montfort (who died in 1399) was recognized as the duke under the style of John IV. His reign was frequently troubled, specifically by his struggles with Olivier de Clisson (1336-1407). This was followed by the reign of John V, who was noted for his pacific and able policy.

## Conflict and Annexation
Following the period of growth under Arthur III and his immediate predecessors, Francis II (1435-1488) engaged in military conflicts against Louis XI, notably during the War of the Public Weal, and later against Charles VIII during "The Mad War" (*La Guerre Folle*). 

The death of Francis II led to an invasion of Brittany by the king of France, who forced Francis's daughter, Anne of Brittany, to marry him in 1491. This event prepared the reunion of Brittany and France. After the death of Charles VIII, Anne married Louis XII. The definitive annexation of the duchy was settled by Francis I, who married Claude (the daughter of Anne and Louis XII), through a contract in 1532. This contract guaranteed the maintenance of the liberties and privileges of Brittany.

## Regional Governance and Resistance
Until the time of the Revolution, Brittany maintained its own estates. However, the royal power attempted to reduce the province's privileges as much as possible. This effort often faced vigorous resistance, particularly during the 18th century. A keen struggle occurred between 1760 and 1769, during which the duc d'Aiguillon (E. A. de V. du Plessis Richelieu) fought against both the parliament and the estates.

## Earlier Ducal History
The evolution of the duchy was preceded by various shifts in power. In the 10th and 11th centuries, the counts of Rennes were predominant. Geoffrey, son of Conan, took the ducal title in 992. Later, the Plantagenets established themselves in the region when Henry II of England became master of Brittany after Conan IV appealed for help against revolted nobles. Henry II's son, Geoffrey, was recognized as duke. 

This dynasty ended abruptly when Geoffrey's posthumous son, Arthur, was assassinated by John of England in 1203. Arthur had previously been entrusted to the keeping of Hubert de Burgh, the chief justiciar of England. Following Arthur's death, his sister Alix succeeded to his rights and married Pierre de Dreux in 1212, initiating a ducal dynasty of French origin that lasted until the end of the 15th century.
