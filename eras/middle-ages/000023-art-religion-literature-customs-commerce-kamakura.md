# Art, Religion, Literature, Customs, and Commerce in the Kamakura

The Kamakura period was characterized by the establishment of a civilization centered in the military capital of Kamakura, which served as the focus of a society radically different from that of Kyoto. Founded by Yoritomo, who selected the site to preserve the tradition of Minamoto power and to avoid the demoralizing and debilitating influence of the Imperial metropolitan society, Kamakura grew from a fishing hamlet into a city of at least 250,000 inhabitants. For 150 years, it remained the center of military society, aiming to develop the special characteristics of the *buke*.

## Art and Horticulture
During this era, Japanese art split into two distinct schools: the Kyoto school and the Kamakura school. The Kyoto school was centered in the Imperial Court and evolved from the styles of Chinese masters of the Tang and Sui dynasties into a national school with a decorative tendency. In contrast, the Kamakura school was centered in the Court of the Hojo and borrowed from the academies of the Yuan and Sung. This school became the favorite of the literary classes and preserved Chinese traditions, focusing primarily on seascapes and landscapes. Its preference for the melancholy aspects of nature appealed specifically to cenobites and students.

These distinctions extended to calligraphy and horticulture. Kyoto penmanship followed the graceful, cursive *o-ie-fu* (domestic fashion) modeled after Ono no Tofu and Kobo Daishi. Kamakura calligraphists adopted the *karayo* (pure Chinese mode), exemplified by priests such as Ichinei (I Ning) and Sogen (Chu Yuan). In painting, Kamakura was represented by masters such as Mu Hsi, Hsia Kwei, and Ma Yuan of the pure Southern Academy of China, who were later followed by Shubun, Kao, and Sesshu.

Horticulture also transitioned from the artificial *no-niwa* (moor garden) style toward the pure landscape park. The first landscape garden in Japan is attributed to the Chinese bonze Ichinei. A delegate from Kublai Khan who arrived in Kyushu during the days of Hojo Sadatoki, Ichinei was eventually invited to Kamakura and assigned to the Kencho-ji temple, where he designed gardens that remain models today.

## Religion and Architecture
The Hojo regents—specifically Tokimune, Tokiyori, and Yasutoki—were earnest disciples of Buddhism. They established five great temples in the Bakufu capital, assigning them to Chinese priests from the Yuan and Sung eras who arrived as propagandists or official envoys. Because these Chinese bonzes superintended the construction, the resulting architecture reflected a blend of Japanese and Chinese art-canons.

The Hojo regents' support for these religious institutions was not solely motivated by faith or art. They utilized foreign priests as channels to gather political information regarding the neighboring empire and sought to create an artistic and literary atmosphere in Kamakura that was independent of Kyoto and free from its enervating elements.

## Literature
Following the Shokyu disturbance of 1221, a long period of peace under the Hojo led to the custom of compiling anthologies. Teika, seeking sincerity and simplicity over elegance and affectation, withdrew to a villa on Mount Ogura to compile the *Hyakunin-isshu*, a collection of one hundred poems by ancient authors that remains a classic.

Literary students of the era were divided into two groups: those who adhered to the pure Japanese style of the Heian epoch and those who borrowed freely from Chinese literature. Notable works of the period include the *Izayoi-nikki*, a journal written by Abutsu-ni. An authoress who traveled to Kamakura to sue a step-son for the return of the manor of Hosokawa, her writing is regarded as a model of sentiment and style.
