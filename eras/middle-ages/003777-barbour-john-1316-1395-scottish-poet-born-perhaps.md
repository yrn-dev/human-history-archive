# BARBOUR, JOHN

John Barbour (? 1316-1395) was a Scottish poet and official born early in the 14th century, approximately 1316, possibly in Aberdeenshire. He is regarded by some as the father of Scots poetry and potentially one of the most voluminous writers in Middle English.

## Professional Career and Education
Barbour held several ecclesiastical and administrative positions throughout his life. A letter of safe-conduct from 1357, which permitted him to study at Oxford, describes him as the archdeacon of Aberdeen. He is mentioned in a similar letter in 1364, and another letter from 1368 granted him permission to travel to France, likely for further studies at the university of Paris.

In the later part of his career, Barbour served in government capacities. He was one of the auditors of exchequer in 1372 and served as a clerk of audit within the king's household in 1373. He may have continued to hold his position as an exchequer auditor after his first appointment, as the role was renewed as part of his later rewards.

## Major Works and Literary Contributions
Barbour's most famous poem is *The Brus*, composed in 1375, a date he provides along with his age at the time, which was 60. For this work, he received a gift of ten pounds in 1377 and a life-pension of twenty shillings in 1378. His pension was later increased by an additional ten pounds.

Beyond *The Brus*, Barbour's bibliography is a subject of considerable controversy. Wyntoun's *Chronicle* mentions a "Treteis" created by Barbour as a genealogy of "Brutus lynagis," and the poem contains references to "Stewartis Oryginale." While some have identified this "Brut" with a Troy-book based on Guido da Colonna's *Historia Destructionis Troiae*, this ascription was first made by Henry Bradshaw and is now generally opposed by critical opinion. Two preserved fragments of a Troy-book, totaling 3,713 lines, focus entirely on Trojan matters, leading some to doubt they could be part of a Scottish genealogy.

## Disputed Works and Attributions
Several other works have been attributed to Barbour, though their authorship remains debated:

*   **Legends of the Saints**: A Scots poem of over 33,000 lines based on the *Legenda Aurea* and other legendaries was discovered by Henry Bradshaw in the Cambridge university library. The poem excludes English saints but includes St Ninian and St Machar (the patron saint of Aberdeen). While C. Horstmann upholds Barbour's authorship, other critics dispute it based on vocabulary, assonance, and rhymes. However, the author is certainly from the north-east of Scotland.
*   **Troy Fragments**: These fragments have been printed by C. Horstmann and discussed by various scholars, including Skeat and J. T. T. Brown.
*   **Other Attributions**: References exist regarding the *Alexander Book* and Gospel-story evidence, though these are discussed in the context of scholarly debate.

## Later Life and Death
The final years of Barbour's life are documented primarily through the "Register of Aberdeen," where his signature appears as a witness to various deeds as late as 1392. According to the obit-book of the cathedral of Aberdeen, Barbour died on March 13, 1395. State records confirm that his life-pension ceased after this date.

## Legacy
Barbour's influence extended to later Scottish literary figures; for example, the poet Burns is described as an heir to Barbour, distilling the spirit of the old poet's epic into a battle chant.
