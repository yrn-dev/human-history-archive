# AUTHORITIES: DIGARD, FAUCON AND THOMAS, LES REGISTRES DE BONIFACE

The scholarly study of the papacy of Boniface VIII and the related political conflicts of the early 14th century relies upon a variety of primary registers and secondary historical analyses. Central to these authorities is the work *Les Registres de Boniface VIII*, published in Paris starting in 1884 by Digard, Faucon, and Thomas.

## Primary and Specialized Literature on Boniface VIII
The historical record of Boniface VIII is supported by several key texts and academic discussions. In addition to the registers compiled by Digard, Faucon, and Thomas, significant analysis is found in the *Kirchenlexikon* (vol. ii, 2nd ed., 1883) and the *Realencyklopadie* (vol. iii, 3rd ed., 1897), the latter of which provides an elaborate bibliography. Other essential historical accounts include J. Loserth's *Geschichte des spateren Mittelalters* (1903) and H. Finke's *Aus den Tagen Bonifaz VIII.* (1902), a work described as epoch-making despite being dreary.

Further academic scrutiny is found in the *Gottingische gelehrte Anzeigen* (1904) and R. Scholz's *Die Publizistik zur Zeit Philipps des Schonen und Bonifaz VIII.* (1903). The nature of Boniface VIII's beliefs has also been questioned, specifically in K. Wenck's piece "War Bonifaz VIII. ein Ketzer?" published in *Historische Zeitschrift* (1905).

## Related Historical Figures and Conflicts
The authorities regarding Boniface VIII often intersect with the history of his contemporaries and successors. Pierre Dubois, a French publicist and royal advocate from Coutances, wrote a series of anti-clerical pamphlets during the reign of Philip the Fair. Dubois aligned himself with Philip's secularizing policy against Boniface VIII, producing the *Supplication du pueble de France au roy contre le pape Boniface le VIII^e* (1304), which serves as a heated indictment of temporal power.

Following the papacy of Boniface VIII, Benedict XI (Niccolo Boccasini) served as pope from 1303 to 1304. Benedict worked to conciliate enemies created by Boniface VIII, including the Colonnas, France, and King Frederick II of Sicily. However, Benedict excommunicated William of Nogaret and the Italians who had captured Boniface in Anagni. Records of Benedict's own administration are found in C. Grandjean's *Registres de Benoit XI* (Paris, 1883 ff.).

## Other Figures Named Boniface
The archive distinguishes the aforementioned Boniface VIII from other historical figures of the same name:
*   **Boniface I**: Bishop of Rome (418–422) who was recognized by Emperor Honorius after a dispute with the deacon Eulalius.
*   **Boniface II**: Pope (530–532), a Goth who attempted to nominate his own successor.
*   **Boniface III**: Pope (606), who received recognition of the "headship of the church at Rome" from Phocas.
*   **Boniface VII**: A pope credited by some accounts with the invention of "popish jubilees," pretending to revive an ancient institution.
*   **Boniface of Savoy**: Archbishop of Canterbury (d. 1270) and uncle to Eleanor of Provence. He is noted for his role in the council of Merton (1258) and his royalist stance during the barons' war.
