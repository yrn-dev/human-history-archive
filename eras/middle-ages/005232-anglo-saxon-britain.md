# Anglo-Saxon Britain

## Origins and Early History
The history of Britain following the withdrawal of Roman troops is characterized by obscurity, though evidence suggests the provinces suffered devastating raids by the Scots and Picts for many years. According to the writer Gildas, the Britons decided to call upon the Saxons for protection against these incursions. While their allies initially achieved a decisive victory, they later turned their weapons against the Britons, claiming they had not been sufficiently paid for their services.

An alternative account, likely of English origin and found in the *Historia Brittonum*, suggests that the first Saxon leaders, Horsa and Hengest, arrived as exiles seeking protection from the British king, Vortigern. After entering his service, they successfully expelled the northern invaders. However, they eventually overcame the Britons through treachery by persuading the king to allow them to bring over large numbers of their own countrymen. Tradition attributes the origin of the kingdom of Kent to these adventurers. Various English, Gaulish, and Welsh authorities place the date of the first invasion between 428 and 455 (the joint reign of Valentinian III and Martian).

## The Kingdom of Kent
Kent was one of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, with dimensions that appear to correspond to the modern county. Tradition identifies it as the first area occupied by the invaders. While Welsh accounts date the arrival of the Saxons to 428, English tradition places it between 450 and 455. The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* records that Hengest reigned from 455 to 488, followed by his son Aesc (Oisc) until 512, though these dates are considered of little value.

Documentary history for Kent begins with Aethelberht, the great-grandson of Aesc, who likely reigned from 560 to 616. Aethelberht held supremacy over all English kings south of the Humber. His marriage to Berhta, the daughter of the Frankish king Charibert (or Haribert), is believed to have contributed to the success of Augustine's mission, which landed in 597. Aethelberht was succeeded in 616 by his son Eadbald, who initially renounced Christianity and married his stepmother before being converted by Laurentius, Augustine's successor. 

Eadbald was succeeded in 640 by Erconberht, who enforced Christianity throughout the kingdom. He was followed in 664 by his son Ecgbert, and then by Ecgbert's brother Hlothhere in 673. Hlothhere's reign was marked by an invasion by Aethelred of Mercia. Hlothhere and his nephew Eadric (son of Ecgbert) issued a code of laws; however, a quarrel between the two in 685 led Eadric to call in the South Saxons. Hlothhere died of his wounds, and Eadric reigned for less than two years.

## Law, Society, and Language
The legal systems of the West Saxons and Mercians indicate that British conquests continued even after these groups became Christians. Legal values were assigned to different classes of people: a free Saxon was valued at 200 shillings, a Thane at 1200 shillings, and a "Wallus" or "Cambricus" (homo) who possessed a hyde of land was fixed at 120 shillings. Notably, the laws of the four kings of Kent do not mention the existence of any subject Britons.

The people of this era sometimes referred to themselves as "Anglo-Saxons." This is evidenced by charters in which Aethelstan (934) called himself "Ongol-Saxna cyning and Brytaenwalda eallaes thyses iglandes" and Eadred (955) used the title "Angul-seaxna cyning and casere totius Britanniae." The dialects of the *Seaxan* and *Engle* were both old forms of what became English speech. It is probable that the terms *Engle* and *Englisc* were not initially applied to all inhabitants of Teutonic Britain, but only to a specific part of them.

## Technology and Economy
Before arriving in Britain, the Anglo-Saxons lacked corn-grinding instruments other than the quern (*cweorn*), which remained in use in remote districts until late times. Grinding was primarily performed by female slaves, though donkey-mills (*esolcweorn*) were occasionally used. The proper mill (*mylen*), derived from the Latin *molina*, was adopted from the Romans and likely known to the invaders from the start. By the 8th century, references to mills appear, and by the 11th century, every village of significant size seemed to possess one. While most were water-driven, a reference to a windmill exists as early as 833.
