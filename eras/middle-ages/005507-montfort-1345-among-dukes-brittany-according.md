# John of Montfort (d. 1345)

## Ducal Status and Enumeration
John of Montfort, who died in 1345, is a figure of genealogical debate among certain authorities regarding his status as a duke of Brittany. According to one enumeration, he is counted among the dukes of Brittany. This classification alters the numbering of subsequent rulers: under this system, his son, the younger John, is designated as John V rather than John IV, and the successor to the younger John is designated as John VI rather than John V.

## The War of Succession
The period following the death of John III in 1341, who died without a direct heir, was marked by a war of succession that lasted until 1364. This conflict pitted the house of Montfort against the house of Blois. The struggle was effectively an incident of the broader Hundred Years' War, as the two factions sought support from different crowns: the partisans of Montfort were supported by the kings of England, while the partisans of Blois were supported by the kings of France.

## Territorial Actions and the City of Brest
During this period of instability, the city of Brest held significant medieval importance, reflected in the saying, "He is not duke of Brittany who is not lord of Brest." In 1342, John of Montfort gave the city up to the English. Brest remained under English control until 1397.

## Legacy and Successors
The conflict eventually concluded in 1364 when John of Montfort (d. 1399), the son of the John of Montfort who died in 1345, was recognized as the duke of Brittany under the style of John IV. The reign of John IV was characterized by constant trouble, specifically his struggle with Olivier de Clisson (1336-1407). He was later assisted in Brittany by the English soldier Sir Robert Knolles, who acted as John's representative.

Following John IV, the duchy was led by John V (d. 1442), who was noted for an able and pacific policy. Under John V and his successors—Arthur III, Peter II, and Francis I—ducal authority developed remarkably. The dukes established a standing army and implemented the levying of hearth taxes, known as *fouages*, throughout Brittany.
