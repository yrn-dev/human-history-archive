# Avicenna

Avicenna (Abū 'Alī al-Husain ibn 'Abdallāh ibn Sīnā), an Arabian philosopher who lived from 980 to 1037, was born at Afshena in the district of Bokhara. His father was a Persian from Balkh who served as a tax-collector in Harmaitin under the Samanid amir of Bokhara, Nūh II. ibn Mansur. Following the birth of Avicenna's younger brother, the family moved to Bokhara, a major city of the Moslem world renowned for its culture.

## Early Education and Intellectual Development
Avicenna demonstrated early precocity, knowing the Koran and various Arabic poetry by rote by the age of ten. His education began with a tutor and included arithmetic, learned from a greengrocer. He further studied under a wandering scholar who provided lessons for the young and cures for the sick; through this teacher, Avicenna was introduced to the first propositions of Euclid and Porphyry's *Isagoge*. 

Finding his teacher to be a charlatan, Avicenna used commentaries to independently master geometry, logic, and the *Almagest*. By the age of sixteen, he had mastered medical theory and claimed to have discovered new treatment methods through his attendance on the sick. He spent the following eighteen months studying higher philosophy. During periods of difficulty, he would seek clarity through prayer in the mosque. He is said to have read Aristotle's *Metaphysics* forty times until the text was memorized, though he only understood its meaning after purchasing a commentary by Fārābī for three dirhems.

## Professional Career and Political Life
At the age of twenty-two, Avicenna lost his father. His first major appointment was as physician to the amir, whom he treated for a dangerous illness in 997. As a reward, he was granted access to the royal library of the Samanids. When this library was later destroyed by fire, enemies accused him of burning it to hide the sources of his knowledge.

Following the end of the Samanid dynasty in December 1004, Avicenna declined offers from Mahmūd the Ghaznevid and moved west to Urjensh. There, a vizier provided him with a small monthly stipend. Due to the insufficiency of this pay, he traveled through Merv and Nishapur to the borders of Khorasan. Around 1012, while seeking asylum with the ruler of Dailam, Shams al-Ma'ālī Qābūs, Avicenna suffered a severe illness. Qābūs himself was starved to death by revolted soldiers around that time.

Avicenna later found a patron in Jorjān, near the Caspian, where he lectured on astronomy and logic. He eventually settled at Rai, near modern Teheran, under the nominal rule of Majd Addaula. He composed about thirty shorter works there before feuds between the regent and her son, Shams Addaula, forced him to leave. After a brief stay in Kazwīn, he went to Hamadān. There, he served as a medical attendant to the amir and was eventually appointed vizier. However, mutinous Kurdish and Turkish soldiers demanded his death, leading to his banishment. After hiding for forty days in a sheik's house, he was restored to his post due to the amir's own illness.

Following the amir's death, Avicenna hid in an apothecary's house to continue his writing. He attempted to offer his services to Abu Ya'far, the prefect of Isfahan, but was discovered and incarcerated in a fortress by the new amir of Hamadān. In 1024, Isfahan captured Hamadān, and Avicenna returned to the city to continue his literary work before eventually departing with his brother, two slaves, and a favorite pupil.

## Literary Works and Scientific Contributions
Approximately 100 treatises are attributed to Avicenna, ranging from short tracts to multi-volume works. His most famous work, the *Canon of Medicine*, established his European reputation. It saw a Hebrew version in Naples (1491), an Arabic edition in Rome (1593), and about thirty Latin editions based on Gerard of Cremona's translation. In the 15th century, a great commentary on the *Canon* was composed, integrating theoretical and practical observations. Other medical works translated into Latin include *Tractatus de Syrupo Acetoso*, *Canticum de Medicina*, and *Medicamenta Cordialia*.

Avicenna's influence extended across the "Arabian circle of the sciences," including physics, astronomy, mathematics, philology, music, and theology. His works on *Physics*, *De Caelo*, *Metaphysics*, and *Logic* provide synoptic views of Aristotelian doctrine. The *Metaphysics* was printed in Venice in 1493, 1495, and 1546. He also wrote a book on animals (translated by Michael Scot) and at least one treatise on alchemy.

His philosophical output includes two encyclopedic treatises: the larger *Al-Shifā* (*Sanatio*) and a shorter version called *An-najāt* (*Liberatio*). Part of the *Al-Shifā* regarding *De Anima* was published in Pavia (1490) as *Liber Sextus Naturalium*. Shahrastani's account of Avicenna's philosophy is largely an analysis or reproduction of the *Al-Shifā*.

In the field of metallurgy, as cited by Roger Bacon in *De Animâ*, Avicenna distinguished three types of iron:
1. Common iron, used for anvils and hammers.
2. Steel, which is purer and better for cutting tools.
3. Andena (or Alidena), which is intermediate between iron and steel and is malleable under low heat.
