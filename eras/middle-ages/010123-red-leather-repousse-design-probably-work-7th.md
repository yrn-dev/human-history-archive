# St. Cuthbert's Gospels Binding

The *St. Cuthbert's Gospels* is a book preserved at Stonyhurst featuring a binding of red leather. This covering is characterized by a repousse design, with fine lines that were impressed by hand and subsequently painted in yellow and blue. Based on available archival evidence, the binding is probably the work of the 7th or 8th century.

## Historical Context of Illuminated Manuscripts
From approximately the 7th to the 16th century, illuminated manuscripts were held in the highest esteem. These works were noted for their exquisite miniature painting and calligraphy. The internal gorgeousness of the illuminations often dictated the quality of the external coverings. Consequently, splendid bindings were produced using materials such as carved ivory, enamels, and metals adorned with jewels.

Examples of such high-status bindings from this era include:
*   The *Gospels of Theodolinda* at Monza (7th century).
*   The *Gospels of Charlemagne*, located in the Victoria and Albert Museum at South Kensington.
*   The *Lindau Gospels*, currently in America.
*   The Irish cumdach of the *Stowe Missal*.
*   14th-century Limoges enamel bindings found in the British Museum.

While similar intrinsically precious English bindings may have once existed, many have disappeared; it is suggested they may have been melted down by Edward VI or Henry VIII.

## Book Ornamentation and Style
During the 7th century, a native school of book ornamentation developed in Ireland. This Celtic style was a mechanical art characterized by patient, skillful elaboration and mathematical accuracy. The designs—found in initials, borders, and full-page layouts—featured intricate knots, spirals, zig-zag ornaments, and interwoven patterns. These were often combined with interlaced ribbons and entangled zoomorphic creatures, as well as designs worked in red dots.

These patterns were painted with thick pigments in harmonious colors, giving the work an appearance similar to enamel, though gold was never used. The artists likely modeled these designs on classical jewelry, mosaics, enamels, and early metalwork. A primary example of this style is the *Book of Kells* from the latter part of the 7th century, preserved in Trinity College, Dublin.

## Related Craftsmanship and Materials
The use of leather and repousse techniques appears in various historical contexts beyond bookbinding:
*   **Homeric Times:** Leather working was one of the chief industries, performed by the *skutotomos*.
*   **Japanese Art:** In the Shoso-in storehouse at Nara, the archive records the presence of polished mirrors with repousse backs and narrow leather belts decorated with jade or silver. Additionally, brass, bronze, or silver braziers were produced with chiselled or repousse decorative designs.
*   **Palæolithic Period:** During the Magdalenian stage, deep reliefs and line engravings were created on bone. It has been suggested that designs drawn around bones were used to print colored designs onto leather.

## Evolution of Binding Techniques
Later developments in binding moved toward different materials and methods. By the 16th century, collectors like Jean Grolier and Tommaso Maioli utilized morocco leather with gold tooling. In more recent history, a modern school of design for gold-tooling on book-covers was founded by T.J. Cobden-Sanderson, following the initial impetus of William Morris. This school included practitioners such as Douglas Cockerell, who utilized red niger morocco, and Miss E.M. MacColl, who invented a method for producing small curved gold lines using a tiny wheel.
