# Constantinople

Constantinople served as a central point of interaction, conflict, and political negotiation between the East Roman (Byzantine) Empire and various Western European forces, most notably during the Crusades.

## The First Crusade and Bohemund I
In the late 11th century, Constantinople was a target of Norman ambition. Between 1080 and 1085, Robert Guiscard led a great attack on the East Roman Empire. His eldest son, Bohemund I, commanded the Normans from 1082 to 1084 during Guiscard's absence, penetrating as far as Larissa in Thessaly before being repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early conflict established a hostility toward Alexius that influenced Bohemund's future career.

Bohemund I, whom his father had destined for the throne of Constantinople, later became a leader of the First Crusade. After gathering a Norman army and crossing the Adriatic, he reached Constantinople in April 1097. Despite his previous hostilities, Bohemund maintained a "correct" attitude toward Alexius and performed homage to the emperor upon his arrival. While he may have negotiated with Alexius for a principality at Antioch, the archive indicates he received little encouragement in this matter. Other participants of the First Crusade, such as Baldwin II (then known as Baldwin de Burg), also appeared at Constantinople as part of the forces under Godfrey of Bouillon.

## The Fourth Crusade and Latin Rule
The relationship between the Latin West and the Greek Empire shifted drastically during the Fourth Crusade (1202–1204). Rather than fighting the Turks, the Latin Church turned its focus toward the Greek Empire. Led by the trading city of Venice, the crusaders stormed and captured Constantinople in 1204.

Following the capture, the Venetians annexed most of the islands and coasts of the Byzantine Empire. A "Latin" emperor, Baldwin of Flanders, was established in Constantinople. During this period, the Latin and Greek Churches were declared reunited. This era of Latin predominance in Constantinople lasted until 1261, when the Greeks recaptured the city and shook themselves free from Roman rule.

## Diplomatic and Religious Ties
Constantinople continued to be a site of diplomatic refuge and religious appointment for leaders of the Crusader states. In 1164, Bohemund III, the prince of Antioch, was captured by Nureddin at Harenc. He was ransomed by his brother-in-law, Emperor Manuel, and traveled to Constantinople. Upon his return from the city, Bohemund III was accompanied by a Greek patriarch.
