# The Shift and Spread of Greek Monachism

## Transition to Mount Athos
During the middle ages, the center of Greek monachism shifted from Constantinople to Mount Athos. The establishment of the monastery of St Athanasius around 960 marked the beginning of this transition. Over the subsequent three or four centuries, a large number of monasteries were founded on Mount Athos, including Greek, Slavonic, and one Latin institution. Approximately twenty of these monasteries continue to survive.

## Basilian Monachism and its Influence
Basilian monachism, based on the Rules of St Basil, spread from Greece into Russia and Italy. St Basil's institute was particularly suited to European ideas, and while an eastern preference for eremitical life persisted, the Basilian cenobitic form eventually prevailed throughout Greek-speaking lands through civil legislation (the Justinian Code) and ecclesiastical legislation (the council of Chalcedon).

In Italy, Rufinus translated St Basil's Rules into Latin around 400, and these rules were adopted by certain Italian monasteries. St Benedict was influenced by these ideas, referring his own monks to "the Rule of our holy Father Basil," and owed more of the ground-ideas of his Rule to St Basil than to any other monastic legislator.

## Expansion into Italy and Russia
Greek monasteries were present in Rome, south Italy, and Sicily during the 6th and 7th centuries. Between the 8th and 10th centuries, invasions by Arabs, Saracens, and others drove crowds of fugitives from Sicily and Greece into southern Italy. Consequently, from the middle of the 9th century, Basilian monasteries populated by Greek-speaking monks were established in great numbers in Calabria and spread north to Rome. While many existed into the 18th century, the only surviving institution is the monastery founded by St Nilus around 1000 at Grottaferrata in the Alban Hills. Many chief collections of Greek manuscripts in western Europe, such as those at the Vatican and the Bessarion at Venice, originated from the spoils of these Italian Basilian houses.

The importation of Basilian monachism into Russia was of even greater importance, as it became the norm for all Slavonic lands. Greek monks were significantly involved in the evangelization of the Slavs; the first Russian monastery was founded at Kiev around 1050 by a monk from Mount Athos.

## Monastic Law and Structure
Greek monachism experienced a period of general decadence in the second half of the 8th century. Around the year 800, Theodore became the abbot of the Studium monastery in Constantinople and sought to restore the primitive vigour of St Basil's spirit. To provide a permanent reformation, Theodore drew up constitutions to serve as a practical code of laws supplementing St Basil's Rules. These constitutions spread from the Studium to other monasteries across the Greek empire. Today, the Constitutions of Theodore the Studite, the Rules of Basil, and the canons of the Councils form the primary basis of Russian and Greek monastic law.

Monasteries are categorized into three types:
*   **Cenobia proper**: Where personal poverty and full monastic common life are observed.
*   **Idiorrhythmic**: Where monks lead a more free and mitigated life and are permitted the use of private means.
*   **Lauras**: Where the monastic life is semi-eremitical.

## Contemporary Life and Characteristics
In the Russian empire, there are approximately 400 monasteries for men and 100 for women, many of which operate schools, almshouses, and hospitals. There are also numerous monasteries in Greece and no fewer than 100 Greek monasteries in Turkish dominions. Greek and Slavonic monks are characterized by the wearing of a black habit.

Modern accounts of life on Mount Athos, such as those by Robert Curzon (1834, 1837) and Athelstan Riley (1887), describe a life primarily dedicated to contemplative devotional exercises. These accounts note that church services are extremely long, manual labor has almost disappeared, and intellectual study is rarely cultivated. Mount Athos also hosts many hermits.
