# Metaphysics in Historical Thought and Literature

Metaphysics, as a field of study, appears across various eras of human history, ranging from early Grecian philosophy to the works of medieval scholars and later empirical thinkers. Its treatment varies from a dialectical investigation of first principles to a subject often contrasted with physical science and empirical observation.

## Early Grecian Metaphysics
In the sixth century B.C., the transition from physics to metaphysics was marked by thinkers such as Anaximander. Anaximander introduced a word signifying a principle or beginning, initiating a metaphysical discussion that persisted throughout the period of Grecian philosophy. This discourse focused on several central tensions: the relationship between the one and the many, the continuous versus the variable, and the distinction between that which exists eternally and that which is subject to ever-changing manifestations.

Unlike the hypothesis of Thalês, which was based on physical considerations and encouraged the observation of physical phenomena for verification, Anaximander's hypothesis was conducted dialectically. His reasonings were expressed in general language and, while occasionally using experience for illustration, they did not rely on it as a necessary support. Throughout Grecian philosophy, the departments of physics and metaphysics were never clearly separated; subsequent physical philosophers often integrated their explanations of nature with metaphysical additions.

## Avicenna and Aristotelian Doctrine
The medieval scholar Avicenna produced a vast body of work, with approximately 100 treatises ascribed to him. Among these are several works intended to provide a synoptic view of Aristotelian doctrine, including his treatises on *Logic*, *Physics*, *De Caelo*, and *Metaphysics*. 

The *Metaphysics* of Avicenna has been printed on multiple occasions, with notable editions appearing in Venice in 1493, 1495, and 1546. Additionally, Avicenna's philosophical contributions are detailed in two encyclopaedic treatises. The larger of these, *Al-Shif[=a]* (*Sanatio*), exists nearly complete in manuscript in the Bodleian library. A shorter version of this work is known as *An-naj[=a]t* (*Liberatio*). The philosophy contained within *Al-Shif[=a]* was later analyzed and reproduced in a long account by Shahrastani.

## Bacon and the Empirical Shift
The relationship between metaphysics and the scientific method is highlighted in the works and influence of Bacon. In the *Opus Majus*, Bacon's reasoning was based on a specific view of natural agents, where physical action is defined as "impression" or the transmission of force (*virtus*, *species*, or *imago agentis*) in lines, which must be explained geometrically. This theory of nature is connected to metaphysical problems regarding matter and force.

Regarding the *Opus Tertium*, some scholars, such as Charles, suggest the work was intended to be a preface that would eventually discuss various subjects, including metaphysics, general physics, moral philosophy, grammar, mathematics, and logic. Bacon himself viewed logic as being of little service because he believed reasoning was innate.

In a broader sense, Bacon is regarded as the guiding spirit of the empirical school. While he did not enter into questions of metaphysics as they were commonly understood, his influence in the moral and metaphysical sciences was powerful. He advocated for the restraint of reason in its pursuit of ultimate truth, arguing that the human intellect erroneously pushes investigations deeper rather than resting in general principles. Bacon relegated theological questions to the province of faith rather than reason, asserting that observation and experience are the only remedies for error and prejudice.

## Other Historical References
Metaphysics also appears in more recent academic study. For instance, the metaphysics of Beneke was the subject of a work titled *Die Metaphysik Benekes*, written by A. Wandschneider and published in Berlin in 1903.
