# ASH WEDNESDAY

Ash Wednesday is the first day of Lent in the Western Church. It is named for the ceremonial use of ashes during the service prescribed for the day, which serve as a symbol of penitence. This custom is ultimately based on the "sackcloth and ashes" penance mentioned by the prophets of the Old Testament.

## Observances in the Roman Catholic Church
In the Roman Catholic Church, the day is referred to as *dies cinerum* (day of ashes) or *dies cineris et cilicii* (day of ash and sackcloth). The ashes used in the ceremony are produced by burning the palms, or substitutes for palms, that were used during the previous Palm Sunday. These ashes are placed in a vessel on the altar before High Mass.

The priest, wearing a violet cope, performs a series of rituals:
* He prays for God to send His angel to hallow the ash, intending for it to become a *remedium salubre* for all penitents.
* The ashes are censed three times and sprinkled three times with holy water.
* The priest invites those present to kneel. He dips his thumb in the ashes and marks the forehead of the faithful (or the place of tonsure for clerics) with the sign of the cross.

During this marking, the priest says: *Memento, homo, quid pulvis es et in pulverem reverteris* (Remember, man, that thou art dust and unto dust thou shall return). The celebrant either receives the ash from the highest-ranking priest present or sprinkles it on his own head in silence.

## Observances in Reformed Churches
The use of ashes has been dropped by those reformed Churches that continue to observe the fast. Among these, only the Anglican Church marks the day with a special service, known as the Commination service. The distinctive element of this service is the solemn reading of "the general sentences of God's cursing against sinners," which are gathered from other places of Scripture and the twenty-seventh chapter of Deuteronomy. 

The lections for Ash Wednesday in the Anglican Church are the same as those in the Roman Church: Matthew vi. 16, &c., and Joel ii. 12, &c. In the American Prayer Book, the office of Commination is omitted, though it retains three concluding prayers derived from the anthems and prayers of the Sarum Missal used during the distribution and blessing of ashes.

Historically, the ceremonial use of ashes was not proscribed during the English Reformation. It was enjoined by proclamation under Edward VI in 1550 and by Henry VIII on February 26, 1538. However, the practice had fallen into complete disuse by the start of the 17th century.

## Calendar and Timing
Ash Wednesday occurs 46 days before Rogation Sunday. In the broader liturgical calendar, it is positioned as follows:
* Septuagesima Sunday occurs 9 weeks before Easter.
* The First Sunday in Lent occurs 6 weeks before Easter.
* Ash Wednesday follows the First Sunday in Lent.
* Rogation Sunday is 5 weeks before Easter.
* Ascension day (or Holy Thursday) is 39 days before Easter.
* Pentecost (or Whitsunday) is 7 weeks after Easter.
* Trinity Sunday is 8 weeks after Easter.

## Regional Customs and Carnival
Ash Wednesday often serves as a boundary for carnival festivities. In Rome, later popes restricted public carnival festivities to the six or seven days immediately preceding Ash Wednesday, though municipal authorities allow ten days. Ancient Roman carnival sports included the Testaccio, the spectacular pageant of the Agona, and races in the Corso (formerly the Via Lata).

Other regional variations include:
* **Italy:** After Rome, Venice was historically a principal home of carnival. Other cities with competing programs include Florence, Milan, Turin, and Naples. Florence was once known for the licentiousness of its carnival, as evidenced by the *Canti Carnascialeschi* (carnival songs) of Lorenzo de' Medici.
* **Spain:** The carnival lasts four days, and this period includes Ash Wednesday.
* **France:** Merry-making is almost entirely restricted to Shrove Tuesday (*mardi gras*).
* **Russia:** Carnival gaieties last a week from Sunday to Sunday, and Ash Wednesday is not observed.
