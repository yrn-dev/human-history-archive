# Jean de Bar

## Forgery and the Cardinal de Bouillon
Jean de Bar was an intimate companion of the cardinal de Bouillon. He organized a "regular manufactory" of forged documents designed to support certain pretensions. These efforts followed earlier attempts to establish false lineages, such as the spurious genealogies published by Christophe Justel in 1645 in the *Histoire de la maison d'Auvergne*, and a forged deed included in 1672 by Chorier, the historian of Dauphiny, which attempted to link the La Tours of Auvergne with the La Tours of Dauphiny.

The rogues led by Jean de Bar were highly skilful in their fabrications, managing to deceive several illustrious scholars. On July 23, 1695, a unanimously favourable report was issued by experts including Baluze, Dom Thierry Ruinart, and Dom Jean Mabillon, the founder of Diplomatics.

## Legal Consequences and Aftermath
The activities of Jean de Bar eventually drew the attention of the cardinal de Bouillon's many enemies, sparking a war of pamphlets. In March 1698, Baluze wrote a *Letter* in response to these challenges, though it proved nothing. In 1700, Jean de Bar and his accomplices were arrested. Following a searching and long inquiry, they were declared guilty in 1704.

Despite the verdict, Baluze remained obstinate, convinced that the documents in question were genuine. With the financial encouragement and support of the cardinal de Bouillon, Baluze produced a *Table genealogique* in 1705 and a *Histoire genealogique de la maison d'Auvergne* in 1709. This latter work included "Proofs" that contained the deeds already pronounced spurious.

## Decline of Baluze
In 1710, Baluze was caught up in the disgrace of his patron, the cardinal de Bouillon. As a result, he was exiled from Paris and stripped of his pensions, benefices, and appointments. During his exile, he continued his scholarly work, publishing the *Historiae Tutelensis libri tres*, a history of his native town, in 1717. Baluze eventually returned to Paris, where he died on July 28, 1718, remaining unconvinced of his errors. Analysis of his correspondence with the cardinal suggests that Baluze may have been the victim of clever cheats rather than an accomplice.
