# Sigismund Korybutovic and the Bohemian Conflict

## Political Transition in Bohemia
Royal authority in Bohemia ceased following a meeting of the diet at Caslav on June 1, 1421, where Sigismund was deposed. The diet decided that a Polish prince should be selected as the sovereign. In the interim, a provisional government was established, consisting of twenty men representing various parties.

In 1422, Sigismund invaded Bohemia once more but suffered a decisive defeat at Nemecky Brod (Deutschbrod) at the hands of Zizka. Following this, the Polish prince Sigismund Korybutovic arrived in Bohemia. He was recognized as regent by a large majority of the population; however, his initial tenure lasted only a few months before he was recalled by the rulers of Poland due to the influence of the papal see.

## Internal Strife and Reunification
The departure of Prince Korybutovic triggered the first outbreak of civil war between the advanced Taborite party and the moderate Hussites, who were also known as Utraquists or Calixtines. While isolated disturbances between these groups had occurred previously, this period marked a more significant conflict.

The Bohemians were eventually reunited by two primary factors: the return of Prince Korybutovic and the threat of an invasion by German forces. This unification led to a decisive victory for the Bohemians over the Germans at Aussig in 1426.

## Departure and Aftermath
Shortly after the victory at Aussig, in which Korybutovic had participated, he incurred the dislike of the extreme Hussites. As a result, he was forced to leave Bohemia. His departure marked the end of all hopes to establish an independent Slav dynasty within the region.

Following these events, several German princes launched a new crusade against the Hussites in 1427. This invading force included 1000 English archers serving as the bodyguard to Henry Beaufort, the bishop of Winchester, who acted as the papal legate. The crusaders experienced sudden panic at Tachau and Mies (Stribro) upon approaching the Hussites, leading to a hurried flight across the mountains into Bavaria. Despite further internal disturbances, the Bohemians utilized this success to take the offensive, repeatedly invading the German states and Hungary.
