# Avicenna's Synoptic Treatises

The works of Avicenna include a vast body of writing, with approximately 100 treatises ascribed to him. These works vary significantly in scale, ranging from short tracts of a few pages to extensive volumes. While he is most renowned in Europe for the *Canon of Medicine*, his intellectual influence extended across the "Arabian circle of the sciences," encompassing fields such as philology, mathematics, astronomy, music, theology, and physics.

## Synoptic Treatises on Aristotelian Doctrine

Among Avicenna's contributions to philosophy and science are several treatises designed to provide a synoptic view of Aristotelian doctrine. These specific works include:

*   ***Logic***: A treatise providing a summary of Aristotelian logical principles.
*   ***Metaphysics***: A synoptic work on metaphysics that has been printed multiple times, including editions in Venice in 1493, 1495, and 1546.
*   ***Physics***: A treatise offering a synoptic overview of physical doctrine.
*   ***De Caelo***: A treatise providing a synoptic view of the Aristotelian perspective on the heavens.

## Other Philosophical and Scientific Works

Beyond these synoptic treatises, Avicenna produced other significant philosophical and scientific texts. He wrote at least one treatise on alchemy, though the archive notes that several other works on this subject have been falsely attributed to him. His book on animals was translated by Michael Scot.

Avicenna also authored two encyclopaedic treatises on philosophy. The larger of these, *Al-Shif[=a]* (*Sanatio*), exists in manuscript form in the Bodleian library and other locations. A portion of this work concerning *De Anima* was published at Pavia in 1490 under the title *Liber Sextus Naturalium*. Additionally, the philosopher Shahrastani provided a long account of Avicenna's philosophy that appears to be largely a reproduction or analysis of the *Al-Shif[=a]*. A shorter version of this encyclopaedic work is known as *An-naj[=a]t* (*Liberatio*).

## Medical and Poetical Writings

Avicenna's reputation in Europe was primarily established by the *Canon of Medicine*. This work saw an Arabic edition published in Rome in 1593 and a Hebrew version in Naples in 1491. The Latin version, based on the original translation by Gerard of Cremona, had approximately thirty editions. During the 15th century, a great commentary on the *Canon* was composed, which integrated various theoretical imaginations and practical observations.

Other medical works translated into Latin include:
*   *Medicamenta Cordialia*
*   *Canticum de Medicina*
*   *Tractatus de Syrupo Acetoso*

In addition to his formal treatises, Avicenna wrote shorter essays on logic and medicine that took a poetical form; a poem on logic was published by Schmoelders in 1836.
