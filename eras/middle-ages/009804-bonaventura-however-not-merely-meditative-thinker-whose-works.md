# Bonaventura

Saint Bonaventura, also known as John of Fidanza, was a prominent Franciscan theologian born in 1221 at Bagnarea in Tuscany. Destined for the church by his mother, he is said to have received the name Bonaventura from St Francis of Assisi, who performed a miraculous cure upon him.

## Life and Career
Bonaventura entered the Franciscan order in 1243. His academic training took place in Paris, where he studied under John of Rochelle and possibly Alexander of Hales. By 1250, his reputation had grown sufficiently to grant him permission to read upon the *Sentences*. He succeeded John of Rochelle in his chair in 1253 and attained the degree of doctor in 1255.

His rising fame led to his election as general of his order in 1256. In this capacity, he interdicted Roger Bacon from lecturing at Oxford and required him to be under the surveillance of the order in Paris. Bonaventura was also instrumental in the election of Gregory X, who appointed him bishop of Albano and a cardinal. He attended the great council of Lyons in 1274, where he died.

## Theological and Philosophical Thought
While some may view his works as manuals of devotion, Bonaventura was a high-ranking dogmatic theologian. He is recognized as the "Seraphic Doctor," noted for a combination of moderation and subtilty in his approach to scholastic philosophy. He agreed with Albertus Magnus in the view that theology is a practical science, asserting that its truths are specifically adapted to influence the affections.

In contrast to contemporaries such as Thomas Aquinas (representing perfect Aristotelian scholasticism) and Roger Bacon (representing early physical science), Bonaventura utilized a Platonizing and mystical mode of speculation. This approach was influenced by Bernard of Clairvaux, Richard and Hugo of St Victor, the Alexandrian school, and the author of mystical works known as Dionysius the Areopagite. His Platonism was specifically that understood by St Augustine.

Bonaventura rejected the authority of Aristotle, attributing much of the era's heretical tendencies to Aristotelian influence and vigorously combating doctrines such as the eternity of the world.

## Scholastic Contributions
Bonaventura provided reasoned decisions on several disputed questions of scholastic thought:

*   **Universals:** He considered universals to be ideal forms that pre-existed in the divine mind, serving as the patterns according to which things were shaped. He rejected the theory that ideas exist *in rerum natura*.
*   **Matter:** He held that matter is pure potentiality. It receives its determinateness and individual being from the formative power of God acting according to the ideas.
*   **Intellectus Agens:** He maintained that the *intellectus agens* does not have a separate existence.
*   **Divine Attributes:** He conducted careful discussions regarding the meaning and nature of the attributes of the divine.

## Reason, Faith, and the Soul
Bonaventura viewed all sciences as handmaids to theology. While he acknowledged that reason could discover some moral truths that form the groundwork of the Christian system, he argued that other truths can only be apprehended and received through divine illumination.

To achieve this illumination, the soul must utilize specific means:
1.  **Prayer**
2.  **Exercise of the virtues**, which makes the soul fit to accept divine light.
3.  **Meditation**, which can lead to an ecstatic union with God.

The supreme end of life is defined as this union—an intense absorbing love and contemplation in intellect. Bonaventura taught that this union cannot be fully reached during this life and remains a hope for the futurity. In the process of contemplating God, the mind moves through distinct stages or grades, beginning with the senses, which provide empirical knowledge of the external world and discern the traces (*vestigia*).
