# Divine Qualities: Eternity, Truth, and Goodness

In the study of divinity across various philosophical and theological traditions, the attributes of eternity, truth, and goodness are frequently examined as central qualities of the divine nature. These concepts are often analyzed in relation to the nature of the soul, the structure of the universe, and the relationship between reason and faith.

## Eternity and the Divine Nature
Eternity is consistently presented as a fundamental attribute of the deity. In certain philosophical frameworks, God is described as a being who is eternal and immutable. This quality of being eternal and unchangeable is a primary reason why humans regard the gods with admiration. Other perspectives define God not as an abstract substance, but as an everlasting process or activity. In this view, the self-generation of God involves an immanent process of self-consciousness, which is mirrored through the eternal and impersonal wisdom of God.

The concept of eternity also intersects with the nature of time and existence. Some arguments suggest that for the eternal to be complete, it must involve the reality of existence as a process; without this process, the eternal is not truly eternal. Conversely, some theological debates have focused on whether the world itself is eternal. For instance, Bonaventura vigorously combated the doctrine of the eternity of the world, contrasting his views with those of Aristotle.

## Truth and Divine Intelligence
Truth is often linked to the divine mind or intelligence. In the Platonizing mode of speculation, ideas are not found in the nature of things but exist as thoughts of the divine mind, serving as the blueprints according to which actual things were formed. In this context, the divine intelligence exists with God from all eternity as an aggregate of ideas.

Different philosophical schools diverge on how truth relates to the divine will. One perspective suggests that truth and good originated in a transcendent act of will. In this view, God's will is conceived as absolutely arbitrary, meaning that even necessary truths of reason spring from God's determination rather than preceding it. Consequently, the divine intelligence is seen as determining truths—such as the fact that two and two make four—by an arbitrary act. Under this framework, truth is not conceived as flowing from God like rays from the sun, but is instead something given as an external limit to the finite nature of man.

## Goodness and Divine Virtue
Goodness is frequently cited as the most glorious and divine of all qualities. While immortality and power are attributed to the gods, these are also found in nature; for example, empty space possesses immortality, and elements like thunderbolts or rushing waters possess great power. However, justice, equity, and goodness are regarded as belonging to the gods alone because of their reason and intelligence.

The relationship between divine goodness and human virtue is a point of significant reflection. It is noted that when a human being possesses great power and authority and acts justly, they appear like a god; conversely, if they do not act justly, they appear like a wild beast. In some systems, "Good" is described as the final end of the world, possessing being only while it constantly produces itself.

## The Trinity and Divine Attributes
The concept of a trinity has been applied to divine substances and attributes in various ways. Some hypotheses suggest the Divine Essence is composed of three coequal and coeternal beings—three distinct and infinite minds or substances. Other interpretations, specifically regarding Plato, argue that there is no trinity of substances, but rather that the "three pretended principles" are actually attributes of the Deity or of matter.

In the context of the divine image within humans, the ability to reserve assent for the clear and distinct perceptions of intelligence is seen as a way for finite beings to re-enact the divine law, repeating the act of will in which truth and good had their origin.
