# Bridge Construction Materials and Evolution

The construction of bridges has evolved from the use of basic natural materials in classical and medieval times to the application of advanced metals and reinforced concrete in the modern era. This progression has allowed for a significant increase in the length of spans, moving from modest distances to achievements between 500 and 1800 feet.

## Classical and Medieval Construction
In classical and medieval periods, bridges were primarily constructed using timber or masonry. Later developments in these eras introduced the use of brick and concrete. Roman engineering provides several early examples of these methods:
*   **Timber:** The Pons Sublicius, the first known bridge at Rome over the Tiber, was built of timber.
*   **Masonry and Stone:** The Pons Milvius was reconstructed in stone in 109 B.C., with arches spanning between 51 and 79 feet. Other intact or partially intact masonry structures include the Pons Fabricius (c. 62 B.C.) and the Pons Cestius (c. 46 B.C.). The Pons Aelius, built by Hadrian in A.D. 134, featured eight arches with a maximum span of 62 feet.
*   **Stone Blocks:** The bridge of Alcantara in Spain, constructed by Trajan, reached a total length of 670 feet and stood 210 feet above the stream; it was built using stone blocks without the use of cement.
*   **Composite and Large Scale:** Trajan is also credited with a bridge over the Danube (A.D. 104) that may have been 3000 to 4000 feet long, featuring masonry piers and timber arches. Additionally, Roman aqueducts dating from 300 B.C. onwards utilized arched bridge structures generally made of concrete and brick.

In Great Britain, some of the earliest stone bridges are the cyclopean bridges on Dartmoor. These consist of stone slabs bridging stone piers; for example, the bridge over the East Dart near Tavistock used slabs measuring 15 feet by 6 feet and is reputed to have lasted 2000 years.

## Timber Bridges and Early Modernity
Timber continued to be a primary material into the 18th and 19th centuries. In the mid-18th century, the brothers Grubenmann erected a timber bridge at Schaffhausen with spans of 172 and 193 feet. Their Wittingen bridge, with a span of 390 feet, is likely the longest timber span ever constructed.

In America, large-span timber bridges were common between the late 18th and mid-19th centuries. Notable examples include the Bellows Falls bridge (1785-1792) with two spans of 184 feet, and the Colossus bridge (1812), a flat arched truss with a 340-foot span. By 1840, American engineers adopted trusses made chiefly of timber but reinforced with cast-iron shoes and wrought-iron tension-rods. Examples include the 1830 Howe truss (timber chords and struts with vertical iron ties) and the 1844 Pratt truss (vertical struts and inclined ties). These were generally limited to 150-foot spans until the Howe truss was stiffened with timber arches to accommodate heavier railway loads.

In England, early railways—specifically the Manchester, Sheffield & Lincolnshire and the Great Western—used laminated arches or braced trusses of timber, though most were eventually replaced due to decay at the joints.

## Transition to Metals
The late 18th century marked the introduction of wrought iron, initially used in combination with cast iron or timber. Cast iron was employed for arches and early railway bridge girders, though its modern use is limited to arched bridges of moderate span.

Wrought iron became prominent in the early 19th century for suspension road bridges. By the middle of the 19th century, wrought iron was used exclusively for massive girder bridges, such as those at Saltash and over the Menai Strait. From 1880 onwards, mild steel—which possesses greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron—superseded it for all girders with a span exceeding 100 feet.

## Modern Specialized Structures
Recent advancements include the use of ferro-concrete, armoured concrete, or concrete strengthened with steel bars for arched bridges. Other specialized designs include:
*   **Suspension Bridges:** The Niagara bridge (1852-1855), designed by J. A. Roebling, featured a span of 821 feet and four cables, each containing 3640 parallel wires. Its original stiffening girder was a box-shaped braced timber structure 18 feet deep and 25 feet wide.
*   **Combination Bridges:** These use timber for compression members and steel for tension members. An example is the cantilever bridge at Roseburgh, Oregon, which features a total distance of 584 feet between anchor piers.

## Erection Methods
The construction of these bridges involves several methods of erection:
1.  **Staging or Falsework:** Using timber piles, earth banks, or iron staging. For instance, the Kuilenburg bridge in Holland used a timber staging containing 81,000 cubic feet of timber to support a 2150-ton superstructure.
2.  **Floating:** Floating the structure to the site and raising it.
3.  **Rolling:** Putting girders together on an embankment and rolling them over piers using rollers, levers, and ratchets.
4.  **Member-by-Member:** Building out the bridge where the completed section serves as the stage for the next addition.
