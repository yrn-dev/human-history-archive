# Königinhof

Königinhof, known in Czech as Dvur Kralove, is a city of very ancient origin located in north-eastern Bohemia. It is situated on the left bank of the Elbe, approximately 160 kilometres from Prague. The city serves as the seat of a provincial law-court and a provincial district. Its primary industries include cotton-weaving, corn-milling, and brewing. The population of the city is approximately 11,000.

## History and Foundation
The city was founded by King Wenceslaus II of Bohemia, who reigned from 1278 to 1305. He gave the city to his wife, Elizabeth, which led to the name Dvur Kralove, meaning "the court of the queen." Throughout history, the town was a site of conflict during the Hussite wars, where it was taken and retaken several times by opposing parties. Later, on June 29, 1866, a battle took place partly within the streets of the town, resulting in the total defeat of the Austrian army by the Prussians.

## The Königinhof Manuscript
In the 19th century, Königinhof became widely recognized as the location where a manuscript (MS.) was discovered that was long believed to be among the oldest written documents in the Czech language. In 1817, Wenceslas Hanka—who later served as the librarian of the Bohemian museum for a long period—claimed to have found the manuscript in the town's church tower during a visit. The document, known as the Kralodvorsky Rukopis (manuscript of Königinhof), contained lyric and epic poems.

Despite the initial discovery, the genuineness of the manuscript was contested. Dobrovsky, the most prominent Czech philologist of that era, expressed suspicions from the beginning. Nevertheless, the manuscript was accepted as genuine for a long time, leading to it being translated into most European languages and frequently printed.

## Proof of Forgery
Doubts regarding the document persisted and grew stronger after Wenceslas Hanka was convicted of fabricating other false Bohemian documents. Eventually, the manuscript was proven to be a forgery through a series of articles and works written by Professors Masoryk, Gebauer, and Goll. Consequently, almost no Bohemian scholars today believe the document is genuine. It is now categorized alongside the so-called MSS. of Zelena Hora (Gruneberg) as being almost certainly forgeries from the early part of the 19th century.

## Context of Early Bohemian Literature
While the Königinhof manuscript is considered a forgery, the archive records other genuine ancient documents of the Bohemian language. These include several legends—most notably those of St Dorothy and St Catherine—and several hymns. Additionally, ancient epic fragments have been preserved, including *Tandarias a Floribella* and the *Alexandreis*. Since the 19th-century revival of Bohemian literature, these writings have been printed. 

Another document of significant historical value is the rhymed chronicle often wrongly called the chronicle of Dalimil. The author of this chronicle likely lived during the reign of King John (1310-1346) and recorded Bohemian history from its earliest period up to the reign of King Henry of Carinthia, who was John's immediate predecessor. This chronicle is characterized by a strong feeling of racial antipathy toward the Germans.
