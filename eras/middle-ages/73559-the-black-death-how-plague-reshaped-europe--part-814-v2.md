# The Black Death: The Silent Revolution of the Vernacular

While previous installments in this series have examined the demographic collapse and the subsequent rise of wages for the peasantry, we must turn our attention to a more subtle, yet permanent, transformation: the linguistic shift of Europe. The Great Mortality did more than kill people; it killed the undisputed hegemony of Latin as the sole language of administration, law, and literature.

For centuries, Latin was the *lingua franca* of the educated elite, the Church, and the state. However, the plague created a vacuum of expertise that forced a pragmatic pivot toward the "vernacular"—the native languages spoken by the common people, such as Middle English, Old French, and Tuscan.

## The Collapse of the Clerical Monopoly

The plague struck the clergy with devastating precision. Because priests were required to administer last rites to the dying, they were exposed to the *Yersinia pestis* bacterium at far higher rates than the general population. This resulted in a catastrophic loss of Latin-literate professionals.

To fill these gaps, the Church was forced to ordain new priests rapidly. Many of these recruits were less educated and lacked a deep mastery of Latin. Consequently, religious instruction and local administration began to slip into the local dialects. This shift democratized information; when the language of the law and the spirit became accessible to the layperson, the psychological grip of the institutional elite began to loosen.

## The Rise of National Literatures

The mid-to-late 14th century saw a surge in writing that prioritized the spoken word over the scholarly tongue. In England, the death of Latin-trained scribes coincided with the rise of writers like Geoffrey Chaucer, who chose to write *The Canterbury Tales* in Middle English. This was a revolutionary act, signaling that the common tongue was capable of the same poetic complexity and intellectual depth as Latin.

Similarly, in Italy, the influence of Dante and Boccaccio—whose *Decameron* is set explicitly against the backdrop of the plague—helped solidify the Tuscan dialect as the standard for Italian literature. By validating the vernacular, the post-plague era laid the groundwork for the Renaissance and the eventual rise of national identities.

## Scholarly Debate: Cause or Catalyst?

Historians continue to debate whether the plague *caused* this linguistic shift or merely accelerated a process already in motion. Some argue that the vernacular was already gaining ground in courtly love poetry and trade. However, most agree that the sheer scale of the clerical deaths provided the necessary "shock" to break the Latin monopoly, turning a slow trend into a societal landslide.

### Key Facts
* **Clerical Mortality:** In some dioceses, up to 40-50% of the clergy perished, leading to a shortage of Latin speakers.
* **The Decameron:** Written by Giovanni Boccaccio, this work is one of the first major literary pieces to use the vernacular to describe the plague's social impact.
* **Administrative Shift:** Local courts began recording proceedings in native languages to accommodate the lack of trained Latin notaries.
* **Educational Reform:** The plague led to the founding of new universities that, while still using Latin for teaching, began to incorporate local languages in practical instruction.

### Did You Know?
* **English Ascendance:** Before the plague, French was the language of the English nobility; the loss of French-speaking elites helped English emerge as the dominant language of England.
* **The Printing Press Link:** The shift toward vernacular languages created the market demand that would later make Gutenberg’s printing press a commercial success.
* **Lay Literacy:** The shortage of priests encouraged the growth of "lay literacy," where merchants and artisans learned to read and write in their own tongues for business purposes.
