# Dempster

Dempster was a scholar noted for his versatility and an extraordinary memory, which allowed him to work across multiple disciplines, including history, biography, law, criticism, and philology. While he achieved a great position in the history of scholarship, his work is characterized by certain defects. His writing style is described as often barbarous, and his output was impacted by restlessness and impetuosity. Furthermore, a sense of personal and patriotic vanity led him to engage in "absurd exaggerations" regarding Scottish questions and a "habit of romancing" when documenting his own life.

## Major Works and Contributions

Dempster's most well-known work is the *Historia ecclesiastica gentis Scotorum*, published in Bologna in 1627. In this text, he attempted to prove that several figures—specifically Alcuin, Boniface, Joannes Scotus Erigena, and Bernard (Sapiens)—were Scots. His claims in this work extended even to Boadicea, whom he identified as a Scottish author.

Despite the criticisms directed at his historical claims regarding Scotland, his work on antiquarian subjects is viewed differently. His edition of Benedetto Accolti's *De bello a Christianis contra barbaros*, published in 1623, is noted for having great merits. Additionally, some of his Latin verse was included in the first volume of *Delitiae poëtarum Scotorum*, published in Amsterdam in 1637.

## Relationship with Robert Balfour

Dempster was a contemporary of the Scottish philosopher Robert Balfour (c. 1550–1625), who was educated at the university of Paris and St Andrews and served as the principal of Guienne College at Bordeaux. Balfour was a mathematician and a philosopher skilled in Latin and Greek, contributing to the European reputation of the *praefervidum ingenium Scotorum*. Dempster held Balfour in high regard, describing him as a "philosopher profoundly skilled in the Greek and Latin languages" and the "phoenix of his age," further stating that he was a mathematician who could be compared with the ancients.
