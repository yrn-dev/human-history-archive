# England

## Early Inhabitants and Tribal Movements
The early history of the region is marked by the presence of native tribes and subsequent migrations. In 54 B.C., during Julius Caesar's second expedition, the British chieftain Cassivelaunus (also known as Cassivellaunus), who ruled the territory north of the Thames, led native tribes against the Roman forces. Following several indecisive engagements, Caesar captured the camp of Cassivelaunus. Although peace was established on the condition of providing hostages and paying tribute, the tribute was never paid. Bede notes in his *Hist. Eccl.* that the remains of the entrenchments of Cassivelaunus remained visible seven or eight centuries later.

Later periods saw the arrival and intermixture of various German tribes. Bede enumerates these tribes, including the Anglo-Saxons, and some sources suggest the Frisians were also mingled among them. The Anglo-Saxons are noted for a persistent practice of publicly selling their youths in the market of Rome. However, according to the laws of Ina, such individuals could not be lawfully sold beyond the seas.

## Political Structure and Conquest
The political landscape of early England was characterized by various kingdoms. While the term "Heptarchy" is used, some historians reject it on the basis that seven independent kingdoms never existed simultaneously. Bede, however, enumerates seven kings—comprising four Angles, two Saxons, and one Jute—who achieved an indefinite supremacy of power and renown. This supremacy was the result of conquest rather than law. Examples of this expansion include one king who subdued Anglesey and the Isles of Man, and another who imposed tribute upon the Picts and the Scots.

## Christianization and Papal Relations
The christianizing of England involved significant interaction with the papacy. Pope Boniface V (619–625) contributed to these efforts, writing encouraging letters to Justus, the bishop of Rochester, and Mellitus, the archbishop of Canterbury. Bede quotes three of these letters: one to Justus, and others to Aethelberga and King Eadwin of Northumbria. A letter from 625 provided by William of Malmesbury further establishes Canterbury as the permanent metropolitan see of Britain.

Pope Honorius I (625–638) continued the work of Gregory the Great in England. Bede records a letter from Honorius to King Edwin of Northumbria, urging the king to study the writings of Gregory. At the request of Edwin, Honorius conferred the pallium upon the bishops of York and Canterbury. Additionally, Honorius commissioned Birinus to preach Christianity in Wessex and admonished the Irish for failing to follow the Catholic Church's customs regarding the celebration of Easter.

## Vernacular Bible and Literary Development
The history of the English vernacular Bible is divided into two periods: Manuscript Bibles, translated from the Latin Vulgate, and Printed Bibles, translated from the original Greek and Hebrew. 

Early biblical translations often took a poetical form. In the 7th century, Bede testifies that Caedmon sang about the history of Genesis, the creation of the world, the origin of the human race, the entrance into the promised land and the egress of the sons of Israel from Egypt, the incarnation, passion, resurrection, and ascension of the Lord, the doctrine of the apostles, and the advent of the Holy Spirit. It remains doubtful, however, if the poetry attributed to Caedmon is his genuine work.

The first prose rendering of the Bible likely appeared in the 8th century. Bede translated the first portion of the Gospel of St John (chapters i through vi. 9) into the vernacular for the benefit of the church of God, though no part of this translation survives. 

By the 9th and 10th centuries, the period was characterized by independent translation attempts and interlinear glosses on the Book of Psalms. Twelve manuscripts of "glossed Psalters" are known, divided into those representing the Gallican Psalter (such as the Lambeth 48 and Salisbury Cath. 150) and the Roman Psalter (including Eadwine's Psalter and the Vespasian Psalter).
