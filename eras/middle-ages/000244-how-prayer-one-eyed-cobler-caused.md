# THE PRAYER OF THE ONE-EYED COBLER AND THE MOVING MOUNTAIN

The account of the one-eyed cobler and the subsequent movement of a mountain is a narrative found within the records of the travels of Marco Polo. The event is centered on a conflict between a Caliph and the Christian population in his land, culminating in a miracle attributed to the prayers of a virtuous tradesman.

## The Character of the Cobler
The central figure of the narrative is a Christian cobler known for his exceptional holiness, justice, and righteousness. He led a life characterized by chastity and uprightness, abstaining from sin and fasting. His daily routine included attending church to hear Mass and donating a portion of his daily earnings to God.

The cobler was one-eyed due to a self-imposed penance. While taking the measure of a woman's foot and leg for a pair of shoes, he became conscious of sinful thoughts. Recalling the teaching of the Holy Evangel—which states that if an eye offends, one should pluck it out and cast it away rather than sin—he used his stitching awl to destroy his own eye immediately after the woman departed.

## The Vision and the Request
The events leading to the miracle began when a group of Christians, consisting of both men and women, gathered in prayer for eight days and eight nights. During this time, a Holy Angel of Heaven appeared in a vision to a Bishop, who was described as a very good Christian. The angel revealed that the Bishop should ask the one-eyed cobler to pray to God, as God would grant the prayer because of the cobler's holy life.

When the Bishop and the Christians approached the cobler with this request, the cobler initially made many excuses, claiming he was not as good a man as they believed. However, the Christians persisted with such sweetness that he eventually agreed to pray for them.

## The Confrontation with the Caliph
The miracle took place against a backdrop of religious tension. The Caliph had expressed a desire to slay all the Christians in his land, leaving the community in great dismay. On the appointed day, more than 100,000 Christians—men, women, small and great—gathered for Holy Mass. Following the service, they formed a great procession, carrying a precious cross and singing and weeping as they moved toward a plain in front of a mountain.

At this location, they were met by the Caliph and his armed Saracen host. The Saracens, who did not believe God would grant such a favor to the Christians, stood ready to slay the procession if the Christians would not change their faith. Despite their fear and doubt, the Christians placed their hope in Jesus Christ.

## The Prayer and the Miracle
After receiving a benison from the Bishop, the cobler knelt before the Holy Cross and stretched his hands toward Heaven. He prayed to the Lord God Almighty, acknowledging himself as a servant full of sin and unworthy of the request, but beseeched God's power and mercy to grant grace to His people so that they would not perish and their faith would not be flouted, abused, or cast down.

The result of this prayer was the movement of the mountain. In a related account involving a figure named Bauduin, the Caliph is described as witnessing the event from his palace window. He exclaimed in alarm that the Mountain of Thir was being carried away by the "déable d’enfeir" (devil of hell) and was moving through the air toward the valley of Joaquin to the westward.

## Aftermath and Literary Influence
Following the miracle, the Caliph expressed vexation in his council, admitting he did not know how to disprove the faith of the Christians. 

The story of the virtuous cobler and the moving mountain also appears in later works, such as the 14th-century poem *Li Romans de Bauduin de Sebourc IIIᵉ Roy de Jhérusalem*. In this version, the cobler remains a part of the narrative but plays a secondary role to the hero, Bauduin.
