# Charlemagne

Charlemagne was a pivotal figure in European history who expanded the Frankish realm and revived the title of Latin Emperor. He began his reign in 768, following the death of his father, Pepin, who had previously extinguished the last descendants of Clovis to take the kingly state and title. Charlemagne's grandfather, Charles Martel, the Mayor of the Palace, had established the family's power as the practical overlord of Europe north of the Alps, stretching from Hungary to the Pyrenees, and had decisively defeated the Moslims at Poitiers in 732.

## Territorial Conquests and Administration
Under Charlemagne, the Frankish realm grew to a size that prompted the revival of the imperial title. His conquests included North Italy, and he established himself as the master of Rome. In 774, he conquered Lombardy, and by 776, he had reached Dalmatia. He also conquered the district of Carniola (a name derived from the Slavonic word *Krajina*, meaning frontier), the majority of which he bestowed upon the duke of Friuli.

In other regions, Charlemagne's influence altered local governance. In the city of Asculum, which had been the capital of Picenum, the period starting from Charlemagne saw the city come under the rule of its bishops. These bishops held the title of prince and possessed the right to coin money, a system that continued until 1185.

## Imperial Title and Religious Conflict
In the year 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne Emperor of the West. This revival of the empire sparked a divergence of views regarding the relationship between spiritual and temporal power. Charlemagne conceived of a Christian Empire maintained in its orthodoxy by a great Cæsar who would rule even the Pope. Conversely, the view held in Rome was that the Christian Cæsar must be guided and anointed by the Pope, who would possess the power to depose or excommunicate the emperor.

Charlemagne's own literary accomplishments were considered questionable, though it is known that he read, or had read to him, St. Augustine’s *City of God*.

## Succession and the Division of the Empire
Charlemagne died in 814 and was succeeded by his son, Louis the Pious. To prevent the clergy from claiming influence over the monarchy, Louis was commanded to take the crown from the altar and place it on his own head, signifying it as a gift from God, his father, and the nation. This established a lineal descent of the Carlovingian sceptre for four generations, reducing the role of the popes to the honor of crowning and anointing hereditary princes.

However, the empire began to undermine from the center after Charlemagne's death. Following a war that cost the lives of one hundred thousand Franks, the empire was divided by treaty among the three sons of Louis the Pious. This partition forever separated the kingdoms of France and Germany. The Imperial dignity of Lothaire included Italy and the provinces of Gaul between the Rhine, the Meuse, the Alps, and the Rhone. Later, the realm of Italy became the patrimony of Lewis the Second, the eldest son of Lothaire. Upon the death of Lewis the Second without a male heir, the throne became vacant and was disputed by cousins and uncles, providing an opportunity for the popes to judge the claims of the candidates.
