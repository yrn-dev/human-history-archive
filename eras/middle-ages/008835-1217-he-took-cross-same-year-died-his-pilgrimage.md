# Henry de Bohun

## Family Origins and Early History
The Bohun family was a prominent lineage in English history during the 13th and 14th centuries. The family name originated from a village located in the Cotentin, situated between the estuary of the Vire and Coutances. While the Bohuns arrived in England at or shortly after the Norman Conquest, the details of their early history in the country remain obscure.

The family's rise to prominence was established by Humphrey III, who served as a *dapifer* (steward) in the royal household during the later years of Henry I. Humphrey III married the daughter of Milo of Gloucester. During the reign of Stephen, he occupied an ambiguous position, initially siding with the king before shifting his support to the empress. Humphrey III remained loyal to Henry II throughout various changes and fought against the rebels of East Anglia at Farnham in 1173. He lived until 1187.

## Henry de Bohun and the Great Charter
Humphrey III was succeeded in the family estates by his grandson, Henry, after outliving his eldest son, Humphrey IV. Henry was the son of Margaret, a sister of William the Lion, which connected him to the royal house of Scotland. This alliance likely aided Henry in obtaining the earldom of Hereford from John in 1199.

Under Henry's leadership, the Bohuns became foremost among the Marcher barons, as the family lands were primarily located on the Welsh Marches. Henry de Bohun was one of the twenty-five barons elected by his peers to ensure the terms of the Great Charter were enforced, appearing alongside the earls of Gloucester and Clare.

## Civil War and Pilgrimage
During the subsequent civil war, Henry de Bohun fought on the side of Louis. This conflict led to his capture at the battle of Lincoln in 1217. In that same year, Henry took the cross. He died on June 1, 1220, while on his pilgrimage.

## Succession of Humphrey V
Henry was succeeded by his son and heir, Humphrey V. Unlike his father, Humphrey V initially returned to a path of loyalty. Before 1239, he was permitted to inherit the earldom of Essex from his maternal uncle, William de Mandeville. However, by 1258, Humphrey V shifted his allegiance from the royal cause to the baronial cause. During the Oxford parliament of 1258, he served as a nominee of the opposition on a committee of twenty-four tasked with reforming the administration. Humphrey V eventually returned to his allegiance following the alliance between Montfort and Llewelyn of North Wales.
