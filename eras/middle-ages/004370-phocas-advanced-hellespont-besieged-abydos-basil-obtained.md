# The Hellespont and Abydos in Classical and Medieval History

The Hellespont, a strategic waterway, served as a focal point for numerous military engagements and political shifts across different eras, particularly involving the city of Abydos and the struggle for control between various powers.

## Ancient Conflicts and the Role of Abydos

In the early histories of the region, Abydos was a critical point of passage. During the expeditions of the Hellenes from Mycale to the Hellespont, forces led by Leotychides and the Athenian commander Xanthippos arrived at Abydos to find that the bridges they expected to find stretched across the water had been broken up. While the Peloponnesians decided to return to Hellas, the Athenians remained, crossing from Abydos to the Chersonese to begin a siege of Sestos. Sestos served as a major stronghold where Persians and their allies gathered, including Oiobazos of Cardia, who had brought the ropes for the bridges to the town.

During the Peloponnesian War, Abydos frequently shifted its allegiance. The city, which shared colonial ties with Milêtus, revolted from Athens in response to the arrival of the Spartan Derkyllidas, who had traveled by land from Miletus to join Pharnabazus. This revolt was followed two days later by the neighboring town of Lampsakus. In response, the Athenian Strombichidês sailed from Chios with twenty-four ships and transports of heavy infantry. After capturing Lampsacus, Strombichidês attempted to take Abydos, but the inhabitants refused to capitulate. Following failed assaults, he established Sestos as the center for the defense of the Hellespont.

## Naval Maneuvers and the Peloponnesian Fleet

The waters around Abydos were the site of complex naval strategies involving the Spartan admiral Mindarus. Mindarus sought to bring his fleet to the Hellespont while avoiding a general battle with the Athenian Thrasyllus. To achieve this, he utilized a system of secrecy and conditional orders sent to the Peloponnesian squadron guarding the Hellespont at Abydos. These orders instructed the squadron to prioritize the safe arrival of the main fleet, even if it meant leaving the Hellespont unguarded for a time.

This strategic reserve at Abydos had significant consequences. When Athenian triremes at Sestos detected signals of Mindarus's approach, they fled in the middle of the night toward Elæûs. Because the Peloponnesian guardships at Abydos were holding themselves in reserve to assist Mindarus, they neglected the escaping Athenian squadron.

Later, a major engagement occurred near the headland of Kynossêma, opposite Abydos. Mindarus deployed eighty-six triremes extending from Abydos to Dardanus on the Asiatic shore. The Athenian fleet, led by Thrasyllus and Thrasybulus, faced them along the European coast. Although Mindarus's maritime hoplites were considered superior, the Athenians possessed more skillful pilots. The battle saw the Athenian center weakened and driven against the land, but the wings under Thrasyllus and Thrasybulus managed to frustrate Mindarus's attempts to outflank them.

## Shift in Power and the Siege of Kyzikus

By the middle of one winter, the balance of power shifted as the fleet of Mindarus at Abydos gained superiority over the Athenian fleet at Sestos. This was due to reinforcements for the Peloponnesians and the dispersion of the Athenians, who suffered from a lack of pay. Consequently, the Athenians abandoned their position in the Hellespont and took station at Kardia.

The situation changed again when the Athenian fleet concentrated under the leadership of Theramenês, Thrasybulus, and Alkibiadês, who had escaped from Sardis. This concentration rendered the Athenian fleet superior in number to that of Mindarus. Shortly thereafter, Mindarus moved his fleet from the Hellespont to Kyzikus, where he engaged in a siege of the city in coordination with Pharnabazus and Persian land forces.

## Medieval Political Unrest

In a later era, the name Phocas appears in the context of the Roman Empire in the East. Basil II, known as the "slayer of Bulgarians" (Bulgaroktonos), was crowned emperor on April 22, 960. For a period following the death of his father, Romanus II, in 963, Basil and his brother Constantine were nominal emperors while their stepfather, Nicephorus Phocas, and later John Tzimisces, held actual power.

The throne faced threats from the landed aristocracy of Asia Minor. One such threat was Bardas Sclerus, whose rebellion was suppressed in 979 by another general, Bardas Phocas. Bardas Phocas had previously been interned in a monastery but was recalled to oppose Sclerus. Phocas continued as the general in the East until 987, at which point he rebelled and was proclaimed emperor by his troops. This rebellion was fueled by dissatisfaction with the energy displayed by Emperor Basil II.
