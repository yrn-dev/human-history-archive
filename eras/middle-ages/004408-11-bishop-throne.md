# Bishop's Throne

In the context of early Christian ecclesiastical architecture and governance, the bishop's throne serves as both a physical seat of authority within a basilica and a symbolic representation of the episcopal office (the "see").

## Architectural Placement and Design
In early basilicas, the bishop's throne was typically situated within the apse. In the Basilica of S. Clemente in Rome, the throne is identified as a specific feature of the church's layout. At the cathedral on the island of Torcello near Venice—originally constructed in the 7th century and repaired around A.D. 1000—the bishop's throne occupies the center of the arc and is accessed via a steep flight of steps. This arrangement is complemented by six rows of stone benches for presbyters that follow the curve of the apse on either side, resembling theater seating. The design at Torcello is described as singularly plain and almost rude.

Similarly, the basilica of Parenzo in Istria, dating to approximately A.D. 542, retains a bishop's throne and a bench for presbyters that appear unaltered. In this smaller structure, the apse walls are inlaid with rich marble and mother-of-pearl.

## The Throne as a Symbol of Office
The term "throne" is frequently used in historical records to denote the episcopal seat or the position of power held by a bishop. The occupation of this "throne" was often a point of intense religious and political contention:

*   **Alexandria:** Athanasius experienced multiple periods of exile and return to his see. After the death of Constantius, he returned in February 362 and "once more seated himself upon his throne." He was later driven from power again by an imperial mandate, eventually returning for the final time in February 366.
*   **Milan:** During the reign of Constantine, the orthodox bishops who resisted were deposed, and Auxentius of Cappadocia was installed into the seat of Dionysius, the bishop of Milan.
*   **Carthage:** The church of Carthage, noted as the second of the ecclesiastical thrones of the West in rank and opulence, suffered a double election that led to a long-standing division between the factions of Cæcilian and Majorinus (and later Donatus).
*   **Antioch and Alexandria:** In a dispute involving the Eusebians, the council of Antioch confirmed the degradation of Athanasius, and a stranger named Gregory was seated on his throne.

## Relation to Imperial and Secular Power
The relationship between the episcopal throne and the imperial throne varied by region and era. In the East, the patriarch of Constantinople is described as a "domestic slave" to the emperor, moving between the convent and the throne at the master's nod. In contrast, the bishops of the West, particularly the Roman bishop, operated from a more independent throne. The pope in Rome acted as the head of the Christians, and his dictates were followed by figures such as Placidia and her son Valentinian.

In the provincial assemblies and annual synods of the Spanish clergy, bishops sought to "exalt the mitre above the crown." In the national councils of Toledo, episcopal policy guided the "free spirit of the Barbarians." In these instances, the bishops and palatines were responsible for filling vacancies of the regal throne. Furthermore, bishops and palatines held a fundamental privilege protecting them from being imprisoned, tortured, exiled, or punished with death unless judged by their peers.
