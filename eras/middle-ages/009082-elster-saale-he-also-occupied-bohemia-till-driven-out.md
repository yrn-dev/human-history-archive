# Boleslaus I and the Expansion to the Elster and Saale

## Rise of Boleslaus I
Boleslaus I, known as "The Great," was the king of Poland who died in 1025. He was the son of Mieszko, the first Christian prince of Poland, and the Bohemian princess Bona (also called Dobrawa). The court had been converted from paganism to Catholicism by Bona's chaplain, Jordan. Boleslaus succeeded his father in 992 and utilized his skills as a warrior to transform the struggling Polish principality on the Vistula into a great power.

Boleslaus is characterized as a man of insight and progress who recognized the essential superiority of Christianity. He demonstrated this by associating himself with the new faith and showing sagacity in receiving Adalbert, the bishop of Prague, who was a fugitive. Following Adalbert's martyrdom at the hands of pagan Slavs on April 23, 997, Boleslaus purchased the saint's relics and placed them in the church of Gnesen. This church, founded by his father, subsequently became the metropolitan see of Poland.

## Royal Coronation and Imperial Relations
In the year 1000, Boleslaus hosted Emperor Otto III at Gnesen with such magnificence that the emperor declared him too worthy to be merely a *princeps*. Consequently, Otto III conferred the royal crown upon him. Despite this, Boleslaus felt it necessary to crown himself king a second time in the final year of his life, twenty-five years later.

## Military Campaigns and Territorial Expansion
Following the death of Otto III, Boleslaus launched an invasion of Germany. His forces penetrated as far as the Elbe, and during this advance, he occupied Meissen and Stralsund. He further extended his dominions to reach the Saale and the Elster. 

In addition to these gains, Boleslaus took advantage of internal troubles in Bohemia to occupy the city of Cracow, which had previously been a Czech city. He also occupied Bohemia itself, though he was eventually driven out of the region in 1004 by Emperor Henry IV.

The conflict with Germany concluded in 1018 with the peace of Bautzen. This agreement was highly advantageous to Boleslaus, as it allowed him to retain Lusatia. Following this peace, Boleslaus directed his military efforts against Jaroslav, the grand duke of Kiev. Boleslaus routed Jaroslav's forces on the banks of the Bug, which served as the boundary between Poland and Russia at that time.

## Geography of the Region
The territories mentioned in Boleslaus's expansions include Lusatia, a name derived from the Lusitzi, a Slav tribe that inhabited Lower Lusatia during the 9th and 10th centuries. In the earliest times, Lower Lusatia extended from the Spree to the Black Elster. The region was conquered in the 10th century by the margrave Gero and the German king Henry the Fowler. For approximately three centuries, the land was organized as a separate march that was occasionally independent of, or attached to, the margraviate of Meissen.
