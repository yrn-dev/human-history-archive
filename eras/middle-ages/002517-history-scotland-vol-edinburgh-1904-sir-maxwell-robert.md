# History of Scotland

The historical record of Scotland is documented through a wide array of primary sources, genealogical studies, and comprehensive histories. These records cover the nation's royal successions, the influence of powerful noble houses, the development of its financial systems, and the lives of its notable figures.

## Royal Succession and the Maid of Norway
A significant period of instability in the Scottish crown occurred in the late 13th century. In 1284, the estates of Scotland determined that should King Alexander III die without children, the crown would pass to his granddaughter, Margaret. Following the death of Alexander in March 1286, Margaret became the titular queen of Scotland. Known generally as the "maid of Norway," she was the daughter of Margaret (daughter of Alexander III) and Eric II, the king of Norway.

During this time, King Edward I of England closely monitored Scottish affairs. In 1289, a marriage was arranged between the infant queen and Edward's son, who later became Edward II. However, Margaret died in the Orkneys around the end of September 1290 while sailing from Norway; this event was officially communicated in a letter dated October 7, 1290. Her death was followed by a period of mystery; approximately 1300, a woman from Leipzig claimed to be Queen Margaret, though this individual was later burned as a witch at Bergen.

## The House of Douglas and the Peerage
The House of Douglas and Angus represents a major lineage in Scottish history. David Hume of Godscroft, who served as secretary to Archibald Douglas, the 8th earl of Angus, authored a history of the house and race of Douglas and Angus, printed in 1644. Other modern authorities on this lineage include Sir H. Maxwell and Sir William Fraser.

The history of the peerage is marked by complex successions and disputes. For example, the title of earl of March was conferred in 1697 upon William Douglas, the second son of William, 1st duke of Queensberry. The title later passed to his grandson, William, the 3rd earl of March, who became the 4th duke of Queensberry in 1778. Upon the 4th duke's death without surviving male issue in 1810, the titles were divided among different branches of the house of Douglas. While some authorities, such as Sir Bernard Burke, state the earldom of March devolved upon Francis, 8th earl of Wemyss, other records, specifically those of Sir Robert Douglas in *The Peerage of Scotland*, suggest the earldom became extinct in 1810 because Francis was not an heir of the body of the 1st earl of March.

Other notable noble transitions include the lineage of the earls of Morton. James Douglas, 3rd Lord Dalkeith, became the earl of Morton in 1458 through his marriage to Lady Joan Stewart, the third daughter of James I. Later, Elizabeth, a daughter of the 3rd earl of Morton, married James Douglas in 1543, who then became the 4th earl of Morton.

## Regional History and Banking
The southern regions of Scotland, particularly Galloway, have been the subject of extensive descriptive and literary histories. Key works on the area include the *History of Dumfries and Galloway* by Sir Herbert Maxwell and *A Large Description of Galloway* by Rev. Andrew Symson.

The financial history of Scotland is documented through studies of its banking systems and the "Scottish Banker." Key historians of this sector include A. W. Kerr, who wrote on Scottish banking between 1865 and 1896, and Robert Somers, who analyzed the Scotch banks and their system of issue. Additionally, the history of banking in specific cities, such as Dundee, has been recorded in works like *A Century of Banking in Dundee*.

## Notable Figures and Cultural History
Scottish history is also defined by its cultural figures, most notably the poet Robert Burns. The primary authorities for the life of Burns are his own letters, as well as biographies written by J. Gibson Lockhart, Prof. J. Campbell Shairp, and Sir Leslie Stephen. Various essays on Burns have been contributed by figures such as Thomas Carlyle, R.L. Stevenson, and Lord Rosebery.

Other figures of historical interest include Sir W. Kirkaldy, whose adventures and memoirs have been documented by J. Grant and L. A. Barbé, and St Kentigern, whose "Life" is recorded in fragments used by John of Fordun and a complete version by Jocelyn of Furness.
