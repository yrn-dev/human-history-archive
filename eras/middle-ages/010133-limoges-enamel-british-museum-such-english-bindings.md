# Limoges Enamel and English Bindings in the British Museum

## Limoges Enamel Bindings
Within the British Museum, the archive records the presence of magnificent book bindings from the 14th century featuring Limoges enamel. These works represent a period between the 7th and 16th centuries when illuminated manuscripts were held in the highest esteem. During this era, the exquisite nature of the calligraphy and miniature paintings found inside manuscripts prompted a corresponding level of gorgeousness for their exterior coverings. This resulted in the creation of splendid bindings utilizing materials such as carved ivory, jewels, enamels, and metals.

## English Bindings and Their Disappearance
While the British Museum holds the aforementioned Limoges enamel examples, the archive notes a significant absence of similar intrinsically precious English bindings. It is believed that any such English bindings that may have existed have entirely disappeared. The most likely cause for this loss is that they were melted up during the reigns of Edward VI or Henry VIII. 

In contrast to these lost precious metal works, other early English examples survive elsewhere. For instance, at Stonyhurst, there is a volume known as *St Cuthbert's Gospels*, which dates probably to the 7th or 8th century and is bound in red leather featuring a repousse design.

## Tudor Period Enamels and Materials
During the Tudor period, several small bindings made of gold and ornamented with enamels were produced. These surviving items are described as charming little jewels and were characterized by the inclusion of a ring at the top, intended for attachment to a girdle.

In the 16th century and late 15th century in England, another trend emerged where fine printed books were bound in satin and velvet. These bindings were sometimes embroidered or set with enamels. Because these books utilized strong threads of metal, they have endured better than expected. Some of these decorative works made for Henry VIII remain in excellent condition.

## Evolution of English Royal Bindings
The practice of ornamenting royal English books with heraldic designs is considered to have begun during the reign of Edward IV. This fashion continued without interruption. While initially reserved for royal volumes, the trend of using heraldic designs in books belonging to private owners began during the later Tudor period and has continued in various degrees since that time.
