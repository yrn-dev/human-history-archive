# THE GREAT FESTIVAL OF THE NEW YEAR

## Overview of the New Year Festival
The Great Kaan celebrates a significant festival on New Year's Day, which is referred to as the White Feast. According to the archive's records, the beginning of the New Year occurs in the month of February. On this occasion, the Great Kaan and all of his subjects observe a feast.

## The Chinese Calendar
The timing of the New Year is governed by the Chinese year, which typically consists of twelve months. However, in seven out of every nineteen years, there are thirteen months. These thirteen-month periods are known as intercalary years. 

The structure of the calendar involves a combination of "long" months consisting of 30 days and "short" months consisting of 29 days. Specifically, an interval of 10 years comprises 235 lunar months, generally divided into 125 long months and 110 short months, though this may vary slightly to 124 long and 111 short months, alongside 7 intercalary months.

## Comparison with Other Court Festivals
The New Year's Day celebration is one of the most prominent events at the Kaan's court. While the Tartars hold high festivals yearly on their birthdays—with the Great Kaan's birthday falling on the 28th day of the September moon—the festival held on New Year's Day is noted as being of greater significance than the birthday feast.

During the birthday celebrations, the Kaan receives presents from all Tartars in the world and from all governments that owe him allegiance. These presents are offered according to the ability of the giver or as fixed by prescription and orders. Additionally, various groups, including Christians, Saracens, and Idolaters, perform solemn devotions, burn incense, and light lamps to pray for the Emperor's health, happiness, and long life.

## Imperial Customs and Raiment
The Great Kaan maintains a tradition of extreme munificence during his great festivals. On these occasions, the Kaan dresses in robes wrought with beaten gold. He is joined by 12,000 Barons and Knights who wear robes of the same color as the Sovereign's, although they are less costly. These garments are made of silk and gold, and each man is provided with a girdle of gold, all of which are gifts from the Sovereign. Some of these suits are adorned with pearls and precious stones, with single suits valued at 10,000 golden bezants.

There are several sets of such raiment, as a different color is assigned to each festival. While some sources suggest these distributions of dress occur thirteen times a year—corresponding to the thirteen moons of the year—other records indicate the number may be three.

## Religious and Cultural Practices
The court of the Kaan is characterized by the simultaneous presence of various religious practitioners. Christian, Saracen, and Idolater priests all follow the court, offering prayers and blessings for the Kaan. 

In broader Chinese festivities, the *Bacsi* engage in specific rituals. They request items through Barons who stand around the Great Kaan; once these items are granted, the *Bacsi* hold feasts in honor of their gods. These ceremonies involve grand illuminations and the use of various aromatic spices for incense. They cook meat and set it before idols, sprinkling broth to signify that the idols have received their "bellyful." These idols possess their own names and specific feast-days. The *Bacsi* operate out of immense Minsters and Abbeys, some of which are as large as small towns and house more than two thousand monks. These monks are characterized by shaven heads and beards, and some are permitted by their rules to marry and have children.
