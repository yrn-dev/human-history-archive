# Fyrd

The fyrd was the name used to describe the militia or army of the English during the Anglo-Saxon period. The first mention of the fyrd appears in the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* under the date 605.

## Organization and Leadership
The fyrd maintained a local character throughout its existence. During the period of the Danish invasions, records specifically identify the fyrd of individual regions, such as those of Devon, Somerset, and Kent. The responsibility for calling out and leading these local forces likely fell to the sheriff or the ealdorman of the shire.

## Composition and Service
Attendance at the fyrd was compulsory for all holders of land, as it was included in the *trinoda necessitas*. However, service was not limited strictly to landowners. The laws of King Ine of the West Saxons, dated approximately 690, outline specific penalties—known as *fyrdwite*—for those who neglected their duty to the fyrd:
*   A *gesithcund* man who owned land was required to pay 120 shillings and forfeit his land.
*   A *gesithcund* man who did not own land was required to pay 60 shillings.
*   A ceorlish man was required to pay 30 shillings.

While it has been suggested that early Anglo-Saxon armies were primarily peasant forces because ceorls made up the bulk of the population, available evidence does not strongly support this. Regulations from at least the beginning of the 9th century required one warrior for every five or six hides. If the fighting force had been drawn from the *ceorlisc* class, it is argued that a warrior would have been required from every single hide. 

Consequently, serious fighting was likely the responsibility of the *gesiðcund* classes, who were essentially military in nature and possessed effective weapons and horses. While ceorls did participate in military expeditions, they may have served as camp-followers and attendants, focusing on carrying provisions and constructing bridges and stockades.

## Tactics and Evolution
During the 9th century, the fyrd was composed of mounted warriors, although they apparently fought on foot once engaged. Indications suggest this practice may have also been in place as early as the 7th century.

Over time, the fyrd was gradually superseded by the gathering of retainers and thegns. Despite this transition, the fyrd was still occasionally called out for defensive purposes even after the Norman Conquest.
