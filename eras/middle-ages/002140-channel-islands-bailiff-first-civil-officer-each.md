# The Bailiff

The term bailiff refers to a variety of civil, judicial, and administrative officers across different jurisdictions and historical periods, ranging from high-ranking crown representatives to under-officers of the court.

## The Bailiff in the Channel Islands
In the Channel Islands, the bailiff serves as the first civil officer in each island. Appointed by the crown, the bailiff generally holds this office for life. His responsibilities are broad, encompassing both judicial and administrative duties: he presides over the royal court, where he takes the opinions of the jurats, and he also presides over the states. Additionally, he represents the crown in all civil matters. While legal training is not a strict requirement for the position, those selected for the role are usually individuals who have previously held an appointment at the island bar.

## English Legal and Manorial Roles
In England, the term was originally applied generally to the king's officers, including mayors and sheriffs, and specifically to the chief officer of a hundred. The term persists in several specific capacities:

**Court and County Officers**
The chief magistrates of various towns and keepers of royal castles retain the title, such as the bailiff of Dover Castle and the high bailiff of Westminster. Under the County Courts Act 1888, high-bailiffs are appointed by the judge and may be removed by the lord-chancellor. Their duty is to execute warrants, precepts, writs, and serve all orders and summonses. High-bailiffs may appoint assistant bailiffs, whom they can dismiss at will. These officers are responsible for their own acts and defaults, as well as those of their assistants. Per the Law of Distress Amendment Act 1888, a person cannot act as a bailiff to levy distress for rent unless authorized by a county-court judge.

**Sheriff's Bailiffs**
A bailiff of a sheriff is an under-officer employed within a county to execute arrests, distraints, processes, and writs. Because the sheriff is liable for the actions of these officers, bailiffs are annually bound to the sheriff with sureties, leading to the term "bound bailiffs" (also referred to as "bum-bailiffs" or "bums"). Special bailiffs may also be appointed by a sheriff at a plaintiff's request for a specific process; this appointment relieves the sheriff of responsibility until the party is delivered into the sheriff's actual custody.

**Manorial and Franchise Roles**
Under the manorial system, the bailiff was a key officer who superintended the cultivation of the manor and managed its property. In the case of a liberty or franchise, the bailiff is appointed by the lord of the franchise to impanel juries and execute processes and writs.

## Historical Development in France
In France, the bailiff (*bailli*) or seneschal was the principal officer of any noble importance, holding the feudal court of assizes in the lord's absence. Royal bailiffs first appeared definitely under Philip Augustus, specifically in an 1190 ordinance arranging the administration of the kingdom while the king was on crusade. 

Initially, royal bailiffs surveyed royal provosts (*prevots*), led men who owed military service to the king, and concentrated the produce of provostships. Over time, their judicial functions expanded; they held periodical assizes and heard appeals from seigniorial justices and inferior royal judges. The *bailliage* (or *senechaussee*) served as an important administrative and judicial unit.

As civil court procedures became more "learned" under the influence of canon and Roman law, bailiffs began delegating judicial functions to lieutenants. By the 16th century, these lieutenants became royal officers and stripped bailiffs of their judiciary prerogatives. The *bailliage* eventually became a stationary court of justice based in the chief town and served as the electoral district for the states-general. Despite these changes, the *bailli d'epee* (ancient bailiff) continued to exist; judgments were delivered in his name, and he remained responsible for their execution and the organization of military service (*ban* and *arriere ban*) until the end of the 17th century.

## Regional Variations: Scotland and the United States
In Scotland, the term evolved into "bailie," referring to a magistrate or superior officer of a municipal corporation. Appointed from within the council for terms of no more than three years, bailies possess administrative and judicial powers within their burgh, acting as licensing courts in larger burghs and sitting as police-court magistrates assisted by a legal adviser called an "assessor." Historically, the *Bailie of Holyrood* (or *Bailie of the Abbey*) had jurisdiction over civil debts contracted within the sanctuary, and a *Bailie to give sasine* appeared for a superior during sasine ceremonies until 1845.

In the United States, the word bailiff carries no special significance. It is occasionally used to describe the officer who waits upon the court and takes charge of juries. The role corresponding to the English sheriff's bailiff is instead called an under-sheriff or deputy.
