# Normandy and William I of England

## Origins and Early History
Normandy was established by Rolf Ganger, an earl who traveled west to Valland, where he plundered and subdued a great earldom. He peopled this land with Northmen, which gave the region its name. In 912, Charles the Simple ceded Neustria to the Normans. During this early period, the city of Caen existed as a large and important place, though the exact date of its foundation is unknown.

The region was ruled by the earls of Rouen, who were descendants of Rolf Ganger. This lineage included Richard the son of William Long Spear and another Richard, who was the father of Robert Longspear. The earls of Rouen maintained a strong kinship with the chiefs of Norway and were known as the greatest friends of the Northmen; consequently, any Northman seeking a friendly country found one in Normandy. In the autumn of 1013, King Olaf visited Normandy, remaining in the river Seine in peace throughout the winter of 1014.

## William I (William the Bastard)
William, known as William the Bastard and the Earl of Rouen, was the grandson of Robert Longspear. He is described as a stout, strong warrior and a great horseman. While considered a sensible man, he was also viewed as stern and not a person to be relied upon.

William's claim to the English throne arose following the death of his relation, King Edward. When Harald Godwinson was crowned and consecrated as king of England, William believed he possessed a better right to the kingdom due to his relationship with King Edward. Additionally, William sought to avenge an affront Harald had committed regarding his daughter. To pursue this claim, William gathered a great army and sufficient transport-shipping in Normandy. Upon his departure from his castle to the ships, William struck his wife with his heel, driving his spurs so deep into her breast that she fell dead.

Accompanied by his brother, Archbishop Otto, William sailed to England in 1066, where he began plundering and taking possession of the land.

## Norman Influence and Administration
Following the conquest of England, William I (the Conqueror) introduced various customs to the region. It is said that beheading was introduced to England from Normandy by William; the first individual to suffer this was Waltheof, earl of Northumberland, in 1076. 

There is also historical discussion regarding the institution of chivalry. While some suggest it was a Norman innovation, others argue it was already known in Europe. However, the fact that Harold received knighthood from William of Normandy suggests that the custom may have developed a "more special meaning" in Normandy by the middle of the 11th century than it did in England.

Norman barons also played significant roles in the administration of newly acquired territories. For example, Guy (or Guido) de Baliol, a Norman baron who held the fiefs of Vinoy, Harcourt, Dampierre, and Bailleul in Normandy, accompanied William the Conqueror to England. He received lands in the north of England from William II.

## Regional Conflicts and Caen
Under the dukes of Normandy, and specifically under William the Conqueror, the city of Caen increased rapidly and became the capital of lower Normandy. In later centuries, the city was a site of conflict, being besieged and taken by Edward III of England in 1346 and again by the English in 1417. It remained under English control until 1450. Henry VI of England founded the university in Caen in 1436.

Beyond the English conquests, the dukes of Normandy engaged in conflicts with neighboring regions. The duke of Normandy attacked Conan II, the grandson of Geoffrey (who had become duke of Brittany in 992), during a period when Conan was threatened by revolting nobles. Later, in 1085, William was defeated by Alain Fergent, one of Conan's successors, which forced William to make peace.
