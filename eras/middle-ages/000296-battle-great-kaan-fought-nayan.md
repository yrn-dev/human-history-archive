# THE BATTLE OF THE GREAT KAAN AND NAYAN

## The Origins of the Rebellion
Nayan was a young Tartar Chief, approximately thirty years of age, and the uncle of the Emperor Cublay Kaan. As a liegeman to the Great Kaan, Nayan was bound to hold his lands from his nephew, as his ancestors had done. Nayan held a very great dominion consisting of four great provinces: Chorcha, Cauly, Barscol, and Sikintinju.

Driven by the insolence of youth and his own great power, Nayan sought to cease being a vassal and intended to wrest the empire from Cublay Kaan. To achieve this, Nayan contacted Caidu, another potent Tartar Prince and kinsman of the Kaan. Caidu was also a lawful liegeman but was already in a state of bitter enmity and rebellion against his sovereign. Nayan proposed a coordinated attack, suggesting that by attacking Cublay from two sides simultaneously with their combined forces, they could seize the dominion. Caidu agreed to the plan and mustered a host of one hundred thousand horsemen.

## The March of the Great Kaan
Cublay Kaan regarded Nayan's presumptuous rebellion as a matter too serious and perilous to be handled by his sons or barons. Consequently, he personally led the campaign, which was the only time the Great Kaan ever went on a campaign. 

The Kaan marched with great speed and secrecy, covering a distance in twenty marches that normally took thirty. To ensure total surprise, he ordered strict watches and scouts in every direction; anyone encountered was instantly captured. This strategy was successful, as Nayan remained entirely unaware of the Kaan's approach. Nayan's confidence was such that he kept no vedettes in front of or behind his camp, which was located in a remote wilderness. When the Great Kaan's army finally arrived, Nayan was asleep in the arms of a wife for whom he was extravagantly fond.

## The Battle
The conflict took place on a great plain. Upon the breaking of day, the Great Kaan positioned his host upon a hill overlooking the plain where Nayan lay in his tent. The scale of the engagement was immense; taking both sides into account, there were no fewer than 760,000 horsemen, in addition to a very numerous number of footmen.

The battle was described as fierce, fearful, and parlous, lasting from morning until noon with fortunes shifting between the two sides. Ultimately, the Great Kaan achieved victory and Nayan's forces were utterly routed. Nayan and his barons were taken prisoner and forced to surrender all their arms.

Alternative accounts in the archive provide further details on the engagement. One account notes the battle occurred near the river Liao and mentions that the Emperor was aided by Liting leading the Chinese, Yusitemur leading the Mongols, and Tutuha leading the guards and troops of Kincha. In this version, a "Fire-Pao" was discharged, causing panic among Nayan's ill-disciplined troops. Another record suggests the suppression of the rebellion lasted four years, with the primary battle occurring in South-Eastern Mongolia in 1287, after which Nayan fled across a mountain range before being captured by forces from Kuang-ning and Shin-chow.

## The Execution of Nayan
Upon learning of Nayan's capture, the Great Kaan ordered that he be put to death immediately and in secret to prevent others from seeking pity or pardon for Nayan, as he was of the Kaan's own flesh and blood. 

To avoid spilling the blood of the Line Imperial upon the ground or exposing it before the Sun and Heaven, the Kaan ordered a specific method of execution: Nayan was wrapped in a carpet and tossed to and fro mercilessly until he died. Following the victory, the people and barons of Nayan's four provinces renewed their fealty to the Great Kaan.

## Aftermath and Religious Discourse
Nayan had been a baptized Christian and carried the cross on his banner. Following the battle, various groups—including Saracens, Idolaters, and others who did not believe in God—mocked the Christians, questioning why the cross had not helped Nayan.

The Great Kaan sharply rebuked those mocking the Christians. He stated that the cross had done right by not helping Nayan, because Nayan was a disloyal and traitorous rebel who deserved his fate. The Kaan asserted that the cross would not give help against the right. The Christians agreed, stating that their cross would take no part in evil deeds or wrongdoing.

Following the defeat of Nayan, the Great Kaan returned to his capital city of Cambaluc to take his ease and hold festivities. The Tartar Lord Caidu, upon hearing of Nayan's death and defeat, remained in readiness for war but lived in fear of being handled in the same manner as Nayan.
