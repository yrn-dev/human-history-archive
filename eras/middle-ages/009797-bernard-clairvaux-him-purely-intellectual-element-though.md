# Bernard of Clairvaux

Bernard of Clairvaux was a prominent monk, saint, and abbot who exerted significant influence through his speech, writing, and actions. Born into a noble family in Burgundy approximately eight years before the first conquest of Jerusalem, he entered the monastery of Citeaux at the age of twenty-three during a period of primitive fervor within the institution. Two years after joining Citeaux, he established its third colony, or daughter house, in the valley of Clairvaux, located among the woods near Bar sur Aube in Champagne. Bernard remained the abbot of this community until his death.

## Intellectual and Philosophical Influence
Bernard is identified as a figure who expressed a mystical and Platonizing mode of speculation. In this philosophical approach, the purely intellectual element—while never entirely absent—is considered to be of inferior interest when compared to the living power of the heart or the affections. 

His intellectual legacy is further preserved in his literary contributions. He is the author of a Latin hymn, *Salve caput cruentatum* (translated into German in 1659 by Paul Gerhard as "O sacred Head, surrounded By crown of piercing thorn!"), which has been sung by warriors centuries later. Additionally, a curious epitaph written for Bernard, likely by a monk of Clairvaux, is noted for its unique structure as a dialogue in Latin verse.

## Role in the Crusades and Public Life
Bernard is described as one of the most honorable among the holy orators whose voices kindled zeal and silenced reason, encouraging the emigration of pilgrim warriors to the Holy Land. He played a key role in the movements associated with the Crusades, specifically preaching for the second crusade.

Beyond his religious and oratorical roles, Bernard exercised political influence. He was instrumental in bringing an end to the struggle between Pope Honorius II and Louis VI of France.

## Character and Writings
Contemporary accounts and historical analyses suggest that Bernard stood high above his rivals and contemporaries in terms of eloquence and wit, maintaining a degree of reason in his compositions. Despite the records provided by his companions and disciples regarding his own miracles, Bernard is noted for never asserting that he himself possessed the gift of miracles; instead, he recorded the miracles of his friend, St. Malachi.

Regarding his personal preferences, it is noted that Bernard would have been averse to the pomp associated with the church and monastery.
