# BOKHARA (Capital of Bokhara)

Bokhara (Bokkara-i-Sherif) is the capital of the state of Bokhara. It is situated in a fertile plain on the left bank of the Zarafshan and on the irrigation canal of Shahri-rud. Geographically, it is located at 39 deg. 47' N. lat. and 64 deg. 27' E. long., positioned 162 m. by rail west of Samarkand and 8 m. from the Bokhara station of the Transcaspian railway.

## History
Known to the ancients as Sogdiana, the region was too far east to ever fall under Roman dominion, though it was deeply affected by the revolutions of Asia. The foundation of the capital is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana became part of the Seleucidae empire. Later, the nomad Yue-chi moved into the valley of the Oxus from the east, establishing a territorial power in Sogdiana and Bactria; the dominions of their king, Kadphises I (who ascended the throne around A.D. 45), extended from Bokhara to the Indus.

The district was later reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand. Islam was introduced following the Arab conquest of Persia (640-642) and quickly became the dominant faith. During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the region—then called Sughd or Ma wara 'l Nahr (Transoxiana)—was a flourishing district of central Asia. While Samarkand was generally the seat of government, Bokhara earned a high reputation as a center of religion and learning.

In the late 11th century, Malik Shah of the Seljuk dynasty subdued the country. In 1216, Bokhara was subdued by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm, but this was overturned by Jenghiz Khan in 1220, who destroyed the city. The city was subsequently rebuilt and recovered some prosperity under the humane Ogdai Khan. The posterity of Ogdai Khan held possession until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand.

## City Structure and Architecture
The present city was begun in A.D. 830 on the site of an older city. It is enclosed by a stone wall 8 m. long and 28 ft. high, featuring semicircular towers and eleven gates, though these offer little defensive value. The city's water supply is noted as being very unhealthy.

The city is characterized by its numerous religious buildings, including no fewer than 360 mosques. Notable among these is the Mir-Arab, built in the 16th century with beautiful lecture halls, and the Mejid-kalyan (or Kok-humbez), the chief mosque of the emir. Adjacent to the Mejid-kalyan is a brick minaret 203 ft. high; until 1871, state criminals were thrown from its top.

The principal square is the Raghistan. On one side of this square is the citadel, which is built on an artificial eminence 45 ft. high and surrounded by a 1 m. long wall. The citadel contains the prison, water-cisterns, the houses of chief functionaries, and the palace of the emir. Most houses in the city are one-storeyed with flat roofs, constructed from unburned bricks.

## Education, Religion, and Society
Bokhara has long been a center of religious life and Mussulman erudition in central Asia. It is home to 140 *madrasas* (theological colleges or seminaries) where nearly 10,000 pupils are educated. Primary schools are numerous, existing in the capital, other cities, and even villages, with most mosques maintaining one. Approximately one-fourth of the population is said to be literate.

The city's religious history includes the spread of mysticism from Persia during the middle ages. While many rich *madrasa* libraries were lost to conflagrations, confiscated by emirs, or scattered, private libraries still contain literary treasures. Consequently, bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan visit Bokhara, making it the principal book-market of central Asia.

Socially, the *mullahs* (priests) wield great influence, and the population is described as very superstitious, believing in spirits, omens, the evil eye, and witchcraft. Women occupy a low position in the social scale, although slavery has been abolished due to Russian influence. The population is estimated between 50,000 and 100,000, and includes a large, ancient colony of Jews.

## Governance and Economy
The emir of Bokhara is an autocratic ruler whose power is limited only by traditional Mussulman custom (*sheriat*). While he maintains an army of approximately 11,000 men, he is a vassal of the Russian Empire and subject to its control.

Bokhara is the most important trading town in central Asia. Its bazaars specialize in the sale and manufacture of carpets, metal wares (particularly copper), silk stuffs, and Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins.

Near the railway station, 8 m. from the city, is "New Bokhara" (or Kagan). This Russian town was established on territory ceded by the emir and is growing rapidly, with a population of 2,000.
