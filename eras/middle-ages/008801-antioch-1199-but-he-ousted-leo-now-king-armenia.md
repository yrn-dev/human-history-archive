# The Principality of Antioch (1163–1287)

The Principality of Antioch during the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries was characterized by dynastic instability, territorial disputes with the Kingdom of Armenia, and the eventual loss of the city to the Egyptian sultanate.

## The Reign of Bohemund III
Bohemund III succeeded his mother, Constance, in the principality of Antioch in 1163. In 1164, he served as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem while Amalric I conducted an expedition to Egypt. During this period, Bohemund III was defeated and captured at Harenc, east of Antioch, by Nureddin in August 1164. He was subsequently ransomed by his brother-in-law, Emperor Manuel, and returned to Antioch from Constantinople accompanied by a Greek patriarch.

Bohemund III's personal life led to religious sanctions; in 1180, he was excommunicated after deserting his second wife, Princess Orguilleuse, for Sibylla. By Orguilleuse, he had two sons: Raymund and Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV).

## Succession Disputes and Armenian Intervention
The final years of Bohemund III's reign were dominated by a conflict over his successor. His eldest son, Raymund, had married Alice, the daughter of the Armenian prince Rhupen and niece of Leo of Armenia. Following Raymund's death in 1197, the dispute centered on whether the principality should pass to Raymund's son, Raymund Rhupen, or to Bohemund III's younger son, Bohemund.

Leo of Armenia championed the cause of his great-nephew, Raymund Rhupen. Leo had previously attempted to claim Antioch in his own right; in 1194, he captured Bohemund III at Gastin and tried to force him to cede the principality. Around 1199, the younger Bohemund vigorously pursued his own claim and evicted his father from Antioch. However, Bohemund III was restored to the city after the younger Bohemund was ousted by Leo, who was then king of Armenia by the grace of Emperor Henry VI. Bohemund III died in possession of his principality in 1201.

## The Era of Bohemund IV and V
Bohemund IV succeeded his father in 1201, excluding Raymund Rhupen. This sparked a prolonged dispute that required the attention of Pope Innocent III. Bohemund IV was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and the Templars. Conversely, Leo of Armenia was supported by the Hospitallers and appealed to both Innocent III and Emperor Otto IV between 1210 and 1211.

In 1216, Leo captured Antioch and installed Raymund Rhupen as prince. This arrangement lasted less than four years before Bohemund IV regained control. Following the death of Leo in 1220 and Raymund Rhupen in 1221, Bohemund IV reigned over both Antioch and Tripoli. He became a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, leading to his excommunication in 1230. He had a fluctuating relationship with Emperor Frederick II during the crusade of 1228–29, from which he was excluded from the 1229 treaty. Bohemund IV died in 1233, shortly after concluding peace with the Hospitallers and being released from excommunication by Gregory IX.

He was succeeded by his son, Bohemund V, who also held the county of Tripoli. Bohemund V maintained an alliance with the Templars and faced continued hostility from Armenia. He connected Antioch with Cyprus through his 1225 marriage to Alice, the widow of Hugh I. Bohemund V resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving Antioch to be governed by a commune and its bailiff until his death in 1251.

## Final Years and Extinction
Peace with Armenia was finally achieved in 1251 through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund VI, who succeeded his father in 1251 and was knighted by St Louis in 1252, established himself in Antioch while leaving Tripoli to its own administration. In 1257, he secured the recognition of his nephew, Hugh II, as king of Jerusalem.

To counter the advance of the Egyptian sultan, Bohemund VI allied himself with the Mongols. Despite this, he lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268. By the time of his death in 1275, he held only the county of Tripoli. He was succeeded by his son, Bohemund VII. Bohemund VII's reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli, and in 1287, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue in 1287. Because Tripoli was captured within two years of his death, the county of Tripoli is considered extinct with him.
