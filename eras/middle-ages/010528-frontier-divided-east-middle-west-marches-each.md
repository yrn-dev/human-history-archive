# The Marches of England and Scotland

The marches were the debatable grounds located between England and Scotland. While the southern kingdom's attention to the condition of these lands began later than its attention to Wales, specific arrangements were eventually established for their garrisoning and administration.

## Administrative Structure
The frontier was organized into three distinct sections: the East, Middle, and West Marches. Each of these sections was under the control of two wardens—one English and one Scots. These wardens were typically eminent and capable individuals tasked with several critical responsibilities:
* Maintaining the peace.
* Enforcing punishments for those who breached the law.
* Ensuring that neither country encroached upon the boundary of the other.

The position of warden was considered one of the most important in that quarter of the kingdom and was also lucrative. Wardens were entitled to ration and forage for their retinues, and a portion of the forfeits and fines collected fell to them. To manage matters of common interest, the wardens generally conferred once a year. While these meetings were usually conducted in a friendly spirit, tensions occasionally flared, such as in 1575 when a display of temper resulted in the Raid of Reidswire.

## Geography and Landmarks
Roughly speaking, the marches encompassed the modern counties of Cumberland and Northumberland, as well as a tract of land on the Scottish side of the border. The landscape was characterized by a chain of peel towers extending from coast to coast; these served as beacon-stations and were often the homes of marauding chiefs.

Other notable strongholds existed along the frontier. Some English fortifications, including Naworth, Ford, Chillingham, and Alnwick, were modernized over time. Others, such as Warkworth, Wark, and Norham, became picturesque ruins. In contrast, most Scottish fortresses were either demolished and built over or reduced to grass-grown mounds.

## Historical Transition
The necessity of protecting the marches ceased shortly after James VI of Scotland acceded to the English throne. Upon his first public progress to London, James I of England attended service at Berwick church on March 27, 1603, to give thanks for his peaceful entry into his new dominions.

In an effort to erase the memory of a bitter past, James I forbade the use of the word "Borders," expressing hope that the term "Middle Shires" would be used instead. As part of this policy, frontier garrisons were reduced to nominal strength and fortresses were dismantled. Although the king's proposed name change was sentimental and eventually proved meaningless—as the term "Borders" remained too geographically convenient to be dropped—the policy succeeded in its goal of stabilizing the region.

The marches now hold only legendary and historical significance.

## Other Uses of the Term
The term "march" has appeared in other historical and geographical contexts. Around the year 1200, Patrick de Dunbar, the earl of Dunbar, referred to himself as the earl of March, deriving the name from the "merse" or "march," which was a tract of land located in Berwickshire. Additionally, the term was used in continental Europe: there was a county of La Marche in France under the *ancien régime*, and a county of La Marck in north-east Germany, which is currently part of the kingdom of Prussia.
