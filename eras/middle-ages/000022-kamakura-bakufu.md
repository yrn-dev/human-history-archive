# The Kamakura Bakufu

The Kamakura Bakufu was a military government that existed during a period of Japanese history characterized by a complex relationship between the military regime in Kamakura and the Imperial Court in Kyoto. While it is often suggested that the Bakufu brought the entire country under a single administrative control, this is a modification of the fact; total feudal rule over all sixty provinces was not achieved until the seventeenth century during the Tokugawa era.

## Relations with the Imperial Court
During the Kamakura era, a social and geographic divide existed between the capital and the military government. Court magnates continued to despise those adherent to the Bakufu, and the distance between Kyoto and Kamakura gave the latter an element of rusticity. 

The relationship between the two powers was often marked by a surface-level deference from the Bakufu toward the Throne. For example, when Emperor Go-Toba abdicated in 1198 in favor of his son Tsuchi-mikado, he did so without consulting the Kamakura Bakufu. The Kamakura chief, Yoritomo, did not intervene, maintaining a habitually reverent attitude. Similarly, when Tsuchi-mikado was compelled to abdicate in 1210 in favor of his brother Juntoku, the Bakufu—then under the shogunate of Sanetomo—offered no remonstrance due to Sanetomo's deference to the Court.

## Administrative Regulations and Governance
The Bakufu maintained strict control over its vassals and the clergy within its domains. Bakufu vassals were strictly forbidden from soliciting rank or office directly from the Imperial Court; they were required to obtain a special recommendation from Kamakura. Once they were invested with Court rank, however, they could be promoted without further recommendation and were free to accept the position of *hebiishi*.

Similar restrictions applied to the Kwanto clergy, who faced summary removal from their benefices if they appealed to Kyoto for promotion. An exception was made for Zen-shu priests, who received only a "gentle admonition" from an influential member of their sect. The Bakufu also intervened if clergy within its domains neglected their services or squandered revenues. Conversely, monasteries and priests outside the Bakufu domain remained virtually independent unless the Bakufu was instructed to interfere by an Imperial decree.

## The Hojo Regency and Cultural Influence
Under the Hojo regents—specifically Yasutoki, Tokiyori, and Tokimune—the Bakufu sought to establish an artistic and literary atmosphere independent of Kyoto. These regents, who were earnest disciples of Buddhism, assigned Chinese priests from the Yuan and Sung eras to build five great temples in the Bakufu capital. These structures reflected a blend of Chinese and Japanese art-canons. The Hojo regents utilized these foreign priests not only for religious and artistic purposes but also as channels for gathering political information about the neighboring empire.

## Internal Decline and Social Pressures
Over time, the Bakufu suffered from internal demoralization. While the regency was staffed by unpretentious men focused on efficiency and justice, the officials of the shogun's court, known as *banshu*, became associated with luxury. Veterans of the Bakufu, unable to gain riches through military action during peaceful Hojo rule, began to covet sinecures in the shogun's household. This led to a rise in extravagant spending on fine buildings, rich garments, and banquets, forcing some to mortgage their estates.

Between 1252 and 1330, the Bakufu issued eight edicts attempting to curb these excesses by fixing commodity prices, prohibiting expensive clothing, and vetoing costly residences. These efforts were largely unsuccessful, and the ascendancy of Kyoto's social standards within Kamakura is regarded as a primary factor in the Bakufu's eventual downfall.

## Conflict and Collapse
Tensions between the Court and the Bakufu eventually escalated into open conflict. During the Shokyu era (1219-1222), Emperor Go-Toba's relationship with the Bakufu soured following two key incidents: the Bakufu's refusal to support Go-Toba's protest regarding an estate in Settsu province given to a *shirabyoshi* (danseuse) named Kamegiku, and the refusal of Yoshitoki to restore a manor owned by Nishina Morito, a Bakufu retainer who had entered Imperial service. In 1221, Go-Toba arranged for the abdication of his son Juntoku, issued a decree declaring Yoshitoki a rebel, and summoned troops to attack Kamakura.

Later, Go-Daigo actively conspired to overthrow the Hojo. He secured the support of soldier cenobites by making Prince Morinaga (under the name Oto no Miya) the lord-abbot of the Hiei-zan monastery. Although the plot remained hidden for five years, the Bakufu discovered it in 1331 after subjecting plotters to judicial examination. The situation reached a crisis point when the cloistered Emperor Go-Fushimi warned the Bakufu that the situation would elude control after Go-Daigo summoned the Enryaku monks to his aid.
