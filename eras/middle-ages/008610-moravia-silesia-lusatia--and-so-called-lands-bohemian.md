# Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia: The Lands of the Bohemian Crown

The territories of Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia have historically been associated with the Kingdom of Bohemia, often collectively referred to as the "lands of the Bohemian crown." These regions have functioned as integral parts of the Bohemian state at various intervals, serving as points of expansion, constitutional unity, and later, centers of national and racial contention within the Habsburg Empire.

## Early Expansion and Integration
The integration of these territories into the Bohemian sphere began in the early medieval period. Christianity reached Bohemia via Moravia, where it was taught by Cyrillus and Methodius, Greek monks from Thessalonica. This connection facilitated the baptism of the Bohemian prince Borivoj around the year 873.

Under the rule of Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its frontiers significantly. Boleslav II established authority not only over Bohemia and Moravia but also over a large portion of Silesia and a part of Poland (the current Austrian province of Galicia). However, this empire was short-lived; his son, Boleslav III, lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great, king of Poland.

In the 12th century, the Bohemian crown began to acquire further territories. In 1156, Emperor Frederick I. Barbarossa ceded Upper Lusatia to Prince Vladislav II. Later, in 1368, Emperor Charles IV acquired Lower Lusatia—which had previously been purchased by the margrave of Brandenburg in 1303—thereby bringing both Upper and Lower Lusatia under Bohemian rule.

## Constitutional and Political Unity
By the 15th century, the administrative and constitutional link between these regions was formalized. In 1446, a general meeting of the estates took place involving Bohemia, Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia. This meeting was of exceptional importance to Bohemian constitutional history, as it decreed that members of the estates be divided into three bodies known as *curiae*.

The relationship between these lands and the central Bohemian throne was occasionally strained by succession crises and external conflicts. During the struggle between King Matthias of Hungary and Prince Vladislav of Poland for the Bohemian throne, a treaty concluded at Olmutz in 1478 settled the dispute. While Vladislav secured Bohemia, Matthias was permitted to retain the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia—during his lifetime, with the agreement that they would be restored to Bohemia upon his death. Lusatia specifically recognized Matthias Corvinus as sovereign in 1469 before returning to the rule of the Bohemian king in 1490.

## National and Racial Dynamics
In later centuries, particularly under Austrian rule, the "lands of the Bohemian crown" became focal points for national aspirations. In Bohemia, where over three-fifths of the population were Czech, a strong movement led by figures such as Palacky and Rieger demanded the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy in its fullest extent, specifically including Moravia and Silesia.

The demographic composition of these regions created varying political alignments:
* **Bohemia:** Racial feeling was strong, supported by historic law and a desire for a self-governing kingdom.
* **Moravia:** Czechs in Moravia supported the Bohemian movement, though a strong German party existed in Bruenn.
* **Silesia:** In this region, Germans held a considerable majority. Coupled with a large Polish element that did not support the Czechs, the Silesian diet refused to recognize the claims of the Bohemians.

## Modern Political Struggles
During the 19th century, the desire to re-establish the ancient constitution of the Bohemian lands persisted. In 1848, following the February revolution in Paris, a petition demanded a general diet comprising all Bohemian lands. Emperor Ferdinand responded by stating that the question of reuniting Moravia and Silesia with Bohemia would be left to a general meeting of representatives from all parts of Austria.

By 1879, under the government of Count Eduard Taaffe, Bohemian representatives participated in the parliament of Vienna. They did so while explicitly maintaining the view that Bohemia, together with Moravia and Silesia, constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary.
