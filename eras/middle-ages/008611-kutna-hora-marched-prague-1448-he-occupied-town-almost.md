# Kutna Hora and the Political History of Bohemia

## Overview of Kutna Hora
Kutna Hora (also known as Kuttenberg) is a town in Bohemia located 45 miles east-southeast of Prague. As of 1900, its population was 14,799, consisting mostly of Czechs. The town is characterized by several significant Gothic structures, including the church of St Jacob from the 14th century, the five-naved church of St Barbara (begun in 1368), and the Late Gothic Trinity church from the end of the 15th century. Historically, the town housed the Wälscher Hof, which served as a mint and royal residence at the end of the 13th century. The Steinerne Haus, a Gothic building that has served as the town hall since 1849, contains one of the richest archives in Bohemia. The town's industry includes the manufacture of leather goods, agricultural implements, cotton and woollen stuffs, brewing, and sugar-refining.

## Sigismund and the Siege of Prague
In the early 15th century, the death of the king led to rioting in various Bohemian cities, including Prague, resulting in the expulsion of many Germans who were mostly adherents of the Church of Rome. Sigismund, claiming the Bohemian crown as the successor to his brother, arrived at Kutna Hora in the following year. Following a crusade proclaimed against Bohemia by Pope Martin V on March 1, 1420, Sigismund assembled a large army of Bohemians and men from various other countries.

On June 30, 1420, Sigismund occupied the castle of Prague. The crusaders viewed the city as a heretical community because its inhabitants accepted other evangelical truths and used the sacred chalice. However, the attempt to conquer the city failed. After the Taborites, led by Zizka, repulsed an attack on the Vitkov (Zizkov) hill, Sigismund abandoned the siege. He suffered a further defeat on November 1, 1420, at the battle of the Vysehrad while attempting to relieve a besieged garrison on the outskirts of Prague.

## Succession and the National Party
Following the formal recognition of the Compacts at Iglau in Moravia, Sigismund was accepted as king in Prague, though he died the following year in 1437. He was succeeded by Albert of Austria, who reigned for less than two years. His death sparked a succession crisis regarding whether the Bohemian crown was hereditary or elective. Two noble factions emerged: a national party led by George of Podebrad and a Roman or Austrian division led by Ulrich of Rosenberg.

The parties reached an agreement called the "Letter of Peace" (_list mirny_), in which they pledged to support the Compacts and recognize John of Rokycan as the archbishop of Prague, despite the Church of Rome's refusal to recognize him. In exchange, the national party abandoned the candidacy of Prince Casimir of Poland. This paved the way for Albert's son, Ladislas, who was born on February 22, 1440, and was formally acknowledged as king by the Bohemians.

## George of Podebrad and the Election of 1471
George of Podebrad, the only Hussite king of Bohemia, initially maintained a prosperous reign through an alliance with Matthias Corvinus, the king of Hungary. However, George eventually entered a struggle with the papal see. In 1452, George assembled the estates at Prague and declared his commitment to the communion in both kinds. By 1466, Pope Paul II excommunicated George and released Bohemians from their oath of allegiance to him. This led to a confederacy of Romanist nobles at Zelena Hora and subsequent warfare. Matthias of Hungary eventually invaded Moravia and was proclaimed king of Bohemia on May 3, 1469.

Following the death of George of Podebrad in 1471, the Bohemian nobles were divided between the claims of Matthias of Hungary and Prince Vladislav of Poland. On May 27, 1471, Prince Vladislav was elected king at Kutna Hora. This election represented a victory for the national party and reflected strong anti-clerical sentiment, as Vladislav's envoys promised to maintain the Compacts, whereas Matthias was an unconditional adherent of Rome.

## Later Conflicts and Imperial Occupation
Warfare between Vladislav and Matthias continued until 1478, when a treaty at Olmutz secured Bohemia for Vladislav, while Matthias retained Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia for his lifetime. Despite his promises, Vladislav's policy was generally reactionary; he appointed officials in Prague from the section of the Utraquist party closest to Rome and persecuted the Bohemian Brethren. This led to riots in Prague and the formation of a league by nobles of the same faith. A meeting of all estates at Kutna Hora in 1485 temporarily restored peace.

In the 17th century, after the deposition of Ferdinand and the brief reign of Frederick, elector palatine, the imperial forces under the duke of Bavaria, Tilly, and Bouquoi entered Bohemia in September 1620. On November 8, 1620, the imperialists defeated the Bohemians at the "Bila Hora" (White Hill) and occupied the port of Prague. Later, Ferdinand arrived in Prague on July 20 with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries, occupying the city almost without resistance. Ferdinand revised the ancient privileges of the towns, specifically Prague, and imposed royal town-captains and town-judges to exercise unlimited control over municipal affairs.
