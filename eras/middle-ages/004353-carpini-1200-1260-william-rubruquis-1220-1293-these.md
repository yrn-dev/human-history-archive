# Carpini and William of Rubruquis

Joannes de Plano Carpini (c. 1200–1260) and William of Rubruquis (1220–1293) were European friars of the Franciscan Order who traveled to the court of the Great Kaan in Mongolia during the 13th century. Driven by curiosity and zeal, they provided Catholic Christendom with its first revelations of the Mongol world and served as primary literary memorials of European overland expansion prior to the travels of Marco Polo.

## Joannes de Plano Carpini
Friar Joannes de Plano Carpini undertook his mission as an older man. Records from the annals of his order describe him as a "fat and heavy man" (*vir gravis et corpulentus*), noting that during his time preaching in Germany, he had to ride a donkey, which departed from Franciscan precedent. Despite this, his narrative is characterized by an absence of personal complaint or authorial egotism.

Carpini recorded his findings in a work known by several titles in various manuscripts, including *Liber Tartarorum* (or *Tatarorum*) and *Historia Mongalorum quos nos Tartaros appellamus*. The treatise is structured into eight extensive chapters covering the history, policy, tactics, religion, character, manners, climate, and country of the Tatars, as well as the most effective methods for opposing them. A ninth chapter details the regions he passed through.

His work is noted for including four specific name-lists: the Mongol princes, the nations conquered by the Mongols, the nations that had successfully resisted them between 1245 and 1247, and the witnesses who could verify the truth of his narrative, including merchants from Kiev. While his work is considered the best treatment of Tatar customs and history by a medieval Christian writer, it is noted for some credulity regarding "childish absurdity" in the Oriental tales he incorporated.

## William of Rubruquis
William of Rubruquis was a younger brother of the Franciscan Order and served as a noteworthy envoy for Louis IX to the Mongols. His writings are regarded as superior to Carpini's in terms of both geographical and personal detail. Rubruquis is credited with a specific acumen in identifying that the people he encountered at the Court of the Great Kaan were identical to the "Seres" of classic fame.

Rubruquis's observations provided early reports to Europe regarding the existence of a great and civilized country known as Cathay, although he did not reach its frontiers himself.

## Observations on Mongol Culture
Both travelers documented the customs and etiquette of the Mongol court. They noted specific rules regarding the threshold of dwellings; Carpini reported that breaching this etiquette could be punished by death. Rubruquis specifically recorded being warned not to touch the ropes of tents, as these were viewed as representing the threshold.

The two friars also observed the strict dress codes during official assemblies. Carpini described the inauguration of Kuyuk Kaan, where nobles wore white *pourpre* on the first day, ruby *pourpre* on the second, blue *pourpre* on the third, and the finest baudekins on the fourth. Similarly, Rubruquis described a four-day festival at the court of Mangku Kaan where attendees wore different colored raiment each day, with everything from the turban to the boots matching in color for that specific day.

## Encounters with the Bashkirs
Carpini and Rubruquis were the first European writers to mention the Bashkirs, whom they encountered in the upper regions of the river Ural. They referred to this people as "Pascatir" and asserted that, at the time of their visit, the Bashkirs spoke the same language as the Hungarians.
