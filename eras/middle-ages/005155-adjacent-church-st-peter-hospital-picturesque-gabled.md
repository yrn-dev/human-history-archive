# Gabled Architecture and Associated Structures

The presence of gabled houses and ecclesiastical gables is a recurring feature in the architectural landscapes of various European cities and historic sites, often contributing to a picturesque aesthetic.

## Gabled Residential Architecture
In several European cities, gabled houses are characteristic of the old town districts. In Bristol, the inner part of the city contains narrow streets with ancient gabled houses described as extremely picturesque. Similarly, the German city of Cassel (or Kassel) features narrow and crooked streets in its old town containing many picturesque gabled houses, generally dating from the 17th century.

In the Netherlands, the seaport of Hoorn is distinguished by its old-world air and the beauty of numerous gabled houses from the 16th and 17th centuries. Many of these structures are decorated with bas-reliefs and inscriptions, some of which commemorate the 1573 battle on the Zuider Zee where the Beggars defeated the Spaniards under Count Bossu. In Danzig, the city preserves a medieval aspect featuring grand old patrician houses from its Hanseatic glory, characterized by balconied windows and lofty, often elaborately ornamented gables. In northern Spain, the town of Fuenterrabia contains curious gable-roofed houses within its picturesque walled fortress, which stands in contrast to the newer suburbs and fishermen's quarter.

## Ecclesiastical Gables and Bell-Cots
Gables also serve specific functional and decorative purposes in religious architecture. A bell-gable (also known as a bell-cot or bell-turret) is a structure used to hang one or more bells in small churches or chapels that lack towers. These are common in Switzerland and France, where they are frequently made of wood and attached to the ridge. In the Roman Church, a bell-cot or gable may hold the "Sanctus-bell," which is rung during the canon of the Mass at the consecration and elevation of the Elements; such bells are generally positioned on the top of the arch dividing the chancel from the nave, though they have also been placed over the gables of porches.

In other ecclesiastical contexts, gables define the structural silhouette of the building. For example, a church in the Somme region featured side-aisles with live gables and five separate roofs positioned at right-angles to the great nave. Additionally, the façade of a church in Moreuil, which resembles St. Peter's Church at Montdidier, comprises the gable of the nave and a square belfry tower above two large porches.

## Associated Public and Hospital Buildings
Various cities combine these architectural styles with public institutions. In Hoorn, the picturesque St John's hospital, dating to 1563, is currently used for military purposes. The town also contains a hospital for aged men and women from the beginning of the 17th century. In Bristol, the general hospital is located on an island formed between the New Course of the Avon and the Floating Harbour, situated near the Temple and St Mary Redcliffe churches. In Khartum, a city laid out by Lord Kitchener in 1898, the urban plan includes a large and well-equipped civil hospital and a military hospital located to the east of the governor-general's palace.
