# HOW THE OLD MAN CAME BY HIS END

The account of the demise of the figure known as the Old Man of the Mountain is detailed in the records of the travels of Marco Polo. This individual was the leader of a sect of adepts known as the *Fidáwí*, Ismailites, Assassins, or *Muláhidah*. These sectaries were known for committing notable murders and were organized into different branches, with some acknowledging a Chief residing at Alamut as their High Priest or Imaum.

## The Siege of the Castle
In the year of Christ’s Incarnation 1252, Alaü, the Lord of the Tartars of the Levant, became aware of the "great crimes" attributed to the Old Man. In response, Alaü resolved to bring about the Old Man's end. He dispatched a Baron leading a great army to the Old Man's castle to conduct a siege.

The castle proved to be exceptionally strong, and the army was unable to capture it for three years. The archive notes that had the inhabitants possessed a sufficient supply of food within the walls, the castle might never have been taken. However, after three years of being besieged, the defenders ran short of victuals.

## The Fall of the Old Man
Following the depletion of their food supplies, the castle was finally taken. The Old Man was put to death along with all of his men. In addition to the execution of the leadership and the followers, the castle and its Garden of Paradise were levelled to the ground.

The records state that since this event, the Old Man has had no successor, marking the end of all his "villainies." While the sect faced this apparent destruction by Hulákú, some sources indicate its survival to a later time.
