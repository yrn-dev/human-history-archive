# Bote

The term "bote" is an old form of a word derived from the Old English *bot*, which is common to Teutonic languages. It is related to the Gothic *bota* (meaning "good" or "advantage"), the Old High German *Buoza*, and the Modern German *Busse* (meaning "fine" or "penance"). It is also cognate with the word "better," the comparative form of "good." In its primary sense, "bote" refers to profit or advantage.

## Legal Applications and Estovers
In old legal terminology, "bote" survives within various compound words to describe specific rights of "estover." Estover is a Norman French term that corresponds to the Saxon "bote." Examples of these particular rights include:
*   **House-bote**
*   **Fire-bote**
*   **Hedge-bote**

Additionally, the term appears in the expression "thief-bote," which refers to the customary compensation paid for injuries under Old English law.

## Primitive Law and Compensation
Beyond specific rights of estover, "bote" was part of a broader system of primitive law used to address homicide. In early legal systems, homicide—regardless of whether the act was innocent—typically subjected the slayer to the lawful vengeance of the slain person's kindred. This practice, known as *lex talionis* (a life for a life), often led to blood feuds or private justice.

To avoid such violence, a slayer could make a composition with the kindred of the deceased. This was managed through a system of assessed prices for every man's life, which were payable to the individual's next of kin and his chief. This system of compensation consisted of three elements: wite, bote, and wer.

## Linguistic Survival
The original sense of "bot" as profit or advantage survives in the modern English word "bootless," meaning unavailing or useless. It also persists in archaistic expressions such as the question, "what boots it?"
