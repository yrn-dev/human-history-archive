# Domesday Hundreds and Shire Boundaries

The Domesday Survey, executed for William the Conqueror, was a comprehensive record completed in 1086. The scheme for this survey was discussed and determined during the Christmas assembly of 1085. To conduct the survey, royal officers known as *legati* visited each county to hold public inquiries, which likely took place at the county court and were attended by local lords and representatives from every township.

## The Hundred as a Unit of Inquiry
The primary unit of inquiry for the Domesday Survey was the Hundred, which served as an administrative subdivision of the county. For each Hundred, a return was sworn to by twelve local jurors, consisting of six Normans and six English. While the Domesday Book was compiled from these returns, it is distinct from the returns themselves. Full transcripts of the original returns are preserved for several Cambridgeshire Hundreds, and detailed information is also found in the *Inquisitio Eliensis*, the "Exon Domesday" (preserved at Exeter), and the second volume of the Domesday Book.

## Regional Variations in Administrative Divisions
While the Hundred was the standard unit in many areas, other regions exhibited different administrative structures due to foreign influence. In several counties—including Yorkshire, Lincolnshire, Nottinghamshire, Rutlandshire, Leicestershire, Derbyshire, and parts of Northamptonshire—the land was divided into "wapentakes" instead of hundreds. This division, along with the grouping of shires around county towns, is attributed to Scandinavian or Danish influence. 

This Norse influence extended to social classifications found in Domesday documents, such as *hold*, *lysing*, *thrall*, *bonde*, *karlman*, *karl*, and *dreng*. Additionally, these districts utilized a duodecimal system of reckoning and assessed taxation by *carucates* rather than by *virgates* and hides.

## Stability and Alteration of Shire Lines
The degree to which shire boundaries and hundred divisions remained stable after the Domesday Survey varied by region.

### Buckinghamshire
The shire of Buckingham was likely formed by aggregating pre-existing hundreds around the county town following the division of Mercia during the reign of Edward the Elder. Since the Domesday survey, the shire has altered little on its outer lines. While the eighteen hundreds recorded in the survey were reduced to eight, three of the Chiltern hundreds—Stoke, Burnham, and Desborough—remain unaltered in name and extent. Aylesbury continues to be designated as "the three hundreds of Aylesbury."

### Dorset and Kent
In contrast, other regions saw significant changes. In Dorset, the *Inquisitio Geldi* (drawn up two years before the Domesday Survey) listed 39 pre-Conquest hundreds. Although some original names persist in the 33 hundreds and 21 liberties of the present day, the boundaries suffered much alteration. For example, 8,000 acres of Dalwood and Stockland recorded in the Dorset Domesday were later annexed to Devon.

In Kent, the Domesday Survey recorded sixty hundreds and a further division into six "lests." Four of these lests—Wiwart, Limowast, Estre, and Borowast—existed as early as the 9th century and were named after meeting places of the Kentish Council (Wye, Lymne, Eastre, and Burgwara, the latter being Canterbury). By the reign of Edward II, the *Nomina Villarum* mentioned sixty-six modern hundreds.

## Inter-County Relations and Governance
Historically, some shires maintained close administrative ties. Derbyshire and Nottinghamshire shared local customs and held their shire-courts together for the Domesday Inquest; they remained under a single sheriff until the time of Elizabeth. Similarly, Buckinghamshire and Bedfordshire shared a common sheriff until the reign of Queen Elizabeth, and Dorset and Somerset were united under one sheriff until the same period.
