# Military and Naval Concentration in Historical Conflict

The strategic concentration of forces—the gathering of troops or naval assets to achieve superior strength for decisive action—is a recurring theme across various historical eras and theaters of war. This practice is employed to secure territorial gains, defend strategic ports, or execute large-scale raids.

## Naval Concentration and Strategic Shifts
In the early 20th century, the growth of the German Navy necessitated a shift in British naval strategy. To ensure safety, the British Fleet was concentrated in Home Waters, a process initiated by Lord Fisher in 1904. This strategy involved reducing the number of small, old vessels previously scattered globally to "show the flag" and replacing them with stronger, more homogeneous squadrons at home. This move also served as a financial economy.

Further concentration measures included the recall of battleships from China and the eventual withdrawal of battleships from the Mediterranean. While the Cabinet decided to maintain a powerful force in the Mediterranean—including an armoured cruiser squadron and four battle cruisers based on Malta, with plans for a Dreadnought battle squadron by 1916 to match the Austrian fleet—the withdrawal of battleships made Britain appear dependent on the French Fleet. Simultaneously, France moved all its heavy ships into the Mediterranean, fostering mutual reliance between the two navies.

In the Outer Seas, the principle of avoiding the division of forces remained critical. During operations along the West Coast of South America, Admiral Cradock emphasized the need for British forces on each coast to be strong enough to bring enemy heavy cruisers to action should they concentrate. Cradock sought to concentrate his own forces at the Falkland Islands to avoid division, ordering the *Canopus* there and instructing the *Monmouth*, *Glasgow*, and *Otranto* not to proceed north of Valparaiso until German cruisers were located.

## Land Force Concentrations and Raids
Concentration of manpower has been utilized for both offensive raids and defensive maneuvers. In planned operations against British targets, specific force requirements were calculated: a raid on Blyth to destroy the Elswick Works and ships in the Tyne was estimated to require at least 10,000 men to overcome sporadic opposition from the population and unmobilised territorials. A separate proposed raid on Harwich required the secret concentration and embarkation of no less than 20,000 men to stop the regular army from proceeding to France.

In the context of the British Expeditionary Force, debates occurred regarding the location of its concentration. Some authorities suggested concentrating the force around Amiens due to the fact that British mobilisation began three days later than the French. However, the "forward school" and Sir John French prevailed, ensuring the force was positioned to help France in the manner the French Staff deemed most effective.

## Historical Examples of Force Concentration
Historical accounts detail various instances where the concentration of forces led to decisive outcomes:

*   **Nidaros:** Earl Svein and Einar Tambaskelfer gathered a large armed force of nearly 2,000 men. They entered Gaulardal and descended upon Nidaros. King Olaf’s men, alerted by a horseback guard, evacuated the town by ship before the earl's men arrived, set fire to the houses, and seized Christmas provisions.
*   **The Bay of Honduras:** In April 1754, Spaniards organized an inland expedition of 1,500 men at the town of Peten to expel British log-wood cutters. This force was routed by 250 British. Later, on September 15, 1779, the Spaniards organized a larger force that attacked and destroyed the establishment at Belize.
*   **Venezuela:** During the revolution, Bolivar operated against Spanish troops on the Magdalena river. Despite having no more than 500 men, he forced his way to Truxillo and Merida, raising the population to his support. After a decisive battle at Lastoguanes, where the troops of General Monteverde were crushed, Bolivar entered Caracas on August 4, 1813.
*   **Syracuse:** Timoleon faced a concentrated Carthaginian force at the river Krimesus. While the enemy numbered 70,000, Timoleon led a force of 5,000 foot and 1,000 horse. Although some of his paid force retreated out of fear, Timoleon encouraged the remaining men to proceed.
