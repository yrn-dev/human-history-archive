# Bagdad: Urban Infrastructure and Historical Decline

Bagdad has served as a major center of religious and commercial activity, though its status has fluctuated significantly over the centuries. Once the capital of the Abbasid dynasty and the religious head of Islam, the city experienced a period of extreme prosperity and splendor before a series of political and military catastrophes led to its decline.

## Commercial and Urban Infrastructure
The city's commercial life is centered around extensive and well-stocked bazaars. While these are not as fine in construction as those found in other Eastern cities, they are noted for being more interesting in their industries and contents, largely because Bagdad has been less affected by foreign innovations. The architecture of the bazaars varies; some are vaulted with brickwork, while the majority are covered with flat beams supporting roofs made of grass, branches of trees, or dried leaves. This roofing extends across the entire business section of the city, providing grateful shelter from the sun in summer, though the resulting darkness and damp, chilly atmosphere are described as trying for foreign visitors during winter.

For the reception of merchants, pilgrims, and their goods, Bagdad is said to have approximately thirty *khans* or caravanserais. Most of these are not considered important as buildings, with the exception of the khan el-Aurtmeh. This structure, which formerly belonged to the adjoining Marjanieh mosque, dates from A.D. 1356 and is said to be built on the site of an ancient Christian church; it is recognized as a fine specimen of Saracenic brickwork due to its vaulted roof. In addition to these traditional structures, modern travel demands have led to the establishment of an English club-house and a hotel providing accommodation according to European methods. The city also contains about fifty baths, though these are generally inferior in terms of accommodation and construction.

## The Abbasid Period and Early Decline
The downfall of Bagdad began with the rise of the Turkish body-guard under Mo'tassim, the successor to Mamun. Mo'tassim founded Samarra, and for fifty-eight years the court and caliph deserted Bagdad. In A.D. 865, the caliph Mosta'in returned to Bagdad to escape the tyranny of the Turkish guard, but the city was besieged and taken. From that point until the final collapse of the dynasty, the Abbasid caliphs remained the religious heads of Islam and the city remained its capital, but the caliphs were mere puppets. Real power was held successively by the Seljuks, the Buyids, and the Turkish guard.

Despite this political instability, Bagdad remained a city of the first rank and was, for much of this period, the richest and most splendid city in the world. This era is evidenced by the number of great shrines dating from that time. However, a more rapid decline began in the final century of the caliphates. By the close of the 12th century, the eastern half of the city contained large uninhabited spaces, and travelers described the western city as being almost in ruins.

## The Mongol Conquest and Aftermath
In 1258, Bagdad was captured and destroyed by Hulagu (Hulaku), the grandson of Jenghiz Khan. The Mongols, who had become bitterly hostile to Moslems, perpetrated a massacre of the city's entire population. This event marked the extinction of the Abbasid caliphate and the end of Bagdad's status as the primary religious center of Islam.

The destruction extended beyond the city walls. Hulagu implemented a deliberate policy to eliminate settled civilized systems in favor of nomadism. The Mongols destroyed the immemorial irrigation system of Mesopotamia, which had supported a prosperous and populous region since the early days of Sumeria. This system had endured for at least eight thousand years, sustaining a succession of cities including Nineveh, Babylon, Ctesiphon, Eridu, Nippur, and Bagdad. 

Following the destruction of the irrigation works, the fertility of the region ceased, and Mesopotamia became a land of ruins, malarious swamps, and desolation. Bagdad and Mosul eventually revived, but only as second-rate towns. Despite this fall from eminence, the city retained a certain glamour, continuing to be referred to as the "glorious city" in Turkish official documents.
