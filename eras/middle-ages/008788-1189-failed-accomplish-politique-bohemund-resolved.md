# The Bohemunds of Antioch and Tripoli

The history of the Bohemunds is inextricably linked to the history of the principality of Antioch and, from the era of Bohemund IV onward, the county of Tripoli. This dynasty of Norman origin played a central role in the First Crusade and the subsequent establishment of Crusader states in the East.

## Bohemund I and the First Crusade
Bohemund I (c. 1058–1111), born Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*. Early in his career, he served under his father during attacks on the East Roman empire between 1080 and 1085. During Guiscard's absence from 1082 to 1084, Bohemund commanded the Normans and penetrated into Thessaly as far as Larissa, where he was repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early hostility toward Alexius significantly influenced his future career and the trajectory of the First Crusade.

Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a conflict arose between Bohemund and his younger brother Roger over the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This dispute was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions. In 1096, while attacking Amalfi, Bohemund was inspired by the passing crusaders. He viewed the First Crusade as an opportunity to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* and sought to establish an eastern principality for himself. Leading a fine Norman army, he crossed the Adriatic and arrived in Constantinople in April 1097, where he did homage to Emperor Alexius. Bohemund acted as the real leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch, guiding the host across Asia Minor.

## Succession and the Early Principality
Bohemund I was succeeded by his son, Bohemund II (1108–1131), who was born in the year of his father's defeat at Durazzo. Bohemund II arrived in Antioch from Apulia in 1126 and married Alice, the daughter of Baldwin II, in 1127. After participating in an attack on Damascus with Baldwin II, Bohemund II was defeated and killed in 1131 by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo on his northern frontier.

## Bohemund III and the Third Crusade
Bohemund III, the son of Constance (daughter of Bohemund II) and Raymund of Antioch, succeeded his mother in the principality of Antioch in 1163. In 1164, while serving as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem during Amalric I's expedition to Egypt, he was defeated and captured by Nureddin at Harenc. He was ransomed by Emperor Manuel and returned to Antioch with a Greek patriarch.

During this era, Saladin became the ruler of Egypt in 1169 and preached a Jehad (Holy War) against the Christians. This counter-crusade led to the retaking of Jerusalem in 1187, which provoked the Third Crusade in 1189. This expedition, planned by Emperor Frederick I, the King of France, and King Richard I of England, succeeded in saving the principality of Antioch for a time, although it failed to retake Jerusalem.

Bohemund III's later years were marked by personal and political turmoil. In 1180, he was excommunicated after deserting his second wife, Orguilleuse, for Sibylla. He spent his final years embroiled in a succession dispute between his grandson, Raymund Rhupen, and his younger son, Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV). Leo of Armenia championed Raymund Rhupen and even captured Bohemund III at Gastin in 1194 to force the cession of Antioch. Bohemund III died in 1201 while still in possession of his principality.

## Bohemund IV and the County of Tripoli
Bohemund IV became the count of Tripoli in 1187 and succeeded his father in Antioch in 1201. His reign was characterized by a long dispute with Leo of Armenia over the claims of Raymund Rhupen. Bohemund IV was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and by the Templars. Conversely, the Hospitallers supported Leo. 

Bohemund IV's relationship with the papacy and the empire was volatile; he was excommunicated in 1230 for his enmity toward the Hospitallers and shifted his allegiance to and from Emperor Frederick II during the crusade of 1228–29. He died in 1233, having recently concluded peace with the Hospitallers and been released from excommunication by Gregory IX.

## Later Bohemunds and the Fall of Tripoli
Bohemund V (d. 1251), son of Bohemund IV and Plaisance, succeeded his father as prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. He maintained an alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia until 1251, when peace was achieved through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund V resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving Antioch to be governed by a commune and its bailiff.

Bohemund VI (b. 1237, d. 1275), son of Bohemund V and Luciana, succeeded his father in 1251. He allied himself with the Mongols against the Egyptian sultan, but lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268. By the time of his death in 1275, he was only the count of Tripoli.

The dynasty ended with Bohemund VII, son of Bohemund VI and Sibylla. His reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli, and in 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue, and the county of Tripoli became extinct shortly thereafter when the city was captured.
