# Outskirts of Empire in Asia and the Lands of the Eastern Caliphate

The study of Asian empires and their peripheral territories is documented through various historical accounts and geographical surveys, ranging from the administration of the Eastern Caliphate to the expansion of the Mongol and British empires.

## The Eastern Caliphate and Asia Minor
The geography and administration of the Eastern Caliphate are detailed in the works of G. Le Strange, specifically in *The Lands of the Eastern Caliphate* (1905) and *Baghdad under the Abbasid Caliphate* (1901). These studies contribute to the broader understanding of the region, alongside the work of W. M. Ramsay regarding the historical geography of Asia Minor and his accounts of St Paul the Traveller.

In Asia Minor, specific urban centers illustrate the transition from ancient to later periods. Kir-sheher, located in the Angora vilayet on a tributary of the Kizil Irmak (Halys), serves as a primary example. Originally known as Mocissus, the town gained importance during the Byzantine period when Emperor Justinian enlarged it, renamed it Justinianopolis, and established it as the capital of a large division of Cappadocia. In more recent records, Kir-sheher is noted for its production of excellent carpets and a hot chalybeate spring located on its outskirts. Its population was recorded at approximately 9,000, including 700 Christians, the majority of whom were Armenians.

## The Mongol Empires and Central Asia
The land ways of Asia were dominated by the Great Empire of Jengis Khan and his successors. This era included the rise and victories of the Mongols, the travels of Marco Polo, and the establishment of the Yuan Dynasty by Kublai Khan. The Mongol influence extended into various regions, including the Kipchak Empire, the Mongol Empire of India, and the territories of the Tsar of Muscovy, as well as the later activities of Timurlane.

Central Asian geography, particularly the regions between South Russia, Turkestan, and the triangle of Manchuria, is characterized by climatic insecurity. These lands, consisting of steppes, deserts, forests, and frozen lands, are described as treacherous to man due to cycles of killing droughts and varying rainfall.

Further documentation of Central Asia is found in the works of Vambery, including *Travels in Central Asia* (1864), *Sketches of Central Asia* (1868), and *History of Bokhara* (1873), as well as Khanikov's *Bokhara* (1845) and Lipsky's *Upper Bukhara* (1902). Other relevant surveys include Fedchenko's "Sketch of the Zarafshan Valley" (1870) and Lord Ronaldshay's *Outskirts of Empire in Asia* (1904).

## British Imperialism in India
The British experience in India represents a different form of imperial expansion, where a London trading company dominated an empire more populous than the domains of the British crown. To the English public, India was viewed as a remote and romantically unreal land of "countless brown millions" and mysterious temples.

This distance created a lack of effective supervision over the company's proceedings. English generals and officials, operating in a land they found strange, were sometimes accused of cruelties and extortions. Notable figures in this administration included Clive, who faced a vote of censure from Parliament and committed suicide in 1774, and Warren Hastings, who was impeached in 1788 and subsequently acquitted in 1792.

## European Perceptions of Asia
European exploration was often driven by the desire to reach Asia. In 1492, Columbus sailed west, believing he was sailing round the world to Asia. Upon landing in the new world, he brought back gold, cotton, and "wild-eyed painted Indians," leading to the belief that he had found India. Consequently, the islands he discovered were named the West Indies. Columbus maintained the belief that he had reached Asia until his death.
