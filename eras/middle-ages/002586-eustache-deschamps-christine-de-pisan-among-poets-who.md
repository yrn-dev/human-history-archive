# Eustache Deschamps and Christine de Pisan

Eustache Deschamps and Christine de Pisan were among the poets who most abundantly cultivated the ballade, a highly-finished and elaborate form of poetry that flourished in France during the reign of Charles V. This poetic form, which originated from the Italian *canzone de ballo* and took a modern form in Provencal literature, became instantly popular in France, leading to an incalculable output of such poems.

## Eustache Deschamps: Life and Career
Eustache Deschamps, also called Morel, was a French poet born in Champagne at Vertus around 1346. He received an education at Reims, where it is stated that his uncle, Guillaume de Machaut, provided him with lessons in the art of versification. Around 1360, Deschamps moved to the university of Orleans to study the seven liberal arts and law.

His professional life was closely tied to the French crown. In approximately 1367, he entered the service of the king as a royal messenger, a role that took him on missions to Moravia, Hungary, and Bohemia. By 1372, he had been appointed *huissier d'armes* to Charles V. Deschamps held several other significant offices throughout his career, including governor of Fismes, squire to the Dauphin, and *bailli* of both Senlis and Valois.

## Political Views and Personal Hardships
Deschamps' life and work were deeply affected by the conflicts between France and England. As a child, he witnessed the English invasion of 1358, the march on Chartres, and the siege of Reims; he also saw the signing of the treaty of Bretigny. In 1380, the same year his patron Charles V died, the English burnt his house at Vertus.

These experiences fostered a violent hatred of the English, which Deschamps expressed through numerous appeals to move the war into England. He famously prophesied that England would be destroyed so completely that no one would be able to point to its ruins. His temper was further embittered by his own misfortunes and the general miseries of France. He frequently railed against women, lamented the woes of his country, and complained of poverty.

## Literary Contributions and Style
Deschamps is regarded as a master of the chant royal and the ballade. His historical and patriotic poems are noted for their value because they provide a faithful picture of the anarchy in France without the glamour found in the works of Froissart. He used his poetry to inveigh against the vices of the clergy, heavy taxes, and those who enriched themselves at the people's expense. A typical example of this is his ballad featuring the refrain "*Sà, de l'argent; sà, de l'argent*."

In addition to his patriotic works, Deschamps wrote the *Miroir de mariage*, a satire against women consisting of 13,000 lines, which is credited as the prototype for the mother-in-law found in French farce. He also authored an *Art poétique* titled *L'Art de dictier et de fere chancons, balades, virelais et rondeaulx*. In this work, he distinguished between "music proper" (which he called artificial because it can be acquired through study) and poetry (which he called natural, describing it as a gift rather than an acquired art). He emphasized the harmony of verse, operating under the contemporary assumption that all poetry was intended to be sung.

Deschamps' work represents a transition in French poetry; alongside Guillaume de Machaut, he moved away from the impersonal, long, and formless narrations of the *trouvères* toward more exacting and complicated verse that reflected the poets' own personal affairs.

## Relationship with Contemporaries
Deschamps maintained connections with other notable figures of his time. He sent a copy of his works to Geoffrey Chaucer, accompanied by a ballade (No. 285) in which he addressed Chaucer as a god of worldly love in Albie and of the Rose in the "terre Angélique."

## Christine de Pisan
Christine de Pisan is identified as one of the poets who abundantly cultivated the ballade during the period of its popularity in France. In her work *Faiz du Sage Roy Charles* (V. of France), she provided practical explanations regarding naval warfare. Specifically, she described the use of light vessels to throw pots of hot powder or "mol savon" (soft soap) at enemy ships. She explained that when these pots broke, the soap would make the decks slippery, preventing the adversaries from maintaining their footing and causing them to fall into the water.
