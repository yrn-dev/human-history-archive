# Krestanskych and Besedni reci

## Overview of Thomas of Stitny
The works *O obecnych vecech Krestanskych* (on general Christian matters) and *Besedni reci* (roughly translated as "learned entertainments") are attributed to Thomas of Stitny, who lived approximately from 1331 to 1401. These texts are considered to be of significant value and are reflective of the high intellectual level attained by Bohemia during the 14th century. Stitny is recognized as one of the most important writers of that period to utilize the national language for philosophical and religious subjects.

## Intellectual Context and Influence
The ideas contained within the writings of Thomas of Stitny share many similarities with those of his contemporary, Wycliffe. Along with other contemporaries whose Bohemian works have since perished, Stitny is identified as a forerunner of Huss. 

Jan Huss, like many of his peers in Bohemia, contributed greatly to the development of the Bohemian language, writing in both Latin and his native tongue. Among Huss's most important Bohemian works is the *Vyklad viry, desatera Boziho prikazani, a patere* (exposition of the creed, the ten commandments and the Lord's Prayer), written in 1412. Other notable works by Huss include the *Postilla* (a collection of sermons), a large collection of letters—including touching letters written while in prison—and treatises titled *O poznani cesty prave k spaseni* (the true road to salvation) and *O svatokupectvi* (on simony).

### Early Church and Social Issues
In the early Christian church, Paul dealt with concerns regarding human nature and the influence of heathenism. One specific incident involved a man who married his stepmother after his father's death, a case of incest that Paul felt was imperative to remove from the church, noting that such acts were generally abominated by Greeks and the Bani Israel. Paul also addressed questions of asceticism—the Greek view that nature was vile and marriage wrong—and the social freedom introduced by Christianity. This included questions regarding the permissible degree of freedom for Christian women, specifically whether women of lower classes must adhere to the restrictions of higher social classes and if a woman could participate in Christian public meetings as a free child of God.

### Integration in Norway
In Norway, King Hakon sought to introduce Christianity to a heathen country characterized by heathenish sacrifice. To avoid alienating the common people and powerful figures, Hakon initially practiced his faith in private, observing Friday fasts, Sundays, and certain holy-days. He mandated that the festival of Yule begin at the same time as it did for Christian people, requiring the brewing of malt into ale to keep the Yule holy. Hakon eventually worked to baptize those closest to him and later requested a bishop and teachers from England to proclaim Christianity across the land. This process involved consecrating churches, appointing priests, and summoning bondes to a Thing to invite them to accept the faith.

### Theological and Social Critiques
Some historical analyses suggest gaps between the theological assertions of Christianity and the gospels. It is noted that the gospels provide little support for doctrines generally deemed necessary for salvation by various denominations, such as the Atonement, the Trinity, or the claim that Jesus was the Jewish Messiah or part of the godhead. Furthermore, while many Christian cults observed a Sunday (transferred from the Jewish Sabbath), it is noted that Jesus broke the Sabbath, stating it was made for man.

In later periods, the idea of "Christian communism" provided a focal point for common people, led by educated dissentient priests and doctors of the Wycliffe type. However, this contact between the educated class and the illiterate masses was interrupted as Lutheranism shifted leadership toward Protestant Princes, a transition that destroyed the poor scholar and priest class.

## Perceptions of Christianity in the Roman Empire
During the Roman Empire, the heathen populace often labeled Christians as "atheists," shouting, "Away with the atheists! To the lions with the Christians!" This charge of atheism was likely based on the absence of idolatry within Christian worship.
