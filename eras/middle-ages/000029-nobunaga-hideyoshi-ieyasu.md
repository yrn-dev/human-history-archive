# Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, and Ieyasu

The second half of the sixteenth century in Japan was defined by the combined efforts of three men—Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu—who worked to restore peace and order to an empire that had been torn by war for five consecutive centuries. Born within eight years of one another (Nobunaga in 1534, Hideyoshi in 1536, and Ieyasu in 1542), these three figures emerged as central leaders during a period of intense regional conflict.

## Oda Nobunaga
Oda Nobunaga (1534–82) was a member of the Oda family, one of the "8 Generals of Kwanto." His career was marked by significant military achievements, including a victory at Okehazama and the seizure of Ise. Nobunaga established alliances with Shingen and Ieyasu and eventually escorted the shogun to the capital, effectively acting as shogun. He is noted for his friendly attitude toward Christians and for implementing currency reform.

Nobunaga's character was described as impetuous and lacking in patience and discrimination. He treated high-ranking barons with the same lack of consideration as humble retainers, a trait that often hindered his plans. This temperament is captured in a famous Japanese couplet where Nobunaga is represented as saying, "I'll kill the cuckoo if it won't sing." Despite these faults, his demeanor in battle reflected a philosophy that life is short and that a man should make his death glorious. Nobunaga perished at the age of forty-nine.

## Toyotomi Hideyoshi
Hideyoshi, who served under Nobunaga, was a critical factor in Nobunaga's success. Born Nakamura Hiyoshi and known in adulthood as Tokichi, he later took the name Hashiba before finally becoming known as Toyotomi Hideyoshi. He led Oda armies in collisions with the Mori family and later served as a commander in Korea.

Following Nobunaga's death, Hideyoshi rose to prominence, though his relationship with the remaining Oda family was complex. He treated Nobunaga's son, Nobukatsu, with a mix of diplomacy and pressure. During the Komaki War, Hideyoshi eventually sought peace with Nobukatsu and Ieyasu, likely influenced by the threat of Chosokabe Motochika, who had brought the island of Shikoku under his sway and posed a danger to Osaka.

Hideyoshi's influence extended to the administration of the empire through his laws and the appointment of senior ministers, such as Ukita Hideiye and Kagekatsu. He also formed a personal bond with Ieyasu by arranging for Ieyasu to take Hideyoshi's younger sister into his household, while Hideyoshi sent his own mother to Okazaki as a hostage to ensure friendly relations.

## Tokugawa Ieyasu
The Tokugawa chieftain, Ieyasu, was a long-time ally of Nobunaga, fighting side-by-side with him on multiple occasions. A significant military collaboration occurred when Nobunaga's eldest son, Nobutada, and Ieyasu united their forces to destroy the army of Takeda Katsuyori at Temmoku-zan. As a reward for this victory, Nobunaga granted the province of Suruga to Ieyasu.

Ieyasu's position was severely jeopardized by the Mitsuhide incident. While visiting Sakai, Ieyasu learned of the catastrophe and initially wished to avenge the assassin. However, due to the small size of his following, he was forced to flee eastward, avoiding highroads to escape efforts by Mitsuhide to waylay him.

In the years following the Komaki War, Ieyasu remained aloof from Kyoto until September 1586, when he finally visited Osaka and Kyoto. Later, Ieyasu provided assistance to Nobukatsu, who had plotted to assert the administrative supremacy of the Oda family after the suicide of Nobutaka and the marginalization of Samboshi. Ieyasu's decision to aid Nobukatsu was partly driven by his previous alliance with Nobunaga and a desire to avoid accepting a position inferior to that claimed by Hideyoshi.

## The Oda Succession and Decline
The transition of power after Nobunaga's death saw the decline of the Oda house. Nobunaga's son Nobutada died in 82, and his other son, Nobutaka, committed suicide. Nobunaga's grandson, Samboshi (who later took the name Oda Hidenobu), was the nominal heir but was considered incompetent. Consequently, he was denied an eminent position and instead became a vassal of Hideyoshi with an income of three hundred thousand koku.

The house of Hideyoshi eventually fell due to the actions of his son, Hideyori. Hideyori had been born to a concubine who was one of the three daughters of Asai Nagamasa. Following her advice, Hideyori acted in defiance of Ieyasu, which led to the collapse of Hideyoshi's house and served as an unconscious vengeance for the fate of Nobunaga.
