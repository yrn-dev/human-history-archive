# Bavaria and Thuringia

Bavaria and Thuringia are regions of Germany that have featured prominently in the religious, political, and cultural history of the area, from early missionary efforts and the formation of the German kingdom to the later developments of the Holy Roman Empire and the 19th century.

## Early Religious Missions
In the early 8th century, the missionary Wynfrith, known as Saint Boniface (680-754), worked to evangelize Germany. Born into a Saxon family in Devonshire and educated at Exeter and Nutcell, Boniface was commissioned by Pope Gregory II in 719 to counteract the influence of Irish monks and spread the faith. After crossing the Alps, Boniface visited both Bavaria and Thuringia. 

His work was interrupted by the political climate in Frisia; he had previously been forced to leave that region due to the hostility of King Radbod, who was at war with Charles Martel. However, upon hearing that Radbod had died, Boniface hurried back to Frisia to preach under the direction of Willibrord, the first bishop of Utrecht. Following this three-year period, Boniface returned to Thuringia and Hesse around 722, where he converted and baptized many heathens and won over several chieftains.

## Political Formation and the Carolingian Era
The regions of Bavaria and Thuringia were central to the division of the Carolingian empire. Louis "the German" (804-876), the third son of Emperor Louis I and Irmengarde, received Bavaria and its neighboring lands in 817, though he did not begin governing until 825. Louis's tenure was marked by conflicts with Slavonic tribes on the eastern frontier and internal family struggles involving his stepmother Judith and his brothers.

Following the death of the elder Louis in 840 and a subsequent defeat of Lothair at Fontenoy in 841, the Treaty of Verdun was concluded in August 843. Through this treaty, Louis the German received the majority of the Carolingian lands east of the Rhine. His territories included Franconia, Saxony, Thuringia, and Bavaria, the latter of which served as the center of his government at Regensburg. Louis is regarded as the founder of the German kingdom, during which time he held synods and assemblies to decree laws for the government of state and church.

## Medieval Power Struggles and Territorial Shifts
During the 12th century, the regions remained sites of political instability. Following the death of Henry V in 1125, Lothair was elected German king. To secure support against Frederick II of Hohenstaufen, Lothair gave his daughter Gertrude in marriage to Henry the Proud, the new duke of Bavaria. This period of struggle saw disturbances in Thuringia, Saxony, and Lorraine, though order was eventually restored once the Hohenstaufen resistance was defeated.

In the 13th and 14th centuries, Bavaria experienced internal divisions. Louis IV (also known as Louis V), surnamed the Bavarian (c. 1287-1347), inherited Upper Bavaria and the Palatinate jointly with his brother Rudolph. After a period of variance and a demand for partition, Upper Bavaria was divided in 1310, with Louis receiving the north-western portion. Louis later became a candidate for the German throne and gained renown after defeating Frederick I of Austria at Gammelsdorf on November 9, 1313.

Other notable territorial developments occurred on the eastern frontier of Bavaria. In 976, Leopold, a member of the Franconian Babenberg family, became the margrave of the East Mark. This district, approximately 60 miles in breadth, eventually grew into the duchy of Austria.

## Later History and Culture
By the 19th century, Bavaria continued to be a significant political entity. The crown prince Louis (who became king in 1805) emerged as a leader of the anti-French party in Bavaria. Despite strained relations with Napoleon, Louis won military laurels in the campaigns of 1807 and 1809. In 1815, he attended the Congress of Vienna, where he worked to secure the return of art treasures from France and the restoration of Lorraine and Alsace to Germany.

The regions also contributed to the arts and sciences. The novelist Eugenie John (1825-1887), writing under the pseudonym E. Marlitt, was born in Arnstadt, Thuringia. Additionally, the artist Cornelius was called to Munich by the crown prince (later Louis I) to direct decorations for the Glyptothek, eventually becoming the director of the Munich Academy between 1824 and 1825.

In the field of geology, the "Bunter" (from the German *Buntsandstein*) refers to a subdivision of Triassic rocks. In Thuringia, Hesse, and northern and central Germany, these deposits are typically conformable with the underlying Permian formation.
