# Plato and the Transition to Christian Thought

The intellectual relationship between the philosophy of Plato and early Christian theology is characterized by a complex transition, most notably exemplified in the life and writings of St Augustine in the West and Synesius of Cyrene in the East. These figures represent the difficult movement from the Platonic tradition to the teachings of Christ.

## The Platonic System and the Divine Nature
Plato sought to explore the mysterious nature of the Deity, drawing upon his own meditations or the traditional knowledge held by the priests of Egypt. In his contemplation of the first self-existent and necessary cause of the universe, Plato encountered difficulties in conceiving how a simple unity of essence could encompass the infinite variety of successive and distinct ideas that form the intellectual world. Furthermore, he struggled to understand how a purely incorporeal Being could execute a perfect model and mould the independent and rude chaos with a "plastic hand."

To address these difficulties, Plato considered the divine nature through a threefold modification:
*   The first cause.
*   The reason, or *Logos*.
*   The soul or spirit of the universe.

In the Platonic system, these three original principles were sometimes represented as three Gods, linked by an ineffable and mysterious generation. The *Logos* was specifically viewed as the Creator and Governor of the world, and as the Son of an Eternal Father. These secret doctrines were whispered in the gardens of the academy and, according to later disciples, required thirty years of assiduous study to be fully understood.

## St Augustine and the Philosophers
St Augustine's relationship with the philosophers was marked by a perceived difference in the freedom of expression. Augustine noted that while philosophers speak with free words (*liberis verbis loquuntur philosophi*), the Christian position differs in its rejection of the notion of two or three principles, or two or three Gods.

Despite this distinction, Augustine's influence remained profound in later centuries. In 17th-century France, the theistic religion of the Cartesian Malebranche borrowed its imagery from Augustine. Additionally, the Jansenist revival led by Jean Du Vergier de Hauranne (M. de St Cyran) and Cornelius Jansen was rooted in the mysticism of St Augustine. Du Vergier sought to reform Catholicism on Augustinian lines, arguing that Augustine had preserved the spirit of St Paul by coupling it with a high sacramental theory of the Church.

## Comparative Theological Perspectives
The transition from Plato to Christ is a central theme in the theological literature of the 4th century. While the Greek fathers—such as the Cappadocians (Basil the Great, Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus)—were philosophically trained defenders of the faith, the works of St Augustine, particularly his *Confessions*, are regarded as possessing a depth and general human interest that surpasses the poetical autobiography of Gregory of Nazianzus.

In the broader context of early church philosophy, other figures also attempted to reconcile these traditions. Boetius, who was deeply versed in the philosophies of both Aristotle and Plato, explained the unity of the Trinity through the "indifference" of the three persons.
