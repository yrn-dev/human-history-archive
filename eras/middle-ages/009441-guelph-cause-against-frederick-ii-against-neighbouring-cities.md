# The Guelph Cause Against Frederick II and Neighboring Cities

The Guelph cause represented a political and social faction in medieval Italy and surrounding regions that frequently aligned itself against the authority of the Holy Roman Empire, most notably during the reign of Emperor Frederick II. This struggle was characterized by intense urban warfare, shifting alliances between city-states, and a recurring conflict between imperial power and the papacy.

## Conflict with Frederick II

The opposition to Frederick II was deeply intertwined with the interests of the papacy. Frederick's relationship with several popes was marked by broken promises and mutual distrust. Pope Innocent III had conditioned Frederick's succession as Emperor on promises to suppress heresy in Germany, relinquish the crown in South Italy and Sicily, and free the German clergy from taxation. Frederick failed to keep these promises, preferring to reside in Sicily. 

Subsequent popes continued this friction. Pope Honorius III sought to ensure Frederick fulfilled his oath to undertake a crusade, but Frederick's focus on reforms in Sicily caused significant trouble. By the papacy of Gregory IX, the conflict escalated; Gregory excommunicated Frederick on September 29, 1227, citing the emperor's continued neglect of his vows and refusal to crusade. Gregory further challenged Frederick by raising an insurrection in Germany in 1230 and later releasing Frederick's subjects from their allegiance on March 24, 1239. In response, Frederick invaded the Papal States and blocked a general council scheduled for Easter 1241.

## The Guelph Cause in Bologna

Bologna was a free and independent city by the time of its first commune constitution around 1123. Between the 12th and 14th centuries, the city was frequently engaged in war and became a strong supporter of the Guelph cause. This support manifested as opposition not only to Frederick II but also to neighboring cities in Emilia and Romagna. A significant event in this struggle occurred in 1249, when the Bolognese captured Enzio, the son of the emperor, and held him prisoner for the remainder of his life.

## The Guelph-Ghibelline Strife in Florence

In Florence, the division between the Guelph and Ghibelline parties emerged from a private feud in 1215 between the families of Uberti and Buondelmonte. Following the killing of Buondelmonte de' Buondelmonti by the Amidei and Uberti, the city's seventy-two families split: thirty-nine became Guelphs under the leadership of the Buondelmonte, while the remainder became Ghibellines under the Uberti.

The conflict intensified in 1248 when Frederick II sent his natural son, Frederick of Antioch, into Florence with 1,600 German knights. The Guelphs were driven from the city, seeking refuge in Capraia and Montevarchi, while the Ghibellines destroyed the palaces and towers of the Guelph nobles. However, a popular rebellion eventually reduced the power of the podesta and established a more democratic constitution. Following the death of Frederick II in December 1250, the exiled Guelphs were recalled. The Ghibellines were later expelled entirely in July 1258, and the towers of the Uberti were razed.

## Regional Extensions and Later Conflicts

The struggle between these factions extended to other Italian cities and territories. In Padua, the powerful Carraresi family initially sided with the Ghibellines but later switched to the Guelph cause, which brought them into conflict with Ezzelino da Romano.

In Lucca, the Ghibelline Castruccio Castracani degli Antellinelli was exiled as a youth by the ascendant Guelphs. Castruccio later returned as a condottiere and lord of Lucca, warring incessantly against the Florentines. He served as imperial vicar under Frederick of Austria and later under Emperor Louis the Bavarian. Castruccio's career ended in 1328, and the fortunes of his children were subsequently wrecked by a "Guelphic triumph."

Outside of Italy, Frederick II also faced opposition from Duke Frederick II of Austria (known as the Quarrelsome). The emperor claimed certain rights in Austria and summoned the duke to Italian diets. When the duke refused to appear, the emperor placed him under a ban and invaded the country in 1236, aided by the king of Bohemia and the duke of Bavaria. Although the emperor declared the duchies of Austria and Styria vacant and made Vienna an imperial city, the duke regained his power by 1239.
