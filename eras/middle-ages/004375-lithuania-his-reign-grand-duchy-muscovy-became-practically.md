# Lithuania

## Relations with Muscovy
The interactions between Lithuania and the grand-duchy of Muscovy were characterized by shifting political alliances, territorial disputes, and ecclesiastical influence. During the reign of Basil I. Dmitrevich (1389–1425), the grand-duke of Muscovy married Sophia, who was the daughter of Vitovt, the grand-duke of Lithuania. Despite this familial connection, the two powers often clashed. In 1425, Vitovt elevated the Bulgarian Gregory Tsamblak to the metropolitan see of Kiev; this ecclesiastical appointment had the immediate political effect of weakening Muscovy's hold over the south-western Russian states.

Later, during the reign of Basil II. (1425–1462), the realm of Muscovy suffered severe depredations as it was continually overrun by both the Tatars and the Lithuanians.

## Territorial Transitions and Smolensk
Certain regions experienced transitions of power between Lithuanian and Muscovite control. The town of Byelev, located in the government of Tula on the left bank of the Oka, belonged to Lithuania at the end of the 14th century. By 1468, Byelev was raised to the rank of a principality that remained dependent on Lithuania. However, toward the end of the 15th century, this principality began to attach itself to the grand-duchy of Moscow, and it was ultimately united to Russia by Ivan III.

In 1512, Basil III. (1505–1533) captured Smolensk, which served as the great eastern fortress of Poland. This capture was achieved primarily through the assistance of Prince Michael Glinsky, a rebel Lithuanian who provided Basil III. with engineers and artillery from western Europe. The loss of Smolensk represented the first serious injury Muscovy inflicted upon Poland, and Sigismund of Poland was compelled by exigencies to acquiesce in its surrender in 1522.
