# Ladislas and the Succession of the Bohemian Throne

## The Accession of Albert of Austria
Following the formal recognition of the Compacts at Iglau in Moravia, Sigismund proceeded to Prague, where he was accepted as king. Upon Sigismund's death in 1437, he was succeeded by his son-in-law, Albert of Austria, who was chosen as king by the estates. Albert's reign over Bohemia was brief, lasting less than two years before his death.

## The Succession Crisis
The death of Albert created a period of political instability regarding the succession to the throne. At the time, it was known that Albert's widow, Elizabeth, was pregnant and would shortly give birth to a child. However, the legal nature of the Bohemian crown—specifically whether it was hereditary or elective—remained an undecided question until 1627.

This uncertainty led to the formation of two rival factions among the nobility:
*   **The National Party:** Led by George of Podebrad.
*   **The Roman or Austrian Division:** Led by Ulrich of Rosenberg.

## The Letter of Peace
To resolve the conflict, the two parties reached an agreement known as the "Letter of Peace" (*list mirny*). Under the terms of this agreement, the signatories pledged to recognize the Compacts and to support John of Rokycan as the archbishop of Prague. John of Rokycan had been chosen by the estates according to an agreement made with the Compacts, despite the fact that the Church of Rome refused to recognize him. In exchange, the national party withdrew the candidature of Prince Casimir of Poland for the throne, which cleared the path for the eventual succession of Albert's heir.

## The Birth and Reign of Ladislas
On February 22, 1440, Queen Elizabeth gave birth to a son named Ladislas. The Bohemians formally acknowledged Ladislas as their king, although this occurred only after Albert, duke of Bavaria, had declined the crown. 

Despite his recognition as king, Ladislas remained in Austria. He was placed under the guardianship of his uncle, Frederick, duke of Styria, who later became Emperor Frederick III. During this period, Bohemia lacked a regular government and continued to be characterized by constant conflicts between the rival parties of the nobility.

## Constitutional Developments in Bohemia
In 1446, a general meeting of the estates took place, involving those from Bohemia as well as Moravia, Lusatia, and Silesia (referred to as the "lands of the Bohemian crown"). This meeting is noted for its exceptional importance to the constitutional history of the region. It was decreed that members of the estates should be divided into three bodies, known as *curiae*.
