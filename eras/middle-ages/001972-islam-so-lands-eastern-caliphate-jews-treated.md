# The Eastern Caliphate and the Status of the Jews

## The Foundation and Splendor of Bagdad
The city of Bagdad, the old or round city, was established in A.D. 762 by the caliph Mansur of the Abbasid dynasty. Located on the west side of the Tigris, just north of the Isa canal, the city was designed in concentric circles with a diameter of one mile. At its center stood the palace of the caliph and the mosque, and the city featured four gates oriented toward the four points of the compass.

Bagdad experienced rapid growth, including the immediate development of the suburb of Rusafa on the eastern bank. Following the capture of the round city by Mamun in 814, Rusafa became the most important part of the capital. The city reached its peak of prosperity between its foundation and the death of Mamun in 833. During this era, the walled area encompassing both sides of the river spanned five miles across and is said to have housed a population of 2,000,000 people. Bagdad served as the political capital for the majority of Islam and the religious capital for all of Islam. In terms of wealth and commerce, it far surpassed Cordova, with which it shared global supremacy in science, art, and literature.

## Treatment of the Jews in the Eastern Caliphate
In the lands of the eastern caliphate, as was the case in Spanish Islam, the Jews were treated relatively with favour. This environment allowed Bagdad to become the capital of oriental Judaism. The seat of the *resh galutha*, or *exilarch*, was transferred to Bagdad from Pumbedita in Babylonia. From that period onward, the Jews played a significant role in the city.

## Political Transitions and the Abbasid Decline
The Abbasid dynasty eventually faced a downfall that began with the rise of a Turkish body-guard under Mo'tassim, the successor to Mamun. Mo'tassim founded the city of Samarra, leading the court and caliph to desert Bagdad for fifty-eight years. In A.D. 865, Mosta'in returned to Bagdad in an attempt to escape the tyranny of the Turkish guard, but the city was besieged and captured. 

From that point until their final collapse, the Abbasid caliphs were mere puppets. Real power was held successively by the Seljuks, the Buyids, and the Turkish guard. Despite this loss of political autonomy, the caliphs remained the religious heads of Islam, and Bagdad remained its capital. Although it fell from its initial eminence, the city continued to be one of the richest and most splendid in the world.

## The Fall of Bagdad
The decline of the city accelerated during the final century of the caliphates. By the close of the 12th century, the western portion of the city was described by travellers as being almost in ruins, while the eastern half contained large uninhabited spaces. The definitive end of the Abbasid caliphate of Bagdad occurred in 1258, when the city was captured by Hulagu (Hulaku), the grandson of Jenghiz Khan. With this event, Bagdad's status as the religious center of Islam passed away, and it ceased to be a city of the first rank, though it continued to be referred to as the "glorious city" in Turkish official documents.
