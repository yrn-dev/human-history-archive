# The Aftermath of the Death of John Huss

The execution of John Huss on July 6, 1415, served as a catalyst for widespread civil unrest and prolonged religious conflict within Bohemia. Huss, a learned Czech and rector of the university of Prague, had delivered lectures based on the doctrines of the Oxford teacher Wycliffe. His teachings led to his excommunication in 1412. Despite receiving a letter of safe conduct from Sigismund, king of the Romans, Huss was tried for heresy by the Council of Constance. He was ordered to recant his opinions but refused to do so unless he was convinced of his error through the authority of Scripture or logical argument. Consequently, he was burnt alive.

## The Protestatio Bohemorum and the Outbreak of War

The immediate result of Huss's death was the outbreak of civil war in Bohemia, where the majority of the population held him in great reverence. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia convened in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum* to the Council of Constance. In this document, they protested the execution of Huss, describing him as a "good, just and catholic man" who had been known for years by his fame, conduct, and life, and asserting that he had been convicted of no offence.

The nobles further declared that anyone claiming heresy existed in Bohemia was a "vile traitor," a "liar," and a "son of the devil." They pledged to defend the law of Jesus Christ and its steadfast, humble, and pious preachers at the cost of their own blood, regardless of human decrees. This protest functioned as a declaration of war against the Roman church and marked the beginning of the Hussite wars. Although the council summoned the nobles to appear before its tribunal, they refused.

## The Hussite Movement and Internal Divisions

The resulting Hussite movement was largely democratic in nature. This influence was reflected in the literature of the period, which utilized the national Bohemian language rather than Latin. The movement eventually split into different factions:

*   **The Taborites:** The extreme party of the Hussite movement.
*   **The Calixtines (or Utraquists):** The more moderate faction.

Much of the controversial literature from this era was later systematically destroyed by the Jesuits following the battle of the White Hill in 1620, particularly the writings of the Taborites. However, some works from the moderate Calixtines survived, including *Lives of the Priests of Tabor* and *Of the Great Torment of the Holy Church*, both of which were written with hostility toward the Taborite community. Additionally, the *Postilla* by Archbishop John of Rokycan was preserved.

## The Crusades Against the Hussites

In 1419, an insurrection of the Hussites occurred, initiating a series of religious wars. In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade to destroy the Hussites, Wycliffites, and all other heretics in Bohemia. This invitation attracted soldiers of fortune and "drifting blackguardism" from across Europe. 

Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites—who conducted their affairs on extreme democratic lines—resisted the invaders. The crusaders besieged Prague but failed to capture the city and suffered a series of reverses that led to their retreat. A second crusade in 1421, as well as two subsequent crusades, also failed. Eventually, the Hussites suffered from internal dissensions, which encouraged a fifth crusade in 1431 led by Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg. The Catholic Crusaders were eventually dissolved before the Hussites at Domazlice in 1431, and the Hussites came to terms with the church in 1436.

## Political Context in Bohemia

The religious conflict occurred during the reign of Wenceslas IV, a sovereign characterized by weakness and irresolution. The hold of the Roman Church on Bohemia had been weakened even before Huss, during the reign of King Charles, by priests such as Waldhauser and Milic who attacked clerical immorality. The Great Schism, featuring rival pontiffs, further alienated the Bohemian people. While Wenceslas was often irresolute, Queen Sophia openly favored the cause of Huss, who served as her confessor for a time.

## Legacy and Influence

The spirit of John Huss's refusal to recant without logical or scriptural proof was later mirrored in the 16th century by the monk Luther. In 1517, Luther challenged the Papacy at Wittenberg, specifically opposing the selling of indulgences. When summoned to the diet of Worms by Emperor Charles, Luther refused to recant his views unless convinced of his error, acting "entirely in the spirit of Huss." However, unlike Huss, Luther was protected by powerful princes and did not suffer the same fate.
