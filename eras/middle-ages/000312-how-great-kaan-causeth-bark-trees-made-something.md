# The Paper Currency of the Great Kaan

The Great Kaan established a system of currency throughout his country utilizing a material derived from the bark of trees. This substance, processed to resemble sheets of paper, was mandated to pass for money across the entirety of his realm.

## Production and Material
The process begins with the selection of a specific tree, identified by Marco Polo as the Mulberry tree, which is so abundant that entire districts are filled with them. The leaves of these trees serve as food for silkworms. To create the currency, a fine white skin or bast is extracted from the tree, located specifically between the thick outer bark and the wood.

This material is then manufactured into black sheets that resemble paper. While Polo identifies the source as the Mulberry tree, other archival notes suggest the material may actually be derived from the *Broussonetia papyrifera*, a tree belonging to the same order as the Mulberry (Moraceae).

## Denominations and Value
Once the black sheets are prepared, they are cut into various sizes, with each size corresponding to a specific monetary value. The archive records the following denominations:
*   The smallest size is worth a half tornesel.
*   The next size is worth one tornesel.
*   Larger sizes are valued at half a silver groat of Venice, one whole groat, two groats, five groats, and ten groats.
*   Higher denominations exist, starting at one Bezant of gold, and increasing to three and up to ten Bezants.

The Great Kaan produces such vast quantities of this money annually that it is said to equal all the treasure in the world, despite the fact that the production costs him nothing.

## Authentication and Legal Status
The paper money is issued with the same authority and solemnity as if it were made of pure gold or silver. To ensure authenticity, a variety of officials are required to write their names and apply their seals to every piece of paper. 

The final step of authentication is performed by the chief officer deputed by the Kaan. This officer uses a seal smeared with vermilion to impress a red print upon the paper. Once this red seal is applied, the money is considered authentic. The state enforces the integrity of this system with extreme severity; any individual found forging the currency is punished with death.

## Historical Observations
The ability of the Great Kaan to create money at will from tree bark has been likened to the possession of the true Philosopher's Stone. This system allowed for a massive volume of currency to circulate, though archival notes indicate that the exchange rate of these bank-notes was exceedingly low. For example, records mention 3,600,000 *ting* of silver in bank-notes, which equated to 180 million *liang* of silver.
