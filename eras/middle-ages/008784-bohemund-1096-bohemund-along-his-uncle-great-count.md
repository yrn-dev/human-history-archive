# Bohemund

The name Bohemund is associated with a dynasty of princes who ruled the principality of Antioch and, from the time of Bohemund IV, the county of Tripoli. The history of these rulers is inextricably linked to the establishment and maintenance of these eastern territories.

## Bohemund I
Bohemund I (c. 1058–1111), originally named Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, the *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*, and his first wife. He served his father during a major attack on the East Roman empire between 1080 and 1085. During Guiscard's absence from 1082 to 1084, Bohemund commanded the Normans and penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly, though he was repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early conflict with Alexius significantly influenced his future career and the trajectory of the First Crusade.

Following Robert Guiscard's death in 1085, a war broke out between Bohemund and his younger half-brother, Roger, who had succeeded to the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. The conflict was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions. In 1096, while attacking Amalfi alongside his uncle, the count of Sicily, Bohemund was inspired by the passing crusaders. Seeking to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* and carve out an eastern principality, he led a Norman army across the Adriatic to Constantinople.

Arriving in April 1097, Bohemund did homage to Emperor Alexius. While he may have negotiated for a principality at Antioch, he received little encouragement. Nevertheless, Bohemund became the real leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch, guiding the host across Asia Minor. He visited Jerusalem in 1099 with Baldwin I.

## Bohemund II
Bohemund II (1108–1131) was the son of Bohemund I and Constance of France. Born in the year his father was defeated at Durazzo, he arrived in Antioch from Apulia in 1126. In 1127, he married Alice, the younger daughter of Baldwin II, and joined Baldwin II in an attack on Damascus. His reign ended in 1131 when he was defeated and killed on his northern frontier by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo.

## Bohemund III
Bohemund III succeeded his mother, Constance (daughter of Bohemund II), in the principality of Antioch in 1163. He served as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem in 1164 during Amalric I's expedition to Egypt, but was captured by Nureddin at Harenc in August of that year. He was ransomed by Emperor Manuel and returned to Antioch with a Greek patriarch.

In 1180, Bohemund III was excommunicated after deserting his second wife, the princess Orguilleuse, for Sibylla. He had two sons by Orguilleuse: Raymund and Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV). The later years of his life were dominated by a succession dispute between his grandson, Raymund Rhupen, and his younger son, Bohemund. Leo of Armenia championed Raymund Rhupen and even captured Bohemund III at Gastin in 1194 to force the cession of Antioch. Although Bohemund IV evicted his father from Antioch around 1199, Leo ousted the younger Bohemund, and Bohemund III died in possession of his principality in 1201.

## Bohemund IV
Bohemund IV became count of Tripoli in 1187 and succeeded his father in Antioch in 1201. His succession was contested for years by Raymund Rhupen and Leo of Armenia, a dispute that required the attention of Innocent III. Bohemund IV was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and the Templars. Conversely, the Hospitallers supported Leo.

Leo captured Antioch in 1216 and installed Raymund Rhupen, but Bohemund IV regained the city before Leo's death in 1220. Following the death of Raymund Rhupen in 1221, Bohemund IV ruled both Antioch and Tripoli. He was a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, leading to his excommunication in 1230. He had a fluctuating relationship with Emperor Frederick II during the 1228–29 crusade. He died in 1233, shortly after concluding peace with the Hospitallers and being released from excommunication by Gregory IX.

## Bohemund V and VI
Bohemund V (d. 1251), son of Bohemund IV and Plaisance, succeeded his father in 1233 as prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. He maintained an alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia. In 1225, he married Alice, the widow of Hugh I of Cyprus, linking the history of Antioch with Cyprus. He resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving Antioch to be governed by a commune and bailiff.

Bohemund VI (b. 1237–1275), son of Bohemund V and Luciana, succeeded his father in 1251 and was knighted by St Louis in 1252. He established himself in Antioch in 1252 and recognized his nephew, Hugh II, as king of Jerusalem in 1257. To counter the Egyptian sultan, he allied with the Mongols. However, he lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268 and died in 1275 as only the count of Tripoli.

## Bohemund VII
Bohemund VII, son of Bohemund VI and Sibylla, succeeded to the county of Tripoli in 1275. His reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. He died without issue, and the county of Tripoli became extinct shortly thereafter when the city was captured.
