# Bagdad

Bagdad is the capital of the Turkish vilayet of the same name and serves as the headquarters of the VI. Army Corps, which also garrisons the Mosul and Basra vilayets. The city is situated on both sides of the river Tigris within an extensive desert plain. Located at latitude 33deg 20' N. and longitude 44deg 24' E., it sits at the point where the Tigris and Euphrates rivers are closest, separated by little more than 25 miles.

## Ancient History and Antiquities
The site of Bagdad has a history antedating Islam. The city of Baghdadu may date back as far as 2000 B.C., with its name appearing in lists from the library of Assur-bani-pal. It is also mentioned on the Michaux stone, which dates to the time of Tiglath-Pileser I (1100 B.C.). On the west bank of the Tigris, remains of a quay built of bricks laid in bitumen bear an inscription of Nebuchadrezzar, king of Babylon; this quay establishes that the ancient city was located on the site of old or western Bagdad. References in the Jewish *Talmud* indicate the city continued to exist at and after the beginning of the current era.

Other significant ruins exist in or near the city:
* **Ctesiphon (or Madain):** Located about 15 miles below Bagdad on the east side of the river, this site contains the ruins of the palace of Chosroes.
* **Akerkuf:** Located in the desert approximately 9 miles west of Bagdad, this landmark consists of a huge tower of unburned brick resting on a hill of debris, rising 100 feet or more above the plain. Inscribed bricks suggest a connection to Kurigalzu, king of Babylon (about 1300 B.C.). The site, known as Akukafa, remained a place of importance regarding canals as late as the Abbasid caliphate.

## The Abbasid Period
According to Arabian writers, when the caliph Mansur founded the Arab city of Bagdad, the site contained nothing but an old convent, though the new city inherited the ancient name. Under the Abbasids, Bagdad became the principal power in the nearer East and a city of the first rank, described as the richest and most splendid city in the world.

The city's prominence fluctuated during the dynasty. Under Mo'tassim, the rise of the Turkish body-guard led to the founding of Samarra, and the court deserted Bagdad for fifty-eight years. In A.D. 865, Mosta'in returned to Bagdad to escape the Turkish guard, but the city was besieged and taken. From that point until the dynasty's end, the Abbasid caliphs remained the religious heads of Islam and resided in Bagdad, though they were puppets of the Turkish guard, the Seljuks, and the Buyids.

## Decline and Mongol Conquest
A more rapid decline began in the last century of the caliphates. By the close of the 12th century, the town occupied the same space as the present city, but travelers described the western city as being almost in ruins and the eastern half as containing large uninhabited spaces.

In 1258, the city was captured by the Mongols under Hulagu (the grandson of Jenghiz Khan). This event extinguished the Abbasid caliphate of Bagdad and ended its status as the religious center of Islam and a city of the first rank. Despite this, the city retained a sense of former grandeur and is referred to in Turkish official documents as the "glorious city."

## Geography and Infrastructure
Bagdad is situated in a plain with few trees or villages. It is connected to the Euphrates by the Sakhlawieh canal (formerly Isa), which leaves the Euphrates above Feluja and the bridge of boats near the ruins of ancient Anbar. The canal spreads into a great marsh as it approaches Bagdad and enters the Tigris below the town via the Masudi canal, which encircles western Bagdad. While the canal was navigable for craft of some size during Chesney's 1838 survey, it now primarily increases periodic floods that threaten the dikes and can turn Bagdad into an island city.

## Religious Sites
The region contains several shrines. Approximately 3 miles west of Bagdad on the Euphrates road is the shrine and tomb of Nabi Yusha (or Kohen Yusha). Moslems venerate this site as the tomb of Yusuf (Joseph), while those who follow the Jewish tradition believe it to be the sepulture of Joshua, son of Josedech, the high priest at the end of the exilian period. This is one of four such shrines in Irak, alongside the tomb of Ezra (Shatt el-Arab), the tomb of Ezekiel (Kefil), and the well of Daniel (near Hillah).
