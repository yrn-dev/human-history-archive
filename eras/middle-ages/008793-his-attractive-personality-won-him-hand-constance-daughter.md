# Marriage and Suitors in Historical Records

The archive's records contain various accounts of marriages, betrothals, and the pursuit of partners across different eras and cultures, ranging from royal alliances to personal attractions and strategic rewards.

## Royal Alliances and Dynastic Marriages

In the records of the nobility and royalty, marriages often served as instruments of state policy or dynastic continuity. Bohemund I married Constance, who was the daughter of Philip I of France. Similarly, Bohemund II married Alice, the daughter of Baldwin II of Jerusalem. Other marriages within this lineage include Bohemund IV's unions with Plaisance and Melisinda, the latter being the daughter of Amalric II. Bohemund V married Alice, a widow of Hugh of Cyprus, and later Luciana, the daughter of the count of Segni. Bohemund VI married Sibylla of Cyprus, the sister of Leo of Armenia.

In the case of Louis XIV of France, personal inclinations were superseded by political necessity. Although the king was strongly attracted to one of the nieces of Cardinal Mazarin, reasons of state prevailed. To cement a new friendship with Spain, it was agreed that Louis XIV would marry his cousin, the Infanta Maria Theresa.

## Marriages as Rewards and Strategic Gains

Some historical accounts describe the hand of a daughter being offered as a prize or reward for specific achievements. In the account of David and Saul, Saul's daughter Michal loved David. Saul, driven by a personal dislike and dread of David, assigned him a military command. Saul promised David the hand of Michal as a reward for success in a perilous enterprise, though he secretly hoped David would perish. David ultimately won his wife. Earlier, Saul had promised his daughter to whoever could overthrow Goliath; however, his eldest daughter, Merab, was eventually given to Adriel of Abel-Meholah.

In Greek accounts, Theseus and Peirithous traveled to Epirus to seek the daughter of Aidoneus, the king of the Molossians. Aidoneus required that any suitor for his daughter's hand must fight his dog, Cerberus. Because Theseus and Peirithous arrived as ravishers rather than wooers, they were imprisoned; Peirithous was killed by the dog.

## Personal Attraction and Social Influence

Attraction and social standing frequently played roles in the formation of relationships. Alexander the Great, while restraining himself from the captive daughters and wife of Darius, did attach himself to Barsine. Barsine was of royal descent—the daughter of Artabazus, who had married one of the king's daughters—and was described as naturally attractive and educated in the Greek style. Alexander's willingness to attach himself to her was further influenced by the solicitations of Parmenio.

In the 16th century, the daughters of Roemer Visscher, Anna and Tesselschade, were noted for their singular beauty, graceful manners, and universal accomplishment. Their qualities attracted many of the most gifted youths of the time, who became suitors; however, neither Anna nor Tesselschade granted their hand in marriage.

In later periods, social gifts and beauty continued to influence social circles. Lucie Duff-Gordon, who married Sir Alexander Duff-Gordon in 1840, inherited her mother's beauty and social gifts, allowing her to gather a brilliant circle of friends and achieve extraordinary success as a hostess.

## Other Notable Relationships

The archive also records instances of illicit or forced unions. In the history of Greece, the unwarlike Ægisthus, son of Thyestês, seduced Klytæmnêstra, the wife of Agamemnôn. This was achieved after Ægisthus removed a venerable bard who had served as Klytæmnêstra's monitor and guardian. Additionally, Theseus is recorded as having carried off Helen from Sparta while she was still a child, though some accounts suggest she was handed over to him by Tyndareus for protection.
