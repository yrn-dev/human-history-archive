# Leopold and the House of Babenberg

The House of Babenberg was a Franconian family that held the duchy of Austria prior to the rise of the Habsburgs. The family's earliest known ancestor was Poppo, a count in Grapfeld during the early 9th century. The family's influence in Franconia was centered around their castle of Babenberg on the upper Main, which gave the family its name. However, a period of intense rivalry with the Conradine family, known as the "Babenberg feud," occurred at the beginning of the 10th century during the reign of King Louis the Child. This conflict resulted in the deaths of two Babenberg brothers; the survivor, Adalbert, was beheaded in 906 despite a promise of safe-conduct.

## Leopold and the East Mark
Following the loss of their influence in Franconia, a member of the family named Leopold, who served as a count in the Donnegau, emerged as a significant figure in 976. He is described as the margrave of the East Mark, a district on the eastern frontier of Bavaria approximately 60 meters in breadth. Leopold likely received this mark as a reward for his fidelity to Emperor Otto II during the Bavarian rising of 976.

Under Leopold's administration, which was conducted with vigour and success, the East Mark began to grow into the duchy of Austria. Leopold extended the area of the mark at the expense of the Hungarians. He was succeeded in 994 by his son, Henry I, who continued his father's policies.

## Expansion and the Duchy of Austria
The Babenbergs ruled Austria until the family became extinct in 1246. Through their foresight and skill, they elevated the mark to a prominent position among the German states. A primary objective of the dynasty was the eastward expansion of the frontier down the Danube valley through colonization. This progress was marked by the relocation of the capital from Poechlarn to Melk, then to Tulln, and finally to Vienna around 1140.

The territory grew to incorporate the country as far as the Leitha. In 1156, the district between the Inn and the Enns was added to the mark. To restore peace to Germany in that year, King Frederick I raised Austria to the rank of a duchy and granted it exceptional privileges. These included the provision that if there were no male heirs, the duchy could descend to females, and the duke was permitted to nominate a successor if he had no children. The investiture was granted to Duke Henry and his second wife, Theodora. The duke's primary obligations to the Empire were to send a contingent to the imperial army for campaigns bordering Austria and to attend any diet held in Bavaria.

## Later Babenberg Rulers and Extinction
The Babenbergs generally maintained a policy of loyalty toward the emperors, often cemented by marriages. However, there were notable exceptions. Margrave Leopold II deserted Emperor Henry IV during the investiture struggle, leading to his temporary loss of Austria to Vratislav II, duke of the Bohemians. Leopold II regained possession of the mark in 1083 and reconciled with the emperor.

Later, Duke Frederick II, known as "the Quarrelsome," clashed with Emperor Frederick II. The emperor claimed certain rights in Austria and summoned the duke to Italian diets. After Frederick II refused to appear three times, the emperor placed him under the ban in 1236 and declared the duchies of Austria and Styria vacant. Although the emperor invaded and made Vienna an imperial city, Duke Frederick returned to power in 1239. The Babenberg line ended in June 1246, when Duke Frederick was killed in battle by the Hungarians.

Other notable family members include Duke Leopold I, who in 1186 made a treaty with Ottakar IV, duke of Styria, bringing upper Austria and Styria to the Babenbergs in 1192. Duke Leopold II later purchased lands from the bishop of Freising in 1229, taking the title of lord of Carniola.

## Legacy and Memorials
The family's early history is linked to the town of Bamberg, which grew around the Babenberch castle. Following the extinction of the Babenbergs, the town passed to the Saxon house. In the vicinity of Baden, the abbey of Heiligenkreuz contains a Romanesque church dating from the 11th century, which houses the tombs of several members of the Babenberg family.
