# Thursday Markets in English Boroughs

Across various English boroughs, the establishment of markets on Thursdays served as a cornerstone of local commerce and civic administration. These markets were often formalized through royal charters or granted by lords of the manor, reflecting the economic growth and legal evolution of the towns.

## Arundel and Banbury
In Arundel (historically Harundell), markets were held within the borough on Thursdays and Saturdays from very early times. The town, which appears in Domesday as a port and thriving borough, saw further commercial expansion in 1285 when Richard Fitzalan, earl of Arundel, obtained a grant for two annual fairs on December 17 and May 14. By 1586, Queen Elizabeth acknowledged the rights of the burgesses and mayor to hold two weekly markets and four annual fairs, rights they claimed to have exercised from time immemorial.

Similarly, Banbury (Beranbyrig or Banesberie) has records of a market as early as 1138. Henry II later confirmed a Thursday market by charter and granted a fair at Whitsun. The Thursday market was further solidified in 1553 through a charter of incorporation granted by Queen Mary, which also established a common council and a court of record. This charter was confirmed by James I in 1608, who added a horse-market, a weekly wool-market, and two additional annual fairs.

## Marazion and Lostwithiel
In Marazion, a market on Thursdays was included in a charter attributed to Robert, count of Mortain, which granted liberties to St Michael's Mount. This market was initially held on the mainland. Over time, the location of the markets shifted; Richard, king of the Romans, decreed that three markets and three fairs (held at Mid-Lent and on the two feasts of St Michael) be moved from the demesne lands of the Bloyous in Marazion to the land of the prior at Marchadyou. In response to this loss, Ralph Bloyou procured a Monday market and a fair on the morrow, feast, and vigil of St Andrew in 1331. Despite this rivalry, the Thursday market prevailed, with later accounts from Carew (1602) and Norden (1582) stating that the term "Marcajewe" signified the Thursday market.

Lostwithiel's market history is tied to the castle of Restormel. Pipe Rolls from 1194-1203 indicate that Robert de Cardinan paid ten marks annually for a market there. Later, Richard, king of the Romans, granted the burgesses a weekly market and a yearly fair. In 1325, this charter was confirmed, and the market was specifically ordered to be held on Thursdays. However, this arrangement changed in 1733 when a Friday market was substituted for the Thursday market; the Friday market continues to be held.

## Burton-upon-Trent and Kingston
Burton-upon-Trent (Burhton) established its weekly Thursday market through a charter granted by King John in 1204. This same charter granted an annual fair of three days' duration on October 29, the feast of St Modwen. The town's commercial landscape was later influenced by the development of a brewing industry around 1708, which by 1748 had expanded its reach to Baltic ports and St Petersburg.

In Kingston (Cyningestun), the market structure evolved over time. While James I granted a Saturday market and Charles II granted a Wednesday market, the corporation later added a cattle-market specifically for Thursdays.
