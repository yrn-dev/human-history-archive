# Roger Bacon and Francis Bacon

The archive contains records regarding two distinct figures named Bacon: the medieval scholar Roger Bacon and the later English philosopher Francis Bacon. While both are associated with the development of science and philosophy, their contributions and the nature of their fame differ significantly.

## Roger Bacon: Scientific Pursuits and Popular Legend

In popular estimation, the fame of Roger Bacon has rested primarily on his mechanical discoveries. However, research indicates that very little can be accurately attributed to him. While he describes a method for constructing a telescope, there is no evidence to conclude he actually possessed the instrument. He was likely acquainted with the principles of spectacles, though it does not appear he made them, and burning-glasses were already in common use during his time.

Bacon's beliefs included astrology, the doctrine of signatures, and the philosopher's stone; he also believed the circle had been squared. His predictions, found in the *De Secretis*, are viewed with skepticism. Regarding gunpowder, some have claimed he invented it based on a passage in his *De mirabili potestate artis et naturae*. Popular legend further attributes the forging of a speaking brazen head to him, a story reflected in works such as the *Famous Historie of Fryer Bacon* (1615) and R. Greene's *Friar Bacon and Friar Bungay*.

## Roger Bacon's Academic Life and Works

Roger Bacon received scientific training largely through the study of Arab writers. This education led him to recognize defects in the systems of contemporary doctors, who relied on authority or custom rather than Aristotelian experiment. He noted that Greek was not learned by professors and that erroneous versions of the Scriptures were being abandoned in favor of Peter Lombard's *Sentences*.

Bacon withdrew from scholastic routine to focus on experimental research and languages. He respected only one teacher, Petrus de Maharncuria Picardus (possibly Petrus Peregrinus of Picardy), who may have written a treatise titled *De Magnete*. Bacon expressed violent indignation toward the fame of "fluent young doctors" who lacked depth, specifically targeting Alexander of Hales and an unnamed professor in his *Opus Minus* and *Opus Tertium*.

After gaining renown in Paris and receiving the title *doctor mirabilis*, Bacon entered the Franciscan order around 1250. His fame at Oxford was accompanied by doubts regarding his orthodoxy and suspicions that he dealt in the black arts. In 1257, Bonaventura interdicted his lectures, and Bacon spent ten years under supervision in Paris. In 1266, Pope Clement IV ordered him to write a treatise on the sciences. Despite a lack of funds and materials, Bacon produced three large treatises in eighteen months: the *Opus Majus*, *Opus Minus*, and *Opus Tertium*. He returned to Oxford in 1268, where he continued his experimental work and wrote the *Compendium Studii Philosophiae* (1271), which attacked the ignorance of monks and clergy.

## Francis Bacon: Philosophy and Method

Francis Bacon (1561-1626) is credited with beginning the unbroken course of English philosophy. A master of English prose, his writing was influenced by his experience as an orator, his involvement in affairs, and his use of Latin. He sought to understand and control nature by overcoming "idols," which he defined as the inherent paralyzing frailties of human judgment.

His major conceptual works include:
*   *The Advancement of Knowledge* (1605) and its Latin expansion, *De Augmentis Scientiarum* (1623), which mapped out science.
*   *Novum Organum* (1620), which detailed the methods of inductive logic and the errors of scholasticism.
*   *New Atlantis*, which described a moralized and ideally equipped scientific community.
*   *The Essays* (1597, 1612, 1625), a diary of his opinions on business and human life.

## Critique of the Baconian Method

The inductive method proposed by Francis Bacon has faced significant criticism. Some logicians argue that his logical method was "mechanical" due to a false conception of causal relations, specifically the assumption that every phenomenon has only one cause. Critics point out that science has not actually followed the Baconian method and that no single discovery can be definitely ascribed to his rules. It is argued that the true scientific procedure relies on hypothesis and the deductive method, which Bacon is said to have hardly recognized.
