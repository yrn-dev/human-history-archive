# Antioch and the Cypriot Connexion

## The Princes of Antioch and the County of Tripoli
The name Bohemund refers to a series of princes of Antioch who later became counts of Tripoli. A significant political and familial link was established between Antioch and Cyprus through the marriage of Bohemund V and Alice, who was the widow of Hugh I. This Cypriot connexion was further maintained when Plaisance, the daughter of Bohemund V and Luciana (daughter of the count of Segni and nephew of Innocent III), married Henry I of Cyprus in 1250. Henry I was the son of Hugh I.

Bohemund VI, born in 1237 and the son of Bohemund V, succeeded his father in 1251 and was knighted by St Louis in 1252. In that same year, he established himself in Antioch, while leaving Tripoli to itself. In 1257, Bohemund VI secured the recognition of his nephew, Hugh II (the son of Henry I and Plaisance), as the king of Jerusalem. To counter the advance of the Egyptian sultan, Bohemund VI formed an alliance with the Mongols. However, in 1268, he lost Antioch to Bibars. By the time of his death in 1275, he held only the title of count of Tripoli.

He was succeeded in 1275 by his son, Bohemund VII, who was the son of Bohemund VI and Sibylla, the sister of Leo III of Armenia. Bohemund VII's reign was short and troubled, characterized by conflicts with the Templars established in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue, and because Tripoli was captured within two years of his passing, the county of Tripoli is considered to have become extinct with him.

## Luke the Physician and the Antiochene Church
Internal evidence suggests that Luke practiced his profession in Antioch, and it is probable that he first met Paul there (or in Tarsus). Luke's connection to the church in Antioch is indicated by his local knowledge of points unlikely to be found in external sources, such as the fact that the name "Christians" was first coined in Antioch. Furthermore, the Bezan reading in Acts xi. 27, "when *we* were assembled," may imply a personal memory of these events.

While Luke likely met Paul in Antioch and joined him on his second great missionary enterprise—possibly serving as his medical attendant—some scholars have suggested other connections. Sir W. M. Ramsay and others have proposed that Luke's original home was Philippi and that he might be the "certain Macedonian" Paul saw in a vision at Troas. However, this view is considered precarious. Other theories suggest Luke's connection to the north-eastern Aegean may have occurred after his time in Antioch, during his work with Paul in the province of Asia. This aligns with the argument by Harnack that Luke wrote Acts in Asia and the tradition that Luke died unmarried in Bithynia at age seventy-four.

## Syriac Versions and Antiochene Influence
The city of Antioch played a central role in the development of various Syriac versions of religious texts. The Peshito is recognized by a majority of scholars, following the work of Burkitt, as being based on the Diatessaron and the Old Syriac. It is believed that Rabbula produced the Peshito with the assistance of the contemporary Greek text of the Antiochene Church.

The relationship between the Old Syriac and the Diatessaron is more complex. The most probable theory, proposed by Burkitt, suggests that Tatian brought the Diatessaron from Rome and translated it into Syriac around A.D. 175. Later, during the tenure of Serapion as bishop of Antioch (A.D. 190-203), a new translation of the "separated Gospels" (*Evangelion da Mepharreshe*) was created using manuscripts in use at Antioch.

Additionally, the Palestinian or Jerusalem Version, a lectionary once thought to originate near Jerusalem, has been shown by Burkitt to originate from the neighborhood of Antioch. This version was likely created in the 6th century in connection with Justinian's efforts to abolish Judaism.

## Other Regional Connections
The Maronites, a Christian people in communion with the Papal Church, maintain a presence in and near Antioch, as well as in Galilee, on the Syrian coast, and in Mt Lebanon, Anti-Lebanon, and Hermon. The Maronites also established colonies in Alexandria, the United States of America, and Cyprus, where a large convent exists near Cape Kormakiti. These colonies began to form during the troubles of 1860.
