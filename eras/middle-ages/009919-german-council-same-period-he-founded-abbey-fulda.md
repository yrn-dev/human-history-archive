# Fulda

Fulda is a town and former ecclesiastical center in Germany that owes its existence to its famous abbey. Throughout its history, the town has been a site of significant religious, educational, and political activity.

## The Abbey of Fulda
The great Benedictine abbey of Fulda was founded in 744. Its establishment was instigated by St Boniface and carried out by his pupil, Sturm, who served as the first abbot. Boniface intended the abbey to serve as a center for German monastic culture, and he established a rule for the institution that was stricter than the Benedictine rule. 

The abbey became a center for extensive missionary work and was liberally endowed with land by others and the princes of the Carolingian house, quickly becoming one of the wealthiest and most famous establishments of its kind. In the ecclesiastical history of Germany, the abbey of Fulda holds a position comparable to that of Monte Cassino in Italy, Iona in Scotland, Tours in France, Corvey in Saxony, and St Gall in South Germany.

By approximately 968, the pope declared the abbot of Fulda to be the primate of all abbots in Gaul and Germany. The abbot eventually attained the status of a prince of the Empire.

## Education and Scholarship
Fulda was particularly renowned for its school, which served as the center for theological learning during the early middle ages. Notable teachers at the school included Walafrid Strabo, Alcuin, and Hrabanus Maurus, the latter of whom served as abbot from 822 to 842.

In later centuries, the town continued to be a site of intellectual activity. From 1734 to 1804, Fulda was the seat of a university. Additionally, the town was the birthplace of the German mathematician and scholar Athanasius Kircher (1601–1680), who was educated at the Jesuit college of Fulda.

## Institutional Evolution and Reform
In the early 10th century, the monastery underwent a reform led by monks from Scotland, who restored the Benedictine rule to its original strictness. However, the abbey eventually lost its high position and some of its lands; its period of glory ended some time before the Reformation.

During the Reformation period, Abbot Johann von Henneberg (abbot from 1529 to 1541) expressed some sympathy for the teachings of the reformers. Subsequently, the Counter-Reformation made significant progress in Fulda under Abbot Balthasar von Dernbach.

In 1752, the rank of the abbot was raised to that of a bishop, and Fulda became a prince-bishopric. In the 19th century, a new bishopric was founded at Fulda in 1829.

## Political History and Governance
Fulda became a town in 1208. The middle ages were characterized by frequent struggles between the townsfolk and the abbots. The town's possession changed several times during various conflicts: it was captured by rebels during the Peasants' War and by the Hanoverians during the Seven Years' War.

The prince-bishopric was secularized in 1802. It was held in quick succession by the king of France, the prince of Orange, and the grand-duchy of Frankfort. In 1816, Prussia ceded a smaller portion of the principality to Bavaria and the greater part to Hesse-Cassel. In 1866, Prussia annexed this larger portion.

## Notable Figures and Visitors
The abbey of Fulda was a site of residence for various figures, including the Irish-born chronicler Marianus Scotus (1028–1082 or 1083), who spent part of his later life there. In more recent history, the town has hosted many assemblies of German bishops.
