# Architectural Features of Church Aisles and Vaulting

In ecclesiastical architecture, the arrangement of aisles and the implementation of vaulting vary significantly across different periods, regions, and building types. These elements often define the structural integrity and aesthetic character of the basilica and cathedral plans.

## Basilican and Central Plans
Early Christian architecture utilized two primary plan types: the basilican (axial) and the circular (central). In central-type churches, such as the 4th-century Sta Costanza in Rome, the central space was sometimes surrounded by a vaulted aisle. Other central plans, such as the 5th-century mausoleum of Galla Placidia in Ravenna, used annexes to form a cross, which helped counterpoise the central vault. In the basilican type, vaults were also applied early; for example, the 6th-century St Irene in Constantinople features a long body covered by two domes.

## Roman Basilicas
The grand Roman basilicas exhibit diverse aisle configurations. The original St Peter's featured a nave 80 feet wide, which sat six steps lower than its side aisles. There were two aisles on each side, divided by four colonnades of twenty-two Corinthian columns each. The inner colonnades were characterized by arches and a second clerestory. Additionally, a vaulted crypt ran around the foundation beneath the altar.

The basilica of St Paul, dedicated in 324 A.D. and rebuilt between 388 and 423, possessed a plan and dimensions almost identical to those of St Peter's, including a nave and side aisles. The modernized five-aisled basilica of St John Lateran is noted for a most unusual arrangement: a double-vaulted aisle that runs around the apse.

## Regional Variations in France and England
In Picardy, a common custom involved designing side-aisles where each bay had its own separate roof, forming a right-angle with the roof of the great nave. This is seen in a church where the side-aisles featured live gables and five separate roofs. The vaulting in this instance is attributed to Jean Vaast, an architect of Beauvais Cathedral. Another church in the region featured three naves with low 15th-century vaulting that was lower at the choir end than near the doorway.

In England, the abbey church of St Peter and St Paul in Dorchester, Oxfordshire, displays a chronological progression of aisles: the north choir aisle dates to the close of the 13th century, the south choir aisle to approximately 1300, and the south nave aisle to approximately 1320. In Bristol, the cathedral's nave (constructed by Street in 1877) imitates a 14th-century choir, featuring "curious skeleton-vaulting" within the aisles.

## Other Notable Examples
The basilica of Parenzo in Istria (c. A.D. 542) is noted for having sustained very little alteration; nine pillars divide each of its aisles from the nave. In other contexts, such as the view from the chancel of a church containing St. Joseph’s and St. Anthony’s Chapels, the interior is described as a "forest of mighty pillars" supporting a majestic vaulted roof over seven aisles.
