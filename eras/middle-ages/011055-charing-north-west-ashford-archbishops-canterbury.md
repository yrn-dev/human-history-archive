# Charing and the Archbishops of Canterbury

## Overview of Charing
Charing is located north-west of Ashford in the county of Kent, England. The site is historically significant as a location where the archbishops of Canterbury maintained a residence dating back to pre-Conquest times. Today, ruins of a palace remain at Charing, the majority of which are attributed to the Decorated period.

## Ecclesiastical Administration
Charing has played a role in the religious administration of Kent, a county notable for containing two entire bishoprics: Rochester (the see for West Kent, founded in 600) and Canterbury (the see for East Kent, founded in 597). 

In 1291, Charing was one of the deaneries included within the archdeaconry of Canterbury, which at that time was co-extensive with the diocese. The administrative structure of the area underwent several changes in the 19th century:
* **1845:** The deanery of Charing, along with those of Sutton and Sittingbourne, was incorporated into the newly created archdeaconry of Maidstone.
* **1853:** The deanery of Charing was subdivided into East and West Charing.

## Regional Geography and Context
Charing is situated in the vicinity of Ashford, a market town located 56 miles south-east of London. Ashford is positioned on a gentle eminence near the confluence of the upper branches of the river Stour. The river Stour (or Great Stour) rises on the southern face of the North Downs, flows south-east to Ashford—where it meets the East Stour—and then turns north-east toward Wye and Canterbury.

The broader region is characterized by the North Downs, a range of hills that divide Kent from west to east. To the south of these hills and the Forest Ridges lies the Vale of Kent, an area also known as the Weald.

## Related Local History
The area surrounding Charing and Ashford contains various other historical and industrial sites. To the south-eastern outskirts of Ashford lies the village of Willesborough. Between Ashford and Tenterden, at Bethersden, marble quarries were once worked extensively; this material was used for local churches and the cathedrals of Rochester and Canterbury. 

Additionally, the region hosted several monastic foundations. Near Ashford, Bilsington priory was established in 1253 as a house for Augustinian canons, though only slight remains of the structure exist.
