# Bridgnorth

Bridgnorth is a municipal borough and market town located in Shropshire, England, within the Ludlow parliamentary division. It is situated approximately 150 miles northwest by west from London, positioned on the Worcester-Shrewsbury line of the Great Western railway. The town is divided by the river Severn, which separates the lower town on the left bank from the upper town on the right bank. The upper town is characterized by its picturesque appearance, built upon the summit and acclivities of a rock that rises abruptly 180 feet from the river. A steep line of rail connects the two sections of the town, and the railway passes beneath via a long tunnel.

## Early History and Royal Status
The early history of the town is linked to Æthelfleda, lady of the Mercians, who in 912 raised a mound at the site as part of an offensive policy against the Danes of the five boroughs. Following the Conquest, William I granted the manor of Bridgnorth to Earl Roger of Shrewsbury. The castle and borough were later transferred from Quatford to Bridgnorth by Roger's son, Robert de Belesme. However, in 1102, following the attainder of Robert, the town became a royal borough.

## Charters and Governance
The burgesses of Bridgnorth were granted various privileges and liberties through a series of royal charters. While it is probable that Henry I granted initial privileges, Henry II later confirmed all customs and franchises held during the time of Henry I. In 1215, King John granted the burgesses freedom from toll throughout England, with the exception of the city of London. Further rights were conferred by Henry III in 1227, including a hanse and a gild merchant. Subsequent kings continued these confirmations, with Henry VI adding other privileges and the assize of ale and bread.

Bridgnorth was incorporated by James I in 1546. The town's governance consists of a mayor, 12 councillors, and 4 aldermen, overseeing an area of 3,018 acres.

## Parliamentary and Commercial History
The burgesses of Bridgnorth returned two members to parliament starting in 1295. This continued until 1867, when the representation was reduced to one member; the town was eventually disfranchised in 1885.

Commercial activity was supported by royal grants for fairs. In 1359, the burgesses were granted a yearly fair lasting three days following the feast of the Translation of St Leonard. In 1630, Charles I granted a license for an additional fair to be held for two days following the Thursday before the first week in Lent. Modern industry in the town includes brewing, the manufacture of carpets, and trade in agricultural produce.

## Landmarks and Institutions
The town's architecture features the picturesque half-timbered style in its streets, seen in the town hall (built in 1652) and a house dated 1580. This latter house was the birthplace in 1729 of Thomas Percy, the editor of *Reliques of Ancient English Poetry* and bishop of Dromore.

On the summit of the rock stands the tower of the old castle, which leans approximately 17° from the perpendicular. The town contains two parish churches: St Mary's, built in the classic style of the late 18th century, and St Leonard's. The latter was formerly collegiate and was practically rebuilt in 1862; in 1640, it was held by the famous divine Richard Baxter.

Educational and cultural institutions include:
* A grammar school founded in 1503, located in an Elizabethan building.
* A blue-coat school.
* A college of divinity.
* A literary institute featuring a school of art and a library.

Additionally, the town is supported by large charities. Nearby, a sandstone cave serves as a curious ancient hermitage. To the south-east, at Quatford (1 mile away), the site of a castle dating from 1085 can be traced. This site dominated the ancient Forest of Morf and was the original location of the college before it was moved to Bridgnorth.
