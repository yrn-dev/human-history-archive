# Bohemia up to the Death of Sigismund

## The Transition to the Luxemburg Dynasty
Following the reign of John of Luxemburg's predecessors, the Bohemian throne experienced a period of instability. Albert, king of the Romans, declared Bohemia a vacant fief of the Empire and used intimidation to induce the Bohemians to elect his son, Rudolph, as sovereign. Rudolph's reign lasted only one year. Although Habsburg princes claimed a hereditary right to the throne, the Bohemians asserted their right to elect their own sovereign, choosing Henry, duke of Carinthia, who had married a daughter of King Wenceslas II. Henry eventually became unpopular due to accusations that he unduly favored German settlers, leading to his deposition.

The Bohemians then elected John of Luxemburg, the son of Henry, king of the Romans. This established the Luxemburg dynasty, which ruled Bohemia until its extinction upon the death of Sigismund in 1437. King John became more closely connected to the region through his marriage to Princess Elizabeth, a daughter of Wenceslas II, though he spent most of his life in other lands, campaigning from Italy to Lithuania. His policy was centered on a close alliance with France; he died fighting as a French ally at the battle of Crecy in 1346.

## The Rise of the Hussite Movement
During the reign of King Charles, the influence of the Church of Rome in Bohemia began to weaken due to attacks on clerical immorality by priests such as Waldhauser and Milic. This alienation grew during the church schism, as rival pontiffs engaged in theological strife. Consequently, nearly the entire Bohemian nation supported the cause of Huss. While King Wenceslas was characterized by irresolution during these disputes, Queen Sophia openly favored Huss, who served as her confessor.

Huss traveled to the council of Constance with a letter of safe conduct provided by Wenceslas's brother, Sigismund, king of the Romans. However, Huss was declared a heretic and burnt on July 6, 1415. This event triggered a civil war in Bohemia. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. This document protested the execution of Huss—described as a "good, just and catholic man"—and declared that those claiming heresy existed in Bohemia were "liars, vile traitors and calumniators." The protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church and marked the beginning of the Hussite wars.

## The Conflict Under Sigismund
Following the death of the king, rioting broke out in Prague and other cities, resulting in the expulsion of many Germans who adhered to the Church of Rome. Sigismund, claiming the crown as the successor to his brother, arrived at Kutna Hora (Kuttenberg). On March 1, 1420, Pope Martin V proclaimed a crusade against Bohemia. Sigismund led a large international army, including Bohemians, and occupied the castle of Prague on June 30, 1420, viewing the city as a heretical community because the inhabitants accepted other evangelical truths and used the sacred chalice.

The crusaders failed to conquer Prague after the Taborites, led by Zizka, repulsed an attack on the Vitkov (Zizkov) hill. Sigismund was further defeated at the battle of the Vysehrad on November 1, 1420. By June 1, 1421, royal authority had ceased, and Sigismund was deposed during a meeting of the diet at Caslav. A provisional government of twenty men was established while the Bohemians sought a Polish prince as sovereign.

## Struggles for Sovereignty and Civil War
In 1422, Sigismund invaded Bohemia again but was decisively defeated by Zizka at Nemecky Brod (Deutschbrod). Sigismund Korybutovic, a Polish prince, arrived and was recognized as regent by most inhabitants, but he was recalled by Polish rulers after a few months due to papal influence. His departure sparked the first civil war between the advanced Taborite party and the moderate Hussites, known as the Calixtines or Utraquists.

The threat of German invasion and the brief return of Prince Korybutovic reunited the Bohemians, who won a decisive victory over the Germans at Aussig in 1426. Korybutovic later left Bohemia after incurring the dislike of extreme Hussites, ending hopes for an independent Slav dynasty. In 1427, another crusade involving German princes and 1,000 English archers under papal legate Henry Beaufort failed; the crusaders fled into Bavaria after panicking at Tachau and Mies (Stribro). Following these successes, the Bohemians invaded the German states and Hungary.

## Reconciliation and the End of the Dynasty
Recognizing that Bohemia could not be conquered by force, a council met at Basel to examine Hussite demands. Despite this, Sigismund influenced the Germans to make one final military attempt, which ended in a Bohemian victory at Domazlice (Taus) on August 1, 1431. Negotiations began at Basel under the leadership of Prokop the Great.

While negotiations proceeded slowly, political dissensions arose in Bohemia between the "army of the towns" (Taborites) and the "army of the nobles" (aristocratic Hussites). These forces clashed at Lipan near Kolin on May 30, 1434. The Taborites were defeated, and their leaders, including the two Prokops, perished. This victory for the moderate party allowed for reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund.

The Bohemians recognized Sigismund as their sovereign through a treaty made at Iglau on July 5, 1436. In exchange, the "Compacts" granted the Bohemians the right to preach the gospel freely, the right of communion in both kinds, and limitations on the clergy's ability to acquire worldly goods. The Luxemburg dynasty ended with the death of Sigismund in 1437.
