# Books, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, and an Introduction, for the use of Schools

The phrase "School Books, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, and an Introduction, for the use of Schools" appears as a category in *A Catalogue of the most Vendible Books in England*, published in 1658 by W. London. This publication occurred during the Protectorate, a period when bookselling had increased significantly following the removal of arbitrary restrictions previously introduced by Archbishop Laud. This era of growth followed a history of strict censorship in England; under Henry VIII, the first patent for the office of king's printer was granted to Thomas Berthelet in 1529, requiring books to be licensed. Possession of unlicensed books was a punishable offense, and the Company of Stationers, incorporated in 1556, was granted extensive powers to repress obnoxious books. While the Star Chamber exercised effectual censorship in subsequent reigns, demand for polemical books remained high, leading many to be printed abroad and smuggled into England.

## Hebrew Language and Educational Works

The study of Hebrew has been supported by various grammars and commentaries. Moses Kimhi authored an elementary introduction to the study of Hebrew known as *Mahalak Shebile Ha-daat* (or *Mahalak*). This work was the first of its kind to focus on paradigms, utilizing only indispensable rules and definitions. Kimhi is credited with introducing the now usual sequence in the enumeration of stem-forms and making the verb *paqadh* a model for conjugation. In the first half of the 16th century, *Mahalak* became the favorite manual for non-Judaic scholars; it was translated into Latin by Sebastian Münster and received Hebrew explanations from Elias Levita. Kimhi also wrote commentaries on Ezra, Nehemiah, and Proverbs, though these were falsely ascribed to Abraham ibn Ezra in great rabbinical bibles.

Regarding the technical development of the Hebrew text, the introduction of vowel points likely occurred in the 7th century, potentially borrowed from the Syrians. Two primary systems of punctuation are noted: the Babylonian (superlinear), found in certain Yemen manuscripts, and the Tiberian (sublinear), which is used in all printed editions of the Hebrew Bible.

## Hebrew and Greek Biblical Texts

Several ancient texts demonstrate the relationship between Hebrew and Greek versions. *1 Maccabees* was originally written in Hebrew, as testified by Jerome and the presence of Hebraisms and mistranslations in the Greek version; however, it is preserved only in Greek. The Book of Proverbs, part of the Hebrew "Wisdom-literature" alongside Job and Ecclesiastes, consists of generalizations on human conduct and moral exhortations. It was formed gradually, possibly reaching its final form by the 4th century B.C., though some scholars suggest it dates from the Greek period (beginning 332 B.C.).

Other texts show complex linguistic origins. A large part of one unnamed book was written in Greek, evidenced by the derivation of the name "Adam" from the initial letters of the four quarters (*anatole, dusis, arktos, mesembria*), a derivation impossible in Semitic languages. Conversely, a Hebrew Book of Enoch attributed to R. Ishmael ben Elisha (late 1st to early 2nd century B.C.) is closely related to the Book of the Secrets of Enoch.

## Latin Versions and Manuscripts

The Latin tradition includes the Old Latin version, which represents a Greek original prior to the *Hexapla*. This version circulated in Africa and was used by Cyprian; while some authorities believe it originated in Carthage, others suggest Antioch due to its agreement with early Syriac manuscripts and the Lucianic recension. The Old Latin was eventually superseded after the 6th century by the Vulgate, a Latin translation of the Old Testament produced by Jerome in the last quarter of the 4th century. Jerome translated the Vulgate from Hebrew, though he also utilized Greek versions, particularly those of Symmachus.

Among significant manuscripts is the *Codex Claromontanus* (Paris Nat. Gr. 107), a 6th-century uncial Graeco-Latin manuscript containing the Pauline epistles. It is considered a primary witness to the Greek text used in Italy at an early date. The manuscript once belonged to Beza, who acquired it from the monastery of Clermont, before it was purchased for the French royal library before 1656.

## General Historical and Numerical Records

The intersection of Greek and Latin is also evident in administrative and scientific records. The 13th Edict of Justinian contains numbers that are verified by the agreement of the Greek and Latin texts. In the field of mathematics, some suggest that ciphers were used by Greek and Latin arithmeticians before the age of Boethius, were later adopted by Arabic versions, and were restored to the Latins around the 11th century.
