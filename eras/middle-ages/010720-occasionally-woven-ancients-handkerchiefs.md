# Textiles and Fabrics of the Ancient and Modern World

The history of textiles encompasses a vast array of materials, ranging from common utilitarian cloths to rare curiosities and highly decorative fabrics used for religious, royal, and industrial purposes.

## Asbestos and Fire-Resistant Materials
Asbestos is a fibrous mineral whose name is derived from the Greek word for "unquenchable," referring to its ability to resist fire. While Greek authors such as Dioscorides applied the term to quicklime, Pliny used it in the modern sense of an incombustible material. In antiquity, asbestos was spun and woven into fabrics as rare curiosities. It was occasionally woven into handkerchiefs and, as has been said, into shrouds used during cremation to ensure the ashes of the deceased did not mingle with the wood-ashes of the pyre. Charlemagne is reported to have owned a tablecloth made of asbestos, which could be purified by being thrown into a fire. In 1676, a merchant from China presented a handkerchief of *linum asbesti*, also known as "salamander's wool," to a meeting of the Royal Society.

Beyond decorative use, asbestos served practical roles in antiquity, such as in the wicks of sacred lamps. The Eskimos of Labrador also used it as lamp-wick material. In modern industrial applications, asbestos is valued for its low thermal conductivity, high temperature resistance, and partial resistance to acids. It is used as an electric insulator, for jacketing steam-pipes and boilers, and as a filtering medium for corrosive liquids. It is manufactured into felt, yarn, and millboard for use in machinery joints, glands, and stopcocks. Other applications include fire-proof curtains for the stage, clothing for firemen, flooring, roofing, and the packing of safes. It also enters the composition of paints, plasters, and fire-proof cements. Trade names for asbestos preparations include gypsine, asbestolith, salamandrite, and uralite. Additionally, "Asbestic" is a Canadian plaster created by mixing lime with crushed serpentine rock containing asbestos seams.

## Decorative and Ceremonial Fabrics
Various cultures developed sophisticated weaving techniques for luxury and religious use. Brocade consists of richly decorative shuttle-woven fabrics, often utilizing colored silks and gold or silver threads, which create a low-relief effect on the face of the material. An example of Siculo-Saracenic brocade from the 11th or 12th century features Kufic inscriptions, including the repeated word "Arrahm[=a]n" (The Merciful), alongside patterns of geese and seated animals.

Tapestry-weaving was used for floor and seat coverings, as well as curtains and hangings. In ancient Egypt, priests of Amen Ra used such coverings in temples for religious ceremonies, and they were later used in the palaces of the Pharaohs. Remains from at least 1480 B.C. show Egyptian fabrics made of linen inwoven with colored wools. In Assyria, around 705 B.C., floor covers or rugs were produced, as evidenced by a marble slab from Nineveh depicting a geometrical pattern with lotus flowers. Egyptians also practiced patchwork for ornamental purposes, seen in an elaborately patterned canopy for the bier of an Egyptian queen (the mother-in-law of Shishak).

Other notable luxury textiles include the purple carpets of finest wool described by Callixenus the Rhodian regarding a banquet given by Ptolemy Philadelphus at Alexandria. Similarly, the tomb of Cyrus featured Babylonian tapestry and carpets of wrought purple.

## Regional and Utilitarian Cloths
Different regions utilized available natural fibers for clothing and daily needs. Early Japanese apparel primarily used cloth woven from mulberry bark and hemp. While they produced silken fabrics, the lack of a boiling process for cocoons meant the threads were coarse and uneven; consequently, silk was used mainly for cushions rather than raiment. The Japanese distinguished between *nigi-tae* (fine cloth) and *ara-tae* (coarse cloth), and produced *shidori* (striped stuff) and corrugated cloth woven from the bark of the taku.

In the Bedouin culture, men's dress includes a long cotton shirt and sometimes a black or striped cloak of hair. A handkerchief, folded once and colored black or striped yellow and red, is worn on the head and secured with twine or a hairband. Bedouin women wear a long shirt, wide loose drawers, and a wide piece of dark blue cloth that envelopes the head and figure.

Other utilitarian fabrics include dowlas, a strong, closely woven linen fabric similar to sheeting but coarser. It is used for overalls, linings, pocketing, aprons, and soldiers' gaiters, and occasionally for heavy pillow-cases or workmen's shirts.

## Specialized Handkerchiefs and Accessories
Handkerchiefs have served various cultural and practical functions. The bandana, likely derived from the Hindustani *b[=a]ndhn[=u]*, originated as a method of dyeing by tying cloth to prevent dye from reaching certain areas. This process produced richly colored silk handkerchiefs. Modern bandanas are typically made of cotton in Lancashire using calico printing.

In a modern military context, a wet handkerchief has been used as a rudimentary defense against gas attacks in trenches to provide slight relief from the effects of gas, which otherwise causes acute bronchitis, temporary blindness, and violent sickness.
