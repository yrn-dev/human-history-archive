# Barium

Barium (modern Bari) is a coastal location that served as a significant junction and port throughout various historical eras. In Roman times, it functioned as the meeting point for the coast road and the Via Traiana, with an additional branch road leading to Tarentum. Its harbor, which was mentioned as early as 181 B.C., was likely the primary harbor for the district in ancient times and served as a center for fishery. While it was not considered a place of great importance in early antiquity—evidenced only by the discovery of bronze coins struck there—its strategic value increased in later centuries.

## Medieval Governance and Conflict
The city's importance grew significantly starting in 852, when it became a seat of Saracen power. By 885, it had transitioned to become the residence of the Byzantine governor. In 1071, the city was captured by Robert Guiscard. Barium experienced several periods of destruction over the following centuries; notably, in 1156, the city was razed to the ground. By the 14th century, it had evolved into an independent duchy. Later, in 1558, Bona Sforza left the city to Philip II of Spain and Naples.

## The First Crusade and Peter the Hermit
Barium played a role in the early stages of the First Crusade. In 1095, Peter the Hermit preached the first crusade there. Peter was a native of Amiens in the province of Picardy, France, born into a gentleman's family. He had previously served militarily under the counts of Boulogne. After relinquishing the sword and the world—withdrawing first to a convent and then to a hermitage—he became an austere ascetic.

Following a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, where he witnessed the oppression of Christians and the destruction of the Holy Sepulchre by the Seljuk Turks (who had taken it in 1073), Peter returned to Europe as a fanatic. Upon landing at Bari, he sought out the Roman pontiff, Pope Urban the Second. Despite his small stature and contemptible appearance, Peter possessed a keen eye and a vehement style of speech. Pope Urban the Second received him as a prophet and encouraged him to proclaim the deliverance of the Holy Land.

## Popular Propaganda and the People's Crusade
Invigorated by the Pope's approval, Peter the Hermit conducted a popular propaganda campaign across Italy, France, and Germany. He targeted the common people rather than just knights and princes, haranguing vast crowds in markets, streets, and churches. Peter presented himself as a figure reminiscent of Hebrew prophets: he traveled barefoot, clad in a coarse garment, riding an ass and bearing a huge cross. He denounced the shame of the Holy Sepulchre being in non-Christian hands and described the brutalities and cruelties practiced by the Turks against Christian pilgrims.

This movement, known as the "people's crusade," consisted of crowds rather than organized armies. These groups of common people set out from Central Europe, the Rhineland, and France without proper equipment or leadership. The results were often disastrous:
* Two large mobs entered Hungary, where they committed atrocities against the recently converted Magyars, mistaking them for pagans, and were subsequently massacred.
* A third multitude carried out a great pogrom of the Jews in the Rhineland before also being destroyed in Hungary.
* Two other massive crowds, led by Peter the Hermit himself, managed to reach Constantinople and cross the Bosphorus. However, they were massacred by the Seljuk Turks.

During the journey through Bulgaria and Hungary, the first pilgrims caused significant disorder, forcibly seizing provisions and engaging in quarrels with the local populations. While the Greek praefect of Bulgaria and the Hungarian king retaliated bloodily, about a third of the fugitives, including Peter the Hermit, escaped to the Thracian mountains. They were eventually conducted to Constantinople by the emperor, though the pilgrims later engaged in depredations against the emperor's gardens, palaces, and churches.
