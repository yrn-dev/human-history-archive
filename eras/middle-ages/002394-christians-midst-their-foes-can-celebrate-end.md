# Christians in the Midst of Their Foes

The history of Christians often involves periods of existence within hostile environments, ranging from religious persecution and military conflict to the establishment of sovereign states in foreign lands. These experiences are characterized by a tension between the faith's tenets and the pressures exerted by opposing political or religious powers.

## Persecution and Religious Conflict
In the early Roman era, Christians faced hostility due to their refusal to participate in pagan rituals. For example, the mother of Galerius, described as a superstitious woman and a worshiper of the gods of the mountains, developed a hatred for her Christian servants. This animosity arose because the Christians fasted and prayed instead of joining in the banquets and sacrifices she celebrated nearly every day with the Pagans.

In later periods, Christians in the East and West engaged in "holy war," specifically the Crusades. Participants were persuaded of the lawfulness and merit of these actions, arguing for their peculiar title to the Holy Land and the right to natural and religious defense against foes they characterized as Pagan and Mahometan.

## Military Encounters and Survival
Christian groups have frequently found themselves targeted during religious observances. The Christian Goths, while devoutly celebrating the festival of Easter, were subjected to a surprise attack by Stilicho. The clergy termed this stratagem a sacrilege. Despite the initial confusion caused by the impetuous charge of the Imperial cavalry, the Goths were bolstered by a "pious confidence" that the God of the Christians would assert their cause, which added strength to their valor during the engagement.

In Japan, early Christians faced the anger of Hideyoshi. After receiving reports regarding the master of the *San Felipe*, Hideyoshi viewed Christians as traitors and "serpents" cherished in his bosom. He feared that these foreign influences were creating enemies among his own subjects and family, posing a threat to the child to whom he might leave the empire.

## The Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem
One of the most significant transitions from a state of vulnerability to one of political stability occurred during the First Crusade. Baldwin I, described as one of the "adventurer princes," became the real founder of the Latin kingdom of Jerusalem. He achieved this through alliances with Italian towns and by defending lay power against a nascent theocracy. 

The consolidation of his kingdom was made possible by dissensions among the Mahommedans and his own *virtu*. While he was criticized for greed, a lack of chastity, and the rejection of his Armenian wife to marry the rich Sicilian widow Adelaide in 1113, he is credited with giving his people a fatherland on the "holiest soil of history." His chaplain, Fulcher of Chartres, noted the stark contrast between the beginning of Baldwin's reign—marked by the terrors of a "lonely band of Christians in the midst of their foes"—and the end, which celebrated the formation of a new nation in the East. This transition was summarized by the phrase *qui fuimus occidentales, nunc facti sumus orientales* (we who were westerners have now become easterners).

## Internal Dissensions and Governance
Even when not facing external foes, the Christian community struggled with internal conflicts. In 325, the Emperor presided over a general council of the Church at Nicæa to reconcile fierce dissensions among theologians and create a uniform teaching. The council was stormy; for instance, when Arius spoke, Nicholas of Myra struck him in the face, and others expressed horror at Arius's heresies.

The Council of Nicæa also addressed disputes regarding the ecclesiastical calendar and the proper time to celebrate Easter. While some followed the example of the Israelites (celebrating on the 14th of the moon), the council ordained that Easter should always take place on the Sunday following the full moon after the vernal equinox. This rule was specifically designed to avoid concurrence with the Israelites and the Quartodecimans, who were regarded as heretics.
