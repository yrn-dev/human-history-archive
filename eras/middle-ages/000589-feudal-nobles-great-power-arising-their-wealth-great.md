# Feudal Nobility and Power

Across various historical contexts, the power of feudal nobles has been derived from a combination of material wealth, hereditary tradition, and administrative or military roles. The nature of this power varied significantly by region, ranging from territorial sovereignty to social distinction.

## The Nobility in Bohemia
In the Austrian dominions, specifically in Bohemia, the feudal nobles possessed substantial power rooted in three primary sources: their wealth, the long-standing traditions of their families, and their connections to the court. This influence was further solidified through the electoral law, which ensured that the nobility held a large number of representatives within the diet.

During a period of racial and political tension between Czechs and Germans, some nobles of German descent—including Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg (the cardinal archbishop of Prague), Count Leo Thun, and his brother-in-law Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz—supported the party led by Rieger and Palacky. This party sought the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy in its fullest extent, including Silesia and Moravia. These nobles hoped that a self-governing kingdom of Bohemia would allow them to preserve their power, which they felt was being threatened by German Liberals.

## Feudal Administration in France
In feudal France, the nobility exercised power through specific administrative and judicial offices. The principal officer of noble importance was the *bailli* (or seneschal). In the absence of the lord, the *bailli* held the feudal court of assizes. Additionally, a great noble might employ a *prevote* to handle smaller matters and perform preparatory work for the more significant cases reserved for the assizes.

Over time, the crown integrated these roles. Under Philip Augustus, local bailiffs appeared as part of a general administrative system, as outlined in the ordinance of 1190. Many of the first royal bailiffs were originally seigniorial bailiffs from great fiefs that had been annexed by the crown. Their duties included supervising royal provosts, organizing men who owed military service to the king under feudal rules, and managing the produce of provostships. Their judicial powers expanded over time to include hearing appeals from seigniorial justices and inferior royal judges, as well as presiding over cases where a noble was the defendant.

## The Japanese Nobility and Bushido
In Japan, the nobility were governed by *Bushido* ("military-knight-ways"), an unwritten code of laws and traditional etiquette resulting from centuries of feudalism. This code, associated with the rise of feudal institutions under the first Shogun, Yoritomo, in the late 12th century, emphasized truth and a high sense of honor, which could dictate suicide if offended.

The concept of "Benevolence" was viewed as a princely virtue and a requirement for any ruler of men. According to the teachings of Confucius and Mencius, virtue was the root from which wealth and the loyalty of the people flowed. Under the feudal régime, Benevolence was seen as a safeguard against the worst kinds of despotism and absolutism.

The power of the Japanese feudal chiefs was also subject to the control of the Tokugawa Bakufu. For a time, it was customary for great nobles to send chief retainers or their immediate family to Yedo as hostages to ensure the peaceful conduct of the feudal chiefs. This practice was abandoned in 1665 during the administrative period of Ietsuna, once the power of the Bakufu had been fully consolidated. Later, during the Revolution of 1867, the feudal chiefs themselves had little to do with the change; the movement was led primarily by fifty-five samurai of inferior grade, only thirteen of whom were aristocrats (eight court nobles and five feudal barons).

## European Ducal Power and Sovereignty
The evolution of the nobility in Europe also included the development of dukedoms. Originally, some dukes were elected by their nation for warlike prowess as military chiefs (*herizog*), while kings were chosen from royal families of divine descent. Examples of these national dukedoms include those of the Bavarians, Thuringians, Aquitanians, and Alemanni, as well as the Lombard dukes of Benevento and Spoleto and the Duke of Normandy.

As the feudal system developed, the distinction between official and national dukedoms blurred. By the 13th and 14th centuries, the title became territorial and did not necessarily imply overlordship over other nobles, such as counts, who served as tenants-in-chief of the crown. The ultimate political power of the title depended on the strength of the crown:
* In regions where the crown overcame the feudal spirit of independence, such as Naples or France, the ducal title became a social distinction without political power.
* In Germany and northern Italy, where the crown was too weak to stop disruption, the title eventually implied independent sovereignty.
