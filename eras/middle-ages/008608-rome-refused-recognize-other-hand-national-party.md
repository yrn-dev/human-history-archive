# Recognition and National Parties in Political History

The history of political and ecclesiastical governance is frequently marked by conflicts over legitimacy, where central authorities refuse to recognize specific leaders or claims, often leading to the rise of national parties or factions seeking autonomy and identity.

## Ecclesiastical and Imperial Recognition
In the 4th century, the papacy in Rome experienced significant instability regarding leadership recognition. Following the banishment of Pope Liberius in 355, the Roman clergy, including the deacon Damasus, protested. However, when Emperor Constantius sent Felix II as an anti-pope, Damasus and other clergy rallied to Felix. Upon Liberius's return and Felix's expulsion, Damasus returned to the side of Liberius. Following Liberius's death in 366, Damasus was nominated as successor by a considerable party, but a faction of "irreconcilables" from Liberius's party refused to pardon Damasus's shifting loyalties. They instead appointed another deacon, Ursinus, leading to violent riots and hand-to-hand fighting that partially destroyed the Liberian Basilica (S. Maria Maggiore), resulting in 137 deaths. While the government of Emperor Valentinian avoided ecclesiastical affairs, the prefect of Rome recognized Damasus's claims and supported him, eventually leading to the expulsion of Ursinus and his followers from Rome and central Italy.

In a broader imperial context, Byzantium initially refused to recognize the imperial title of Charlemagne. This stance changed in 812 after a disaster in 810, when Prince Krum of the pagan Bulgarians defeated the armies of Emperor Nicephorus—even using the emperor's skull as a drinking cup—and conquered much of the Balkan peninsula. Weakened by this misfortune, Byzantium formally recognized Charlemagne as Emperor and Augustus via envoys. This marked the revival of the Empire of Rome as the "Holy Roman Empire," though it remained a divided entity with its physical strength north of the Alps and its ideological center in Rome.

## The National Party in Bohemia
In 15th-century Bohemia, the question of whether the crown was elective or hereditary created a divide among the nobility. Two primary factions emerged: a Roman or Austrian division led by Ulrich of Rosenberg, and a national party led by George of Podebrad. These parties eventually reached an agreement called the "Letter of Peace" (_list mirny_). Under this agreement, signatories pledged to recognize the Compacts and support John of Rokycan as the archbishop of Prague. While the estates had chosen Rokycan in accordance with the Compacts, the Church of Rome refused to recognize him. In exchange for these concessions, the national party abandoned the candidacy of Prince Casimir of Poland for the throne, which allowed for the eventual succession of Ladislas, the son of Albert of Austria, who was formally acknowledged as king by the Bohemians in 1440.

## National Movements and Political Opposition in Germany and Austria
In 19th-century Germany, Rudolf von Bennigsen founded the National Verein to create a national party dedicated to the constitutional liberty and unity of the "Fatherland." This society emerged from public excitement following the war between France and Austria and became a significant political power despite attempts by the king of Hanover to suppress it. 

Similarly, in Austria, the German opposition became increasingly racialized starting around 1870. A party of young Germans, beginning at the university of Vienna with a reading club (_Leseverein_), focused solely on the preservation and extension of their nationality. This movement, represented in parliament by Herr von Schoenerer, viewed Bismarck as a national hero and sought union with the German empire. By 1881, various German opposition parties formed the "United Left," demanding that German be expressly recognized as the official language. Tensions eventually grew between the younger, more ardent spirits and the older constitutional leaders, who were viewed as insufficiently decisive in self-defence.

## Other Instances of Non-Recognition and Conflict
Non-recognition has also appeared in various diplomatic and regional contexts:
* **Russia and Rome:** Russian bishops and the people, influenced by their education at Mount Athos, rejected the efforts of Cardinal Isidore to bring the nation under the "Roman yoke." They were scandalized by the Latin cross and the customs of the legate, leading to Isidore's imprisonment in a monastery. The Russians further refused passage to Roman missionaries wishing to convert pagans beyond the Tanais.
* **Bavaria:** In 1831, the king of Bavaria refused to recognize the election of William Joseph Behr to the _Landtag_. Behr, a writer and publicist who agitated for reform, was later arrested for a seditious speech and imprisoned from 1836 to 1839.
* **Austria and the Czechs:** In official documents, the term "Austria" was often avoided or used cautiously due to the refusal of the Czechs to recognize that their country was part of Austria.
* **Colombia:** While Colombia's independence was recognized by other countries, internal stability was threatened when Paez, commanding in Venezuela, refused obedience to the senate and entered open rebellion, supported by a disaffected party seeking separation from the republic.
