# BRIEF

In English legal practice, a brief (derived from the Latin *brevis*, meaning short) is a written statement provided to a barrister to serve as the foundation of his case. It is believed that the term originated from the practice of the brief initially being nothing more than a copy of the original writ.

## Function and Content in England
In the English legal system, the duty of taking charge of a case once it reaches court falls upon the barrister. However, the solicitor is responsible for all preliminary tasks, including the marshalling of evidence, the serving of papers, and the drawing up of the case. Consequently, the brief provides the counsel with a concise summary of the case he is required to plead.

A brief typically includes the following elements:
*   Material facts arranged in chronological order.
*   Observations made by the solicitor regarding the case.
*   The names of witnesses and the "proofs," which describe the nature of the evidence each witness is prepared to provide if called.
*   Suggestions for counsel to use during the cross-examination of witnesses called by the opposing party.

The brief is accompanied by all documents material to the case and copies of the pleadings. For identification and administrative purposes, it is endorsed with the title of the action, the title of the court where the action will be tried, the names of the solicitor delivering the brief, and the names of the counsel. The fee for the counsel is also marked on the document.

The act of delivering a brief grants the counsel the authority to act for the client in all matters involving the litigation. Once the action is concluded, the result is noted on the brief. In instances where the action is compromised, the terms of the compromise are signed by the leading counsel of the opposite side and endorsed on each brief. In Scotland, this document is referred to as a memorial.

## The Brief-Bag
The brief-bag is used by counsel to carry papers to and from court. While now a standard part of a barrister's outfit, its use was strictly limited in the early 19th century to those who had received one from a king's counsel. At that time, king's counsel were few, served as officers of the court, and received a salary of £40 per year along with pens, paper, and purple bags. These bags were distributed as perquisites to rising juniors whose bundles of briefs had become too large to carry by hand; however, these perquisites were abolished in 1830.

Current English legal etiquette dictates the color and use of the bag:
*   **Blue bags:** Provided by barristers when they are first called. It is considered a breach of etiquette to allow a blue bag to be visible in court.
*   **Red bags:** The only bags permitted to be placed on desks. According to etiquette, a red bag is given by a leading counsel to a junior who has proven useful in an important case.

## American Legal Practice
In the United States, the term "brief" has a different application because the American attorney performs all functions that are split between solicitors and barristers in England. Therefore, an English-style brief is not required.

American lawyers may prepare a "trial brief" for their own use during a trial, which is essentially equivalent to the brief a solicitor prepares for counsel in England. However, the more distinct American usage is the brief "in error or appeal" used before an appellate court. This is a printed or written document that embodies the argument on the affected question. 

Most appellate courts require these printed briefs to be filed for the court and opposing counsel by a designated time before the hearing. According to the rules of the circuit courts of appeals and the United States Supreme Court, such a brief must include:
*   A concise statement of the case.
*   A specification of the errors relied upon, including extracts from a charge excepted to or the substance of evidence (the admission or rejection of which is to be reviewed).
*   An argument that clearly exhibits the points of fact or law to be discussed.

In certain states, this printed form of brief is also required for use at trial.

## Other Uses of the Term
In English ecclesiastical law, a brief referred to letters patent issued from chancery to other officers or churchwardens for the purpose of collecting money for the church. These were regulated by a 1704 statute. Although now obsolete, they are still mentioned in one of the rubrics in the Book of Common Prayer's Communion service.
