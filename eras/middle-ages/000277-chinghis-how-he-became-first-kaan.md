# Chinghis Kaan

Chinghis Kaan was the first Lord and sovereign of all the Tartars. Described as a man of great worth, valour, and eloquence, he rose to power in the year of Christ's Incarnation 1187, when the Tartars chose him as their King. Following his election, Tartars from throughout the world gathered to him in astonishing numbers and acknowledged him as their Lord.

## Early Conquests and Governance
Upon assuming leadership, Chinghis Kaan organized his vast multitude by preparing a great furniture of arms, specifically spears and arrows. He embarked on a campaign of conquest that resulted in the acquisition of eight provinces. His method of governance in these conquered regions was characterized by a lack of harm to the people or their property. Instead, he established a system where some of his own men were stationed in the province alongside a proportion of the local population, while the remainder of his forces were led forward to conquer other provinces. This noble treatment and the safety he provided against others led the conquered peoples to become his devoted followers, joining him heart and soul.

## Conflict with Prester John
As Chinghis Kaan's forces grew to a size that seemed to cover the earth, he sought to conquer a greater part of the world. In the year of Christ 1200, he sent an embassy to Prester John to request his daughter as a wife. Prester John reacted with anger, viewing the request as impudence. He asserted that Chinghis Kaan was his serf and liegeman, stating he would rather set his daughter in the fire than marry her to him, and declared that Chinghis deserved death as a rebel and traitor.

Upon receiving this brutal message, Chinghis Kaan was seized with rage and vowed to take a revenge for the insult that had never been so dearly paid for in the world. He mustered a host of unprecedented size and marched against Prester John. Despite the scale of the army, Prester John initially treated the threat as a trifle, though he eventually marshalled a host of many different nations to meet the challenge.

## The Battle of Tanduc
The two armies met at a vast and beautiful plain called Tanduc, which belonged to Prester John. Chinghis Kaan pitched his camp there with a multitude of people too great to be numbered. Prester John pitched his own camp 20 miles away from that of Chinghis Kaan. Both armies remained at rest for two days to ensure they were heartier and fresher for the coming combat.

During this period, Chinghis Kaan consulted astrologers of both the Saracen and Christian faiths to determine the outcome of the battle. While the Saracens could not provide a true answer, the Christians performed a divination using a cane split lengthwise. One half was named for Chinghis Kaan and the other for Prester John. After reading a Psalm from the Psalter and performing incantations, the piece of cane bearing the name of Chinghis Kaan moved without being touched and climbed on top of the piece representing Prester John. This was interpreted as a sign of victory, and as a result, Chinghis Kaan treated the Christians with great respect and regarded them as men of truth.

The subsequent engagement is described as the greatest battle ever seen. Although casualties were very great on both sides, Chinghis Kaan obtained the victory, and Prester John was slain. Following this battle, Prester John's kingdom passed day by day into the hands of Chinghis Kaan until the entire territory was conquered.

## Legacy and Succession
Chinghis Kaan established an imperial lineage that continued through several successors. Following the first Lord, the sequence of reign was as follows:
1. Cuy Kaan
2. Batuy Kaan
3. Alacou Kaan
4. Mongou Kaan
5. Cublay Kaan

Cublay Kaan, the sixth Lord in the succession, is described as being more potent than any of the five who preceded him, possessing more power than all five combined, or even the combined power of all the Christians and Saracens in the world.

According to Tartar custom, all Grand Kaans and descendants of Chinghis Kaan are interred in a mountain called Altay. Regardless of where a sovereign dies—even if the location is 100 days' journey away—the body must be carried to this mountain to be buried with his predecessors.
