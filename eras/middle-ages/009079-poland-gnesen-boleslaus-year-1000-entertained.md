# Poland

## Early Monarchy and Boleslaus I
The Polish state emerged as a struggling principality on the Vistula under Mieszko, the first Christian prince of Poland. Mieszko's court was converted from paganism to Catholicism by Jordan, the chaplain of the Bohemian princess Dobrawa (also known as Bona). Mieszko was succeeded in 992 by his son, Boleslaus I, known as "The Great" (d. 1025). A warrior and man of insight, Boleslaus I expanded the principality into a great power. In 996, he seized Pomerania to gain a seaboard and later occupied the Czech city of Cracow during periods of trouble in Bohemia.

Boleslaus I strongly associated himself with Christianity. He received Adalbert, the bishop of Prague, and after Adalbert's martyrdom at the hands of pagan Slavs on April 23, 997, Boleslaus purchased his relics. These were placed in the church of Gnesen, founded by Mieszko, which became the metropolitan see of Poland. In the year 1000, Boleslaus entertained Emperor Otto III at Gnesen with such magnificence that the emperor conferred upon him the royal crown. Despite this, Boleslaus crowned himself king a second time in the final year of his life.

Following the death of Otto III, Boleslaus invaded Germany, reaching the Elbe and occupying Meissen and Stralsund, extending his dominions to the Saale and the Elster. He also occupied Bohemia until Emperor Henry IV drove him out in 1004. The conflict with Germany ended in 1018 with the peace of Bautzen, allowing Boleslaus to retain Lusatia. He further extended his reach by routing Jaroslav, the grand duke of Kiev, on the banks of the Bug.

## Boleslaus II and Boleslaus III
Boleslaus II, called "The Bold" (r. 1039–1081), was the eldest son of Casimir I. While Casimir I had restored domestic order, the kingdom had shrunk territorially. Boleslaus II sought to restore Poland's importance, though his initial war against Bohemia was unsuccessful and ended with the marriage of his sister Swatawa to the Czech king Wratyslaus II. However, Boleslaus II assisted the Magyar prince Bela in winning the crown of Hungary. In 1069, he placed Izaslaus on the throne of Kiev, establishing Polish overlordship over Russia. 

Boleslaus II crowned himself on December 26, 1076, indicating that the royal title was not yet considered secure. He maintained good relations with Gregory VII, though he eventually fell from power due to the "cruel vengeance" he took on the *milites* (the noble order) who sought to limit royal power. He died in exile in Hungary around 1081.

Boleslaus III (r. 1086–1139), son of Wladislaus I and Judith of Bohemia, succeeded his father in 1102. His early reign was marked by conflicts with the emperor Henry V and his half-brother Zbigniew. In 1109, Henry V invaded Silesia, leading to a war of sieges at Glogau, Lubusz, and Breslau. The Germans were eventually harried into a disastrous retreat from Breslau, an event known as "the field of dogs," which secured Polish independence for twenty-five years. Boleslaus III spent this period attempting to subjugate the pagan Slavonic race of Pomerania to secure sea access. Despite heavy losses—including 18,000 fallen followers and 8,000 expatriated—he eventually forced the submission of Prince Warceslaus of Stettin between 1120 and 1124.

## Casimir III the Great
Casimir III, known as "The Great" (r. 1310–1370), was the son of Wladislaus Lokietek and Jadwiga of Kalisch. Educated in Hungary under Italian masters, Casimir preferred diplomacy and the council-chamber to the battlefield. When he ascended the throne in 1333, Poland consisted primarily of the reunited provinces of Little and Great Poland and faced threats from the Teutonic Order and John of Luxemburg of Bohemia.

Casimir employed a calculating dynastic policy to ensure safety. He established a truce with the Teutonic Order at Thorn and induced the king of Bohemia to drop his claims to the Polish throne via the conference of Trencsen in 1335, in exchange for a free hand in Silesia. He also attended the congress of Visegrad in late 1335.

## Domestic Reform and Education
Casimir III is noted for extensive domestic reforms, aided by Jaroslaw Skotowicki, the archbishop of Gnesen. In 1347, they codified the laws of Little and Great Poland, and in 1357, a supreme court of appeal was established. Casimir earned the title "the Peasants' King" due to his severity toward aristocratic oppression and tyranny. He specifically addressed the violence of the nobility in Great Poland through a severe statute that empowered the king's justices.

In the field of education, Casimir established and endowed the first university of Cracow on May 12, 1364. The university initially featured one master of arts, two professors of physics, three of Canon law, and five of Roman law. To secure the kingdom, Casimir built a cordon of fortresses on the north-eastern borders and gained dominant influence over the principality of Masovia.
