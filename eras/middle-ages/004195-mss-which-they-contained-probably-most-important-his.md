# Manuscripts in Historical and Textual Records

The study of manuscripts (MSS.) is central to the reconstruction of historical texts, the analysis of ancient languages, and the documentation of individual lives. These documents range from early biblical uncials and classical Greek orations to personal correspondence and travelogues.

## Biblical Manuscripts and Textual Criticism

In the study of the New Testament, Greek uncials dating back to the 4th century were previously considered the most decisive source of evidence. However, current perspectives suggest that versions and quotations by early writers may be more important, as they indicate the existence of text types from the 2nd and 3rd centuries that differ from extant Greek MSS.

Among the valuable uncials are the *cod. Regius* (8th century, Paris), containing the Gospels; the *cod. Laudianus* (7th century, Oxford), a Latino-Greek MS containing the Acts; the *cod. Coislinianus* (6th century), containing fragments of the Pauline epistles; and the *cod. Augiensis* (9th century, Trinity College, Cambridge), a Graeco-Latin MS related to *cod. Claromontanus*. While many minuscules exist, very few are considered of real importance.

Textual critics such as Sanday, Corssen, Wordsworth, White, Burkitt, and Harris have focused on the Vulgate and Old Latin, while Burkitt specifically examined the Old Syriac. Other contributions were made by Blass, Bousset, Schmidtke, Rendel Harris, and Chase. Historically, J.A. Bengel began the practice of grouping MSS. into "African" (old) and "Asiatic" (late) categories, giving preference to the former. Conversely, J.J. Wetstein preferred late MSS., arguing that earlier copies were contaminated by Latin. Wetstein is also noted for introducing a system of notation later amplified by Gregory.

## Syriac Versions

The Old Syriac version is currently known through two gospel manuscripts containing the *Evangelion da-Mepharreshe* (separated gospel). These are:
*   **The Curetonian:** A 5th-century MS. Fragments include MS. Brit. Mus. addit. 14.451, brought from the monastery of St. Mary in the Nitrian desert in 1842, and three leaves in Berlin (MS. Orient. Quart. 528).
*   **The Sinaitic:** A palimpsest discovered in 1892 at the library of St. Catherine's monastery on Mt. Sinai. The upper writing, dated A.D. 778, was written by John, an anchorite of Beth Mari Qanon.

While the *Diatessaron* was the normal Syriac text until the Peshito, references in the works of Ephraem and Aphraates show the Old Syriac was known. It is believed the Old Syriac also contained the Pauline epistles and Acts, though no such MSS. are currently known.

## Classical and Historical Manuscripts

In the study of Lysias, all collated MSS. derive from the *Codex Palatinus X* in Heidelberg. The *Laurentianus C* (15th century) is the next most valuable. Other consulted MSS. include those from Venice (Marciani F, G, I, K), Florence (Laurentiani D, E), the Vatican (M, N), Paris (Parisini U, V), and Urbinas (O).

Regarding the works of Marco Polo, the "Geographic Text" is considered an exact transcript of his words taken in the prison of Genoa. A second type of MS provides a refined edition, likely under Polo's instructions. A third type is embedded in the Ramusian edition, containing later supplementary recollections. A small portion of these Ramusian peculiarities was discovered in a MS. at Venice, and a curious version exists in the Irish language.

For the life of Burghley, the most significant documents are ten thousand papers at Hatfield, calendared in eight volumes. Additional records are held in the British Museum and the Record Office, particularly the Lansdowne MSS., which contain extensive correspondence. Relevant catalogues include the Royal, Sloane, Egerton, Cotton, Harleian, and Additional MSS.

## Institutional Collections and Rare Finds

The village of Grottaferrata in Italy houses a Greek monastery of Basilians founded in 1002. Its library contains valuable MSS., including one written by S. Nilus in 965. The monastery also maintains a palaeographical school dedicated to copying MSS. in the ancient style.

In other archives, the Escurial library possesses paper MSS. dating as far back as the 4th or 5th century of the Hegira. Historical testimony from the librarian Casiri indicates that paper was first imported from China to Samarcand in A.H. 30 and introduced at Mecca in A.H. 88.
