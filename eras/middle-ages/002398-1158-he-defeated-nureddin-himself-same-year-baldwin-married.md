# Baldwin III of Jerusalem

## Accession and Early Reign
Baldwin III was the eldest son of Melisinda and Fulk of Jerusalem. Born in 1130, he became the king of Jerusalem in 1143. He was the first king of Jerusalem to be a native of the soil of Palestine, whereas his three predecessors had been emigrants from the West. Because of his youth, his mother, Melisinda, served as regent until 1152.

Baldwin III ascended the throne during a period of decline for the power of the Latin kingdom of Jerusalem. The position of the Franks in northern Syria was being fatally weakened by attacks from Zengi on Edessa and by the Greeks in Cilicia. A significant blow to the kingdom occurred the year after Baldwin's accession with the loss of Edessa, which served as the most important and farthest outpost in northern Syria.

## Military Campaigns and Diplomacy
In 1147, the second crusade took place. Baldwin III, then approximately eighteen years of age, participated alongside Louis VII and Conrad III. In 1148, he received these leaders in Jerusalem and collaborated with them to plan an attack on Damascus, though the effort resulted in a signal failure.

Following the failure of the crusade, Baldwin III traveled to Antioch in 1149. His presence was required there due to the fall of Raymund, the husband of Princess Constance. While in Antioch, Baldwin III regulated affairs and sought to strengthen the north of Palestine against Nureddin, the successor to Zengi. To achieve this, he renewed a politic alliance with Damascus that had been interrupted since 1147 and ceded Tellbashir—the final remnant of the county of Edessa—to Manuel of Constantinople.

## Conflict with Melisinda
In 1152, a struggle emerged between the young king and his mother, as Melisinda had ruled with vigour and wisdom during the regency and was reluctant to relinquish power. Baldwin III initially considered a solemn coronation to signal his emancipation; however, he was dissuaded from this path and instead wore his crown publicly in the church of the Sepulchre.

The ensuing struggle led to an agreement where Baldwin III retained Tyre and Acre for himself, while his mother remained in possession of Nablus and Jerusalem. Baldwin later repented of this bargain, leading to a new struggle and subsequent fighting, through which he recovered possession of his territory.

## Character and Personal Life
Unlike his predecessors, who were described as being half clerical and half military, Baldwin III was of a subtler type. He was a studious and clerkly king, similar to his brother Amalric I, and was well-versed in law and points of dogma. He was particularly skilled in the *jus consuetudinarium* of the kingdom, later known as the "assizes of Jerusalem."

William of Tyre noted that Baldwin III had a great affection for history and spent his spare time reading. He possessed a royal faculty for remembering faces and addressing people by name, which made him more popular with all classes of society than any of his predecessors. He was known for his impromptu eloquence, his ability to handle witticisms—both those he directed at others and those directed at himself—and his excessive generosity. He maintained a deep respect for the Church.

Regarding his personal conduct, Baldwin III had been prone to gambling and was considered somewhat lax in his morals in his youth. However, after his marriage to Theodora, he changed his ways. His married life served as a shining example to his people. He practiced abstemiousness in food and drink, believing that excess in either served as an incentive to the worst crimes.

## Death and Legacy
Baldwin III's reign ended in 1162. His character was such that even his enemy, Nureddin, remarked upon his death that the world no longer had a prince like him and that the Franks had lost a great leader.
