# Anglo-Saxons

The Anglo-Saxons were a group of tribes whose history and societal structures are documented through a variety of primary and modern authorities. Their early history is characterized by conquest and the establishment of various kingdoms on the British Isles.

## Primary and Modern Authorities
The study of the Anglo-Saxons relies on several chief primary authorities, including the *Saxon Chronicle*, Bede's *Hist. Eccl.*, and the *Anglo-Saxon Laws*. Other essential primary sources include Nennius's *Historia Britonum*, Gildas's *De Excidio Britanniae*, and the *Cartularium Saxonicum* (Charters). 

Modern scholarly analysis of the period has been provided by authors such as Sharon Turner (*History of the Anglo-Saxons*), Sir F. Palgrave (*Rise and Progress of the English Commonwealth*), and J.M. Kemble (*The Saxons in England*). Other contributors to the historical record include J.R. Green, T. Hodgkin, and F. Seebohm, the latter of whom wrote on both the English village community and tribal custom in Anglo-Saxon law.

## Political Organization and the Heptarchy
Historical accounts by Bede enumerate seven kings—consisting of four Angles, two Saxons, and one Jute—who achieved varying levels of supremacy and renown. These reigns were the result of conquest rather than law. For instance, one king is noted for subduing Anglesey and the Isles of Man, while another imposed tribute upon the Picts and the Scots.

While the term "Heptarchy" is often used to describe this period, Sir F. Palgrave and Sharon Turner have argued against its accuracy. Palgrave asserts that the term is substantially wrong because there was never a period in which seven kingdoms existed independently of one another.

## Social Structure and Regional Variation
Social organization varied significantly between different Anglo-Saxon regions. In Kent, the social structure differed entirely from that found in Wessex and Mercia. While the latter two kingdoms featured two "noble" classes, Kent had only one, initially termed *eorlcund* and later *gesithcund*. 

Additionally, Kentish society included three types of persons known as *laetas* (likely freedmen), a class not found in other kingdoms. The *ceorl*, or ordinary freeman, in Kent commanded a *wergeld* (compensation value) that was two or three times greater than that of a *ceorl* in Mercia or Wessex. These distinctions in fines and compensations are attributed to the fact that the settlers in Kent were Jutes, a different nationality from those who founded the other kingdoms.

## Language and Literature
The Anglo-Saxon language developed into several regional dialects, including Northern, Midland, and Southern English. These are further categorized into Anglian, Saxon, and Kentish branches. 

Literary and linguistic evidence is found in various texts:
* **Northern/Anglian:** The works of Caedmon (660) and the *Durham Glosses* (950-975).
* **Midland/Anglian:** The *Mercian* dialect and the *Rushworth Gloss*.
* **Southern/Saxon:** The works of Alfred (885), the *Vespasian Psalter* (c. 825), and the *Battle of Maldon* (993).
* **Kentish:** The *Kentish Sermons* (1250).

The archive also notes the existence of Anglo-Saxon versions of sacred texts, including the *Book of Psalms* (such as the *Paris Psalter* and the *Regius Psalter*) and the *Holy Gospels* in Northumbrian and Old Mercian versions.

## Cultural and Material Record
The material history of the Anglo-Saxons is preserved through various means, including the *Catalogue of Anglo-Saxon Coins in the British Museum* and studies on *Saxon Obsequies*. Architectural and domestic studies, such as those by M. Heyne on the construction of the hall *Heorot* and R. Henning on the German house, provide further insight into their physical environment.
