# Huss and the Hussites

## John Huss and the Origins of the Movement
John Huss was a learned Czech who, in 1398, began delivering a series of lectures at the University of Prague based on the teachings of the Englishman Wycliffe. These teachings spread rapidly beyond the educated classes, generating significant popular enthusiasm. Huss contributed greatly to the development of the Bohemian language, writing in both Latin and his native tongue. His important Bohemian works include the *Postilla* (a collection of sermons), the *Vyklad viry, desatera Boziho prikazani, a patere* (an exposition of the creed, the ten commandments, and the Lord's Prayer) written in 1412, and treatises concerning simony and the true road to salvation.

The environment in Bohemia had already been primed for religious conflict. During the reign of King Charles, the hold of the Roman Church had been weakened by pious priests such as Waldhauser and Milic, who attacked the immorality of the clergy. This alienation was furthered by the Great Schism, during which rival pontiffs engaged in theological strife. While King Wenceslas IV was characterized by irresolution, Queen Sophia openly favored Huss and served as his confessor for a time.

## Trial and Execution
Between 1414 and 1418, the Council of Constance was convened to settle the Great Schism. Huss was invited to the Council under a promise of safe conduct provided by the emperor (and specifically by Sigismund, king of the Romans and brother of Wenceslas). Despite this guarantee, Huss was seized, put on trial for heresy, and burnt alive on July 6, 1415. A colleague of Huss, Jerome of Prague, was burnt the following year.

The execution of Huss provoked an immediate and intense reaction in Bohemia, where he was revered by the majority of the population. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. In this document, they defended Huss as a "good, just and catholic man" and declared that those who claimed heresy existed in Bohemia were "liars, vile traitors and calumniators." The nobles vowed to defend the law of Jesus Christ and its preachers at the cost of their own blood, effectively declaring war against the Roman church.

## The Hussite Wars and Crusades
The death of Huss led to an insurrection of the Hussites in 1419, marking the first of a series of religious wars that signaled the breaking-up of Latin Christendom. The movement was largely democratic, and the country was characterized by widespread enthusiasm.

In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade for the destruction of the Hussites, Wycliffites, and all other heretics in Bohemia. This invitation attracted unemployed soldiers of fortune and "drifting blackguardism" from across Europe. Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites resisted these forces. The crusaders failed to take Prague and suffered a series of reverses, leading to their retreat. A second crusade in 1421, as well as two subsequent crusades, also failed. In 1431, Catholic Crusaders were dissolved before the Hussites at Domazlice.

However, the Hussites eventually suffered from internal dissensions. This instability encouraged a fifth crusade in 1431, which crossed the frontier under Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg. The conflict eventually reached a resolution in 1436, when the Hussites came to terms with the church.

## Theological Factions and Literature
The period following Huss's death was marked by incessant theological strife. Because the movement was democratic, writers favored the national language over Latin. The Hussites were divided into different parties:
*   **The Taborites:** An extreme party whose writings were entirely destroyed.
*   **The Calixtines (or Utraquists):** A more moderate group. Some of their works survived, including the *Lives of the Priests of Tabor* and *Of the Great Torment of the Holy Church*, though these were written from a perspective hostile to the Taborite community.

Other notable figures of the era included Archbishop John of Rokycan, who wrote a *Postilla* similar to that of Huss. Peter Chelcicky (died 1460) was an independent religious writer who rejected all Hussite parties. His masterpiece, *Sit viry* (the net of faith), reflected his horror of bloodshed and his belief in accepting the decrees of worldly authorities, even if they were unjust.

Much of the controversial literature of this period perished due to the systematic destruction of Bohemian writings by the Jesuits following the battle of the White Hill in 1620.
