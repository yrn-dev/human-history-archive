## The Author and the Work
Charles du Fresne, Sieur du Cange (1610–1688), was a French scholar and critic born in Amiens on December 18, 1610. Educated at the Jesuits' college in Amiens and later in law at Orleans, he became an advocate before the parliament in Paris in 1631. Although he later purchased the office of treasurer in Amiens from his father-in-law, Catherine Dubois' father, he devoted much of his life to historical and literary research. 

Du Cange utilized the archives of Paris to study manuscripts, diplomas, and charters. His most significant contributions include the *Glossarium ad scriptores mediae et infimae latinitatis* (Paris, 1678) and the *Glossarium ad scriptores mediae et infimae graecitatis* (Lyons, 1688). The Latin *Glossarium* was expanded over time, with three supplementary volumes added by the Benedictines of St Maur between 1733 and 1736, and four additional volumes added by Pierre Carpentier (1697–1767) in 1766.

## The Concept of the Banneret
The term "banneret" (derived from the French *banniere* for banner, and the Medieval Latin *banneretus*) refers to a specific rank within feudalism. It is an elliptical term for *chevalier banneret* or *seigneur banneret*. 

In the feudal hierarchy, bannerets were nobles who possessed the right to lead their own vassals into battle under their own personal banner. This position placed them between knights bachelors and barons. Because of this placement, some have suggested that bannerets are the origin of the order of baronets created by King James I. However, the scholar Selden notes that while "old stories" sometimes substitute *baronetti* for *bannereti*, and the title became hereditary in France, the banneret has no actual relation to the later title of baronet.

## Creation and Customs of Knight Bannerets
The title of knight banneret was typically granted for distinguished service during military campaigns. According to the English custom described by Selden, a knight banneret could only be created in the field, provided that the king was present or the royal standard was displayed.

The ceremony of creation mirrored old French traditions. It involved the solemn delivery of a banner featuring the arms of the individual being created. If the individual already carried their arms on a streamer or pennon in the field, the end of that streamer was cut to transform it into a square shape or a banner.

The creation of these bannerets is traceable to the era of Edward I. Bannerets typically had knights bachelors and esquires serving under them, and they received wages based on the number of these subordinates. One of the last authentic instances of this creation occurred during the battle of Edgehill, where Charles I created John Smith a banneret for rescuing the royal standard from the enemy.
