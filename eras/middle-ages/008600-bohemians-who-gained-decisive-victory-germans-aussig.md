# Bohemians and the Victory at Aussig

The history of Bohemia is characterized by periods of internal struggle, the pursuit of sovereign independence, and repeated military conflicts with German forces. These events were often intertwined with the religious movements of the Hussites and the political ambitions of various dynasties.

## The Hussite Wars and the Victory at Aussig
During the early 15th century, royal authority in Bohemia ceased, and Sigismund was deposed during a meeting of the diet at Caslav on June 1, 1421. The Bohemian leadership decided to choose a Polish prince as their sovereign, establishing a provisional government of twenty men from various parties in the interim. Although Sigismund invaded Bohemia again in 1422, he was decisively defeated by Zizka at Nemecky Brod (Deutschbrod).

Following this, Sigismund Korybutovic, a Polish prince, arrived and was recognized as regent by a large majority of the population. However, he was recalled by Polish rulers after only a few months due to the influence of the papal see. His departure triggered the first civil war between the advanced Taborite party and the moderate Hussites, known as the Utraquists or Calixtines.

The Bohemians were reunited by the combined threat of a German invasion and the return of Prince Korybutovic. In 1426, the Bohemians achieved a decisive victory over the Germans at Aussig. Prince Korybutovic participated in this victory, but he later incurred the dislike of the extreme Hussites and was forced to leave the country, ending hopes for an independent Slav dynasty in Bohemia.

## Subsequent Crusades and Conflicts
In 1427, several German princes launched a new crusade against the Hussites. This force included 1000 English archers serving as the bodyguard to Henry Beaufort, the bishop of Winchester and papal legate. The crusaders suffered sudden panic at Tachau and Mies (Stribro) upon approaching the Hussites, leading to a hurried retreat into Bavaria. Following this success, the Bohemians shifted to the offensive, invading the German states and Hungary.

The difficulty of conquering Bohemia eventually led to the convening of a council at Basel to examine Hussite demands. Despite this, Sigismund influenced the Germans to make one final attempt to subdue the region by force. On August 1, 1431, the Bohemians defeated the Germans at Domazlice (Taus) after a short fight.

## Internal Divisions and Reconciliation
Political dissensions eventually split the Hussites into two factions: "the army of the nobles" (the aristocratic Hussites) and "the army of the towns" (the Taborites). These forces clashed at Lipan, near Kolin, on May 30, 1434. The Taborites were defeated, and their leaders, including the two Prokops, perished. This victory for the moderate party allowed for a reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund. The Bohemians recognized Sigismund as sovereign in exchange for the "Compacts," which granted them the right to preach the gospel freely, the right of communion in both kinds, and limits on the clergy's ability to acquire worldly goods.

## Later Political and Religious Struggles
By the time of the Reformation in Germany, Bohemian reformers had evolved. The Bohemian Brethren and advanced Utraquists sympathized with German Protestants. This created tension with Ferdinand, who sought to make Bohemia a hereditary Habsburg kingdom and curtail its ancient constitution.

In 1546, Ferdinand attempted to raise a Bohemian army under the pretext of a Turkish menace, but many nobles and cities—particularly those associated with the Bohemian Brethren—refused to send men. The army that did assemble consisted largely of Protestants who refused to cross the Saxon frontier, forcing Ferdinand to disband them. In 1547, the estates met in Prague without a royal summons to attempt to recover ancient liberties.

Following the imperialist victory at Muhlberg, which crushed German Protestantism, Ferdinand occupied Prague on July 20 with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries. While he treated knights and nobles with forbearance, he dealt severely with the towns, revising their privileges to confiscate municipal lands and installing royal town-captains and town-judges to control municipal affairs. The Bohemian Brethren were also persecuted, and their bishop, Augusta, was imprisoned for many years.

## Early Dynastic History and Geography
Earlier in Bohemian history, Premysl Ottakar became the undisputed ruler in 1197 and was crowned king in 1198. His descendant, Premysl Ottakar II, expanded the dominion to include Styria, Carinthia, Istria, and parts of northern Italy, defeating King Bela of Hungary at Kressenbrunn in 1260. Known as "the man of gold" for his wealth and "the man of iron" for his military power, Ottakar II encouraged German immigration to create a counterpoise to the power of the Bohemian nobility.

Geographically, Bohemia is an irregular rhomb bordered by mountain ranges: the Bohmerwald (Bohemian Forest) to the southwest, the Erzgebirge (Ore Mountains) to the northwest, and the Riesengebirge (Giant Mountains) and Sudetes to the northeast. The pass of Neumark has historically served as the primary approach from Germany and was the site of several major battles, including the 1431 Hussite victory and a 1040 battle where Wratislas II defeated German Emperor Henry III.
