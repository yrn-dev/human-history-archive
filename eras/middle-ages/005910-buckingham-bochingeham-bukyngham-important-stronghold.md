# Buckingham

Buckingham (also referred to as Bochingeham or Bukyngham) has served as a significant English stronghold since pre-Conquest times. Its history is characterized by its evolution from a military site and agricultural center to a corporate borough, alongside its association with various noble titles.

## Town History and Administration
In 918, Edward the Elder encamped his army at Buckingham for four weeks, during which time he established two forts on opposite sides of the water. By the time of the Domesday survey, the town consisted of twenty-six burgesses and, together with the hamlet of Bourton, was assessed at one hide.

Although it functioned as a borough early on, Buckingham did not receive an official charter until 1554. Queen Mary created it a free borough corporate, establishing a steward, twelve principal burgesses, and a bailiff. The boundaries of the borough were defined as extending in length from Padbury Mill bridge to Chackmore bridge, and in width from Thornborowe bridge to Dudley bridge. A subsequent charter granted by Charles II in 1684 was shortly abandoned in favor of the 1554 grant, which remained in force until the Municipal Corporations Act of 1835.

The town's political representation began in 1529, and from 1545 onward, it returned two members to parliament. This representation was reduced by the Representation of the People Act of 1867, which removed one member, and the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885, which removed the second.

## Economy and Trade
Buckingham was formerly a major center for agriculture. Edward III established one of the staples for wool in the town; however, the local trade suffered a severe decay after these staples were moved to Calais. Consequently, Buckingham was listed as one of thirty-six impoverished towns in an act of 32 Henry VIII.

The town also hosted markets and fairs. In 1522, Henry VIII granted the borough of Buckingham to Sir Henry Marney, along with two annual fairs and a Saturday market. Following this, various sovereigns provided numerous additional grants for fairs.

## Noble Titles of Buckingham
The titles associated with Buckingham are diverse, involving several different families and origins.

### The Early Earldoms and the Stafford Line
The origin of the earldom of Buckingham is obscure, though some charter evidence suggests it existed under William Rufus. Walter Giffard, lord of Longueville, is regarded as the first earl, described as "Comes Bucchingehamensis" in 1097 and 1102. After the death of the 2nd earl, Walter Giffard, in 1164, the title was assumed by Richard de Clare (Strongbow) via his wife, Rohais. The title died with him in 1176.

In 1377, Thomas of "Woodstock" was created earl of Buckingham. The title later passed to his son Humphrey, and then to Humphrey's sister Anne. Anne married Edmund Stafford, earl of Stafford, and upon her death in 1438, the title passed to their son, Humphrey Stafford. In 1444, Humphrey Stafford was created duke of Buckingham. This dukedom remained in the Stafford family until 1521, when Edward, the 3rd duke, was executed following an attainder.

Henry Stafford, the 2nd Duke of Buckingham (1454–1483), was a figure of importance to Edward IV due to his vast inheritance and royal blood. He married Catherine Woodville, the queen's sister, in 1466. Later, he became a key ally to Richard, duke of Gloucester, helping him obtain possession of the young king and urging citizens at the Guildhall on June 24, 1483, to accept Richard as king. Henry was rewarded with the offices of constable of England, chamberlain, and justiciar and chamberlain of North and South Wales. However, in August 1483, he withdrew from court to Brecon.

### The Villiers and Other Lines
In 1617, King James I created George Villiers earl of Buckingham, later making him a marquess in 1618 and a duke in 1623. This dukedom became extinct in 1687 with the death of the 2nd (Villiers) duke. A collateral line claimed the earldom until the death of George Villiers, a clergyman, in 1774.

Other titles include the "duke of the county of Buckingham and of Normanby," created in 1703 for John Sheffield, which became extinct in 1735 upon the death of his son Edmund. Additionally, the Grenville family held the title of marquess and duke of Buckingham, derived from the town rather than the county. This began in 1784 when the 2nd Earl Temple became marquess of Buckingham, and in 1822, his son Richard Grenville (1776–1839) was created duke of Buckingham and Chandos.
