# Bohemia

## Historical Conflicts and Territorial Shifts
In the early 11th century, Bohemia was involved in significant territorial disputes with the Polish principality on the Vistula. Boleslaus I, known as "The Great," succeeded his father Mieszko in 992 and expanded Polish power. Taking advantage of internal troubles within Bohemia, Boleslaus I occupied Cracow, which had previously been a Czech city. Later, following the death of Otto III, Boleslaus invaded Germany and occupied Bohemia, though he was driven out by Emperor Henry IV in 1004.

By the reign of Boleslaus II (1039–1081), Poland had shrunk territorially from the era of Boleslaus I. During this period, Poland was compelled to pay tribute to Bohemia for Silesia, Poland's oldest possession. Boleslaus II attempted a Bohemian war to restore Polish dignity, but it was unsuccessful and ended with the marriage of his sister, Swatawa, to the Czech king Wratyslaus II. Later, Boleslaus II used Russian auxiliaries to chastise Bohemia, but Wratyslaus appealed to the emperor for assistance.

## Population and Demographics
As of 1900, Bohemia had a population of 6,318,280, with a density of 315 inhabitants per square mile. This made it the second most populous Austrian province, following Galicia, and third in terms of population density, behind Silesia and Lower Austria. In 1800, the population was slightly over 3,000,000.

The population is divided by nationality, consisting of approximately 65% Czechs and 35% Germans. Czechs primarily occupy the south, south-east, and middle regions of the country. Germans are concentrated in the large towns and near the borders, particularly in the west and north. There are German-speaking enclaves within purely Czech districts, while Czechs have tended to move into manufacturing and mining districts that were purely German. Despite industrial development and rich natural resources, there is a steady flow of emigrants from Bohemia to Russia, Germany, other provinces of the monarchy, and America.

## Administration and Racial Politics
The administration of Bohemia has been marked by intense national rivalry between Czechs and Germans. In the allotment of constituencies, tax-paying capacity was considered, and separate registers were established for each race to ensure that Germans voted for Germans and Czechs for Czechs.

Under Dr. von Koerber (until December 31, 1904), attempts were made to reach a compromise. Koerber proposed dividing Bohemia into ten districts: five Czech, three German, and two mixed. He also suggested a division of the 234 district tribunals into 133 Czech, 94 German, and seven mixed. These proposals were rejected by the Czechs, who demanded that the Czech language be placed on an equal footing with German for all official purposes. The Germans refused this, as it would recognize Czech as a language of internal service. Consequently, Koerber governed largely via imperial ordinance under paragraph 14 of the constitution due to Czech obstruction in the Chamber.

## Language and Education
The contest between the two races was most vehement in Bohemia and Moravia. Over a twenty-year period, there was a constant encroachment of the Czech language upon the German. An 1880 ordinance mandated that government business be handled in the language in which it was introduced. In 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid frequent translations.

The government also founded numerous Czech elementary and middle schools and introduced Czech classes in German schools. A significant point of contention occurred in 1882 when Czech classes were started at the university of Prague, which Germans viewed as a desecration of the oldest German university.

## Political Movements and Historic Law
Racial feeling in Bohemia was supported by appeals to historic law. A major political party, led by Rieger and Palacky, sought the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy in its fullest extent, including Silesia and Moravia. They insisted that the emperor be crowned as king of Bohemia in Prague and that the region hold a position in the monarchy similar to that of Hungary. This party was supported by all Czechs and several German-descent nobles, such as Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg, Count Leo Thun, and Count Heinrich Clam-Martinitz.

Conversely, Germans in Bohemia advocated for centralization, fearing they would fall under Czech control. In Silesia, where Germans held a considerable majority and a large Polish element existed that did not support the Czechs, the diet refused to recognize Bohemian claims.

## Geography and Local Centers
Bohemia is divided into the archbishopric of Prague and three bishoprics: Leitmeritz, Koniggratz, and Budweis. Budweis (Czech: *Bud[ve]jovice*) is a major commercial center in South Bohemia located at the junction of the Moldau and Maltsch rivers. As of 1900, its population was 39,630; by 1908, it was 60% Czech. The town is a center for a German enclave and possesses a varied industry, including the manufacture of paper, chemicals, machinery, matches, bricks, tiles, and black-lead pencils. It is also known for its beer exports to America.
