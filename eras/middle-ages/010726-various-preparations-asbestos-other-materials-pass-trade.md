# Preparations and Trade of Asbestos

Asbestos is a mineral substance valued for its ability to withstand high temperatures, its low thermal conductivity, and its partial resistance to acids. Because of these properties, it is processed into a wide array of industrial and curiosity items, often combined with other materials for specific trade applications.

## Trade Names and Composite Materials
In commercial trade, various preparations of asbestos mixed with other materials are sold under specific names, including gypsine, asbestolith, salamandrite, and uralite. One such Canadian product is known as "Asbestic," which is created by crushing serpentine rock that contains thin seams of asbestos and mixing it with lime to produce a plaster. Additionally, asbestos is combined with fire-clay to create balls used in gas-stoves.

## Industrial Applications and Manufacturing
Asbestos is utilized in the industrial arts for a diverse range of applications, which continue to increase. Its resistance to heat and corrosion makes it suitable for:
*   **Machinery and Piping:** It is used as packing for glands, stopcocks, and joints, as well as for jacketing steam-pipes and boilers.
*   **Construction:** Fire-proof felt and sheathing are employed in roofing and flooring. It is also an ingredient in fire-proof paints, plasters, and cements.
*   **Safety and Insulation:** It is used to pack safes, serve as an electric insulator, and create clothing for firemen and fire-proof curtains for the stage.
*   **Filtering:** Due to its acid resistance, it serves as a filtering medium for corrosive liquids. In laboratory settings, asbestos felt is used in Gooch crucibles to filter lead sulphate.

The material is manufactured into several forms, including millboard, felt, and yarn. In Canada, specifically in the province of Quebec near Thetford and Black Lake, the rock is quarried, cobbed by hand, dried, and crushed in rock-breakers. It is then passed through rollers and "fiberizers" to reduce it to a finer state before being sorted on a shaking screen.

## Textiles and Rare Curiosities
Historically, asbestos was spun and woven into fabrics as a rare curiosity. These items were sometimes referred to as "salamander's wool" or *linum asbesti*. This terminology stemmed from a general belief in the Middle Ages across Europe and Asia that the material was derived from the salamander, an animal believed to pass through fire unhurt. In reality, the material is mineral.

Examples of such textiles include:
*   **Tablecloths:** Charlemagne is said to have owned an asbestos tablecloth that could be purified by being thrown into a fire when soiled.
*   **Handkerchiefs and Sudaria:** In 1676, a merchant from China exhibited a handkerchief of "salamander's wool" to the Royal Society. A *sudarium* (approximately 20 inches long) found in a Pagan tomb on the Appian Way is also displayed in the Vatican Museum.
*   **Clothing:** Madame Perpenté, an Italian experimenter, succeeded in carding and reeling asbestos fibers—which she found could be elicited from a crude mass to lengths of several meters—to create gloves and other items.

## Varieties and Regional Occurrences
Asbestos is categorized by the character of its fibers:
*   **Amphibole-asbestos:** Including tremolite and anthophyllite, these are usually grey or white. They can have very long fibers—some Italian varieties reaching 5 or 6 feet—but are often brittle and harsh.
*   **Serpentine-asbestos:** Known mineralogically as chrysotile, these fibers are shorter (2 to 3 inches) but possess great tensile strength, flexibility, and a silky, yellowish or greenish lustre.

When the fibers are flexible and silky, they are called "mountain flax," and the finer kinds are termed amianthus. If the fibers form a naturally interwoven felted mass, the mineral is known by trivial names such as mountain paper, mountain cork, or mountain leather.

Geographically, asbestos is found in several regions:
*   **Asia:** It is found in Shantung, where natives use it for crucibles and stoves. Marco Polo also noted its use in the region for incombustible paper and small furnaces.
*   **North America:** Beyond the major deposits in Quebec, it is found in the United States in Vermont (serpentine) and Georgia (amphibole), as well as in Arizona, Massachusetts, Virginia, and South Carolina.
*   **Other Uses:** The Eskimos of Labrador have used asbestos as lamp-wicks, a practice mirrored in some ancient sacred lamps.
