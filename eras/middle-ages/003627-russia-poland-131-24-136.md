# Russia and Poland

The historical relationship between Russia and Poland is characterized by centuries of territorial conflict, shifting borders, and the eventual dissolution and rebirth of the Polish state. These interactions were often influenced by the geopolitical ambitions of neighboring powers, including Prussia, Austria, and Sweden.

## Cultural and Religious Foundations
Poland emerged as a center of Slav consolidation during the tenth and eleventh centuries. Unlike Russia, Poland was not "orthodox" but Roman Catholic in its religious orientation. This distinction extended to its literacy and political structure; Poland utilized the Latin alphabet rather than Russian letters, and its monarch did not assume absolute independence from the Emperor. In its origins, Poland was an outlying part of the Holy Empire and Christendom, a status that Russia never shared.

## Territorial Disputes and Fortifications
Control over strategic border regions was a recurring point of contention. The fortress of Dunaburg, founded in 1278 by the Livonian Knights of the Sword, serves as an example of this volatility. In 1559, it was mortgaged to Sigismund Augustus, the king of Poland. Although Ivan the Terrible of Russia captured the site in 1576, it was later restored to Poland, and in 1582, King Stephen Bathori moved the fortress to its current location. Throughout the 17th century, the site was held alternately by Russia and Sweden before being incorporated into Russia in 1772 during the first division of Poland.

Similarly, the region of Samogitia (now the government of Kovno) was part of Lithuania before being annexed to Poland in 1569. This area suffered significantly from the wars between Russia, Sweden, and Poland, as well as the invasion of Charles XII in 1701. The principality of Lithuania was eventually annexed to Russia in 1795.

## The Partitions of Poland
The dissolution of Poland was driven by the interests of Frederick the Great of Prussia, Catherine the Second of Russia, and Maria Theresa of Austria. Frederick the Great viewed Poland's existence as provocative because a Polish arm reached the Baltic at Dantzig, separating his territories in the empire from his ancestral dominions in East Prussia. He incited Catherine and Maria Theresa to launch a joint attack.

The first treaty of partition occurred on August 5, 1772. During this process, Austria acquired Galicia and Lodomeria, while Russia incorporated territories such as Dunaburg. This "first outrage" sparked a national awakening within Poland. The nation underwent a period of rapid development in art, literature, and education. To combat foreign intrigues and impotence, Poland abolished the free veto, established a Parliament modeled after the British system, and made the crown hereditary.

However, these reforms were resented by supporters of the old order, who were backed by Russia and Prussia. This led to a second partition and a fierce patriotic struggle led by the national hero Kosciusko. Eventually, Poland was completely obliterated from the map.

## Struggle for Independence and Recovery
Following its disappearance as a sovereign state, Poland remained a "submerged creature" for 120 years, struggling beneath a political and military net. The passion for republicanism and patriotism grew stronger during this period of suppression. In cities like Lublin, which had been taken by the Russians in 1831, the surrounding countryside became rich with the history of the struggle for independence. Poland finally rose again as a nation in 1918 at the conclusion of the Great War.
