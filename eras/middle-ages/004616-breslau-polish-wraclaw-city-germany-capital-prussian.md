# BRESLAU

Breslau (known in Polish as *Wraclaw* and in Latin as *Vratislavia*) is a German city serving as the capital of the Prussian province of Silesia. It is an episcopal see situated on a wide, fertile plain on both banks of the navigable Oder river, 350 miles from the river's mouth and 202 miles from Berlin via the railway to Vienna. The city is located at the influx of the Ohle.

## Demographics and Geography
As of 1905, the population of Breslau was 470,751. The religious composition of the city was approximately 60% Protestant, 35% Roman Catholic, and nearly 5% Jews. Earlier census data recorded populations of 171,926 in 1867, 272,912 in 1880, 299,640 in 1885, and 335,186 in 1890.

The Oder river divides the city into two unequal halves via several arms and is crossed by numerous bridges. The larger portion of the city lies on the left bank and contains the inner or old town. This area is situated on an eminence known as Liebichs Höhe, which provides a fine view of the surrounding country. The inner town is characterized by narrow streets and medieval buildings. Surrounding the inner town are beautiful promenades located on the site of ramparts that were dismantled after 1813. The new town and extensive suburbs lie across the Oder and outside the inner town, with the Oder quarter in the north and the Schweidnitz quarter in the south featuring spacious squares and handsome streets.

## History
Breslau was probably founded shortly before the year A.D. 1000, when it was first mentioned by the chronicler Thietmar, bishop of Merseburg. It became the seat of a bishop early in the 11th century. After a period as part of Poland, it became the capital of an independent duchy in 1163. Although destroyed by the Mongols in 1241, the city recovered its prosperity and saw a large influx of German colonists. In 1290, the bishop was granted the title of a prince of the Empire.

Following the death of Henry VI, the last duke of Breslau, in 1335, the city was purchased by John, king of Bohemia. It remained under the control of Bohemian successors until approximately 1460. During this time, the Bohemian kings granted privileges that expanded the city's commerce and increased the wealth and independence of its citizens. In 1463, due to a dislike of the Hussites, Breslau placed itself under the protection of Pope Pius II, later coming under the rule of the Hungarian king Matthias Corvinus. After 1490, it returned to Bohemian subjecthood and passed to the Habsburgs in 1526 when Ferdinand was chosen king of Bohemia.

The city remained largely undisturbed through the Thirty Years' War and the Reformation until 1741, when it was forced to acknowledge the authority of Frederick the Great. The Austrians recovered the city in 1757, but Frederick regained it later that same year following his victory at Leuthen. Since then, it has belonged to Prussia, despite brief French occupations in 1807 (after the battle of Jena) and 1813 (after the battle of Bautzen). In March 1813, King Frederick William III issued the appeals *An mein Volk* and *An mein Kriegesheer* from Breslau, making the city the center of Prussian preparations for the campaign that ended at Leipzig. Following the Prussian victory at Sadowa in 1866, William I made a triumphant entry into the city.

## Architecture and Public Buildings
Breslau is rich in fine monuments and historic structures. The cathedral, dedicated to St John the Baptist, was begun in 1148, completed at the end of the 15th century, and underwent enlargements in the 17th and 18th centuries before being restored between 1873 and 1875. It contains a high altar of beaten silver and various sculptures and paintings. Other notable Roman Catholic churches include the 13th- and 14th-century brick Kreuzkirche (church of the Holy Cross), which houses the tomb of Henry IV, duke of Silesia; the 14th-century Sandkirche; and the Dorotheenor Minoritenkirche, founded in 1351 by Emperor Charles IV.

The city's business streets converge on the Ring, a market square containing the town-hall. This Gothic building, constructed between the mid-14th and 16th centuries, houses the Fürstensaal (where the diets of Silesia were held) and the Schweidnitzer Keller, a beer and wine house in use since 1355.

Other significant public buildings include:
* The royal palace and government offices (erected in 1887).
* The Stadthaus (civic hall), the exchange, and the Stadt and Lobe theatres.
* The Silesian museum of arts and crafts and antiquities, located in the former Ständehaus.
* The museum of fine arts, municipal archives, courts of law, and the provincial House of Assembly.

The city also features equestrian statues of Frederick the Great and Frederick William III, a statue of Blücher, and monuments to General Tauentzien, the jurist Karl Gottlieb Svarez, the theologian Schleiermacher (born in Breslau in 1768), and figures such as Bismarck, Moltke, and Emperor William I.

## Education and Culture
The university of Breslau is the city's foremost educational establishment. Founded in 1702 by Emperor Leopold I as a Jesuit college, it was greatly expanded in 1811 by the incorporation of the university of Frankfort-on-Oder. The university is housed in a spacious Gothic building facing the Oder, built between 1728 and 1736 on the site of a former imperial castle. It features the Aula Leopoldina, a hall ornamented with frescoes that can hold 1,200 people. The university library contains 4,000 manuscripts and 306,000 volumes, including the *Bibliotheca Habichtiana*, a valuable collection of oriental literature.
