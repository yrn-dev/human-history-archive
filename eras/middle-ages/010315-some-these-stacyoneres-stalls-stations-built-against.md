# Stationers and Stalls in Historical Context

## The London Stationers
Prior to the Reformation, text writers known as stationers (_stacyoneres_) operated in the neighborhood of St Paul's in London. These individuals sold various manuscripts and copies of books then in use, including the Creed, the Paternoster, the Ave Maria, the ABC, and other copies of prayers. In 1403, these stationers were formed into a gild. Some of these _stacyoneres_ had stations or stalls built directly against the walls of the cathedral itself, a practice similar to that found in some older continental cities.

## University Stationers in Oxford
In Oxford, the supply of books was managed by "sworn" university stationers, such as John More, who held a monopoly to a great extent. These sworn stationers supplied pupils with books and, in the case of John More, also acted as a pawnbroker. This arrangement existed because most students could not afford to purchase books, and the instruction they received was almost entirely oral. Despite the monopoly, there were many booksellers in Oxford who were not sworn. This led to a statute passed in 1373, which noted that the presence of non-sworn booksellers resulted in the cheating of owners and the loss of lawful business for sworn stationers, as books of great value were being sold and carried away from Oxford. Consequently, it was enacted that no bookseller—except for two sworn stationers or their deputies—could sell any book exceeding half a mark in value, whether the book was their own property or belonged to another.

## Ecclesiastical and Architectural Stalls
The term "stalls" also appears in the context of church furnishings across various regions:
* **Dordrecht:** The Groote Kerk of Our Lady, dating from the 14th century, contains finely carved stalls from 1540 created by Jan Terween Aertsz.
* **Asciano:** The Benedictine monastery of Monte Oliveto Maggiore, founded in 1320, features fine inlaid choir stalls in its church, crafted by Fra Giovanni da Verona.
* **Aylesbury:** The church of St Mary contains chancel stalls dating from the 15th century.
* **Cartmel:** The church of the priory of Cartmel (1188) contains stalls of an earlier date than its Jacobean carved oak screens.

## Animal Stalls and Stables
In military and ancient contexts, stalls refer to animal housing:
* **Cavalry Stables:** In troop stables, horses are separated by bails. Officers' stables are typically built in separate blocks and usually feature only one row of stalls, which are divided by partitions. In infirmary stables, stalls and loose boxes are placed in a separate enclosure at a distance from troop stables to provide 2000 cub. ft. of air space per horse.
* **Imperial Volunteer Automobile Corps:** In the stables of this establishment, every stall was occupied by recently requisitioned farm horses.
* **Ancient Bauli:** The villa resort of Bauli near Baiae derives its name from the Greek _Boaulia_, meaning stalls, in which Hercules concealed the oxen of Geryon.
