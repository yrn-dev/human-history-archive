# The Recapture of Constantinople (1261)

The events of 1261 marked a pivotal shift in the political and religious landscape of the East, characterized by the collapse of the Latin Empire of Constantinople and the establishment of a new Greek dynasty under Michael VIII Palaeologus.

## The Fall of the Latin Empire
By the mid-13th century, the Latin Empire had entered a period of significant decline. Baldwin II, the last Latin Emperor of Constantinople, reigned from 1237 to 1261. His rule was characterized by desperate financial straits; the realm he governed had shrunk until it was little more than the city of Constantinople itself. Baldwin II spent much of his reign on "mendicant tours" across western Europe, visiting France, Rome, and Flanders to beg for money and men to recover lost territories. His financial desperation was so extreme that he once handed over his only son, Philip, to merchants as a pledge for loans, though Philip was later redeemed by King Louis IX.

In 1261, this "feeble and tottering" Latin dynasty ended when Constantinople was captured by Michael Palaeologus. Baldwin II escaped the city in a Venetian galley, fleeing first to Negropont, then to Athens, Apulia, and finally France, where he continued to seek help from western powers as a titular emperor.

## Michael VIII Palaeologus and the Greek Restoration
Michael Palaeologus founded a new Greek dynasty upon the recapture of the city. To secure his power and oppose the efforts of Baldwin to recover his fortunes, Michael utilized a specific class of troops known as the Gasmuls (or *Guasmul*). These were "mixt breeds" born of Italian fathers and Greek mothers. According to the historian George Pachymeres, Michael depended on the Gasmuls to man his ships because they combined the "dash and pertinacity of the Latins" with the "military wariness and quick wit of the Greeks." Nicephorus Gregoras notes that Michael manned 60 galleys primarily with this group.

## Geopolitical Alliances and the Genoese Conflict
The recapture of Constantinople was intertwined with the fierce rivalry between Venice and Genoa. A long-standing conflict had erupted in 1255 at Acre over a church called St. Sabba’s, leading to a war that spanned Syria. While Venice initially held the upper hand, driving Genoa from Acre and taking porphyry figures from St. Sabba’s as trophies to St. Mark’s in Venice, Genoa eventually sought revenge.

In an effort to upset the preponderance of Venice on the Bosphorus, Genoa allied itself with Michael Palaeologus. Following the Greek victory, the new emperor handed over the castle of the Venetians to his Genoese allies, who tore it down and sent the stones to Genoa as trophies. This intensified the mutual hatred between the two maritime states, to the point that merchant fleets required convoys and ships fought whenever they encountered one another.

## Religious Implications and the Papacy
The events of 1261 had profound consequences for the Roman Catholic Church and the papacy. The recapture of Constantinople by the Greeks, combined with the fall of the Latin kingdoms in Asia, ended the "eastward ascendancy of the Popes."

Initially, Michael VIII Palaeologus made several "unreal tentatives" at reconciliation with the Pope. However, he eventually broke away from the Roman communion entirely. This occurred during a period when the papacy was already facing a decay of its sustaining forces, largely due to conflicts with the growing power of the French King. While a series of Popes had supported French monarchs—establishing French princes in Naples and Sicily—the overall influence of Rome in the East vanished with the rise of the Palaeologus dynasty.
