# The Black Death: How Plague Reshaped Europe

## Overview
The Black Death (1347–1351) was one of the most devastating pandemics in human history, caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*. Originating in Central Asia and traveling via the Silk Road and maritime trade routes, the plague entered Europe through Sicilian ports in 1347. It is estimated that the pandemic killed between 30% and 60% of Europe's population.

Beyond the immediate demographic catastrophe, the plague acted as a catalyst for profound socio-economic transformation. The sudden scarcity of labor shattered the foundations of feudalism. With fewer peasants to work the land, survivors demanded higher wages and greater freedom, leading to the decline of serfdom in Western Europe. While the Church remained a central pillar of society, its inability to stop the plague led some to question ecclesiastical authority, planting early seeds of skepticism that would later bloom during the Renaissance and Reformation.

## Key Facts
*   **The Vector:** The plague was primarily spread by fleas living on black rats (*Rattus rattus*), though historians and epidemiologists debate the role of human-to-human transmission via respiratory droplets (pneumonic plague).
*   **Economic Shift:** The "Labor Shortage" forced landowners to compete for workers. In England, the government attempted to freeze wages via the *Statute of Labourers (1351)*, but market forces largely prevailed, increasing the standard of living for the peasantry.
*   **Psychological Impact:** The era saw the rise of the *Danse Macabre* (Dance of Death) in art, reflecting a cultural obsession with the universality of death.
*   **Scapegoating:** The pandemic triggered violent pogroms against Jewish communities, who were falsely accused of poisoning wells to spread the disease.

## Did You Know?
1. **Quarantine Origins:** The word "quarantine" comes from the Venetian *quaranta giorni* (forty days), the period ships were required to wait in isolation before docking.
2. **The Malthusian Debate:** Some historians argue Europe was already overpopulated and facing famine (the Great Famine of 1315–1317), suggesting the plague was a "Malthusian check" that corrected the population-to-resource ratio.
3. **Medical Futility:** Medieval doctors used "miasma theory," believing the plague was caused by "bad air." Their remedies—such as smelling sweet herbs or bloodletting—were entirely ineffective.
