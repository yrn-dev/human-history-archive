## Arabian Geographers and Scholars
The archive contains records of several notable Arabian scholars and geographers. Among them is Bakri (Abū 'Ubaid 'Abdallah ibn 'Abd ul-'Azīz ul-Bakrī), an Arabian geographer born in Cordova who lived from 1040 to 1094. Bakri is recognized for two primary works: a general geography of the world, which exists in manuscript form (the section regarding North Africa was edited by McG. de Slane in 1857), and a dictionary of geographical names appearing in the works of poets. This dictionary includes an introduction focused on the seats of the Arabian tribes and was edited by F. Wuestenfeld between 1876 and 1877.

Additionally, the archive references Avicenna, whose life is documented in the *Biographical Dictionary* of Ibn Khallikan (translated by McG. de Slane in 1842) and in F. Wuestenfeld's *Geschichte der arabischen Aerzte und Naturforscher* (1840). His contributions to medicine are noted in Sprengel's *Histoire de la Medecine*, while his philosophy is discussed in Shahrastani (German translation vol. ii. 213-332), K. Prantl's *Geschichte der Logik*, and other academic treatises.

## Ancient Greek Legends and Topography
The archive preserves various accounts of Greek legends and the topography of ancient sites. In the region of Pylos, ancient worship of Hadês and Poseidôn was prominent, accompanied by numerous local legends. Regarding the figure of Nêleus, king of Pylos, Homeric legend suggests he was not killed by Hêraklês. However, later logographers and poets, followed by Apollodôrus, altered this legend to suggest that Nêleus should have been held accountable for the offenses avenged upon his sons. Legendary causes for the conflict include Nêleus's refusal to grant purification to Hêraklês, and historical interpretations suggesting Nêleus aided the Orchomenians in war against the Thêbans and Hêraklês.

Other records discuss Æneas and his voyage to Italy. Dêmêtrius conjectured that Skêpsis served as the regal seat of Æneas, noting a nearby village called Æneia. Following the departure of the Greeks, Ascanius became the king of Ida. In Athens, the acropolis featured four of the eight celebrated statues of Athênê created by Pheidias.

## Architectural and Linguistic Terms
The archive provides technical definitions for specific architectural and linguistic terms. In architecture, an "imbrex" is a covering tile used on ancient roofs. The plain tile is turned up on each side, and the imbrex covers the joint. While typically semicircular, some Greek temples utilized imbrexes with angular tops and vertical sides. In the temple of Apollo at Bassae, these were crafted from Parian marble, with the tile and imbrex worked from a single piece of solid marble.

In linguistics, the archive notes the evolution of the Dutch language. Middle Dutch featured sharpened final consonants. In Modern Dutch, soft consonants are written more frequently than they are sounded to maintain agreement with inflexional forms (for example, Middle Dutch *dach* became Modern Dutch *dag*). The gutturals include *g*, *k*, *ch*, and *h*. The letter *g* is a soft spirant not used in English. The sound *ch* (similar to German *ch* without the *i*-sound) loses its sound when combined with *s* to form *sch* at the end of a syllable, such as in *vleesch*. As an initial consonant, *sch* is pronounced nearly as *sg* (as in *schip*), though Saxon and Frisian dialects retain the old *sk* sound (as in *skip* or *skool*).

## General Terminology
The archive defines "imbecile" as a term derived from the Latin *imbecillus* or *imbecillis*, meaning weak or feeble, specifically in mind. Conventionally, "imbecility" refers to a state of mental degeneration that is less profound than "idiotcy."
