# The Epoch of the Gen (Minamoto) and the Hei (Taira)

The Epoch of the Gen and the Hei refers to a significant period in Japanese history dominated by the struggle and influence of two great military families: the Minamoto (Gen) and the Taira (Hei). These families, often referred to together as the "Gen-pei," rose to prominence as the military class, or *bushi*, gradually acquired influence while performing duties as police and guards.

## Origins of the Minamoto and Taira
The Minamoto (Gen) family originated from the Imperial line. Emperor Saga (810) had fifty children, and those from the sixth son downwards were grouped under the *uji* (sept) of Minamoto. This practice continued under Emperor Seiwa (859-876), who gave the Minamoto *uji* to all his sons except the Crown Prince. Among the early Minamoto, Prince Rokuson (Tsunemoto), a grandson of Emperor Seiwa, was particularly celebrated for combining the science of commanding troops with the art of composing couplets. Appointed governor of Musashi in the Shohyo era (931-937), his descendants formed the principal of fourteen Minamoto houses, known as the Seiwa Genji. Other branches included the Saga Genji and the Murakami Genji (descended from Emperor Murakami, 947-967).

The Taira (Hei) family is described as being descended from Prince Katsurabara. They served as generals of the Imperial guards and were referred to as the Heike. The family gained fame during the time of Taira no Sadamori, who quelled the insurrection of Masakado. The Taira expanded into various branches, including the Ise-Heishi (descended from Sadamori's son Korehira), the Daijo-uji of Hitachi, the Shiro-uji of Mutsu, Dewa, Shinano, and Echigo, and the Soma family, who succeeded to the domains of Masakado.

## The Rise of the Bushi
As the Court relied on these families for force, the *bushi* developed a recognized code of ethics. This period saw the sword become an object of profound veneration. For example, Minamoto Mitsunaka commissioned a swordsmith to forge two blades—"Knee-cutter" and "Beard-cutter"—which were tempered for sixty days and became treasured heirlooms of the Minamoto.

The Imperial Court employed a strategy of "short-sighted shrewdness" by pitting the two families against one another. If the Taira became turbulent, the Court enlisted the Minamoto; if a Minamoto rebelled, a Taira was commissioned to handle the situation. While this provided temporary peace for the Throne, it sowed seeds of discord between the two clans. In the capital, the *bushi* served as palace guards, while in the provinces, they operated with practical independence.

## The Gen-pei Struggle
The tension between the families culminated in a fierce struggle known as the Gen-pei epoch. This period was marked by significant conflict, including the tumult of the Hogen and Heiji eras (1150-1160). During the Heiji tumult, the Taira crushed the Minamoto and held the most important offices.

Key figures in the Taira lineage included Tadamori, who served Emperors Shirakawa, Horikawa, and Toba and subdued pirates of Nankai-do and Sanyo-do, and Kiyomori, who temporarily established Taira supremacy by crushing the Minamoto. However, the Taira eventually faced a conspiracy by Yorimasa and were later defeated by the Minamoto. Notable Taira figures killed during these conflicts include Atsumori (1169-84), who died at Ichi-no-tani.

The Minamoto lineage produced several illustrious chiefs descending from Tsunemoto, including Mitsunaka, Yorimitsu, Yorinobu, Yoriyoshi, and Yoshiiye. Later generations included Yoshitomo, and his sons Yoshinaka (of Kiso), Yoritomo, Noriyori, and Yoshitsune.

## Historical Records and Legacy
The events of this epoch are documented in various historical works. The *Genpei Seisuiki* (Records of Minamoto and Taira) provides an account of the era. Additionally, the *Genji Monogatari* ("Narrative of Minamoto"), written by Murasaki Shikibu, is a noted work from the broader cultural context of the time.

The struggle between the Gen and the Hei eventually led to the supremacy of the military class, transitioning Japan into subsequent eras, including the Kamakura Bakufu.
