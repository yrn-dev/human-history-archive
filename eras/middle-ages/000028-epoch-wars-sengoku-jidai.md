# The Epoch of Wars (Sengoku Jidai)

The Epoch of Wars, known in Japanese as *Sengoku Jidai*, represents one of the most disturbed periods in the history of Japan. While Japanese annalists typically set this interval between the close of the fifteenth century and the end of the sixteenth, the onset of this "evil time" is more accurately dated to the Onin year-period (1467–1469). This era is characterized by a systemic collapse of law, order, and morality, resulting in a century of widespread conflict.

## Origins and Social Collapse
The transition into the Sengoku Jidai was marked by a fundamental shift in the recognition of power. During the Onin era, a principle was established that the right of succession to a family estate justified the use of arms, and that such conflicts existed beyond the jurisdiction of the central authority. This led to disturbances that increased in both intensity and geographical area.

The resulting social upheaval subverted traditional ethics and family bonds; the archive records instances of sons turning against fathers, brothers against brothers, and vassals against their chiefs. This period of "supremacy of the sword" eventually led to the ruin of the Ashikaga shogunate. Despite this chaos, some individuals continued to support the institutions of the State and reverence the Throne, although the Emperor generally lacked the competence to enforce mandates and relied on the voluntary cooperation of influential subjects.

## Economic and Agricultural Factors
Financial disorder is cited as a material contributor to the calamities of the Battle era (the eleven decades ending in 1600). The fiscal system, which was already defective during the Ashikaga age, fell into "measureless confusion" during the Sengoku Jidai. 

A significant factor was the burden of taxes, such as the *hyakusho-yaku* (farmers' dues) levied by the *jito*. Originally set at one percent of the land-tax, this rate was later doubled, and other arbitrary imposts were frequently enacted. This instability had a visible impact on agriculture; rice cultivation area, which stood at approximately one million *did* in the middle of the fifteenth century, decreased by more than fifty thousand *cho* by the close of that century.

## Military Conflict and Power Struggles
The era was defined by the rise of powerful military figures and shifting alliances. One notable conflict was the War of Komaki, which involved Ieyasu and Hideyoshi. In March 1584, Ieyasu set out from Hamamatsu to join Nobukatsu at Kiyosu in Owari. Ieyasu attempted to force Hideyoshi to fight three simultaneous wars by coordinating with Sasa Narimasa in Echizen, Chosokabe Motochika in Shikoku, and military monks in the province of Kii.

Hideyoshi responded with astuteness, commissioning Uesugi Kagekatsu and Maeda Toshiiye to oppose Sasa, Hachisuka to oppose the monks of Kii, and Sengoku Hidehisa to hold Chosokabe Motochika in check at Awaji. The conflict eventually centered on Komaki-yama, where Ieyasu entrenched his forces to await the Osaka army.

Other regional conflicts involved the Shimazu forces of Satsuma. In one instance, Sengoku Hidehisa ignored Hideyoshi's warnings against precipitating warlike measures and directed Otomo troops to attack the Satsuma baron. This resulted in a disaster where the Shimazu took sixteen forts in Bungo, bringing half of Kyushu under their sway. In response, Hideyoshi confiscated Hidehisa's estates and mobilized a massive force, eventually assembling 150,000 men at Osaka to invest the castle of Takashiro.

## Cultural Developments
Despite the prevailing warfare, the period saw the continuation and formalization of certain arts. The *no* (which grew out of *yokyoku*) was a solemn, stately drama performed on open-air stages with historical themes, accompanied by a chorus of musicians playing the flute and drum. Because Buddhist friars held a monopoly on learning, they heavily influenced the gravity and tone of these performances. The *no* was often paired with *kyogen*, a comic and sprightly form of drama designed to relieve the heaviness of the *no*.

The *no* dance was officially invested with the status of a ceremonious accomplishment for military men by the shogun Yoshimasa, and Hideyoshi himself was known to join the dancers on stage.
