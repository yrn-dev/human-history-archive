# The Black Death: How Plague Reshaped Europe

## Overview
The Black Death, which ravaged Europe between 1347 and 1351, was one of the most transformative catastrophes in human history. Caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*, the pandemic is estimated to have killed between 30% and 60% of the European population. While the immediate result was unfathomable loss of life and social chaos, the long-term consequences acted as a catalyst for the collapse of the medieval feudal system and the eventual dawn of the Renaissance.

The plague fundamentally altered the relationship between land and labor. With a drastically reduced workforce, surviving peasants found themselves in a position of unprecedented bargaining power. This shift led to higher wages and the decline of serfdom, as lords were forced to offer concessions to attract laborers. Simultaneously, the failure of the Church to provide spiritual or physical protection against the pestilence led to a gradual decline in papal prestige and a rise in individualistic piety.

## Key Facts
*   **Transmission:** The disease was primarily spread by fleas living on black rats (*Rattus rattus*), though historians and epidemiologists debate the extent to which pneumonic transmission (person-to-person via droplets) accelerated the spread.
*   **Economic Shift:** The "labor shortage" caused by the mass mortality led to the *Statute of Labourers (1351)* in England, an attempt by the elite to freeze wages at pre-plague levels. This tension contributed directly to the Peasants' Revolt of 1381.
*   **Medical Understanding:** Medieval medicine, based on the theory of "miasma" (bad air) and the four humors, was powerless against the bacteria. This failure encouraged a slow shift toward empirical observation and anatomy.
*   **Psychological Impact:** The era saw the rise of the *Danse Macabre* (Dance of Death) in art, reflecting a cultural obsession with the universality of death regardless of social rank.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Caffa Incident:** Some historians cite the Siege of Caffa (1346) as one of the earliest recorded instances of biological warfare, where the Mongol army allegedly catapulted plague-infected corpses over the city walls.
2. **Quarantine Origins:** The word "quarantine" comes from the Venetian *quaranta giorni* (forty days), referring to the period ships were required to anchor offshore to ensure they were plague-free.
3. **Dietary Improvement:** Because there were fewer people to feed, the surviving lower classes often had access to better quality food, including more meat and dairy, which improved general health in the late 14th century.
