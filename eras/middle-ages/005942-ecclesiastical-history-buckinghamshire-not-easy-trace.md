# Ecclesiastical History of Buckinghamshire

The ecclesiastical history of Buckinghamshire is characterized by a lack of local chroniclers, making its early trajectory difficult to trace. However, archival records provide a framework for its jurisdictional evolution and the administrative structure of its parishes.

## Jurisdictional Evolution
The earliest churches in Buckinghamshire were likely subject to the West Saxon see of Dorchester. Following the Norman Conquest, the bishop's stool was transferred to Lincoln, though this move did not result in a change of jurisdiction for the county. Buckinghamshire remained part of the Lincoln diocese for several centuries.

Following the dissolution of the monasteries, a proposal was made to establish a new diocese that would encompass both Buckinghamshire and Bedfordshire. This project was abandoned, and the two counties continued under the jurisdiction of the Lincoln diocese until 1837, at which point the area was transferred to the diocese of Oxford. Currently, Buckinghamshire is almost entirely within the diocese of Oxford, which contains 215 ecclesiastical parishes situated wholly or in part within the county.

## Administrative Structure and Deaneries
The arch-deaconry of the region was likely established toward the end of the 11th century by Bishop Rémy. Shortly after the founding of the arch-deaconry, the region was subdivided into rural deaneries. Records from the 12th century mention a dean of Thornborough. 

The scale of these administrative divisions evolved over time. In the taxation records of Nicholas IV, eight deaneries were listed, which together comprised 186 parishes. By 1855, these deaneries were reconstructed and increased in number to eighteen.

## Proposed Cathedral at Dunstable
During the dissolution of the monasteries, a specific plan was developed to create a new bishopric using the spoils of the religious houses. This proposed scheme would have included Buckinghamshire and Bedfordshire, with the city of Dunstable serving as the cathedral city. Although plans for the cathedral were actually drawn up, the scheme was never realized.

Dunstable itself had a significant religious presence; its priory was endowed by charter with the lordship of the borough and manor, retaining these until its dissolution in 1536-1537. The town also served as a site for major ecclesiastical legal proceedings; in 1533, Cranmer held the court at Dunstable that declared the marriage of Catherine of Aragon to be invalid.

## Historical Context and Authorities
The broader history of the region indicates that the area was reached by the West Saxons in 571 and later became part of Mercia under Offa before falling under the Danelaw following the submission of Mercia to the Northmen. The county's religious and social landscape was further influenced by the presence of institutions such as Eton College, a famous public school founded by Henry VI.
