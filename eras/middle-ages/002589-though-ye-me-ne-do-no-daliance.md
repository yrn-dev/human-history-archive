# Though ye to me ne do no daliance

The phrase "Though ye to me ne do no daliance" appears in the context of medieval English poetry, specifically within the form of the ballade. This expression is used to convey a state of devoted love and submission regardless of whether the object of affection reciprocates such attention.

## The Ballade Form
The ballade is a typically French poetic form that was extensively employed in the English language during the 15th and 16th centuries. During this period, leading English poets—including Lydgate, Gower, and Chaucer—wrote ballades with varying degrees of adherence to French rules. While Gower's surviving ballades are all written in French, the works of Chaucer demonstrate that the specific rhyme arrangements and strophe types of the ballade were established in medieval English.

Following a period of two hundred years in which the form went entirely out of fashion, the ballade was resuscitated in the middle of the 19th century. This revival was led by Theodore de Banville, who published *Trente-six ballades joyeuses* in 1873, a work that subsequently found many imitators.

## Usage in the Works of Chaucer
In an example from Chaucer, the phrase "Though ye to me ne do no daliance" serves as a recurring refrain. The poem addresses a subject named Rosamound, describing her as a "shrine" of all beauty, comparing her radiance to crystal and her cheeks to rubies. The speaker notes that seeing her dance at a revel acts as an "oinement" to his wound. 

The speaker describes a state of emotional duality: while he may weep "of teares full a tine [cask]," the "seemly voice" of the subject causes his thoughts to abound in bliss and joy. The poem concludes with the speaker asserting that it is sufficient for him to love Rosamound as a form of penance, even if she does not offer him any daliance.

## Themes of Devotion and Suffering
The sentiment expressed in the phrase is echoed in other poetic contexts where the speaker identifies with the figure of Tristram. In such instances, the speaker describes himself as being "wallowed and y-wound" in love, comparing his state to a pike wallowed in galantine. This amorous state is described as a "pleasance" in which the speaker burns, noting that his love cannot be "afound" (foundered) or "refrayed" (cooled down). In this state of devotion, the speaker declares a willingness to be a "thrall," regardless of whether the other party engages in daliance.
