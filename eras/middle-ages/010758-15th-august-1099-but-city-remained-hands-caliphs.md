# Ascalon and the Events of August 15, 1099

## Geography and Early History
Ascalon (now 'Askalan) is a well-fortified town located on the Mediterranean coast, situated 12 miles north of Gaza. Historically, it was one of the five chief cities of the Philistines and is mentioned several times in the Tell el-Amarna correspondence. The city served as the seat of worship for the fish-goddess Derketo.

While Ascalon was located within the nominal territory of the tribe of Judah, it was never possessed by the Israelites for any significant length of time. The Bible records an incident of spoliation by Samson in the city, though it is noted that this may have occurred at a different location of the same name in the hill country of Judaea.

In 701 B.C., the city was taken by Sennacherib. Later, the conquests of Alexander led to the hellenization of its civilization, after which the city became a tributary to Egypt and Syria alternately. Herod the Great, a native of Ascalon, contributed greatly to the city's beauty. However, the town suffered severe damage during later wars involving the Romans and Jews.

## Medieval Period and the First Crusade
By the 4th century, Ascalon regained its importance, serving as a center of learning and the seat of a bishopric until the 7th century, when it was conquered by the Moslems.

During the First Crusade, a signal victory was achieved by Christian forces in the plain neighboring the city on August 15, 1099. Despite this victory in the plain, the city itself remained in the hands of the caliphs. It continued under their control until 1157, when King Baldwin III of Jerusalem captured the city following a five-month siege.

## Later Christian and Muslim Control
In 1178, King Baldwin IV gave Ascalon to his sister, Sibylla, upon her marriage to William of Montferrat. The city's status changed again in 1187 when Saladin, having nearly annihilated the Christian army in the plain of Tiberias, encountered only feeble resistance from Ascalon.

Saladin initially repaired and strengthened the fortifications of the city. However, he later ordered the city to be dismantled after the capture of St Jean d'Acre (Acre) by Richard Coeur de Lion in 1191. The English king restored the city the following year, but it was subsequently abandoned.

## Decline and Destruction
Following its abandonment, Ascalon lost much of its former importance. The city's decline culminated in 1270, when Sultan Bibars almost totally destroyed its fortifications and filled its port with stones.
