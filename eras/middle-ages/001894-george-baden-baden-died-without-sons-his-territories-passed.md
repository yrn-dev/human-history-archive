# The Unification of Baden under Charles Frederick

## The Division of the House of Baden
The history of the territories of Baden is characterized by a cycle of unification and partition. In the 15th century, Margrave Charles I (who died in 1475) lost a portion of his territories during a war with the count palatine of the Rhine. These losses were later repaired by his successor and son, Christopher I. In 1503, the family of Baden-Sausenberg became extinct, allowing Christopher to unite the whole of Baden. However, before his death in 1527, Christopher divided the territory among his three sons.

Following the childless death of one son in 1533, the remaining brothers, Ernest and Bernard, shared the deceased brother's lands and made a new division in 1535. This act founded the lines of Baden-Baden and Baden-Pforzheim, the latter of which was renamed Baden-Durlach after 1565.

## Rivalry and Conflict
The partition of the house led to a period of weakness and intense rivalry between the two main branches of the family. This friction eventually escalated into open warfare. Between 1584 and 1622, the territories of Baden-Baden were held by one of the princes of Baden-Durlach. 

This political rivalry was further complicated by the Reformation, as different rulers of Baden adopted different faiths. The house remained divided during the Thirty Years' War, a conflict in which Baden suffered severe damage and both family branches experienced exile. The status quo was eventually restored by the treaty of Westphalia in 1648, leading to a gradual decline in the family rivalry.

## The Unification of 1771
In the late 17th century, the margraviate was ravaged by French troops during the wars of Louis XIV. During this era, Louis William, the margrave of Baden-Baden (who died in 1707), was a prominent figure among the soldiers resisting French aggression.

The fragmented state of the territories ended in 1771. Upon the death of Augustus George of Baden-Baden, who died without sons, his territories passed to Charles Frederick of Baden-Durlach. This transition made Charles Frederick the ruler of the whole of Baden.

## The Reign of Charles Frederick
Although the territories were united under a single ruler in 1771, Baden did not initially form a compact territory. It consisted of isolated districts on either bank of the upper Rhine, totaling approximately 1350 sq. m. Charles Frederick, who began his reign in 1738 and came of age in 1746, dedicated much of his rule to acquiring intervening land to create territorial unity. He is described as a wise and liberal ruler who focused on the administration of justice, education, and the development of commerce and agriculture.

Charles Frederick's opportunities for territorial expansion increased during the Napoleonic wars. Initially, Baden fought for Austria in 1792, resulting in the devastation of the country and the forced cession of territories on the left bank of the Rhine to France in 1796. However, his fortunes shifted in 1803 when, with the assistance of Emperor Alexander I of Russia, he acquired the bishopric of Constance, other small districts, and part of the Rhenish Palatinate, as well as the dignity of a prince elector.

By 1805, Charles Frederick changed sides to fight for Napoleon. Through the peace of Pressburg, he obtained other territories and the Breisgau from the Habsburgs. In 1806, he became a grand-duke and a sovereign prince after joining the Confederation of the Rhine. Further additions were made to his territory following the peace of Vienna in 1809 at the expense of the kingdom of Wuerttemberg. By the time of his death in June 1811, Charles Frederick had quadrupled the area of Baden.
