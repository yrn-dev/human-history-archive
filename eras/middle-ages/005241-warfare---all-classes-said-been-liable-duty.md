# Warfare

The organization and execution of warfare across different historical societies have varied significantly, often reflecting the social hierarchies, class structures, and civic duties of the respective populations.

## Anglo-Saxon Military Service
In early Anglo-Saxon society, it is said that all classes were liable for military service. While the *ceorls* constituted the bulk of the population, evidence suggests that the armies were not essentially peasant forces. By the beginning of the 9th century, regulations required every five or six hides to supply a warrior. Because a warrior was not required from every single hide, it is inferred that the fighting material was not drawn primarily from the *ceorlisc* class.

The *fird* (*fyrd*) consisted of mounted warriors during the 9th century, and indications suggest this was also true in the 7th century, although these warriors apparently fought on foot. The *gesiðcund* classes, who possessed effective weapons and horses, were likely responsible for serious fighting and are regarded as essentially military. While *ceorls* participated in military expeditions, their roles were likely those of camp-followers and attendants, tasked with carrying provisions and constructing bridges and stockades.

## Greek Military Training and Structure
In Athens and other Greek towns, the *Ephebi*—young men aged eighteen to twenty—formed a state-controlled college. To be enrolled, an Athenian youth had to be from the first three property classes and pass a scrutiny (*dokimasia*) regarding his physical capability, civic descent, and age. Upon enrollment, he became liable for military duties and training.

After taking an oath of allegiance in the temple of Aglaurus, the *ephebus* was sent to form a garrison at Acte or Munychia. Following the first year of training and a satisfactory review, the state provided him with a shield and spear, which, along with a *petasus* (broad-brimmed hat) and *chlamys* (cloak), completed his equipment. In the second year, *ephebi* patrolled frontiers, transferred to other garrisons in Attica, and occasionally took an active part in war.

Regarding broader Greek social structures, primitive Aryans utilized a two-class system of nobles and commoners. Warfare was led by a king who served as a leader among equals (*primus inter pares*) and was the head of one of the noble families.

## Japanese Martial Virtues and Strategy
In the feudal era of Japan, the warrior class valued specific virtues. *Gishi* (a man of rectitude) was considered a superior designation to any name signifying mastery of art or learning. Rectitude was viewed as a "twin brother" to Valor. A related concept was *Gi-ri*, which originally meant "Right Reason" or duty—the categorical imperative demanded by Right Reason—though it later evolved into a vague sense of duty expected by public opinion.

In terms of national strategy, some perspectives suggested that the wisest plan for the country was to maintain a facade of commerce and intercourse to gain time for equipping the nation with knowledge of warfare and naval architecture. This strategy emphasized the fortification of coasts and the building of a navy, funded by strict economy exercised by all classes of people.

## Naval Operations and the Marine Force
In the context of 19th-century naval service, the Continuous Service Act of 1853 placed the "blue-jacket" on the same footing as the marine regarding pensions and conditions of service, though the blue-jacket received better pay. 

The distinct purpose of the marine force became limited to duties on shore that were incidental to naval operations of war, such as the seizing of minor positions and the holding of ports. Because modern ships could no longer carry sufficient supernumerary forces for these purposes and relied more on fixed bases for repair and supply, it was suggested that marines could maintain efficiency through rotational embarkation if employed at a naval station headquarters. Furthermore, substituting marines for army garrisons at coaling stations was proposed to relieve the army of duties incidental to naval warfare, which otherwise hindered the army's organization as a mobile field force.
