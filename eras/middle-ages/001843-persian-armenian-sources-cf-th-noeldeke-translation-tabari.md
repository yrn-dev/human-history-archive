# Persian and Armenian Sources

The study of ancient and medieval history, particularly concerning the East, relies heavily on a variety of Persian and Armenian records. These sources provide critical data on the Sasaniden period, the movements of the Moguls, and the interactions between the Roman and Persian empires.

## The Works of Tabari
Tabari (also known as Al Tabari) was a famous Imam of Bagdad and a native of Taborestan. Often described as the "Livy of the Arabians," he completed his general history in the year of the Hegira 302 (A.D. 914). While he originally produced a work consisting of 30,000 sheets, he later reduced it to a more reasonable size at the request of friends. 

The Arabic original of Tabari's work is known only through Turkish and Persian versions. One such Persian translation is noted by D’Herbelot as being enriched with many extracts from the native historians of the Magi or Ghebers. Tabari is considered the most important of the Arabic sources. His chronicles provide specific historical details, such as the governance of Bahrām III in Sejistan (where he was called Saganshah) and Bahrām IV in Kirman (where he was called Kirmanshah). Additionally, the Ms. Chronicle of Tabary records that the Grand Pontiff, or Moubedan Mobed, used his influence to oppose the violation of a treaty.

Modern scholarship has further utilized these texts through translations, such as Th. Noeldeke's 1890 translation, *Geschichte der Perser und Araber zur Zeit der Sasaniden*.

## Armenian Historical Sources
Armenian sources are essential for understanding the history of the region and its relations with neighboring powers. A primary figure is Moses of Chorene, an Armenian historian whose work was translated into Latin. However, the use of his narratives has been subject to critical debate; for instance, it is noted that Gibbon failed to perceive certain chronological difficulties in Moses of Chorene's writing, resulting in an anachronism of thirty years. This error led to events from the reign of Constantine being incorrectly assigned to the reign of Constantius, obscuring the true motives for the wars between Constantine and the Persians.

Other Armenian contributions include the writings of Haithonus (or Aithonus), an Armenian prince and monk of Premontré. He dictated a book titled *de Tartaris* in the French language to his former fellow-soldiers, which was subsequently translated into Latin. Furthermore, accounts from Armenian historians regarding the conquests of the Moguls have been extracted and provided in notes by M. Brosset.

## Integration with Other Regional Sources
Persian and Armenian records are often used in conjunction with other linguistic traditions to reconstruct historical events:

*   **Arabic and Persian:** Beyond Tabari, Arabic material includes the accounts of Ibn Fadlan (921), Ibn Haukal, Istakhry, Mas'udy, and the histories of Ibn el Athir. Persian writers such as Nisavi, the secretary of Sultan Gelaleddin, provided contemporary accounts of the Moguls, though some of these originals are noted to have a romantic air.
*   **Byzantine and Greek:** Information on later periods is derived from Byzantine authors, while earlier notices are preserved by Strabo and Justin.
*   **Other Versions:** The existence of early Persian and Armenian versions of the Scriptures is cited by fifth-century fathers, who asserted that the Scriptures were translated into all "Barbaric languages."
