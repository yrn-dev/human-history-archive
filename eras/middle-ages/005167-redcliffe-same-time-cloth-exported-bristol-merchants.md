# Redcliffe

Redcliffe is a district of Bristol located south of the city center. Historically a manor, it played a significant role in the early development and territorial expansion of the city.

## Early History and Integration
During the reign of King John, Bristol began a process of absorbing the neighboring manor of Bedminster. While the eastern half of Bedminster was held by the Templars, the western half—known as Redcliffe—was sold by Earl Robert of Gloucester to Robert FitzHardinge, who later became Lord Berkeley.

The owners of the manor of Redcliffe were wealthy and maintained their own quay, market, fair, and manorial courts. Because of this independence, they resisted union with the city of Bristol for nearly one hundred years. This long-standing dispute was eventually settled by a charter in 1373, which extended the boundaries of the town to include Redcliffe.

The physical connection between Redcliffe and the city was improved in 1247. In that year, a stone bridge was constructed over the Avon and a new course was cut for the river Frome, which brought Redcliffe and Temple into closer proximity to the city and vastly improved the harbor.

## Architecture and Landmarks
Redcliffe is home to St Mary Redcliffe, which is described as one of the two finest churches in the area. The church was rebuilt by William Canyng, a prominent merchant and five-time mayor of Bristol who lived from 1399 to 1474. 

The churchyard of St Mary contains the memorial of the poet Chatterton, who received his education at a school located in a small house near the church. Within the church, a chest holds records in which Chatterton claimed to have discovered manuscripts that were, in reality, his own.

In the 19th century, the church was the subject of a historical account written or edited by Britton, published in 1813.

## Economic Context
The integration of Redcliffe occurred during a period of significant economic growth for Bristol. During the reign of Edward III, cloth manufacture developed in the city; for example, Thomas Blanket established looms in 1337. By 1353, Bristol had become one of the Staple towns.

The prosperity of the era was exemplified by figures like William Canyng, who conducted a massive cloth trade with the Baltic. During this time, Bristol merchants also exported cloth to the Levant, Spain, and France. The city's trade further expanded through the influence of the Society of Merchant Venturers, which began its records in 1467 and by 1500 directed all of the city's foreign trade.
