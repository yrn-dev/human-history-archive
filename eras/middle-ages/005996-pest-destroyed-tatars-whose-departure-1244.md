# Pest

Pest is a city situated on the left bank of the Danube in the kingdom of Hungary. It is located on a flat sandy plain at a point where the river takes a southward course, positioned where the Carpathians meet the outlying spurs of the Alps, specifically the Bakony Mountains. Historically, the city's name appears to be derived from the old Slavonic word *pestj*, meaning a stove, which suggests the site was an early Slavonic settlement. Unlike the right bank of the river, the Romans never established a foothold in Pest.

## Early History and Medieval Period
When Pest first appears in historical records, it was primarily a German settlement, described by a 13th-century chronicler as "Villa Teutonica ditissima." Christianity was introduced to the area early in the 11th century. In 1241, the city was destroyed by the Tatars. Following their departure in 1244, King Bela IV created Pest a royal free city and repeopled the area with colonists of various nationalities.

While the city experienced a period of considerable prosperity following its reconstruction, it was completely eclipsed by its sister town, Buda. In 1247, King Bela IV built a palace and fortress in Buda, which served as the nucleus for that town's growth. Buda eventually gained great importance and became the capital of Hungary in 1361.

## Turkish Occupation and Recovery
In 1526, Pest was pillaged and taken by the Turks. From 1541 until 1686, Buda served as the seat of a Turkish pasha. During this era, Pest lost its importance entirely and was reduced to little more than a heap of ruins by the time the Turks departed. However, the city began to revive due to its favorable location and the renewal of its former privileges. By 1723, Pest had become the seat of the highest Hungarian officials. Its importance was further increased through the efforts of Joseph II and Maria Theresa.

## Modern Development and Political Unrest
The 19th century marked a period of rapid growth for Pest, allowing it to completely outstrip Buda. The city's vitality was demonstrated in 1838 when it quickly recovered from a disastrous inundation that destroyed 3,000 houses. 

In 1848, Pest became the seat of the revolutionary diet. In the following year, insurgents were forced to retire before the Austrians under Windischgrätz. Although the Austrians later retired in turn, they left a garrison in the fortress of Buda. During the subsequent Hungarian efforts to capture that position, General Hentzi bombarded Pest, causing great damage to the town.

## Unification and Population Growth
In 1872, Pest was united with Buda, Ó-Buda, and Köbánya into a single municipality known as Budapest. In 1896, the city hosted the millennium exhibition to celebrate the thousandth anniversary of the foundation of the kingdom of Hungary.

The population of Pest grew rapidly during the 19th century. In 1799, the joint population of Buda and Pest was 54,179, with Pest accounting for 29,870 residents—the first time Pest's population exceeded that of Buda. By 1869, of the joint population of 270,685, approximately 200,000 lived in Pest. By 1900, the total civil population of the unified Budapest reached 716,476. This population included 578,458 Magyars, 104,520 Germans, 25,168 Slovaks, and various other groups including Russians, Rumanians, Greeks, Armenians, Servians, Croatians, and Gypsies.
