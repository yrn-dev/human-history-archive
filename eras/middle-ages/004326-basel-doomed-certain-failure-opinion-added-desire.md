# The Council of Basel and Related History in Basel

## The Council of Basel and the Papal Conflict
The synod of Basel was viewed by Pope Eugenius IV as being doomed to certain failure. This assessment was based on several factors: the slow appearance of fathers from Germany, France, and Italy; the progress of heresy; reported troubles in Germany; a war between the dukes of Burgundy and Austria; and the small number of fathers who responded to the summons of Martin V. Additionally, Eugenius IV desired to preside over the council himself, but his health was impaired, likely due to cerebral congestion.

On December 18, 1431, the Pope commanded the fathers to disperse and appointed Bologna as the meeting place for a session in eighteen months. His goal was to align the council with conferences in Bologna involving representatives of the Greek church to seek union. This order caused an outcry among the fathers and was deeply disapproved of by the legate Cesarini. Critics argued that such a failure would lead the laity to accuse the clergy of shirking reform and suggest to the Hussites that the Church feared to face them.

## Resistance and the Claim of Supremacy
The fathers of Basel refused to obey the Pope, relying on decrees from the council of Constance which had proclaimed the superiority of the council over the pope in certain cases. They insisted on their right to remain assembled, held sessions, promulgated decrees, and interfered in the government of the papal countship of Venaissin. They also treated with the Hussites and attempted to impose laws upon the sovereign pontiff.

While Eugenius IV resisted this claim of supremacy, he did not openly repudiate the doctrine, as it was considered by many to be the foundation of papal authority before the schism. He attempted a compromise, but the fathers became increasingly intractable, creating an impassable barrier between the two parties.

## Rupture and the Election of Felix V
The conflict intensified over the union with the Greek church. Emperor John Palaeologus, pressured by the Turks, wished to unite with the Catholics and agreed to meet in the west. The fathers of Basel sought a meeting place remote from papal influence, suggesting Avignon, Savoy, or Basel. However, neither the Greeks nor Eugenius IV accepted these locations.

Consequently, Palaeologus accepted the Pope's offers. By a bull dated September 18, 1437, Eugenius IV again pronounced the dissolution of the council of Basel and summoned the fathers to Ferrara. A new synod opened there on January 8, 1438, and was later transferred to Florence, where a momentary union between the Latin and Greek churches occurred on July 6, 1439.

Despite being abandoned by most members and Cesarini, the council of Basel persisted under Cardinal Aleman. On January 24, 1438, the council suspended Eugenius IV, and on June 25, 1439, it pronounced his deposition. This led to a new schism when the council elected Amadeus VIII, duke of Savoy, as pope on November 4, who took the name Felix V.

## Conclusion of the Schism
The schism lasted ten years. The antipope Felix V had few adherents outside of certain universities, the Swiss confederation, and his own hereditary states. While Germany remained neutral and England and Italy stayed faithful to Eugenius IV, Charles VII of France secured the benefits of many Basel reforms through the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges on July 13, 1438.

In 1447, Frederick III, king of the Romans, ordered the burgomaster of Basel to no longer allow the council's presence in the city. The remaining council members migrated to Lausanne in June 1448. Felix V abdicated on April 7, 1449, at the instance of France. Following the death of Eugenius IV on February 23, 1447, the fathers of Lausanne supported his successor, Nicholas V, claiming he accepted the dogma of council superiority. However, the seventeen-year struggle ended in defeat for the council, and the constitution of the Church remained monarchical.

## Other Notable Figures in Basel
The city of Basel also served as a center for art and scholarship in later years. In 1492, the young artist Albrecht Durer arrived in Basel, where he was received by a brother of the deceased master Martin Schongauer. Durer practiced metal-engraving and provided designs for woodcutters; a wood-block of St Jerome executed by him is preserved in the Basel museum, used in an edition of Jerome's letters printed in the city in 1492.

In the realm of scholarship, Johannes Buxtorf (1564-1629), a German Hebrew and Rabbinic scholar, resided in Basel. Attracted by the reputation of J.G. Hospinian and J.J. Grynaeus, Buxtorf eventually took the Hebrew chair at the university in 1591. He was known as the "Master of the Rabbins" for his zeal in studying rabbinic literature and his habit of hosting learned Jews in his home to discuss ceremonial law, despite strict city laws against Jews.
