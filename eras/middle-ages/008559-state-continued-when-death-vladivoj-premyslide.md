# The History of Bohemia

## Early Origins and Slavic Settlement
The region of Bohemia derives its name from the Boii, a Celtic tribe that inhabited the land during the earliest historical period. The Boii established their capital at Boiohemum, but were eventually weakened by constant warfare with neighboring tribes and subdued by the Teutonic tribe of the Marcomanni around 12 B.C. The Marcomanni were later expelled by other Teutonic tribes, leading to the eventual conquest of Bohemia by Slavic tribes. Among these, the Cechs were the most significant. While classical and Byzantine references are often misleading, archaeological research indicates Slavic inhabitants were present in Bohemia as early as the beginning of the Christian era, with the Cechs becoming the masters of the country in the 5th century.

The first historical ruler mentioned is Samo, who defeated the Franks at the battle of Wogatisburg in 630 and also defeated a Turanian tribe known as the Avars, who had previously held overlordship over the region. Following Samo's death, the historical record becomes obscure for approximately 130 years.

## The Premyslide Dynasty
Ancient chroniclers, including Cosmas, record the foundation of a Bohemian principality by Krok and his daughter Libussa. Libussa married a peasant named Premysl, and from this semi-mythic figure, chroniclers developed a continuous list of Premyslide princes, though the specific dates and deeds of these early rulers are not considered historical.

The history of the country became clearer with the introduction of Christianity. Initial attempts to introduce the faith from Germany were unsuccessful due to the Bohemians' innate distrust of their western neighbors. However, Christianity was adopted more rapidly when it arrived from Moravia via Cyrillus and Methodius, Greek monks from Thessalonica. Around 873, Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius.

## Expansion and Internal Conflict
One of the most famous rulers of this era was Wenceslas, surnamed the Holy. In 935, he was murdered by his brother Boleslav, an act that led to war with Germany, as Wenceslas had been a German ally. Boleslav, who succeeded Wenceslas and was later successful in the war, and his son Boleslav II, expanded the frontiers of Bohemia. Boleslav II established rule over Moravia, a large portion of Silesia, and the part of Poland currently known as the Austrian province of Galicia.

This empire did not last. Boleslav III, the son of Boleslav II, lost all foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great, the king of Poland. Boleslav III's reign was marked by constant civil war and dissensions with his brothers, Jaromir and Ulrick. Although Boleslav III eventually expelled his brothers, his cruelty led the Bohemians to dethrone him. They chose the Polish prince Vladivoj—the brother of Boleslav the Great and son of the Bohemian princess Dubravka—as their ruler. To secure his position, Vladivoj sought aid from Germany and agreed to continue paying tribute.

## Evolution of the Bohemian Crown
In 1088, Vratislav obtained the title of king from Emperor Henry IV after assisting him in the contest about investitures with the papal see. This title was conferred on Vratislav personally rather than hereditarily. Later, in 1125-1140, the German king Conrad III granted the Bohemian prince Sobeslav the title of hereditary cupbearer of the Empire, giving Bohemia influence in the election of emperors.

In 1156, Emperor Frederick I Barbarossa conferred the title of king upon Prince Vladislav II and ceded Upper Lusatia to him, provided Vladislav II participated in Italian campaigns. While intended to be hereditary, the title fell into abeyance following Vladislav's abdication in 1173 due to struggles between Premyslide princes.

## The End of the Premyslide Rule
The dynasty's later years saw periods of instability. Following the death of Ottakar, his seven-year-old son Wenceslas II was nominally the ruler, but the country was governed by his uncle, Otto of Brandenburg, as guardian. Otto's unpopularity led to an insurrection and a period of complete anarchy until Rudolph of Habsburg intervened. Power then shifted to Zavis of Falkenstein, a noble who married the king's mother, Kunegunda. Zavis governed until 1290, when he was beheaded by order of Wenceslas II.

Wenceslas II subsequently governed himself, leading a period of great happiness for Bohemia. During his reign, Poland accepted his rule and the Hungarian crown was offered to him. Wenceslas II died suddenly in 1305 while preparing to invade Austria. He was succeeded by his sixteen-year-old son, Wenceslas III, who ruled for only one year. In 1306, while planning an expedition against Poland to maintain Bohemian claims over that country, Wenceslas III was murdered by unknown assassins. His death marked the end of the Premyslide dynasty's rule over Bohemia.
