# Arabic Manuscripts in the Escorial

The library of the Escorial serves as a significant repository for historical and literary manuscripts, particularly those written in Arabic. These collections have been the subject of extensive cataloging and study by scholars of oriental languages and history.

## The Bibliotheca Arabico-Hispana Escurialensis
Between 1760 and 1770, Miguel Casiri, a learned Maronite born in Tripoli, Syria, published a two-volume work titled *Bibliotheca Arabico-Hispana Escurialensis*. Casiri, who served as the principal librarian at the Escorial from 1763 until his death in 1791, compiled this work as a catalogue of over 1,800 Arabic manuscripts found within the library. 

The catalogue classifies these manuscripts by subject. The second volume specifically details a large collection of historical and geographical manuscripts, which provide valuable information concerning the wars between the Christians and the Moors in Spain. Additionally, Casiri's catalogue includes various quotations from Arabic historical works. While Casiri's work remains useful, a more scientific system for treating these manuscripts was later adopted by Hartwig Derenbourg in his 1884 incomplete treatise, *Les Manuscrits arabes de l'Escorial*.

## Works of Averroes
The Escorial is noted for housing a significant portion of the writings of Averroes. While some of his works, such as the *Colliget* (a summary of medical science) and various commentaries on Aristotle, exist in Latin editions, many of his other writings remain in manuscript form in the Escorial and other libraries. These include:
* Treatises on astronomy and jurisprudence.
* Essays on special logical subjects.
* Prolegomena to philosophy.
* Criticisms of Alfarabius (Farabi) and Avicenna.

## Other Geographical and Historical Records
The library's holdings have historically included works of geography. For example, a copy of the work of Idrisi—who wrote *The Gardens of Humanity and the Amusement of the Soul* for William the Bad—was once held in the Escorial. However, this specific copy was destroyed during a fire in 1671.

## Contributions to the Library's Growth
The Escorial's broader collection of manuscripts was enriched by the efforts of prominent Greeks following the fall of Constantinople. Scholars such as Marcus Musurus, John and Constantine Lascaris, Demetrius Chalcondyles, and John Argyropulos collected and transcribed Greek classical writers. Their stores of manuscripts helped populate several major libraries, including the Vatican, the National Library in Paris, St Mark at Venice, and the Escorial.
