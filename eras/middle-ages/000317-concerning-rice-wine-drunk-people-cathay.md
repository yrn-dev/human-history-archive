# CONCERNING THE RICE-WINE DRUNK BY THE PEOPLE OF CATHAY

## Characteristics and Composition
The people of Cathay consume a liquor brewed from rice and a quantity of excellent spice. This beverage is described as being clear, pleasing to the eye, and superior to any other kind of wine. Due to its "very hot" nature, it is noted for making the drinker intoxicated more quickly than other varieties of wine. 

In technical terms, this "wine" is an ardent spirit distilled from rice. It is produced in various brands, some of which possess a moderate strength that allows them to be classified as wine. The production process involves the use of a kind of yeast, which is frequently mixed with a flour prepared from pine-seeds, almonds, dried fruits, fragrant herbs, and other ingredients. This specific rice-wine is referred to as *shao hsing chiu*. It is distinct from *shao chiu* (also known as *samshu* or *araka*), which is a stronger liquor distilled from millet.

## Consumption and Customs
Chinese rice-wine is always consumed hot. To maintain this temperature, pewter wine-flasks are kept standing in vessels of boiling water. The cups used for drinking are small, which allows individuals to consume many of them without immediately feeling overcome. 

At banquets, the consumption of wine is more pronounced. Bumpers are drunk at short intervals, and guests hold their wine-cups upside down to demonstrate that no wine remains in the bottom (no "heel-taps"). Additional bumpers are often required as penalties or forfeits during games such as "guess-fingers" or for failing to cap a verse.

Despite these banquet customs, some diners refuse wine because it causes the face to flush uncomfortably. There is also a social concern regarding the appearance of drunkenness, reflected in the cynical proverb: "A bottle-nosed man may be a tee-totaller, but no one will believe it." While historical poetry and records suggest the Chinese were once a "fairly tipsy nation"—including an official who died in A.D. 639 from drink—modern observations suggest a shift toward moderation. Public drunkenness, such as men lying senseless or tumbling in the streets, is not seen in China, supporting the proverb: "It is not wine, which makes a man drunk; it is the man himself."

## Historical and Comparative Perspectives
The invention of spirits and wine in China is attributed by some to Tu K’ang, and by others to a person named I Ti, who lived during the time of the Emperor Yü.

Various travelers and scholars have compared the rice-wine of Cathay to other famous beverages. Rubruquis noted that, except for its smell, the liquor was indistinguishable from the best wine of Auxerre. Other comparisons have been made to Rhenish, Canary, and Madeira wines. Friar Odoric referred to a wine as *bigni*, though it is suggested this may have been made with the date *Mi-yin*.

## Regional Variations
In certain regions, such as those three days' ride north-east and east of a specific town, the population consists of worshippers of Mahommet. These people are described as "great topers" who find great delight in the wine shop. They consume a good wine that is boiled and are noted for being constantly drunk.
