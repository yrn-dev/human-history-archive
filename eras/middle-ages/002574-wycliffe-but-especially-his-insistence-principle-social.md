# John Wycliffe

John Wycliffe was a 14th-century thinker and rector of Lutterworth, a market town in Leicestershire, England, where he served from 1374 until his death in 1384. His work and doctrines had a lasting impact on religious controversy and social movements, extending far beyond his lifetime.

## Theological and Philosophical Positions
Wycliffe was a Realist in his philosophical outlook. This position allowed him to maintain that God acted according to certain and known universal rules, described as *secundum rationes exemplares*. This contrasted with the Nominalists of the 14th and 15th centuries, who viewed God as possessing an inscrutable omnipotence. According to some historical perspectives, the Nominalist skepticism regarding Universals led to an "obscurantist ecclesiasticism," wherein it was argued that God could only be known through the priesthood via the miraculous intervention of the mass.

In the arena of religious controversy, Wycliffe challenged the orthodox party within the Church by proclaiming that the Bible, rather than Catholic tradition or the Church, served as a man's supreme spiritual authority.

## The English Bible
Because of his belief in the supreme authority of the Scriptures, Wycliffe sought to spread knowledge of the Bible among the people. This zeal is associated with the production of the two earliest complete English renderings of the Holy Scriptures. 

The "Early Version" was likely completed by 1382, and in any event before Wycliffe's death in 1384. A "Later Version," which served as a thorough revision of the first, is ascribed to the year 1388. While tradition associates these translations with Wycliffe, the exact extent of his personal involvement remains uncertain. Some argue the New Testament translation may be his work due to stylistic similarities; however, other evidence suggests that certain Commentaries on the Gospels—which urge the translation of Scripture for the laity—were late productions that quoted the Early Version, making it difficult to assign them to the final years of Wycliffe's life.

## Influence on Social Equality and the Peasantry
The doctrines of Wycliffe were expounded by others, most notably John Ball, an English priest. Ball gained fame as a preacher by combining Wycliffe's doctrines with a strong insistence on the principle of social equality. These views brought Ball into conflict with the archbishop of Canterbury, leading to his excommunication, prohibitions against his preaching in 1366, and three separate imprisonments.

Ball's words had a significant effect in stirring the peasant revolt that broke out in June 1381. After being released from prison at Maidstone by Kentish rebels, Ball preached at Blackheath, using the text, "When Adam delved and Eve span, Who was then a gentleman?" He urged his followers to kill lawyers and the kingdom's principal lords, and he participated in the seizure of Simon of Sudbury, the archbishop of Canterbury, at the Tower of London. Ball was eventually captured at Coventry and executed in the presence of Richard II on July 15, 1381.

More broadly, the period beginning with Wycliffe saw the emergence of equalitarian insurrections among the peasantry. These movements were generally confined to peasant communities in specific localities and spread slowly to other districts facing similar forces.

## Legacy and Commemoration
Wycliffe is commemorated in Lutterworth by a modern obelisk. Relics of Wycliffe are preserved in the church of St Mary. In 1428, his body was exhumed and burned, and his ashes were cast into the river Swift. This event gave rise to a saying that the distribution of his ashes from the river to the ocean mirrored the spread of his doctrines across the world.
