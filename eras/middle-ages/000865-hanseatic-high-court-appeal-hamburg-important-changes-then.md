# Hanseatic High Court of Appeal at Hamburg

The Hanseatic court of appeal, known as the *Oberlandesgericht*, is located at Hamburg. It serves as a higher judicial authority within the legal hierarchy for specific regions, including Lübeck.

## Judicial Hierarchy and Appeals
In the legal system involving Lübeck, there is a court of first instance, the *Amtsgericht*, and a high court of justice, the *Landgericht*. Legal appeals from the *Landgericht* are directed to the Hanseatic court of appeal (*Oberlandesgericht*) at Hamburg. Further appeals from the Hanseatic court of appeal are then directed to the supreme court of the empire, the *Reichsgericht*, which is situated in Leipzig.

## Historical Changes in Hanseatic Judicial Arrangements
The judicial arrangements of the Hanseatic cities have undergone significant changes over time. Lübeck, which maintained a traditional connection with Hamburg and Bremen because the Hanseatic League was never officially dissolved, previously operated under a different appellate system. Until 1879, Lübeck maintained its own court of appeal.

## Related Governance and Administration
The broader administrative context of the region includes the free state, which operates under a republican constitution. According to the fundamental law of 1875 (with amendments in 1905 and 1907), the state is governed by two assemblies:

*   **The Senate:** This body represents the sovereignty of the state and consists of fourteen members. The composition is strictly regulated: eight members must belong to the learned professions (six of whom must be jurists), and five of the remaining six must be merchants. The Senate is presided over by the *Oberbürgermeister*, who holds the title of "magnificence" during a two-year term of office.
*   **The House of Burgesses (*Bürgerschaft*):** This assembly consists of 120 members elected by free suffrage. It exercises its powers both collectively and through a committee of thirty members.

While the Senate holds executive power, the House of Burgesses possesses the right to initiate legislation, including matters regarding foreign treaties. The passing of any new law requires the sanction of both chambers. Additionally, a chamber of commerce—comprising a *praeses*, a secretary, and eighteen members—handles purely commercial matters, including the appointment of underwriters, shipping agents, and brokers, as well as the control of the exchange.
