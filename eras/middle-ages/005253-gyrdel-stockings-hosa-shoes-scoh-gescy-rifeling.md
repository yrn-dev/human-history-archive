# Footwear and Accessories: Girdels, Stockings, and Shoes

The history of human attire across various cultures and eras demonstrates a wide range of functional and ornamental footgear and accessories, varying from simple leather pads to specialized industrial products and ceremonial costumes.

## Early and Ancient Footgear
In early Egypt, sandals were common in the New Kingdom, though princes typically appeared unshod when in the presence of the Pharaoh. These sandals often consisted of a sole made of papyrus or leather secured by two straps—one between the toes and one over the instep—though some versions included a third strap behind the heel or a turned-up front to protect the toes. During the XIIth Dynasty, Semites used black leather sandals for journeys, while those for children and women were more serviceable, with women's sandals sometimes being parti-coloured.

Other ancient civilizations developed stouter protection. The Hittites utilized turned-up shoes bound to the legs with thongs, and some wore protecting leather that reached halfway up the shin. In Asia Minor and Assyria, tight-fitting bandages, laced garters, or buskins were employed. The buskin is described as a high shoe or half-boot strapped under the ankle to protect the shins. In ancient Athenian tragedy, a specific thick-soled boot known as the *cothurnus* was used to increase the height of actors, contrasting with the *soccus* (or "sock"), which was a light shoe used in comedy.

## Regional and Cultural Variations
Clothing and footwear practices vary significantly by region and social custom. In some middle-class families, leather shoes were reserved for going out, while sandals without leather fastenings were used at home. In China, the practice of binding feet to make them resemble "new moons" began with a lady of the tenth century. While modern binding causes great pain and leads girls to hide their bare feet, some groups, such as Manchu women and boat-girls at Canton, have been observed walking unshod or without stockings and shoes.

In the context of bull-fighting, costumes are designed for maximum brilliance. *Espadas* and *banderilleros* wear light silk stockings and heelless shoes. In contrast, *picadores* protect their legs from bull horns using steel armour covered in leather.

## Early European and National Attire
Early national attire, likely in use before the invasion of Britain, utilized wool and linen. Key garments included the *roc* (coat), *brec* (trousers), and various cloaks. Accessories included the *haet* (hat), *glof* (gloves), *gyrdel* (belt), *hosa* (stockings), and shoes, for which the terms *scoh*, *gescy*, and *rifeling* were used. Archaeological evidence from a 4th-century bog-deposit at Thorsbjaerg in Angel confirms the use of long trousers with attached socks, several shoes, and square cloaks.

The *gyrdel* also appears in religious texts; for example, an edition of St Matthew from 1539 describes John the Baptist as wearing a garment of camel's hair and a "gyrdell of a skynne" around his loins.

## Industrial Manufacture of Shoes
In the United States, the cities of Brockton and Lynn, Massachusetts, became major centers for the boot and shoe industry. 

Lynn's industry began in 1636, introduced by Philip Kertland. By 1767, the city produced 80,000 pairs of shoes, and by 1795, 600 journeymen and 200 master workmen produced approximately 300,000 pairs of women's shoes. In 1783, Ebenezer Breed introduced the use of morocco. By 1905, Lynn was second only to Brockton in the value of boots and shoes manufactured in the U.S., though it outranked Brockton in the production of findings and cut stock.

Brockton became the leading U.S. city in boot and shoe manufacture in 1900 and 1905. By 1905, the city's boot and shoe product was valued at $30,073,014, representing 9.4% of the total U.S. product. In 1908, the city housed 35 shoe factories, including establishments such as Packard, Keith, Eaton, Walkover, Ralston, and W.L. Douglas. Between 1905 and 1907, production in Brockton rose from 14 million to 17 million pairs of shoes. The city also produced related items such as shoe fittings, tools, blacking, lasts, and packing boxes made of wood or paper.

## Animal Footwear
The protection of horse hooves has evolved from simple coverings to iron shoeing. Ancient peoples used sandals or socks for horses, and in Japan, straw slippers were attached to horses' feet until the 19th century. The practice of nailing iron rim-shoes to the hoof likely began around the 2nd century B.C., though it did not become common until the close of the 5th century A.D. and was not in regular use until the middle ages. Modern veterinary science has identified several harmful farrier practices, including the use of nails that are too large or too numerous, and the paring of the frog and sole.
