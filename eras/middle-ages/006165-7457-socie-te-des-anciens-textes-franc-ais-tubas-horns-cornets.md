# Tubas, Horns, Cornets, and Bugles

The tubas, horns, cornets, and bugles share a common prehistoric archetype in the horns of animals, such as the ram or bull. These original forms were later copied and modified using materials including wood, bronze, ivory, silver, and brass. Among these descendants, the bugle has most closely retained the characteristic scale and acoustic properties of the original animal horn prototype.

## Acoustic Principles and Classification
Instruments such as the trumpet, tuba, bugle, and horn, in their simplest forms, consist of tubes without lateral openings. Their musical scale depends on the harmonic series produced by "overblowing," which involves increasing the tension of the lips (acting as reeds) and the pressure of the breath across the mouthpiece. This harmonic series is determined by the acoustic principles of the tube and is not influenced by how the tube is bent.

Based on the harmonics employed, these instruments are classified into three groups:
1. **Lower Harmonics (second to sixth or eighth):** This group includes the trombone, post-horn, bugle, and cornet à pistons.
2. **Higher Harmonics (third or fourth to twelfth or sixteenth):** This group includes the trumpet and French horn.
3. **Fundamental Tone and Harmonics (up to the eighth):** This group includes the ophicleide and tubas.

## The Bugle and Military History
In the Middle Ages, the term "bugle" was applied to the ox as well as to its horns, whether used for drinking or as musical instruments. A definition from approximately 1398 describes the bugle as a "fyers beest" like an ox. Early French texts also used "bugle" as a verb and an adjective.

In England, the bugle's history as a military instrument is tied to the development of light infantry. During the 17th century, as infantry abandoned armor due to improvements in firearms, the bugle gradually superseded the drum as the primary signal instrument. By the end of the 18th century, the bugle was the recognized signal instrument for light infantry, while the trumpet remained the instrument of the cavalry. A general order officially introduced the bugle as a minor badge for light infantry on December 28, 1814.

In 1856, the Royal Kent bugle (a keyed version) reached its peak popularity. In the Royal Artillery, a bugle-band was formed as a substitute for the drum and fife band under Trumpet-major James Lawson. Lawson added a valve attachment to the service bugle to make it a chromatic instrument, effectively turning it into a saxhorn. This led to the addition of bass tubas, euphoniums, and horns in E flat and B flat. In 1869, this ensemble became the R.A. Brass Band.

## The Cornet
The term "cornet" refers to several distinct instruments. The obsolete wood wind cornet (also known as the *Zinck* or *cornetto*) featured a conical bore without a bell and a cup-shaped mouthpiece. These existed in curved and straight forms. Straight cornets included a "sweet-toned" variety (known in Italy as *cornetto muto* and in Germany as *stiller* or *sanfter Zinck*) which featured a funnel-shaped mouthpiece carved directly into the wood, rather than a detachable cup.

The modern cornet à pistons is a brass instrument with a timbre situated between that of the trumpet and the horn. While it lacks the noble sonorousness of the trumpet, it is valued for its simplicity, the ease with which it "speaks," and its technical versatility. A three-valved cornet can perform chromatic scales, arpeggios, shakes, and leaps in any key. It also allows for double and triple tonguing to produce a staccato effect. In some orchestras, particularly in France, the cornet has begun to supersede the trumpet, though some argue this detriments the overall tone color.

## The Horn and Tuba
The origin of the horn dates to prehistoric times, where breaking the tip of a short animal horn produced rough, powerful notes. The modern horn is characterized by a mellow, sonorous timbre rich in harmonics. Its "hand-stopped" notes provide a veiled, mysterious quality. The introduction of piston notes, which utilize a mixed cylindrical and conical bore rather than a strictly conical one, results in a more resonant tone similar to the trombone.

The tuba, along with the bugle, is considered an ultimate development of the evolution of the animal horn. Along with the ophicleide, it is characterized by its ability to produce the fundamental tone and harmonics up to the eighth.

## Ritual and Ancient Use
The Israelites used wind instruments for both ritual and military purposes. The *shofar*, a ram's or cow's horn, was used during the siege of Jericho. While the Israelites also possessed metal instruments—such as the *Khatsotsrah* (a long straight trumpet or tuba similar to those found in ancient Assyrian and Egyptian musical scenes)—they continued to use the primitive *shofar* due to its special ritual significance. The earliest known code of signals is found in the directions for sounding trumpets of beaten silver described in Numbers x. Additionally, Gideon used a massed band of three hundred *shofars* to defeat the Midianites, and Saul used them for a call to arms.
