# BRIDGET OF SWEDEN, SAINT

Saint Bridget (also known as Brigitta or Birgitta) was a prominent religious figure of the northern kingdoms, living from approximately 1302 until 1373. She was born the daughter of Birger Persson, who served as the *lagman* (provincial judge) and governor of Uppland and was among the wealthiest landowners in the country.

## Early Life and Family
In 1316, Bridget married Ulf Gudmarson, the lord of Nericia. The couple had eight children, one of whom was later honored as St Catherine of Sweden. Bridget became widely known for her charitable and saintly life, eventually exerting a significant religious influence over her husband. Between 1341 and 1343, the couple undertook a pilgrimage to St James of Compostella. Shortly after their return, in 1344, Ulf died in the Cistercian monastery of Alvastra in East Gothland. Following his death, Bridget devoted herself entirely to religious pursuits.

## Revelations and the Bridgittines
Bridget experienced visions as a child, which became more frequent in her later life. She kept records of these "revelations," which were translated into Latin by her confessor, Peter (prior of Alvastra), and Matthias, a canon of Linköping. These writings gained significant popularity during the Middle Ages.

Around 1350, Bridget founded the order of St Saviour, also known as the Bridgittines. This was a "double order" of Augustinian canonesses; each convent was governed by an abbess but included a small community of canons who served as chaplains. The order's principal house was established at Vadstena (by Lake Vetter), which received rich endowments from Queen and King Magnus II. The order expanded to a total of 80 houses, spreading widely through Norway and Sweden, where it played a notable role in the promotion of literature and culture. The head house at Vadstena remained active until 1595.

In England, Henry V founded and royally endowed the Bridgittine convent of Syon at Isleworth, Middlesex, in 1415. Syon became one of the most influential and richest nunneries in England. Though suppressed, it was among the few houses restored during the reign of Mary. Upon the accession of Elizabeth, the community migrated to the Low Countries, then to Rouen, and finally to Lisbon in 1594. The community continued to recruit from England until 1861, when they returned to England and established Syon House at Chudleigh in Devon. This remains the only English community with an unbroken conventual existence since pre-Reformation times. Currently, the order consists only of women, with some six other convents remaining on the Continent.

## Later Years and Canonization
Around 1350, Bridget traveled to Rome. Her objectives were twofold: to seek papal authorization for her new order and to fulfill a self-imposed mission to improve the moral tone of the era. While she became universally beloved in Rome for her good works and kindness, the rule of her order was not confirmed by Pope Urban V until 1370.

Bridget remained in Rome until her death on July 23, 1373, though she made occasional pilgrimages, including one to Jerusalem in 1373. She was canonized in 1391 by Pope Boniface IX. Her feast day is celebrated on October 9.
