# Kolberg

Kolberg (also known as Colberg) is a seaport and town located in the Prussian province of Pomerania, Germany. Situated on the right bank of the Persante river, which flows into the Baltic approximately one mile below the town, it serves as a junction for railway lines connecting to Gollnow and Belgard. As of 1905, the town had a population of 22,804.

## Early History and the Polish Conquest
Originally established as a Slavonic fort, Kolberg is among the oldest settlements in Pomerania. In its early history, it served as the seat of a bishop. During the era of Boleslaus III, King of Poland (who reigned from 1102 to 1139), the region of Pomerania was inhabited by a savage and valiant Slavonic race related to the Wends. This population remained obstinately pagan and utilized their seaboard to maintain fleets and develop relatively large towns, including Kolberg and Stettin. From these bases, the Pomeranians were able to ravage the territories of their southern neighbors, the Poles, at will.

To defend against these incursions, Boleslaus III sought to subdue Pomerania to gain sea access. This struggle began in 1109 with a significant Polish victory at Nackel. Later, between 1120 and 1124, Boleslaus was forced to return to the region due to a rebellion by his vassal, Prince Warceslaus of Stettin. The resistance was intense; Boleslaus suffered the loss of 18,000 followers and the expatriation of another 8,000 before Warceslaus finally submitted. These events convinced Boleslaus that the region required christianization.

## Municipal Development and Trade
Kolberg obtained municipal privileges in 1255. By approximately 1276, it had become the most important location within the episcopal principality of Kamin. In 1284, the town joined the Hanseatic League. 

The town's architecture is highlighted by the citadel and the council-house (Rathaus), designed by Ernst F. Zwirner. One of the most notable structures in Pomerania is the 14th-century red-brick church of St Mary, which features five aisles. The town also contains a theatre, a gymnasium, a school of navigation, an exchange, and four additional churches.

## Military Conflicts
Kolberg was a site of significant conflict during several major European wars:
* **The Thirty Years' War:** The town was captured by the Swedes in 1631. Following the Treaty of Westphalia, it passed to Frederick William I, the elector of Brandenburg, who improved the town's fortifications.
* **The Seven Years' War:** The town was a center of conflict. Russian forces besieged Kolberg in 1758 and 1760 without success, though they eventually captured the town in 1762. It was subsequently restored to Brandenburg.
* **Napoleonic Era:** In 1806 and 1807, the French launched vigorous attacks on the town. Kolberg was saved by the prolonged resistance of its inhabitants and the efforts of the patriot Joachim Christian Nettelbeck (1738-1824). A monument to Nettelbeck and Gneisenau was erected in 1903 to commemorate these events.

The town's fortifications remained until 1887, when they were razed.

## Economy and Modern Status
Kolberg maintains a harbour at the mouth of the Persante, equipped with a lighthouse. The local economy is supported by the manufacture of machinery, spirits, and woollen cloth. Additionally, the town benefits from important lamprey and salmon fisheries and an extensive salt-mine located in nearby Zillenberg.

In the modern era, Kolberg has evolved into a fashionable watering-place. Its bathing establishments attract nearly 15,000 annual summer visitors. The town center features a handsome market-place containing a statue of Frederick William III, and it possesses extensive suburbs, the most significant being Münde.
