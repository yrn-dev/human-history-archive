# The Distinction Between the Gesiðcund and Ceorlisc

In the social structure of early England, specifically within the kingdoms of Wessex and Mercia, the population was divided into distinct social classes. Among these, the terms *gesiðcund* and *ceorlisc* served as broad descriptors for the higher and lower strata of society, respectively. While the exact nature of the distinction between these two classes is not clearly explained in the available records, it is known to have been hereditary and likely of considerable antiquity.

## Social Classification and Terminology
The *gesiðcund* term was used to describe the two higher social classes, while *ceorlisc* referred to the lowest class. In the kingdoms of Wessex and Mercia, these divisions were further refined by the amount of *wergild* (man-price) assigned to an individual. The higher classes were categorized as *twelfhynde* (with a wergild of 1200 shillings), *sixhynde* (600 shillings), and *twihynde* (200 shillings). 

These terms were utilized across different regions, including Kent, although the social system in Kent differed in character. In Kent, the *ceorlisc* class had a wergild of 100 shillings, and there was only a single *gesiðcund* class with a wergild of 300 shillings. Furthermore, Kent possessed classes below the *ceorlisc* known as *laetas*, who had wergilds of 80, 60, and 40 shillings; no analogous classes to the *laetas* are found in the other kingdoms.

## Socio-Economic Distinctions
Broadly, the *gesiðcund* and *ceorlisc* may be defined as nobles and commons, or more accurately as gentry and peasants. The distinction between the higher *twelfhynde* and *sixhynde* classes was at least partially hereditary, but evidence suggests it also arose from land ownership. Those in the *twelfhynde* class possessed at least five hides of land, which was practically equivalent to a village, whereas the *sixhynde* were landless or lacked that specific amount of land.

Subdivisions also existed within the *ceorlisc* class, though these were not distinguished by differences in wergild. The *gafolgelda* (or *tributarius*), a tribute-payer, was a ceorl who possessed at least one hide of land. In contrast, the *gebur* owned no land of his own and received his outfit as a loan from his lord.

## Legal and Military Roles
The distinction between these classes extended beyond wergild to include the value attached to their oaths, the fines to which they were liable, and the compensations they were entitled to receive for various injuries. Generally, these legal proportions mirrored the proportions found in the wergilds.

In terms of military service, all classes were considered liable for duty. However, the nature of their service differed significantly. While ceorls formed the bulk of the population, they likely served as camp-followers and attendants rather than primary warriors. Their duties included carrying provisions and constructing bridges and stockades. 

The *gesiðcund* classes, possessing horses and more effective weapons, were responsible for the serious fighting. During the 9th century, the *fird* (*fyrd*) consisted of mounted warriors, although they typically fought on foot. The military nature of the *gesiðcund* is further supported by regulations stating that every five or six hides of land should supply a warrior; had the fighting force been drawn from the *ceorlisc* class, a warrior would have been required from every single hide.
