# BOLGARI

Bolgari, also referred to as Bolgary, is a ruined town located in Russia within the government of Kazan. It is situated at 55 degrees North latitude, approximately four English miles from the left bank of the Volga and 83 miles from Kazan. The site is currently occupied by a village known as Bolgari, also called Uspenskoye or Bolgarskoye, located in the district of Spask.

## Historical Significance and Governance
Bolgari is generally considered to have been the capital of the Bulgarians, a Turanian race akin to the Huns, Avars, Petchenegs, Finns, and Tatars. This kingdom existed from the 5th to the 15th century on the middle Volga, spanning territories that now include the provinces of Saratov, Simbirsk, Samara, and North Astrakhan, and potentially extending into Perm.

The town, referred to in Russian annals as Velikij Gorod (Great Town), served as a major center for the Bulgars of the Volga. These people were of Turkish origin and may have assimilated Slavonian and Finnish elements. While they were initially a horde of wild horsemen governed by nobles (*boyars*) and chiefs (*khans*), they transitioned from a nomadic existence to establishing permanent settlements. By the 10th century, the Arab geographer Ibn Haukal reported that the town had 10,000 inhabitants.

## Religion and Culture
The religious history of Bolgari is characterized by several shifts. The population was converted to Islam in 922, a transition noted by Ibn Foslan. Prior to this, it appears they may have professed Christianity. However, a 14th-century Mahomedan writer claimed the people had later renounced Islam in favor of the worship of the Cross.

Following their conversion to Islam, the Bulgars began constructing forts and stone houses, the latter of which were built by Arabian architects. The city's intellectual life included the work of Yakub Ibn Noman (also referred to as an Arabian cadi), who wrote a history of the city, the *Tarikh Bulgar*, in the first half of the 12th century, though this text is not known to be extant.

## Commerce and Economy
Bolgari was a significant commercial hub. It served as a meeting place for trade involving the Bulgarians of the middle Volga, Russia, Armenia, Byzantium, and Persia. The city maintained trade relations with the Russians, the Khazars, Persia, India, and undoubtedly Biarmia in the Urals. The primary riches traded by the Bulgars included leather, wool, wax, nuts, furs, and other goods. In addition to trade, the people engaged in cattle breeding and agriculture.

## Decline and Destruction
The city suffered several periods of destruction and changing sovereignty:
* **Mongol Conquest:** The country was first invaded by the Mongols under Subutai in 1223. The city was captured in 1225 and destroyed in 1238 (some sources cite 1237).
* **Golden Horde:** Following the Mongol conquest, Bolgari served for a time as the seat of the khans of the Golden Horde of Mongols.
* **Tamerlane:** The city was destroyed again in the early 14th century (specifically 1391) by Tamerlane.
* **Kazan and Russia:** In the second half of the 15th century, Bolgari became part of the principality (or kingdom) of Kazan, losing its political and commercial importance. It was eventually annexed to Russia after the fall of Kazan.

## Archaeological Remains
The ruins of Bolgari were practically discovered during the reign of Peter the Great and were later described by Humboldt and Pallas. Surviving structures include old towers and walls. The area is surrounded by numerous *kurgans* (burial-mounds). 

Excavations of these tombs have yielded:
* **Artifacts:** Utensils, implements, weapons, and coins made of copper and silver.
* **Inscriptions:** Findings include inscriptions in Turkic, Armenian (dated 557, 984, and 986), and Arabic (dated 1222–1341). The Arabic inscriptions include both ordinary Arabic and Kufic (epigraphic Arabic).
* **Numismatics:** Coins from the 10th century struck by the Kings of Bolghar have been described, as well as coins from the Mongol period. The latest known coin is dated A.H. 818 (A.D. 1415–16).

Antiquities collected from the site in 1722 are currently preserved in museums in St Petersburg, Moscow, and Kazan.
