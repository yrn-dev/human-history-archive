# BOHEMUND

The name Bohemund is associated with a lineage of princes and counts who governed the principality of Antioch and, in later generations, the county of Tripoli. The history of these individuals is closely tied to the history of these two territories.

## Bohemund I
Bohemund I (c. A.D. 1058–1111), originally named Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, the *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*, and his first wife, Alberida. He served his father during an attack on the East Roman empire between 1080 and 1085, and commanded the Normans from 1082 to 1084. During this period, he penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly before being repulsed by Alexius Comnenus.

Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a conflict arose between Bohemund and his half-brother Roger (the son of Sicelgaeta), who had succeeded to the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This war was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions. In 1096, while attacking Amalfi, Bohemund was inspired by the passing crusaders. He gathered a Norman army and crossed the Adriatic to Constantinople, arriving in April 1097. Although he did homage to Emperor Alexius, Bohemund is regarded as the leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch. He married Constance, the daughter of Philip I of France.

## Bohemund II and Bohemund III
Bohemund II (1108–1131) was the son of Bohemund I and Constance of France. Born in the year his father was defeated at Durazzo, he arrived in Antioch from Apulia in 1126. In 1127, he married Alice, the younger daughter of Baldwin II, and joined Baldwin II in an attack on Damascus. He was killed in 1131 on his northern frontier by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo.

Bohemund III was the son of Raymund of Antioch and Constance (the daughter of Bohemund II). He succeeded his mother in the principality of Antioch in 1163. In 1164, while serving as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem during Amalric I's expedition to Egypt, he was captured by Nureddin at Harenc. He was ransomed by his brother-in-law, Emperor Manuel, and returned to Antioch from Constantinople with a Greek patriarch. 

Bohemund III's later life was marked by personal and political strife. In 1180, he was excommunicated after deserting his second wife, Princess Orguilleuse, for Sibylla. He had two sons by Orguilleuse: Raymund and Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV). A succession dispute arose between his grandson, Raymund Rhupen (son of Raymund and Alice, daughter of Rhupen), and his younger son, Bohemund. Leo of Armenia championed Raymund Rhupen and captured Bohemund III at Gastin in 1194 to force the cession of Antioch. Bohemund the younger evicted his father from Antioch around 1199, but was later ousted by Leo. Bohemund III died in 1201 while still in possession of his principality.

## Bohemund IV and Bohemund V
Bohemund IV, the younger son of Bohemund III and Orguilleuse, became count of Tripoli in 1187 and succeeded his father in Antioch in 1201. His reign was characterized by a long dispute with Leo of Armenia over the claims of Raymund Rhupen. Bohemund IV was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and the Templars. In contrast, the Hospitallers supported Leo. Although Leo captured Antioch in 1216 and installed Raymund Rhupen, Bohemund IV regained it by 1220. 

Bohemund IV was a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, leading to his excommunication in 1230. He also had a fluctuating relationship with Emperor Frederick II during the crusade of 1228–29. He died in 1233, shortly after concluding peace with the Hospitallers and being released from excommunication by Gregory IX.

Bohemund V, the son of Bohemund IV and Plaisance (daughter of Hugh of Gibelet), succeeded his father in 1233 as prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. He maintained an alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia until 1251, when peace was achieved through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund V also married Alice, the widow of Hugh I of Cyprus, in 1225. He resided primarily at Tripoli, leaving Antioch to be governed by a commune and its bailiff. He died in 1251.

## Bohemund VI and Bohemund VII
Bohemund VI (born 1237) was the son of Bohemund V and Luciana, daughter of the count of Segni. He succeeded his father in 1251 and was knighted by St Louis in 1252. He established himself in Antioch in 1252 and recognized his nephew, Hugh II, as king of Jerusalem in 1257. To counter the Egyptian sultan, he allied with the Mongols. However, he lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268. By the time of his death in 1275, he held only the title of count of Tripoli.

Bohemund VII, the son of Bohemund VI and Sibylla (sister of Leo III of Armenia), succeeded to the county of Tripoli in 1275. His mother served as regent. His reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue, and the county of Tripoli became extinct shortly thereafter when it was captured within two years of his death.
