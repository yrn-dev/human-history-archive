# La Rochelle

La Rochelle is a location associated with various historical events spanning religious translation, military conflict, and the birth of notable figures.

## Religious and Linguistic Significance
La Rochelle served as a site for significant Basque linguistic contributions. In 1571, a Protestant translation of the New Testament was printed there. This work, produced under the auspices of Jeanne d'Albret and translated by Jean de Licarrague, is considered the standard of the Basque language.

## Military and Political History
The city was a focal point of military efforts and political tension during the 17th century. In 1627, the county of Dorsetshire was reproved for its lack of zeal in the service of the state after it refused to send men to La Rochelle.

Later, Buckingham commanded a final expedition intended for the relief of La Rochelle. During this period, Buckingham faced resistance from officials who believed the enterprise was doomed to failure. While considering a pacification scheme proposed by the Venetian ambassador, Buckingham was assassinated on the morning of August 23 by John Felton. Felton, who had served at Ré, killed Buckingham by stabbing him to the heart as he left his breakfast room. Felton's motives were rooted in private wrongs, including a lack of payment for his services and a disappointment regarding promotion, as well as the declaration by the Commons that Buckingham was a public enemy.

## Notable Figures
La Rochelle is the birthplace of Aime Jacques Alexandre Bonpland (1773–1858), a French botanist and traveller born there on August 22, 1773. Originally named Goujand, Bonpland served as a surgeon in the French army and studied in Paris under J.N. Corvisart. He is noted for accompanying A. von Humboldt on five years of travel through Colombia, Mexico, and districts bordering the Amazon and Orinoco. During these travels, he classified and collected approximately 6,000 plants, most of which were unknown in Europe at the time. 

Following his return to Paris, Bonpland published *Description des plantes rares de Navarre* (1813) and *Monographie des Melastomees* (1806), and he was granted a pension and the superintendence of the gardens at Malmaison. He later moved to Buenos Aires in 1816, where he served as a professor of natural history before exploring central South America. He was detained in Paraguay from 1821 until 1831 by the command of dictator Dr. Francia. He eventually died on May 4, 1858, in Santa Anna, having moved there in 1853.
