# Bucentaur

The bucentaur (Italian: *bucintoro*) was the state galley utilized by the doges of Venice. It served as a vessel for ceremonial purposes, most notably for the annual "wedding the sea" ceremony performed on Ascension day. This tradition continued until 1789.

## Etymology and Design
The term *bucintoro* is derived from the Italian *buzino d' oro*, meaning "golden bark." During the middle ages, this was latinized as *bucentaurus*. This latinization occurred based on a perceived analogy to the Greek *boukentauros* (ox-centaur), combining the Greek words *bous* (ox) and *Kentauros* (centaur). 

This linguistic connection led to a fanciful explanation that the galley's figurehead was the head of an ox. However, representations of the bucentaurs that have survived indicate that the actual figurehead was the lion of St Mark. In general usage, the name bucentaur appears to have been applied to any Venetian galley that was particularly sumptuous and great.

## Historical Records
The use of the bucentaur is documented in historical chronicles. Du Cange quotes from the chronicle of the doge Andrea Dandolo (who died in 1354) a passage describing the arrival of a "solemn and artistic" (*artificioso et solemni*) Bucentaur. The text notes that the vessel arrived at S. Clement, where a more principal and solemn Bucentaur had already arrived with the councillors.

## The Final Bucentaur
The last and most magnificent of these vessels was constructed in 1729. This galley was destroyed by the French in 1798, who dismantled it to obtain its golden decorations. 

Currently, remains of this final bucentaur are preserved in Venice at the Arsenal and the Museo Civico Correr. The Arsenal also houses a fine model of the vessel.
