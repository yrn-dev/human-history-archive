# Achmath the Bailo

Achmath (also referred to as Ah-ha-ma or Ahmed) was a Saracen and a Persian who served as a powerful minister under the Great Kaan in Cambaluc. He was one of twelve persons appointed with the authority to dispose of lands, offices, and other matters at their own discretion. For twenty-two years, Achmath maintained a level of power and influence with the Grand Kaan that exceeded all others.

## Rise to Power and Influence
The Great Kaan held Achmath in such high regard that the minister was permitted to do as he pleased. It was later revealed that Achmath had used sorcery to influence the Kaan, leading the Emperor to place the greatest faith and reliance in everything Achmath said. Consequently, the Kaan followed Achmath's wishes and frequently responded to his suggestions with the phrase, "Do as you think best" or "Do as you think right."

Achmath's authority extended to the disposal of all governments and offices, as well as passing sentence on malefactors. He utilized this power to execute those he hated by accusing them of offenses against the imperial dignity. Because of the Kaan's unbounded reliance on him, no person, regardless of rank or power, felt free from dread. Those accused of capital offenses were unable to provide proofs for their exculpation because no one dared to stand by them or oppose Achmath.

## Oppressions and Iniquities
Achmath amassed an enormous treasure through the collection of heavy bribes from anyone seeking an office. He is also recorded as having committed extensive outrages against the people of the country, specifically the Cathayans. 

Achmath used his position to acquire any beautiful woman he desired. If a woman was unmarried, he forced her to be his wife; if she was already married, he compelled her to consent to his desires. He employed ruffians to approach fathers of pretty daughters, offering to arrange for the father to receive a government or office for three years in exchange for the daughter's marriage to Achmath. Once the daughter was surrendered, Achmath would inform the Emperor that a government was vacant and recommend the girl's father for the post, which the Emperor would grant.

Achmath had approximately twenty-five sons who held important offices. Seven of these sons followed their father's example, forcing and ravishing numerous women.

## The Plot and Execution
The Cathayans, wearied by these abominable iniquities, conspired to slay Achmath and revolt. The conspiracy was led by two Cathayans: Vanchu, a commander of 10,000, and Chenchu, a commander of 1,000 whose wife, daughter, and mother had all been dishonored by Achmath. They planned their strike for a time when the Great Kaan was absent from Cambaluc (as the Kaan typically spent three months in Cambaluc and three months in Chandu).

During the Kaan's absence, Vanchu and Chenchu entered the palace at night and sent a messenger to summon Achmath from the Old City, pretending that the Kaan's son, Chinkin, had arrived unexpectedly. Upon entering the palace and bowing before Vanchu—whom he believed to be Chinkin—Achmath was beheaded by Chenchu.

The plot was discovered by Cogatai, a Tartar and Captain of the 12,000-man standing garrison. Cogatai shot Vanchu dead with an arrow and ordered the seizure of Chenchu. He then proclaimed that anyone found in the streets would be put to death, which prevented the Cathayans from signaling other cities to rise. Cogatai reported the affair to the Great Kaan, who ordered a careful investigation and the punishment of the guilty ringleaders.

## Aftermath and Imperial Reaction
Upon returning to Cambaluc, the Great Kaan learned of the "endless iniquities" perpetrated by Achmath and his sons. In response, the Kaan ordered:
* All of Achmath's accumulated treasure in the Old City to be transferred to the imperial treasury in the New City.
* Achmath's body to be exhumed and cast into the streets for dogs to tear.
* The sons who had followed their father's evil example to be flayed alive.

These events led the Kaan to develop a profound disgust for the "accursed doctrines of the Sect of the Saracens," which he believed excused crimes, including murder, when committed against those of other religions. Consequently, the Kaan prohibited the Saracens from following several religious injunctions. He ordered them to regulate their marriages according to Tartar Law and forbade the cutting of animals' throats for food, commanding instead that they rip the stomach in the Tartar way.
