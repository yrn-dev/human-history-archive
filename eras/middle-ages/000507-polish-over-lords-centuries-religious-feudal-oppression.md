# Polish Over-lords and the Galicia Rising of 1846

## The Social Divide in Galicia
In the region of Galicia, a profound social and cultural divide existed between the ruling class and the peasantry. The Polish over-lords, who were Catholic, held authority over the Orthodox Ruthenian peasantry. This relationship was characterized by centuries of feudal oppression and religious division.

## The Rising of 1846
In 1846, a Polish nationalist movement originated in the small independent republic of Cracow. While the movement initially saw some success due to the incompetence of the Austrian commander, its broader political significance was tied to the reaction of the peasant class. 

The Polish nobles attempted to recruit the Ruthenian peasants into their ranks by offering lavish promises. However, the peasants rejected these offers and instead rose in support of the Austrian government. This conflict culminated in a fight at Gdow on February 26th. During this engagement, the peasants utilized scythes and flails, which caused more devastation among the rebel ranks than the musketry of the Austrian forces.

## The Jacquerie and Aftermath
Despite the events at Gdow, the Polish nobles continued to make offers to the peasants. In response, the peasants sought guidance from local Austrian authorities. Because these authorities were unaccustomed to making decisions without consulting Vienna, they effectively gave the peasants *carte blanche* to act as they wished.

This resulted in a "hideous *jacquerie*" that lasted for three or four days. During this period of violent uprising, cartloads of dead were transported into Tarnow. The local authorities further incentivized the violence by providing rewards to the peasants for every "rebel" they brought in.

## Context of Feudalism and Peasantry
The unrest in Galicia reflects a broader historical pattern where the success of political revolutions often depended on the attitude of the peasant class, as seen previously in France in 1789 and later in Russia in 1906. 

Across various European contexts, feudal systems often created stark hierarchies. In 13th-century England, for example, more than half the population were serfs who lacked the privileges of the Magna Carta, and disputes were settled in the lord's own court. Similarly, under French feudal law, the villein had no appeal from his lord except to God. Such systems frequently allowed for gross injustices, as noted by Cardinal Jacques de Vitry in the early 13th century.
