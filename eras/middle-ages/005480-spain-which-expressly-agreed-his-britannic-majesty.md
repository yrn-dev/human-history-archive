# Spain

## Colonial Claims and Early Americas
Spain was among the first European nations to establish overseas settlements, primarily for the purposes of mining and trade. The Spaniards claimed dominion over the entirety of the Americas. In 1494, the Pope divided the new continent between Spain and Portugal. Under this papal settlement, Portugal was granted Brazil and all territories east of a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, while all remaining territories were granted to Spain. Despite these claims, other nations—including the Dutch, Danes, Swedes, English, and France—ignored the papal settlement and established their own claims in the West Indies and North America.

## The Bay of Honduras and Belize
In the Bay of Honduras, a territory formerly styled in official documents as "His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras," British log-wood cutters (some of whom were former buccaneers) established a presence beginning in 1638. These settlers were later joined by agents of the Chartered Company, which operated pearl fisheries on the Mosquito coast. 

The British settlers frequently raided Spanish log-wood establishments. Spain attempted to expel them, most notably in April 1754. Because the position was difficult to approach by sea, the Spaniards organized an inland expedition of 1,500 men at the town of Peten. However, this force was completely routed by 250 British men as it neared the coast.

In the treaty of 1763, Great Britain agreed to demolish all fortifications English subjects had built in the Bay of Honduras. However, Britain insisted on a clause ensuring that log-wood cutters and their workmen would not be "disturbed or molested, under any pretext whatever" in their areas of loading and cutting. Following this recognition, British settlers continued to encroach on Spanish territory. In response to allegations of smuggling and illicit practices, Spain organized a large force that attacked and destroyed the establishment at Belize on September 15, 1779. The inhabitants were taken as prisoners to Havana and Mérida in Yucatan; many died in Havana. The survivors were liberated in 1782 and sent to Jamaica, though many returned in 1783 to resume cutting woods. On September 3, 1783, a new treaty was signed between Spain and Great Britain, expressly agreeing that the subjects of his Britannic Majesty held the right to cut, load, and carry away log-wood in a specific district.

## Governance and Legal Codes
Under the reign of Philip II, who served as the official protector of Catholicism, Spain maintained strict religious control. Philip II sought to impose this same religious control and political absolutism upon his rebellious subjects in other territories to preserve the cornerstones of his world-power for himself and the church.

In terms of civil and penal law, the Civil Code of Spain (art. 155, 2) expressly reserves the parental right of moderate chastisement. However, the Penal Code of Spain does not recognize flogging as a legal punishment.
