# Saint Boniface

## Early Life and Education
Saint Boniface (680–754), known by his birth name Wynfrith, was born into a distinguished Saxon family in Devonshire, specifically at Kirton or Crediton. From a young age, he entered the monastic life. His academic and spiritual training began with the study of theology and grammar at Exeter, after which he continued his studies at Nutcell, located near Winchester, under the guidance of Abbot Winberht. Boniface quickly gained a reputation as a skilled preacher and scholar.

## Missionary Work in Frisia and Germany
Despite having every reason to remain in his monastery, Boniface set out as a missionary to Frisia in 716, following the precedent established by other Saxon monks. This initial mission was cut short, likely due to the hostility of Radbod, the king of the Frisians, who was engaged in war with Charles Martel.

Following this, Boniface traveled to Rome at the end of 717. In 719, he was commissioned by Pope Gregory II to evangelize Germany and to counteract the influence of Irish monks in the region. After crossing the Alps, he visited Thuringia and Bavaria. Upon learning that King Radbod had died, Boniface returned to Frisia. There, he preached successfully for three years under the direction of Willibrord, a fellow countryman and the first bishop of Utrecht.

Around 722, Boniface expanded his efforts to Thuringia and Hesse, where he baptized many heathens and won over several chieftains.

## Episcopal Consecration and Expanded Mission
After reporting his successes to Pope Gregory, Boniface was summoned back to Rome. On November 30, 722, he took an oath of obedience to the pope and was consecrated as a bishop. His mission was subsequently enlarged; he returned to the field with letters of recommendation for Charles Martel. His new mandate included not only the conversion of heathens but also the suppression of heresy.

## Historical Context of Monasticism
The activities of Boniface were part of a broader trend of Anglo-Saxon monasticism. The Anglo-Saxons had been converted by Benedictine monks in 600 and began sending monks to convert Germany shortly after 700. This tradition of monasticism was rooted in the Rule of St. Benedict, which emphasized hard work and organized the monastery into a structured community. The Rule includes instructions for the abbot, regulations for the "Work of God" (the canonical office), and guidelines for the community of goods and the reception of new monks.

The influence of the Benedictine rule was spread throughout Latin monasticism largely due to Pope Gregory the Great (540–604), the first monk to become pope. Gregory was known for sending successful missions to the unconverted, specifically targeting the Anglo-Saxons.
