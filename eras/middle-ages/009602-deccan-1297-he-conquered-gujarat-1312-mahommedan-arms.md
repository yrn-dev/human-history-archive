# Western and Southern India: Gujarat, the Deccan, and Bombay

The regions of Gujarat, the Deccan, and the surrounding territories of western and southern India are characterized by a complex history of shifting dynasties, diverse linguistic groups, and varied geographical landscapes. This area has been shaped by the movements of native dynasties, the arrival of Mussulman powers, and later European colonization.

## Geography and Geology
The region is divided into five distinct tracts: Sind, Gujarat, the Konkan, the Deccan, and the Carnatic. Sind is a flat lower valley of the Indus with scanty vegetation. Gujarat consists largely of rich alluvial plains watered by the Nerbudda and Tapti rivers. The Konkan is a rugged territory between the sea and the Western Ghats, while the plains of the Deccan and Khandesh are watered by large rivers but often remain bleak due to uncertain rainfall. The Carnatic plain, south of the Kistna river, is known for its extensive black or cotton soil.

Geologically, the Deccan Trap series of horizontal lava flows covers nearly all of Bombay south of Gujarat, extending into Cutch and the Kathiawar peninsula. In Cutch, these are underlaid by Neocomian and Jurassic beds; the latter are marine and contain Ammonites. The Runn or Lake of Cutch is a unique feature that varies by season between a salt marsh, an inland lake, or an arm of the sea.

## Early History and Native Dynasties
The islands of Bombay were originally inhabited by the Kolis, a race of fishermen and husbandmen. The area was dominated by successive dynasties, including the Rashtrakutas, Chalukyas, Mauryas, and Satavahanas. Between 450 and 750, the city of Puri on Elephanta Island served as a principal location in the Bombay harbour.

In Kathiawar, a dynasty founded by a chief named Bhatarka at Valabhi lasted from approximately 500 until 770. Around 600, the Gurjaras established power over Gujarat and north-eastern Kathiawar. During the empire of Harsha (who died in 647 or 648), Gujarat, Malwa, and Kathiawar were under his sway. In the 7th century, the Chalukyas absorbed smaller kingdoms south of the Nerbudda after overcoming rivals such as the Rashtrakutas. By 750–950, a balance of power existed between the Rashtrakutas and the Gurjaras.

## Mussulman Conquests and Independent Kingdoms
Mussulman domination began with raids; in 1023, Mahmud of Ghazni invaded Gujarat and destroyed the Hindu idol of Somnath. Mahommed Ghori also invaded the region, leaving a garrison in the capital. However, full conquest of the south began after power was established in northern India. Emperor Ala-ud-din first invaded the Deccan in 1294 and conquered Gujarat in 1297. By 1312, Mahommedan arms were triumphant in the Mahratta country, and by 1319, Malabar had fallen.

In the mid-14th century, provincial governors revolted against the weakening Delhi sovereigns, leading to the creation of independent kingdoms in Ahmednagar, Gujarat, and the Bahmani kingdom in the Deccan. These were later reannexed by Akbar, who conquered Gujarat in 1573, Khandesh in 1599, and Ahmednagar in 1600. In the late 17th century, the Mahrattas rose to power, gaining sway over almost every part of the Bombay presidency.

## European Influence and the Portuguese
The Portuguese arrived at Calicut in 1498. After defeating the Egyptian fleet at Diu, they established mastery over the Indian Ocean. They captured Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511, and Ormuz in 1515. Taking advantage of the decline of the Gujarat kingdom, they occupied Chaul (1531), Diu (1535), Daman (1559), and Bassein, including Bombay, in 1534. The islands of Bombay had been part of the province or kingdom of Gujarat since 1348, when Mahikavati (founded by King Bhima) was captured by Mahommedan forces, until Sultan Bahadur ceded them to the Portuguese in 1534.

## Demographics, Language, and Religion
As of 1901, the Mahrattas were the dominating race after Europeans, numbering 3,650,000 (including 1,900,000 Kunbis, 1,400,000 unspecified Mahrattas, and 350,000 Konkanis). Hinduism is the predominant religion in Gujarat, while Islam has been predominant in Sind since the 8th century. In the Carnatic, 45% of the population embraced Lingayatism, a 12th-century Hindu reformation.

Linguistically, the region is diverse. Sindhi is spoken in Sind, and Gujarati and Hindustani in Gujarat. Mahratti is the chief language in Thana, the central division, and Khandesh. The Marathi of the Deccan is distinct from the Konkani dialect spoken near Goa. These languages evolved as "Outer Band" Aryan speakers settled in the Deccan plateau and Konkan coast, imposing their tongue upon the aborigines.

## Recent History: Plague and Famine
The late 19th and early 20th centuries were marked by disaster. Bubonic plague broke out in Bombay in September 1896; by October 1902, over 531,000 deaths occurred. Severe mortality was recorded in Poona, Surat, Thana, Satara, Kolaba, and the states of Palanpur, Baroda, Kolhapur, and Cutch. This period saw social unrest, including the assassination of two European officials in Poona in 1897 and a riot by Mahommedan weavers in Bombay in March 1898.

Famine also struck frequently. A monsoon failure in 1896 affected 7 million people across 46,000 sq. m. of the Deccan, requiring Rs. 1,28,000,000 in relief expenditure. Another famine occurred in Gujarat in 1899.
