# Civil Unrest and Political Conflict in Bohemia

The history of Bohemia is marked by recurring periods of rioting and civil strife, often driven by religious disputes, succession crises, and nationalist movements. These conflicts frequently centered on the city of Prague and involved tensions between the Bohemian population, the ruling monarchy, and the Church of Rome.

## Religious Conflict and the Hussite Wars
Incipient religious conflict emerged during the later reign of King Wenceslas. The influence of the Church of Rome had been weakened by pious priests, including Waldhauser and Milic, who attacked the immorality of the clergy. This alienation was furthered by a church schism in which rival pontiffs engaged in medieval theological strife. Consequently, nearly the entire Bohemian nation supported the cause of Huss. While Wenceslas was characterized by irresolution, Queen Sophia openly favored Huss, who served as her confessor.

The conflict escalated when Huss was tried by the council of Constance. Despite a letter of safe conduct provided by Sigismund (Wenceslas's brother and king of the Romans), Huss was declared a heretic and burnt on July 6, 1415. This event triggered a civil war in Bohemia, where Huss was revered by the majority of the population. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. This document protested the execution of Huss—described as a "good, just and catholic man"—and declared that those claiming heresy existed in Bohemia were "liars, vile traitors and calumniators." The protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church and marked the start of the Hussite wars.

## The Reign of Sigismund and the Siege of Prague
Following the death of the king, renewed rioting broke out in Prague and various other Bohemian cities, leading to the expulsion of many Germans, most of whom were adherents of the Church of Rome. Sigismund, claiming the Bohemian crown as his brother's successor, arrived at Kutna Hora early the following year. On March 1, 1420, Pope Martin V proclaimed a crusade against Bohemia.

On June 30, Sigismund led a large international army, including Bohemians, to occupy the castle of Prague. The crusaders viewed the city as a heretical community because its inhabitants accepted other evangelical truths and used the sacred chalice. However, the attempt to conquer Prague failed. After the Taborites, led by Zizka, repulsed an attack on the Vitkov (Zizkov) hill with "desperate bravery," Sigismund abandoned the siege. He suffered a further defeat on November 1, 1420, at the battle of the Vysehrad while attempting to relieve a besieged garrison.

## Later Religious and Dynastic Struggles
Religious tensions persisted into the reigns of later monarchs. George of Podebrad was supported by the town of Budweis, though the town was taken by crusaders under Zdenko of Stenberg in 1468. Following George's death, Prince Vladislav of Poland was elected king at Kutna Hora on May 27, 1471. This was seen as a victory for the national party and a reflection of strong anti-clerical feeling, as Vladislav promised to maintain the Compacts, whereas his rival, Matthias of Hungary, was an unconditional adherent of Rome.

Despite his promises, Vladislav's policy became reactionary. He appointed government officials in Prague from the section of the Utraquist party closest to Rome and oversaw the severe persecution of the Bohemian Brethren (extreme Hussites). This led to serious riots in Prague, where advanced Hussites stormed three town halls and nobles of the same faith formed a league for protection. Peace was temporarily restored following a meeting of the estates at Kutna Hora in 1485.

In 1575, Maximilian returned to Bohemia amid demands from the estates for the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg" (a summary of Luther's teaching) and the right to appoint members of the consistory. When Maximilian refused, the majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian brotherhood—created the *Confessio Bohemica*. This profession of faith aligned with Calvin regarding the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. While Maximilian verbally approved the *Confessio*, he refused to make it public or include it in the kingdom's charters.

## Imperial Conflict and Nationalist Movements
In 1619, the Bohemian crown became vacant upon the death of Matthias. The estates deposed Ferdinand and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as king. However, Ferdinand, now emperor, sought to reconquer Bohemia with aid from Spain, Poland, several Italian states, and the duke Maximilian of Bavaria. On November 8, 1620, imperial forces attacked the Bohemian army at the "Bila Hora" (White Hill). The Bohemians were defeated in a few hours, and the imperialists occupied the port of Prague. King Frederick fled the city the next morning.

In the 19th century, unrest shifted toward nationalist and liberal demands. On March 11, 1848, "young Czechs" in Prague drew up a petition for nationalist reforms. Simultaneously, in Vienna, riots involving students and workmen forced the government to concede to petitions, eventually leading to the exile of Metternich on March 13, 1848.

By 1868, the nationalist movement in Bohemia became more active. Czech leaders presented a "declaration" to the diet at Prague demanding the full restoration of the Bohemian kingdom and asserting that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia. This led to serious riots and the government's decree of exceptional laws. Later, the victory of the Czechs was manifested in Prague through the removal of all German street names and a by-law forbidding private individuals from using German tablets for street names.
