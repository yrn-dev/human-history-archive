# The Duchy of Burgundy

The Duchy of Burgundy was a territorial entity in France whose history is characterized by two distinct Capet houses and a period of significant territorial expansion that eventually challenged the French kingship. Originally created by Carolingian kings to resist Boso, the duchy was first granted to Richard the Justiciary, count of Autun. Its early boundaries included the countships of Sens, Auxerre, Nevers, Langres, Chalon-sur-Saône, Mâcon, and Autun, though these designations shifted throughout the 10th century.

## The First House of Burgundy
Following a thirteen-year war ending in 1015, King Robert II reunited the duchy with the French kingdom. In 1032, King Henry I bestowed the duchy upon his brother Robert, establishing the first ducal house of Burgundy. This dynasty flourished until 1361. Generally, these first Capet dukes were pacific princes with limited involvement in the political events of their era or the religious movements of the region, such as those at Cîteaux, Cluny, or among the disciples of William of St Bénigne of Dijon. Notable figures included Duke Hugh III (1162–1193), active in the early wars of Philip Augustus's reign; Odo III (1193–1218), a supporter of Philip Augustus against King John of England; and Hugh IV (1218–1272), who acquired the countships of Auxonne and Châlon. Odo IV (1315–1350) expanded the holdings by gaining the countship of Artois through his wife, Jeanne of France.

## The Second House of Burgundy
The duchy returned to the French crown in 1361 upon the death of Duke Philip de Rouvres. In 1363, King John II (the Good) granted the duchy and hereditary rights to his son, Philip the Bold, founding the second Capet house of Burgundy. Philip the Bold significantly expanded the house's power:
* **Territorial Acquisitions:** Through his marriage to Margaret of Flanders, he added the countships of Flanders and Burgundy in 1384 following the death of Louis of Male. In the same year, he purchased the countship of Charolais from John, count of Armagnac.
* **Political Influence:** Philip and his brothers, the dukes of Berry and Anjou, held the regency after the death of Charles V in 1380. Philip led Charles VI to victory against rebellious Flemings at Roosebeke in 1382.
* **Administration:** During a period of diminished power under the "Marmousets," Philip established audit-offices (_chambre des comptes_) at Lille and Dijon in 1386.

Philip the Bold was succeeded in May 1404 by his son, John the Fearless. This transition marked the beginning of an intense hostility between the party of Burgundy and the party of Orleans, sparked by the recall of the princes of the blood following the madness of the king in 1392.

## Consolidation of Belgian Principalities
The Burgundian dukes played a central role in the political unification of the Belgian principalities. By the end of the 14th century, Burgundy possessed the duchy of Burgundy and the counties of Artois and Flanders. The scale shifted further when Duchess Jeanne of Brabant gave the duchy of Brabant to her niece, the wife of Philip the Bold. In 1404, Brabant and Limburg went to Philip's youngest son, Antoine, while John the Fearless received Flanders and Artois.

Antoine later annexed the duchy of Luxemburg in 1409. His son, John IV—who founded the University of Louvain in 1425—added the seigneurie of Friesland and the counties of Zeeland, Holland, and Hainaut through marriage to Jacqueline of Bavaria. In 1430, the states of Brabant offered these possessions to Philip the Good, head of the Flemish branch. Having purchased the county of Namur in 1429, Philip the Good brought nearly all Belgian principalities under one rule, with the exception of the ecclesiastical principalities of Utrecht, Liège, and Cambrai.

## Decline and Later History
The Burgundian state faced collapse following the death of Charles the Bold at the battlefield of Nancy. His death led to the independence of Alsace, Lorraine, and Liège. Louis XI annexed the duchy of Burgundy, the Franche-Comté, Artois, and the cities of Picardy and the Somme to France. This left Charles's daughter, Mary of Burgundy, in a precarious position, leading to her marriage to Maximilian of Hapsburg, son of Emperor Frederick III. This union established the Hapsburg dynasty in Belgium until the French Revolution.

In later centuries, Burgundy became a military government. From the 16th to the 18th century, it was a _pays d'états_ with an assembly at Dijon. Its boundaries were defined by Bourbonnais and Nivernais to the west, Champagne to the north, Lyonnais to the south, and Franche-Comté to the east. The title of duke of Burgundy was briefly revived in 1682 by Louis XIV for his grandson Louis, who was a pupil of Fénelon. In his *Instructions* to the young duke, Fénelon impressed upon him the axiom of the "balance of power" in international law.

The lineage of the later dukes saw significant loss; Louis XIV's eldest grandson, the duke of Burgundy, died in February 1712, leaving the succession to his son, the duke of Anjou (later Louis XV).
