# The Boot (Instrument of Torture)

The boot, also referred to as boots or the bootikin, was an instrument of torture utilized to obtain evidence from unwilling witnesses or to extort confessions from suspected persons. 

## Origins and Use in Scotland
The instrument originated in Scotland, although the exact date of its first application is unknown. It was frequently employed during the latter years of the 16th century. Recorded instances include a 1579 case of forgery where an attorney and a clergyman were tortured with the device. In 1583, a letter in the London Record Office shows Walsingham instructing the English ambassador at Edinburgh to have an English Jesuit, Father Holt, "put to the boots."

While the device appeared to fall into disuse after 1630, it was revived in 1666 during the Covenanters' rebellion and continued to be used during the reigns of James II and Charles II. Following the accession of William III, the Scottish convention denounced the use of torture in ordinary crimes and without evidence as being contrary to law. Despite this, a warrant signed by the king authorized the torture of Neville Payne, an Englishman suspected of treasonable motives for visiting Scotland, approximately a year later. This is the last recorded instance of the boot's use; torture was finally abolished in Scotland in 1709.

## Use in England
The boot was not used in England after 1640.

## Design and Application
The boot was constructed from iron, or from wood and iron. The device was fastened to the leg, and wedges were driven between the leg and the boot using blows from a mallet. After each blow, the victim was asked a question, and the process continued until the person fainted or provided the requested information. While the wedges were typically placed against the calf, Bishop Burnet noted that they were sometimes placed against the shin-bone.

## Variations
Similar instruments known as "Spanish boots" were employed in Germany. Other variations included iron boots that were heated and applied to the victim's foot. A less cruel version involved a buskin or boot that was made wet, drawn onto the legs, and then dried using fire.
