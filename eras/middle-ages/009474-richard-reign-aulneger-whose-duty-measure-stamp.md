# Aulnegers and the Woollen Trade in Bolton and Bury

The role of the aulneger was a specialized administrative position in the English textile industry, primarily tasked with the measurement and stamping of woollen goods to ensure quality and standardization. This office is notably linked to the early development of the woollen cloth trade in the towns of Bolton and Bury.

## The Aulneger in Bolton
Bolton established a reputation for woollen manufactures in its early history. The significance of the town as a center for the woollen cloth trade is evidenced by the appointment of an aulneger during the reign of Richard I. The specific duty of this official was to measure and stamp all bundles of woollen goods produced in the area.

The industry in Bolton continued to evolve over the following centuries. In 1337, the settlement of a group of Flemish clothiers provided an impulse to the industry, leading to substantial expansion. By 1566, the growth of the trade necessitated an act of parliament to appoint deputies to assist the aulnegers; Bolton was specifically named as one of the locations where these deputies were to be employed. 

While Leland recorded in his 1558 *Itinerary* that Bolton produced "cottons," these were actually woollen goods. Genuine cotton manufacture did not begin in Lancashire until 1641, at which point Bolton became the primary seat for the production of dimities, vermilions, and fustians.

## The Woollen Trade in Bury
In Bury, the woollen trade was established through the influence of Flemish immigrants during the reign of Edward III. The industry grew in importance over time, reaching a level of significance by the era of Elizabeth I that required the appointment of an aulneger to stamp and measure the woollen cloth.

Although woollen manufacture continued in Bury, it was gradually superseded by the cotton trade starting in the early part of the 18th century. Other related industries, such as machine-making, dyeing, and bleaching, were also carried on in the town.

## Related Industrial Developments
The transition from wool to cotton in these regions was marked by significant technological advancements. In Bolton, the cotton trade was greatly accelerated by the inventions of Samuel Crompton (1780) and Sir Richard Arkwright (1770), both of whom were born in the parish. The first spinning factory in Bolton is said to have been established in 1780. By 1851, the town housed 66 cotton mills utilizing 860,000 throstle spindles. Additionally, the bleaching industry had been active since the early 18th century, and large ironworks developed during the latter half of the 19th century.

In Bury, the cotton industry's rise was furthered by the establishment of Robert Peel's print-works in 1770 and the contributions of the Kay family of inventors.
