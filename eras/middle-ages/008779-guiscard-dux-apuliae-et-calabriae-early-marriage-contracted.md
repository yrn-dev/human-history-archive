# Robert Guiscard, Dux Apuliae et Calabriae

Robert Guiscard was a Norman soldier and statesman who rose from the middle order of private nobility to become the Duke of Apulia and Calabria. Born into a race of bannerets or valvassors from the diocese of Coutances in Lower Normandy, his family seat was the castle of Hauteville. He was the eldest of seven sons born to his father, Tancred, and Tancred's second wife.

## Rise to Power
Robert and his brothers joined the Norman camp in Apulia after the three eldest of their family—William, Drogo, and Humphrey—had already established themselves as chiefs of the nation. Following the death of Humphrey, the ambition of a guardian uncle reduced the sons of Humphrey to private estates, which allowed Guiscard to be exalted as the general of the republic and the count of Apulia. 

Guiscard expanded his authority by resuming the conquest of Calabria. Despite facing opposition from a parliament of barons and conspiracies from twelve counts of popular election, he suppressed rebellions and punished the guilty with death. He eventually sought a higher rank to elevate himself above his equals.

## Ducal Title and Papal Relations
Guiscard's path to official power involved a complex relationship with the papacy. Although he had incurred a papal excommunication due to acts of sacrilege or rapine, Pope Nicholas II was persuaded that the Normans were the faithful champions of the Holy See. A synod of one hundred bishops was convened at Melphi, where the Pope conferred the ducal title upon Robert and his posterity. This investiture included Apulia, Calabria, and all lands in Italy and Sicily that could be rescued from unbelieving Saracens and schismatic Greeks.

Guiscard did not immediately announce his elevation, waiting until the conquest of Reggio and Consenza to solicit the suffrage of his troops. Upon their joyful acclamation, he styled himself "By the grace of God and St. Peter, duke of Apulia, Calabria, and hereafter of Sicily."

## Marriages and Heirs
Robert Guiscard contracted two marriages. His first wife was the partner of his humble fortune; however, he later divorced her under the pretence of consanguinity. From this early marriage, contracted before 1059, was born his eldest son, Bohemund (originally named Marc).

Guiscard's second wife was a Lombard princess, the daughter of the princes of Salerno, named Sicelgaeta. This union produced a son, Roger, who was born "in the purple." The Lombards accepted Roger as the lineal successor. Guiscard and Sicelgaeta also had five daughters; one of these daughters was betrothed at a tender age to Constantine, the son and heir of Emperor Michael.

## Ambitions in the East
Guiscard's ambitions extended beyond the possession of Apulia and Calabria. He sought to invade and subdue the Roman Empire of the East. To provide a justification for his arms, Guiscard contrived a fraud involving a pretender who claimed to be Emperor Michael. This individual, described as either a domestic or a monk who had fled his convent, was welcomed by the duke and adorned with imperial titles. Pope Gregory VII supported this effort, exhorting Catholics to fight for the restoration of the pretender.

Between 1080 and 1085, Guiscard launched a great attack on the East Roman Empire. During this campaign, his son Bohemund served under him and commanded the Normans from 1082 to 1084 during Guiscard's absence. Bohemund penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly but was repulsed by Alexius Comnenus.

## Death and Succession
Robert Guiscard died in 1085. Upon his death, a conflict arose between his sons. While Guiscard had destined Bohemund for the throne of Constantinople, the duchy of Apulia and Calabria was succeeded by the younger son, Roger. This war was eventually resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund other possessions and Otranto.

## Physical Description
Contemporary accounts describe Guiscard as a man of commanding dignity and lofty stature, surpassing the tallest of his army. He was noted for his ruddy complexion, broad shoulders, and long, flaxen hair and beard. His voice was compared to that of Achilles for its ability to impress terror and obedience during battle.
