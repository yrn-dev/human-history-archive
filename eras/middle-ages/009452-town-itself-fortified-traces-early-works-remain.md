# Fortified Towns and Settlements

Throughout history, the development of urban centers has frequently been linked to the necessity of fortification. From ancient Greek city-states to medieval European market towns, the use of walls, citadels, and strategic positioning has served both military and social functions.

## Ancient Greek and Mediterranean Settlements
In early Grecian states, the structure of the community was often divided between those living in fortified towns and the surrounding country population. The town proprietors, who frequently composed the governing class, resided within these fortifications. In contrast, the villagers who tilled the land lived in a state of painful dependence on these proprietors and were often distinguished by their own habits and dress. The population within the town included the governing order, small self-working proprietors (αὐτουργοὶ), and those without land who engaged in commerce, arts, or handicrafts.

The strategic value of fortified positions is evident in the history of the Dorians. It is reported that Têmenus and his invading Dorians seized and fortified a small village on the hillock of Temenion near Argos, using it as an armed post to wage war against the Achæans and Tisamenus. Similarly, the eminence of Solygeius near Corinth was believed to be a spot fortified by Dorian assailants against the pre-existing Corinthians. Because fortified towns were generally planted in highly defensible positions, invaders often resorted to entrenching themselves in the neighborhood to harass inhabitants and ruin produce until terms were reached.

In other Mediterranean regions, such as Sicily, early efforts toward town-building are seen in Sikel or Sikan caverns. These cryptæ may have served as retreats for valuables, women, and children during times of extraordinary alarm or danger.

## Medieval and Post-Conquest Fortifications in Britain
In England and Ireland, the establishment of castles and walled towns often followed periods of conquest or the granting of royal charters.

### England
In Bolsover, Derbyshire, a castle was founded by William Peveril shortly after the Conquest, and the town that grew around it was fortified; traces of these early works still remain. In Durham, William the Conqueror ordered the erection of a castle in 1072 across the neck of the peninsula, north of the cathedral. While the keep is a modern reconstruction, other elements such as the Norman gallery and a crypt-chapel remain.

The town of Bury derives its name from the Anglo-Saxon terms *burhg*, *birig*, or *byrig*, meaning a castle, town, or fortified place. It served as the site of a Saxon station, and an old English castle was located in Castle Croft. In Caithness, the presence of numerous fortified keeps serves as evidence of a history marked by reprisals, assaults, and feuds.

### Ireland
Kilmallock, a market town in county Limerick, was walled and fortified during the reign of Edward III, featuring four gates (two of which still exist). While it served as an important military post during the reign of Elizabeth, the majority of its fortifications were later demolished by order of Cromwell.

## Other Global Examples
Fortifications and defensive works are also noted in other regions of the archive:

*   **Russia:** The seaport of Kerch stands on the site of the ancient *Panticapaeum*, clustering around a hill that held an ancient acropolis or citadel. Additionally, the town of Yenikale, located about six miles north-east of Kerch, is an old Turkish fortress. Further south, strong fortified works defend the entrance to the Sea of Azov.
*   **Central Asia:** Near the town of Tzarev, excavations have revealed the remains of Sarai. This site includes mounds, earth-works, walls, and canals. A vast mass of remains surrounded by the traces of a brick rampart is believed to be the position of the Imperial Palace.
