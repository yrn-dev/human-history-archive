# Baptism

Baptism, derived from the Greek words *baptismos* and *baptisma* meaning "ceremonial washing," is a ritual practice of dipping or washing. While the shorter Greek form *bapto* refers to dipping without ritual significance—such as a robe in blood or a finger in water—the ceremonial act has been used across various traditions. Tertullian noted that the votaries of Mithras and Isis were initiated *per lavacrum* ("through a font"), and that in the mysteries of Eleusis and Apollo, men were baptized (*tinguntur*) in hopes of regeneration and exemption from the guilt of perjuries. Additionally, ancient traditions included the practice of those who had committed homicide seeking waters to purge their guilt.

## Formulas and Rituals
Different Christian traditions have employed various formulas and methods for the rite. The formula used in Rome is, "I baptize thee in the name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit." In the East, the formula is "so-and-so, the servant of God, is baptized," with the Greeks adding "Amen" after each person and concluding with the words, "Now and ever and to aeons of aeons, amen."

Regarding the physical act, some traditions utilize trine immersion (dipping the person three times). Tertullian explained this as being "thrice, for the several names, into the several persons," while Jerome stated that being thrice plunged shows forth the "one sacrament of the Trinity." Conversely, other figures—including Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Gregory of Nyssa, Theophylactus, and Leo of Rome—regarded trine immersion as symbolic of the three days Christ was entombed. This interpretation is enjoined in the Armenian baptismal rubric and an epistle from Macarius of Jerusalem (c. 330), where it is further associated with baptism into the death of Christ. In cases where immersion is not possible, instructions exist to pour water upon the head three times in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

## Preparation and Early Practice
Early descriptions of the rite, such as those by Justin (c. 140), indicate that candidates were instructed to pray and entreat God with fasting for the remission of past sins. The person baptizing and the person being baptized were expected to fast, with the candidate fasting for one or two days prior. This process was preceded by "things spoken beforehand," which were moral precepts known as the two ways: the way of life and the way of death. This body of moral teaching may date back to A.D. 80.

## The Feast of the Baptism
The commemoration of the Baptism of Jesus evolved into a significant feast, often associated with Epiphany. In the East—specifically in the churches of Egypt, Syria, Georgia, Russia, Greece, and Armenia—this tradition remained more conservative. In Egypt, Gnostics set the feast on January 6, coinciding with the blessing of the Nile. This date was sometimes called the "Birthday" (*Natalicia* or *genethlia*), leading to a period where the physical birth of Christ was associated with the feast. Some in Sicily believed that the Saviour was reborn through the Holy Spirit during His Baptism, leading them to baptize neophytes on January 6.

However, Pope Leo (c. 447) protested this, arguing that the Lord required no remedy of rebirth or remission of sins. Leo asserted that the power of regeneration was sanctioned not at the Baptism, but when the water of baptism and blood of redemption flowed from Christ's side. Consequently, he argued that neophytes should be baptized only at Pentecost and Easter.

## Historical and Scriptural Context
The New Testament contains various texts relating to baptism, ranging from A.D. 55-60 (such as Romans vi. 3, 4) to A.D. 90-100 (such as John iii. 3-8). There is scholarly discussion regarding whether Jesus instituted baptism as a condition for entry into the Messianic kingdom. While the fourth gospel suggests Jesus baptized on a larger scale than the Baptist, it also notes that only His disciples baptized. Injunctions to baptize are attributed to Jesus in Matthew xxviii. 18-20 and Mark xvi. 16, though some critics have questioned if these reflect the liturgical usage of specific regions.

In other biblical accounts, Jesus speaks of a "baptism" in a metaphorical or sacrificial sense. When James and John, the sons of Zebedee, asked to sit at His right and left in His glory, Jesus asked if they could "be baptized with the baptism that I am baptized with," to which they replied that they could. Jesus confirmed they would be, while noting that greatness in His kingdom is defined by being a servant of all.

## Global Expansion
The practice of baptism expanded globally through missionary efforts. In Japan, in 1573, a baron who adopted the Christian faith proclaimed banishment for those who would not accept the religion. Following the arrival of Jesuits, approximately twenty thousand persons were baptized within seven months, including the bonzes of about sixty monasteries.
