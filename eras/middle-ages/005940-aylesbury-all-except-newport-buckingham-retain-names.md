# Aylesbury

Aylesbury is a market town located in Buckinghamshire, England, situated approximately 38 miles northwest by west of London. It lies on a slight eminence within a fertile region known as the Vale of Aylesbury, which extends northward from the base of the Chiltern Hills. As of 1901, the population of its urban district was 9,243.

## Administration and Governance
While Buckingham serves as the county town, Aylesbury is the assize town for Buckinghamshire. The county's shire court was historically held at Aylesbury. In terms of parliamentary representation, the town is part of the Mid or Aylesbury division, which returns one member to parliament.

Historically, the town is associated with the "three hundreds of Aylesbury." This designation persists from the Domesday survey, where the eighteen original hundreds of the shire were eventually reduced to eight. Aylesbury's hundred was formed by the union of three ancient hundreds.

## Geography and Geology
Aylesbury is situated in a vale characterized by specific geological formations. The area contains Oxford Clay and Kimmeridge Clay, along with Gault. The Portland beds, which are sandy in their lower portions and calcareous above, outcrop from south-west to north-east through Aylesbury; these beds are utilized for fossils and as a source of building stone.

## Infrastructure and Communications
Aylesbury is served by several railway systems. The Great Central, Metropolitan, and Great Western railways all utilize a common station in the town. Additionally, the town is served by a branch of the London & North-Western railway, which connects Aylesbury to Cheddington. 

Water transport is provided via a branch of the Grand Junction Canal.

## Town Features and Economy
The town's layout consists primarily of picturesque, narrow, and irregular streets. The principal public buildings are the town-hall and the county-hall, both of which overlook a broad market-place. Education in the town includes a grammar school founded in 1611.

The church of St Mary is a large cruciform building. While primarily Early English in style, it features additions from later periods, including 15th-century chancel stalls and an ornate clock-turret from the second half of the 17th century. The church contains a transitional Norman font and a small crypt that dates from the pre-Norman period.

Economically, Aylesbury maintains a large agricultural trade and is particularly noted for rearing ducks. Other local industries include printing works, the manufacture of condensed milk, and straw-plaiting.

## History
During the English Civil War, Aylesbury served as a location for an important garrison for the parliament side, as the county was among the first to join an association for mutual defence.

In the vicinity of the town is the Jacobean mansion of Hartwell. Between 1810 and 1814, this residence served as the home of the French king Louis XVIII during his period of exile.
