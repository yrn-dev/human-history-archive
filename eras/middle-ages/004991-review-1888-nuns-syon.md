# Review, 1888, "The Nuns of Syon"

## Overview
"The Nuns of Syon" is an article written by A. Hamilton and published in the *Dublin Review* in 1888. It serves as a reference regarding the Bridgittines, an order of Augustinian canonesses.

## The Bridgittine Order
The Bridgittines were founded around 1350 by St Bridget of Sweden and received approval from Urban V in 1370. Originally established as a "double order," the structure of the community involved a convent with a small attached community of canons who served as chaplains; however, the government of the community remained under the authority of the abbess.

The order saw significant growth in Norway and Sweden, where it played a notable role in the promotion of literature and culture in Scandinavia. This influence is evidenced by the head house at Vastein, located by Lake Vetter, which continued to exist until its suppression in 1595. In total, there were 80 houses of the order across various lands. While the order once included canons, it is now composed exclusively of women, with approximately six Bridgittine convents remaining on the Continent.

## The Convent of Syon
In England, the Bridgittine convent of Syon was established at Isleworth, Middlesex, in 1415. Founded and endowed royally by Henry V, the nunnery became one of the most influential, fashionable, and wealthiest in the country.

Following the Reformation, the community faced significant displacement. During the reign of Mary, Syon was among the few religious houses to be restored, with nearly twenty members of the old community being re-established. However, upon the accession of Elizabeth, the nuns migrated to the Low Countries. After experiencing many vicissitudes, they moved to Rouen and eventually settled in Lisbon in 1594.

## Return to England and Modern Status
The community remained in Lisbon until 1861, during which time they continued to recruit new members from England. Upon their return to England in 1861, Syon House was established at Chudleigh in Devon. This community is distinguished as the only English community that can claim an unbroken conventual existence since the pre-Reformation era.
