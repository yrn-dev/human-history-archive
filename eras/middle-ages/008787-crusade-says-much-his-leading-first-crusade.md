# The Crusades

The Crusades were a series of military expeditions launched by Western Christendom, primarily driven by the Latin Church, to secure the Holy Land and Jerusalem. While the First Crusade was characterized by a profound wave of unifying emotion and zeal, subsequent expeditions became more established processes, eventually losing their vital nature and becoming routine events.

## The First Crusade
The First Crusade began in 1097 with organized forces arriving from Sicily, Southern Italy, England, Normandy, Flanders, and France. The Normans provided the primary will and power for the campaign. Bohemund I, the prince of Otranto and son of Robert Guiscard, is regarded as the leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch. Bohemund, who had previously fought against the East Roman Empire, led a fine Norman army across the Adriatic and did homage to Emperor Alexius Comnenus upon arriving in Constantinople in April 1097.

The crusading forces crossed the Bosphorus and captured Nicæa, though Emperor Alexius seized the city before it could be looted. Following a route similar to that of Alexander the Great, the forces passed through the Cilician Gates and the battle-fields of the Issus, leaving the Turks in Konia unconquered. After a siege lasting nearly a year, they captured Antioch and defeated a relieving army from Mosul. A smaller force, led by Godfrey of Bouillon of Belgium, proceeded to Jerusalem. The city was captured on July 15 after a siege of slightly more than a month, an event marked by terrible slaughter.

## Establishment of Latin Principalities
Following the First Crusade, Latin princes established several small principalities in Syria, including Edessa, acting as a buffer between the Turks and the Greeks. However, the triumph of the Latins created tension with the Greek patriarch of Jerusalem, who found himself in a worse position under the Latins than he had under the Turks. This highlighted a growing struggle between the Latin and Byzantine systems. In 1101, reinforcements arrived with the help of the mercantile fleets of Genoa and Venice, extending the power of the kingdom of Jerusalem.

## Subsequent Crusades and Counter-Crusades
The Second Crusade (1147) was provoked by the fall of Edessa to the Moslems in 1144. This expedition, involving King Louis of France and Emperor Conrad III, was more stately but less successful and enthusiastic than the first. While it failed to recover Edessa, it saved Antioch. During this period, some divisions diverted their efforts; Germans subjugated the pagan Wends east of the Elbe, and Flemish and English contingents captured Lisbon and founded the kingdom of Portugal, both of which the Pope recognized as crusading.

In 1169, a Kurdish adventurer named Saladin became the ruler of Egypt. Saladin reunited the efforts of Baghdad and Egypt and preached a Jehad (Holy War) as a counter-crusade. This movement excited a level of feeling in Islam comparable to that of the First Crusade in Christendom. In 1187, Saladin retook Jerusalem, provoking the Third Crusade (1189). Planned jointly by the King of England (Richard I), the King of France, and Emperor Frederick I (Barbarossa), the Third Crusade was characterized by knightly gallantry and romance, with the papacy playing a secondary role. Although it saved Antioch for a time, it failed to retake Jerusalem, though Christians kept the Palestinian seacoast.

Later expeditions included the Seventh Crusade, led by King Louis IX of France (St. Louis), who was taken prisoner in Egypt and ransomed in 1250. Louis IX also led an expedition to Tunis, where he died of fever. Jerusalem was lost to the Christians again in 1244 after the Sultan of Egypt took the city following a failed Christian intrigue. Jerusalem did not return to non-Moslem control until 1918, when it fell to a mixed force of British, French, and Indian troops.

## Impact on European Society
The Crusades contributed to significant changes in the European military system during the 13th and 14th centuries. The mobilization of vast armies of volunteers demonstrated that pay, the prospect of plunder, and a love of enterprise were as effective for enlisting and maintaining troops as traditional feudal obligations of land tenure and homage. This created a class of seasoned, experienced soldiers who were often landless and available for hire. Additionally, the church placed the profession of arms under its protection, giving the initiation into knighthood a religious character and making knighthood independent of feudalism.
