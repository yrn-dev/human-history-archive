# BOLT

The word "bolt" is an Old English term that shares a relationship with the German word *Bolz*, meaning an arrow. In its primary sense, it refers to a "quarrel" or a shaft used in a cross-bow.

## Linguistic Extensions and Idioms
The concept of the swift flight associated with an arrow has led to several verbal and idiomatic applications of the word. The verb "to bolt" is applied to horses and similar animals to describe sudden flight. Additionally, the expression "bolt upright" is used to mean straight upright. In American usage, "bolt" has been adopted to describe the act of refusing to support a candidate who has been nominated by one's own political party.

## Mechanical and Material Applications
Beyond weaponry, "bolt" refers to a pin used for fastening a door. This sense of a straight pin for fastening has expanded to encompass various other types of appliances. 

From the concept of "fastening together," the term has been applied to textiles. In this context, a "bolt" refers to a definite length of fabric in a roll, such as 40 feet of canvas.

## Sifting and Discussion
A separate derivation of the word comes from "bolt" or "boult," meaning to sift. This usage originates from the Medieval Latin *buretare* or *buletare*, passing through the Old French *buleter*. 

This meaning appears in both literary and figurative contexts:
* **Literary:** In Shakespeare's *Winter's Tale*, the phrase "The fann'd snow, That's bolted by the northern blasts twice o'er" utilizes the term in the sense of sifting.
* **Figurative:** Burke used the term to describe a report that was "examined and sifted and bolted to the bran."

Furthermore, the sense of sifting evolved into a meaning related to mooting or discussing. An example of this is found in Milton's *Comus*, where the text mentions, "I hate when vice can bolt her arguments."
