# Breviary Offices

The Breviary (from the Latin *breviarium*, meaning epitome or abridgment) is the book containing the offices for the canonical hours, which constitute the daily service of the Roman Catholic Church. It is designed for use by every member of a religious order and every clerk in orders, who must either publicly join in or privately read aloud the services allotted for each day.

## Evolution and Development
In the early days of Christian worship, following Jewish custom, the Bible provided all necessary materials, including the psalms and lessons. The evolution of the Breviary began with the separation of the Psalter into a choir-book. Initially, the leader of the choir or the bishop chose psalms as appropriate. By the 4th century, psalms began to be grouped together, a process accelerated by monastic practices of reciting all 150 psalms daily. To manage the time required, monks spread this recitation over a week, dividing each day into hours. In the 6th century, St Benedict established such an arrangement, likely based on an older, less skillful Roman division.

Over time, these choir-books were supplemented with collects (short prayers), responses, and antiphons to assist those unable to improvise metrical compositions. By the 12th century, liturgical author Jean Beleth listed several necessary books for the canonical office, including the *Antiphonarium*, the *Psalterium*, the *Collectarium*, the *Passionarius* (for martyrs), the *Legendarius* (for saints), the *Homiliarius* (Gospel homilies), the *Sermologus* (sermons), and the works of the Fathers, alongside the Old and New Testaments.

## Structure and Contents of the Roman Breviary
The Roman Breviary typically begins with introductory matter, including the calendar, general rubrics, and tables for determining the date of Easter. While the Sarum or Salisbury Breviary—printed as early as 1483 in Venice—never exceeded two parts, modern Breviaries are generally printed in four volumes corresponding to the seasons: winter, spring, summer, and autumn.

Each seasonal part comprises five main sections:
1. **The Psalter**: The collection of psalms.
2. **Proprium de Tempore**: The special office of the season.
3. **Proprium Sanctorum**: Liturgical formularies, psalms, and lessons for saints' festivals. This section consists largely of hagiological biography and has historically encroached upon the *Proprium de Tempore* because approximately 90% of the days in the year have been allotted to saints.
4. **Commune Sanctorum**: General offices for twelve groups of saints (such as virgins, confessors, martyrs, and apostles). These include patristic homilies on the Gospels read at the third nocturn.
5. **Extra Services**: This includes the Office of the Dead (obligatory on All Souls' Day), the Little Office of the Blessed Virgin Mary, and offices specific to a particular diocese.

The Breviary includes lessons drawn from the Bible, the Fathers of the Church, and the Acts of the Saints. In the primitive church, these included works later excluded from the canon, such as the *Shepherd of Hermas* and the letters of Clement of Rome. Lessons are read at Matins, which is divided into three nocturns, while "little chapters" (short lessons) and "versicles" (short responsories) are used during other hours.

## Administration and Revision
Until the council of Trent, bishops had full power to regulate the Breviary of their own diocese, and monastic communities maintained their own versions. However, Pope Pius V (1566–1572) made the Roman Breviary obligatory everywhere, except for those that could prove 200 years of existence.

The Roman Breviary has undergone various revisions:
* **1536**: Francis Quignonez produced a remarkable revision. While not accepted by Rome, it served as the model for the Church of England's 1549 reform. The English Prayer-Book's morning and evening services are simplified condensations of Breviary offices, and some of its prefaces are free translations of Quignonez's work.
* **1588**: Sixtus V introduced the revised Vulgate text.
* **1602**: Clement VIII revised the rubrics through Bellarmine and Baronius.
* **1623–1644**: Urban VIII altered the text of the hymns.

In France during the 17th and 18th centuries, a revision movement led by Jean de Launoy and Louis Sébastien le Nain de Tillemont sought to remove false lives of saints. Influenced by the Port Royal school, these reformed Breviaries (such as those by Archbishops Harlay and de Vintimille) required antiphons and responses to be exact words of Scripture. These were eventually suppressed by an Ultramontane movement during the pontificate of Pius IX, with the diocese of Orleans being the last to surrender in 1875.

## Practice and Usage
The Breviary excludes the Eucharistic office (found in the Missal) and special offices for marriage, baptism, or ordination (found in the Ritual or Pontifical). For the clergy, the daily requirement can take between four to eight hours in choir, depending on the number of high masses and music. Services are typically grouped: Matins and Lauds in the early morning; Prime, Terce, Sext, and None mid-morning; and Vespers and Compline in the afternoon.

To assist in the complex task of harmonizing the *Proprium de Tempore* and *Proprium Sanctorum*, clergy use the *Ordo Recitandi Divini Officii*, a guide providing minute daily directions for reading.
