# THE CITY OF CAMADI AND ITS RUINS

The city of Camadi is described in the itineraries of Marco Polo as a location situated at the beginning of a vast plain known as Reobarles. Once regarded as a great and noble place, by the time of Polo's accounts, the city had become of little consequence, having been ravaged several times by Tartar incursions.

## Geography and Environment
Camadi is located in a very hot region. The surrounding province of Reobarles is characterized by a warmer climate than the mountain passes preceding it. This area is noted for its abundance of tropical fruits, including dates, pistachioes, and "apples of Paradise," as well as other fruits not found in colder climates. 

The local wildlife includes a specific type of francolin bird, distinguished from those in other countries by its black and white coloration and its vermilion-colored beak and feet. The region also hosts vast numbers of turtledoves, which are attracted by the fruit; however, these birds are not taken by the Saracens, who hold them in abomination.

## Identification and Archaeological Sites
The exact location of Camadi has been a subject of study for various explorers and historians, with several theories regarding its modern identity:

*   **The City of Dakianus:** Some sources identify the site as the City of Dakianus, located on an elevated part of the plain on the right bank of the Hali Rúd. This site is described as being thickly strewn with shreds of pottery, glass, and kiln-baked bricks. Local tradition suggests the city was destroyed by a flood long before the birth of Mahomed. According to the journal of Mr. Abbott, the distance from Bamm to the City of Dakianus is approximately 66 miles.
*   **Qumādīn:** General Houtum-Schindler, citing a memoir by Dr. Houtsma, concludes that Camadi can be identified with Qumādīn (or Qamādīn), a suburb of the city of Jíruft.
*   **Sher-i-Jiruft:** Major P. Molesworth Sykes describes exploring the site of Camadi and finding a huge area littered with yellow bricks measuring eight inches square, noting that no broken walls remained to mark the site of what was formerly a great city known as Sher-i-Jiruft.
*   **Other Theories:** Major Smith suggests the city may be a ruined city in the plain of Jíruft near Kerimabad, though he notes the name of that city is apparently lost.

## Historical Context and Route
Historically, the city suffered from instability; Major Sykes notes that Camadi was sacked for the first time following the death of Toghrul Shah of Kermán, during a period of anarchy caused by his four sons.

In the itinerary from Kermán to Hormuz, Camadi appears after a descent of two days from a mountain pass characterized by extreme cold. The route continues from the plain of Camadi through a second difficult pass descending for 20 miles, eventually leading to a well-watered fruitful plain and finally to Hormuz on the shores of the Gulf.

## Associated Hazards and Landmarks
The region surrounding the route from Camadi is noted for the prevalence of robbers, specifically the Carauna (or Karaunahs) and Nigudarian bands. Their presence is attributed to the proximity of the Mekrán and the Bashakird mountains. 

One specific landmark on the road between Camadi (Shehr-i-Daqíánús) and the Nevergún Pass is Conosalmi. This location is identified by some as the ruined town or village of Kamasal (Kahn-i-asal), meaning "the honey canal," located near Vakílábád and Kahnúj-i-pancheh in Jíruft. The nearby Nevergún Pass is described as very difficult due to the presence of many large blocks of sandstone.
