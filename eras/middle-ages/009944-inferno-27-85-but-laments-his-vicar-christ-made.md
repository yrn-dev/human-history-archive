# Dante's Inferno and its Translations

The *Inferno* is a significant work of literature that has seen numerous translations into various modern languages since the 15th century. The history of its reproduction reflects a long-standing effort to capture the original text's meaning and structure across different linguistic traditions.

## Early Translations
The first complete translation of Dante into a modern language is believed to be the Castilian version produced by Villena in 1428. This was followed in 1429 by Andreu Febrer, who rendered the work into Catalan verse. In 1515, Villegas published a Spanish version of the *Inferno*. The earliest French translation was completed by B. Grangier in 1597.

English attempts to translate the work were initially fragmented. While Chaucer rendered several passages and similar fragments appear in the works of Milton and others, the first effort to translate a considerable portion of the poem was made by Rogers, who completed only the *Inferno* in 1782. The entire poem first appeared in English in 1802 via Henry Boyd, who utilized six-line stanzas.

## The Work of Henry Francis Cary
Henry Francis Cary (1772–1844) is credited with providing the first adequate English rendering of Dante. Born in Gibraltar and educated at Rugby, Sutton Coldfield, Birmingham, and Christ Christ Church, Oxford, Cary was a translator and author who took holy orders in 1797. He served as the vicar of Abbott's Bromley in Staffordshire and later the vicarage of Kingsbury in Warwickshire.

Cary's version of the *Inferno* was published in 1805 alongside the original text. His complete translation of the *Divina Commedia* was not published until 1814. Because publishers initially viewed the project as a risk due to the failure of the *Inferno* publication, Cary published the full work at his own expense. The translation gained recognition after being brought to the attention of Samuel Rogers by Thomas Moore, and following praise from Coleridge and an article by Ugo Foscolo in the *Edinburgh Review*. 

Cary's translation is noted for its accuracy, readableness, and idiomatic vigour, preserving the vividness of the original. However, because he used blank verse, it cannot replicate the stately music and close-woven texture of the original *terza rima*.

## Subsequent Translations
Following Cary's work, several other merit-worthy translations appeared. These include blank verse versions by Pollock (1854) and Longfellow (1867). Others utilized *terza rima*, such as Plumptre (1887) and Haselfoot (1887). Prose versions were produced by C. E. Norton (1891) and H. F. Tozer (1904). Additionally, J. A. Carlyle translated the *Inferno* in 1847. In German, the most highly regarded translations are those by Witte and "Philalethes" (the late King John of Saxony), both of whom employed blank verse.
