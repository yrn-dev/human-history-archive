# Improvised and Contemporary Poetry

The concept of poetry being "improvised and contemporary with its facts" is a dictum attributed to Gaston Paris, a noted authority who discussed this principle in his work *Histoire poetique de Charlemagne*. This perspective suggests that early popular poetry was created spontaneously and coincided temporally with the events it described.

## Application to Border Ballads
When this dictum is applied to specific ballads—such as "Jamie Telfer," "Kinmont Willie," "The Bonny Earl o' Murray," and "Jock o' the Side"—the historical accuracy of the poetry is called into question. Evidence suggests that the poets of these works often possessed limited knowledge of the events they described, and the information they did have was frequently incorrect. 

A comparison between the historical Border ballads (particularly those from 1595–1600) and Bain's *Border Papers* (1894–1896) reveals that facts in the ballads are often distorted or confused. This suggests that these poems were likely composed in a later generation based on erroneous oral traditions. In other instances, such as *The Queen's Marie*, the texts appear to have been defaced by the fantastic interpolations of those reciting them. Even as late as 1750, ballads concerning the sons of Rob Roy remained more or less mythopoeic. Consequently, it is probable that the existing forms of most border ballads date no earlier than the generation of 1603–1633, following the union of the crowns. Furthermore, some reciters may have been influenced by printed broadsheets or "stall copies."

## Epic Poetry and Popular Tradition
The relationship between popular song and the development of the epic is a subject of significant study. In Scandinavia, Russia, and Lithuania, Gaston Paris notes that national songs exist in an intermediate form between contemporary poetry and the epic. True epics are found in France, Britain, Germany, Greece, Persia, and India.

In France, the "chansons de geste" (such as the *Chanson de Roland*, traced to the 10th century) were recited by professional *jongleurs*. These are contrasted with shorter lays, or cantilenes, which were sung in chorus as early as the 7th century. While Leon Gautier initially proposed in 1865 that the chansons de geste were formed by joining "bunches" of these earlier cantilenes, he later abandoned this theory in 1878. He instead argued that epic poets were inspired by the spirit, life, and legendary elements of popular songs rather than materially patching together pre-existent pieces, though he admitted some old poems might have been borrowed directly from tradition.

## The Nature of Popular Composition
There is a noted tendency in popular taste to amalgamate diverse compositions into a single work. According to Fauriel, this disposition persists as long as poetry is transmitted via recitation or chant and remains a "common treasure" for the people. This process of amalgamation ceases once poetry is fixed in books and becomes a matter of individual "poetic property" and personal glory. Fauriel suggests that the *Mahabharat* and the *Shah Nameh* of Ferdusi were likely created through this process of amalgamating originally separate epic poems.

## Other Poetic Histories
Various other "poetic histories" appear across different cultures and eras:
* **India:** Eugène Burnouf published a French translation and Sanskrit text of the *Bhâgavata Purâna ou histoire poétique de Krichna* between 1840 and 1847.
* **France:** Julien Auguste Pélage Brizeux, a French poet of Irish origin, wrote *Histoires poétiques* (1855), a work crowned by the French Academy.
* **Greece:** In the songs attributed to Homer, the society is reproduced with its "ferocity of its mœurs" and a mixture of good and evil. Guizot describes this as a "tableau idéal" that captures the truth of the world through its variety and incoherence.
