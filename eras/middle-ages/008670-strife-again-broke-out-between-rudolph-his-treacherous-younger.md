# Strife Between Rudolph II and Matthias

The conflict between Rudolph II and his younger brother Matthias was a period of significant political and religious instability within the Habsburg dominions, characterized by familial betrayal and the exploitation of ecclesiastical controversies.

## Origins of the Rupture
The strife between the two princes was rooted in Matthias's ambition to supplant his brother. The formal cause of the break occurred when Rudolph refused to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey. Matthias had previously concluded this treaty while serving as Rudolph's representative in Hungary. Following this refusal, the Hungarians accepted Matthias as their ruler.

## Expansion of the Conflict
The conflict expanded beyond Hungary as Matthias's forces entered Moravia. During this incursion, the estates of Moravia renounced their allegiance to Rudolph, an action led by Charles, lord of Zerotin. Matthias subsequently invaded Bohemia and invited the kingdom's estates to meet him at Caslav.

However, the Bohemians declined Matthias's overtures due to a sudden revolution of feeling. In March 1608, the estates met at Prague; while they submitted demands regarding ecclesiastical matters to Rudolph, they authorized him to levy troops to defend Bohemia.

## Settlement at Liben
Matthias's forces eventually entered Bohemia and reached Liben, a small town near Prague. Likely disappointed by the Bohemians' refusal to join his cause, Matthias reached an understanding with Rudolph on June 25, 1608. Under the terms of this agreement, Rudolph formally ceded the government of Upper and Lower Austria, Moravia, and Hungary to Matthias. Rudolph, however, retained his rights as the king of Bohemia.

## Aftermath and Religious Tension
Despite the settlement, tensions remained high. Rudolph never forgave Matthias's treachery and engaged in secret negotiations with Christian of Anhalt, a leader of the German Protestants, even as Rudolph appeared to be a champion of Catholicism. This vacillation was noted by the Bohemians and the court of Spain.

In January 1609, the estates of Bohemia again brought demands to the king. Rudolph had previously declined to discuss religious matters while Matthias's troops occupied parts of Bohemia. The estates sought the recognition of the *Confessio Bohemica* as a fundamental law of the kingdom and demanded complete religious liberty for all classes. These demands included:
* Equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants (a term then encompassing Lutherans, Utraquists, and Bohemian Brethren) to hold state offices.
* Limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
* Redress for grievances resulting from Rudolph's misrule.

These demands were formulated in a document of twenty-five "Articles." Rudolph considered the document illegal and demanded its destruction. After protracted discussions with twelve negotiators, including Count Henry Matthias Thurn, Rudolph stated on March 31 that he could grant no concessions regarding religious matters.
