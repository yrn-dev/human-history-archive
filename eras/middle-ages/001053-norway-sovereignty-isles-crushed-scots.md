# Norwegian Sovereignty and the Isles

The historical relationship between Norway and the islands west of Scotland is characterized by periods of expansion, territorial acquisition, and eventual conflict leading to the loss of Norwegian sovereignty.

## Early Expansion and Settlement
During the reign of King Harald, a great multitude of people fled Norway due to his seizure of lands. This exodus led to the discovery and peopling of the Farey Isles and Iceland. Northmen also frequently visited Hjaltland (Shetland Isles). Many of those fleeing King Harald engaged in viking cruises in the West sea, spending winters in the Hebrides and the Orkney Islands, while marauding in Norway during the summer months.

King Harald later conducted expeditions into the West sea to combat these vikings. He cleared the Orkney Islands and Hjaltland of vikings and proceeded to the Sudreys (Hebrides), where he fought many battles and remained victorious. His campaigns extended into Scotland itself and as far west as the Isle of Man, though the inhabitants of Man had fled to Scotland before his arrival.

## Territorial Acquisitions under King Magnus
The reach of the Norwegian kings expanded significantly under King Magnus. During one winter spent in the southern isles, Magnus's forces rowed through the fjords of Scotland and took possession of all islands west of Scotland for the king of Norway. 

King Magnus further extended his rule by taking the Isle of Anglesey, which represented the southernmost point reached by the Norwegian kings of former days; Anglesey constituted a third of Wales. Following this victory, Magnus negotiated a peace treaty with the Scottish king, Melkolm. The agreement stipulated that all islands lying west of Scotland—specifically those between the mainland and the islands where a vessel could pass with its rudder shipped—would belong to the king of Norway. To define this boundary, King Magnus drew a skiff over the strand at Cantire and shipped the rudder, thereby appropriating the land on the farboard side.

## The Battle of Largs and the Loss of Sovereignty
The pretensions of Norway to the sovereignty of the Isles persisted until the 13th century. In 1263, the battle of Largs took place. During this encounter, the claims of Haakon of Norway to the sovereignty of the Isles were crushed by the Scots, who were led by Alexander III.

## Related Regional History
In the region of Ayrshire, traces of Roman occupation exist, and the area was held by the Damnonii during the campaigns of Agricola. Following the Roman withdrawal, Ayrshire was part of the kingdom of Strathclyde before passing to the Northumbrian kings. The area's history includes the 9th-century slaying of the Scottish king Alpin at Dalmellington. 

Later conflicts in the region involved William Wallace, who surprised an English garrison at Ardrossan and burned barns in Ayr used by the forces of Edward I. Robert Bruce, allegedly born at Turnberry Castle, defeated the English at Loudoun Hill in 1307. Additionally, the county saw conflict between 1660 and 1688 involving the Covenanters, specifically at Airds Moss, where their leader Richard Cameron was killed on July 20, 1680.

## Antiquities and Remains
Archaeological evidence in the region includes cairns in Sorn and Galston, and a road attributed to the Romans extending from Ayr through Dalmellington and Dalrymple toward the Solway. On the hills of Dundonald and Knockgeorgan, there are camps attributed to the Danes or Norwegians. Other notable remains include the castles of Dunure, Ardrossan, Portencross, Dundonald, Turnberry, and Loch Doon, as well as the ruins of the abbeys at Crossraguel and Kilwinning.
