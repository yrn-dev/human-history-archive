# Northumberland

Northumberland is a region historically associated with the English-Scottish border, the administration of the marches, and various noble titles and families.

## The Marches and Border Administration
The "marches" referred to the debatable ground between England and Scotland. To protect this area, the southern kingdom established arrangements for garrisoning the region, dividing it into three sections: the east, west, and middle marches. Each section was overseen by a warden. Geographically, these marches roughly encompassed a tract of land on the Scottish side of the border along with the modern English counties of Cumberland and Northumberland. This system of protection became unnecessary following the accession of James VI of Scotland to the English throne.

## The Earls of Northumberland and Related Lineages
The title of earl of Northumberland has been held by various figures throughout history. In the 11th century, Cospatrick (or Gospatrick) was appointed earl of Northumberland by William the Conqueror. Cospatrick was the son of Maldred and grandson of Crinan; his mother, Algitha, was the daughter of Ughtred, earl of Northumberland, and Elgiva, daughter of the Saxon king Æthelred. After being deprived of his position, Cospatrick fled to Scotland, where King Malcolm Canmore granted him lands at Dunbar.

His descendants continued as earls, and by approximately 1174, Waltheof, a descendant of Cospatrick, was styled "Comes de Dunbar." His son, Patrick Dunbar (the 5th earl of Dunbar), served as the keeper of Berwick Castle. Later, Patrick, the 7th earl, acted as the guardian of King Alexander III and Queen in 1255 and signed the Treaty of Perth in 1266, through which Magnus VI of Norway ceded the Hebrides and the Isle of Man to Scotland.

Other notable figures associated with the region include the house of Grey of Howick, one of Northumberland's most considerable territorial families. Charles Grey, 2nd Earl (1764–1845), was the son of General Sir Charles Grey (1st Earl Grey), who served in the Seven Years' War and against colonists in New Jersey and Pennsylvania.

## Political and Legal History
Northumberland has been the site of significant political upheaval and legal executions. In 1076, Waltheof, earl of Northumberland, became the first person to be beheaded in England, a practice said to have been introduced from Normandy by William the Conqueror. Later, the duke of Northumberland was among the high-ranking personages executed by the axe in 1553.

In the 17th century, the earl of Northumberland provided care for George Villiers, 2nd Duke of Buckingham, and his brother Francis. Under the earl's care, the two brothers lived in Rome and Florence while traveling abroad.

## The Earls of Derwentwater
The earls of Derwentwater held forfeited estates in Cumberland and Northumberland. The 3rd earl, James, and his brother Charles both faced condemnation for high treason following events at Preston in November 1715. While James was executed, Charles escaped to the Continent, where he married Charlotte Maria, countess of Newburgh. Charles was eventually captured in 1745 and beheaded on December 8, 1746. 

Following the death of John Radclyffe in 1731, the title was claimed by various heirs. In 1865, a woman appeared in Northumberland claiming to be the granddaughter of the 4th earl and the owner of the estates, asserting that the 4th earl had not died in 1731 but had settled in Germany. She was eventually removed from Dilston Hall by force.

## Early Conflicts and Northmen
In the 10th century, Northumberland was a focal point for conflicts involving Northmen. In A.D. 941, after a battle in which King Eirik and several other kings fell, surviving Northmen escaped to Northumberland to bring the news to Gunhild and her sons. This followed tensions with King Jatmund of England, who had reportedly intended to appoint a different chief over Northumberland.
