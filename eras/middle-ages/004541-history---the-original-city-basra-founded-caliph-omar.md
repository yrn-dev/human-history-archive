# Basra and the Caliphate of Omar

## The Founding and Evolution of Basra
The original city of Basra was founded in A.D. 636 by the caliph Omar. Its initial location was situated on an ancient canal that is now dry, positioned on the edge of a pebbly and stony Arabian plateau approximately 8 miles southwest of the site of the modern city. During its period of prosperity, Basra was a city of significant size and wealth, rivaling Wasit and Kufa. Its fame is preserved in the tales of the *Arabian Nights*.

The city's importance began to decline alongside the waning power of the Abbasid caliphate. This decay was marked by the neglect of the canals and the severance of communication with the Persian Gulf, which eventually led to the total abandonment of the original site. The location of the ancient city is currently marked by ruin mounds near the modern town of Zobeir, which serves as a health suburb for the villas of Basra's wealthy residents.

## Later History and Conquests
The present city of Basra was conquered by the Turks in 1668, after which it became a frequent site of revolutions. In 1777, the Persians, led by Sadik Khan, captured the city following a siege that lasted eight months. Within approximately one year, the Turks regained control, though they were subsequently deprived of the city by the sheikh of the Montefik (Montafiq) Arabs. Control was recovered by Suleiman Pasha in October of that same year after he encountered and defeated the sheikh on the banks of the Euphrates. Since that event, the city has remained under Turkish control.

## The Caliphate of Omar
Omar served as the second caliph, succeeding Abu Bekr. Described as a man of stark simplicity and righteousness, Omar was a Meccan of good family. Some accounts characterize him as just and humane among a race of tyrants, though also noting his active bigotry. It is recorded that Muhammad once stated that if a prophet could arise after himself, it would be Omar.

Omar's leadership was marked by significant territorial expansions and administrative actions:
* **The Hegira:** Omar instituted the Hegira in imitation of the Christian era of the martyrs.
* **Jerusalem:** In A.D. 638, Jerusalem surrendered to Omar. The city had stipulated that it would only surrender to the caliph in person. Omar traveled the six-hundred-mile journey from Medina on a camel with only one attendant, carrying simple provisions such as a waterskin, a wooden platter, and bags of dates and barley. Upon arrival, he expressed rage at the sight of his captains, who were robed in silks and riding richly caparisoned horses, viewing such finery as an insult to the desert warrior tradition.
* **Egypt:** The invasion of Egypt was led by Amrou. While Omar initially had doubts regarding the enterprise due to the greatness of Egypt—noting the strength of its architecture and the barrier of the Nile—he sent an ambiguous mandate to Amrou. The mandate instructed Amrou to retreat if he were still in Syria, but to advance with confidence if he had already reached the Egyptian frontiers. Amrou proceeded to take possession of Farmah (Pelusium) after a thirty-day siege. Under Omar's authority, Amrou later besieged the fortress of Babylon for most of a year, with the garrison surrendering in April A.D. 641.

## Regional Administration and Related Cities
Following the battle of Kadesiya in A.D. 638, the Arabs founded the city of Kufa. Along with Basra, Kufa served as one of the two capitals of the new territory of Irak, with the country divided into districts known as *sawads*. Kufa later became the residence and capital of the caliph 'Ali. However, like Basra, Kufa declined after the capital was moved to Bagdad in the middle of the following century. By the 19th century, the site consisted of extensive ruins, which were later used as brick quarries for the building of Nejef.
