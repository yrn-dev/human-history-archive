# Fra Bartolommeo

Fra Bartolommeo (1475–1517), also known as Baccio (a shortened form of Bartolommeo) or Bella Porta due to his residence near the Porta Romana, was an Italian portrait and historical painter. Born in Soffignano, near Florence, he died in the same city in 1517.

## Artistic Education and Early Works
Bartolommeo received his initial artistic training from Cosimo Roselli. Following this period, he dedicated himself to studying the great works of Leonardo da Vinci. His early productions are characterized by their beauty and grace; among these, the most significant is the fresco of the Last Judgment, a project on which he was assisted by his friend Mariotto Albertinelli.

## Influence of Savonarola and Religious Life
While working on pieces for the convent of the Dominican friars, Bartolommeo became acquainted with Savonarola. Savonarola exerted a great influence over him, and Bartolommeo was so deeply affected by Savonarola's cruel death that he entered the convent. For several years following this event, he abandoned his art.

## Relationship with Raphael
Bartolommeo resumed his painting in obedience to his superior. Around this time, Raphael arrived in Florence, and the two artists formed a close friendship. This relationship resulted in a mutual exchange of artistic knowledge: Bartolommeo learned the rules of perspective from Raphael, who was highly skilled in the discipline. In return, Raphael's own handling of drapery and colouring improved, a change that became noticeable in the works Raphael produced after their meeting.

## Later Career and Style
Some years later, Bartolommeo visited Rome. Upon contemplating the masterpieces of Raphael and Michelangelo, he was struck by a feeling of his own inferiority. Despite this, he maintained a very friendly relationship with Raphael. When Bartolommeo departed from Rome, he left two unfinished paintings in Raphael's care, which Raphael subsequently completed.

Bartolommeo's figures were generally draped and small, qualities that some critics alleged were defects. To demonstrate that his style was not caused by a lack of power, he produced two specific works: the undraped figure of St Sebastian and the magnificent figure of St Mark, the latter of which is located in Florence and considered his masterpiece. The painting of St Sebastian was noted for its beautiful and natural colouring, its strong expression of agony and suffering, and its design; it was eventually removed from the chapel of a convent where it had been exhibited.
