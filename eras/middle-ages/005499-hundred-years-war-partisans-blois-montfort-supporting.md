# The War of Succession in Brittany

The war of succession in Brittany was a conflict fought for the possession of the duchy of Brittany between the houses of Blois and Montfort. This struggle began in 1341 following the death of John III, who died without a direct heir. While centered on the ducal power of Brittany, the conflict was in reality an incident of the broader Hundred Years' War.

## Political Alignments and Foreign Support
The war was characterized by the involvement of the two primary powers of the era, as the opposing Breton houses sought external support. The partisans of the house of Blois supported the kings of France, while the partisans of the house of Montfort supported the kings of England.

## Key Figures and Military Actions
The conflict attracted various soldiers and adventurers. Bertrand Du Guesclin, born at the castle of La Motte-Broons, first gained recognition as a soldier in 1338 at a tournament in Rennes celebrating the marriage of Jeanne de Penthievre and Charles of Blois. During the war of succession, Du Guesclin served as a soldier starting in 1341, leading a band of adventurers on the side of France and Charles of Blois. He notably distinguished himself during the siege of Vannes in 1342. Later, in 1356, Du Guesclin defended Rennes against the duke of Lancaster until the siege was raised in June 1357.

On the English side, Sir Robert Knolles, a soldier from a Cheshire family, served in Brittany during this period. He was among the English prisoners taken by the French in March 1351 following the "combat of the thirty," though he was released shortly thereafter. Knolles took advantage of the "savage civil war" and the distracted state of Brittany to acquire wealth and fame.

## Resolution of the Conflict
The struggle for the duchy continued for over two decades. A pivotal moment occurred in September 1364 at the fight at Auray. During this engagement, Charles of Blois was slain and Bertrand Du Guesclin was captured by Sir John Chandos. Sir Robert Knolles also gained significant fame for his conduct during this battle.

Following these events, John of Montfort (who died in 1399) was recognized as the duke of Brittany under the style of John IV.

## Aftermath and Ducal Authority
The reign of John IV was frequently troubled, specifically by his struggle with Olivier de Clisson (1336–1407). However, the subsequent reign of John V (who died in 1442) was marked by a pacific and able policy. Under John V and his successors—Arthur III, Peter II, and Francis I—ducal authority grew remarkably. The dukes established a standing army and implemented the levy of hearth taxes, known as *fouages*, across Brittany.

Later, Francis II (1435–1488) engaged in conflicts against Louis XI during the War of the Public Weal and against Charles VIII in "The Mad War" (*La Guerre Folle*). Following the death of Francis II, the king of France invaded Brittany and forced Francis's daughter, Anne of Brittany, to marry him in 1491. This event prepared the way for the reunion of France and Brittany. After the death of Charles VIII, Anne married Louis XII. The definitive annexation of the duchy was settled in 1532 through a contract involving Francis I and Claude (the daughter of Anne and Louis XII), which guaranteed the maintenance of Brittany's liberties and privileges.
