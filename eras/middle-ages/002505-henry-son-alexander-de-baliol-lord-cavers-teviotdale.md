# The Baliol Family

The Baliol family was a Norman lineage that played a significant role in the history of Scotland and England. The family was founded in England by Guy (or Guido) de Baliol, a Norman baron who held the fiefs of Vinoy, Harcourt, Dampierre, and Bailleul in Normandy. After arriving in England with William the Conqueror, Guy received lands in the north of England from William II.

## Early History and Barnard Castle
A descendant of Guy—either his son or grandson—named Bernard (or Barnard) de Baliol, established a fortress in Durham known as Castle Bernard. A town grew around the walls of this fortress, becoming Barnard Castle. Around the middle of the 12th century, Bernard granted a charter to the town's inhabitants, making them burgesses with privileges identical to those of the town of Richmond in Yorkshire. 

The elder Bernard was active during the civil war, fighting for King Stephen; he was present at the battle of the Standard in August 1138 and was captured at the battle of Lincoln in February 1141. He was likely succeeded by a son, also named Bernard, who was among the northern barons that took King William the Lion of Scotland prisoner in July 1174 and confirmed the burgess privileges of Barnard Castle. This younger Bernard was succeeded by his son Eustace, and subsequently by Eustace's son Hugh, who took over around 1215.

## John de Baliol and the Oxford College
Hugh's son and successor, John de Baliol, expanded the family's wealth and status through his marriage to Dervorguila, the daughter of Alan, earl of Galloway. He is said to have held one half of the lands in Galloway and thirty knights' fees in England. While he served as one of the regents of Scotland during the minority of Alexander III, he was deprived of this office and had his lands forfeited for treason in 1255. He later fought for Henry III against Simon de Montfort and was captured at the battle of Lewes in 1264. In 1263, he established several scholarships at Oxford; following his death in 1269, his widow founded a college in the family's name.

## John de Baliol, King of Scotland
John de Baliol (1249–1315), the son of the aforementioned John and Dervorguila, became the head of the family in 1278 after the death of his elder brother. He rose to prominence in 1290 when the Scottish throne became vacant following the death of Margaret, the "maid of Norway." Baliol claimed the crown through his maternal grandmother, Margaret, the eldest daughter of David, earl of Huntingdon. His primary rival was Robert Bruce, earl of Annandale.

King Edward I of England was invited to settle the dispute. After demanding and receiving a formal recognition of his position as overlord of Scotland from the Scottish nobles and the competitors, Edward appointed a court of twenty-four Englishmen and eighty Scotsmen to decide the matter. Based on the principles of feudal law for an indivisible inheritance, the court decided in Baliol's favor. Baliol swore fealty to Edward I and was crowned king of Scotland at Scone on November 30, 1292. However, relations between Baliol and Edward I eventually deteriorated. In 1296, John Baliol forfeited his English estates, including Barnard Castle and the lordship of Gainford.

## The Cadet Branch and the Lords of Cavers
A cadet branch of the family descended from Ingelram (or Engelram), a son of the younger Bernard de Baliol. Ingelram married the heiress of William de Berkeley, lord of Reidcastle and chamberlain of Scotland. Their son, Henry, became chamberlain around 1223. Henry married Lauretta, daughter of Philip de Valoines, lord of Panmure, and inherited part of the Valoines family's English fiefs in 1234. Henry, who accompanied Henry III to France in 1242 and sided with English barons against John in 1215, died in 1246.

Henry's son was likely Alexander de Baliol, lord of Cavers in Teviotdale and chamberlain of Scotland. First mentioned as chamberlain in 1287, Alexander was involved in negotiations between Scottish nobles and Edward I, leading to the treaty of Brigham in 1290 and the treaty of Salisbury in 1289. Around 1296, he was likely deprived of his office and may have been imprisoned alongside his kinsman, King John de Baliol. He later fought for Edward in Scotland and was summoned to several English parliaments. Alexander married Isabella de Chilham, through whom he acquired lands in Kent. He died around 1309. He was succeeded by a son, Alexander, and then a grandson, Thomas. In 1368, Thomas sold the estate of Cavers to William, earl of Douglas; Thomas is the final member of the Baliol family mentioned in Scottish records.
