# Bamburgh and the Saxon Chronicles

## Bamburgh Castle
Bamburgh, also known as Bamborough, is a village located in Northumberland, England, situated on the sea-coast. It is positioned 54 miles north of Newcastle and 2.5 miles east of Belford station on the North Eastern railway. Historically, it served as a royal borough prior to the Norman Conquest and returned two members to parliament during the reign of Edward I.

The village is home to an ancient castle situated on a magnificent site atop an almost perpendicular rock that reaches 150 feet in height. This rock is accessible only from the south-east side. According to the Saxon chronicles, the first erection of the castle is ascribed to King Ida of Northumberland. While the initial construction is linked to the Saxon period, the existing castle buildings date from the Norman period and are characterized by their strength and dignity. These structures include a massive keep and the remains of an apsidal chapel dedicated to St Peter.

## Local Landmarks and Figures
The village contains a church dedicated to St Aidan, who served as the bishop of Lindisfarne (or Holy Island) around 634. The church is a fine cruciform building primarily of Early English date, featuring a crypt located beneath the chancel.

Within the churchyard is a monument to Grace Darling (1815-1842). Darling is remembered as the brave rescuer of several crew members from the ship "Forfarshire" in 1838. Her father served as the keeper of the Longstone Lighthouse, which is situated on an outer rock of the Farne Islands. These islands extend for 6 miles north-eastward from the coast at Bamburgh.

## The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle
The mention of King Ida in relation to Bamburgh is found within the tradition of the Saxon chronicles. The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* (also referred to as the *O.E. Chron.*) is a significant literary remain of the Old English period. It is cited as an authority for various historical events and figures, including the birth date of Dunstan and the early history of the kingdom of Kent.

In the records of the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, the fyrd—the English militia or army—is first mentioned under the date 605. The chronicle also provides lists of kings; for example, it records that Hengest reigned from 455 to 488 in Kent, followed by his son Aesc (Oisc) until 512, though these specific dates are noted as having little value.
