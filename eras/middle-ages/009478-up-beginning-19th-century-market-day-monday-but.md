# Market Days and Trade Evolution in English Towns

The historical development of markets and fairs in various English towns reflects a transition from early royal and ecclesiastical charters to the influence of industrialization and changing commercial customs.

## Market Transitions in Bolton and Bideford
In Bolton, the market day was traditionally Monday up until the beginning of the 19th century. Over time, this old chartered market was gradually superseded by a customary Saturday market. The town's market history dates back to 1251, when William de Ferrers obtained a crown charter for a yearly fair and a weekly market. While the annual fair was eventually replaced by four others—primarily focused on cattle and horses—the New Year and Whitsuntide Show fairs did not emerge until the 19th century.

Bideford also maintained a Monday market, as established in an undated charter from Richard de Grenville (likely during the reign of Edward I), which confirmed a previous charter from his grandfather. This was further confirmed in 1271, along with a five-day yearly fair held on the feast of St Margaret (July 20th). In 1573, a charter granted by Elizabeth created Bideford as a free borough corporate and introduced a Tuesday market, which continues to be held. This 1573 charter also granted three annual fairs in November, July, and February, though these have since been discontinued.

## Ecclesiastical and Prescriptive Markets
In Durham, market and fair rights are claimed under a 1179 charter from Bishop Pudsey, which was confirmed in 1565. This charter established a weekly market on Saturday and three yearly fairs of two days each, held on Whit Monday and the feasts of St Cuthbert in March and September. In 1610, the bishop of Durham recovered these rights through a suit in chancery, subsequently leasing them to the corporation for £20 yearly until the corporation purchased them from the Ecclesiastical Commissioners in 1860.

Darlington's markets and fairs were formerly held by the bishop and existed as early as the 11th century. By the time of Leland, Darlington was considered the best market town in the bishopric, with the exception of Durham. However, in 1664, the bishop noted that inhabitants had established a market during a season of the year that was unaccustomed.

## Other Regional Market Customs
In Bicester, a Friday market is mentioned in the 14th century, and Richard II later granted a Monday market. In 1440, an additional market was granted for the area of the town known as Bury-End, which became known as Market-End. Bicester's markets and fairs were well-frequented, particularly the cattle market, which gained fame in the 16th century.

Ilchester's Wednesday market dates from before the Conquest. The town also held a fair on August 29th under a 1203 charter, and several other fairs under a 1289 charter. One of these, held on the Monday before Palm Sunday (fixed as March 25th), was still observed at the end of the 18th century, though it no longer exists.

In Grimsby, no early grant of a market has been found, but records indicate the market day was Wednesday in 1792; by 1888, the market had ceased to exist.

## Commercial Development and Decline
The economic focus of these towns often shifted alongside their market activities. Bideford experienced significant commercial growth in the 16th century due to Sir Richard Grenville, maintaining trade with the Mediterranean, Spain, and America, specifically the import of tobacco from Virginia and Maryland. This declined in the 18th century, shifting toward a coasting trade in coal and timber with Ireland and Wales. Bideford's 17th-century silk industry is now extinct.

Similarly, Ilchester's manufacture of thread lace was replaced by silk weaving around 1750, but this industry eventually decayed. The town's role as a center of county administration ended in the 19th century as rail travel replaced road travel, leading to its decay and the rise of Taunton.

In Durham, attempts to introduce wool and cloth manufacture in the 18th and 17th centuries failed, though carpet manufacture began in 1814. Darlington was noted for the manufacture of flax, worsted, and linen before the 19th century. Its modern importance grew following the opening of the railway between Stockton and Darlington on September 27, 1825.
