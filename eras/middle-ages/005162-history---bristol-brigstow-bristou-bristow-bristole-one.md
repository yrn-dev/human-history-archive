# Bristol

Bristol (historically referred to as Brigstow, Bristou, Bristow, or Bristole) is an English seaport and city located primarily in Gloucestershire, with portions in Somersetshire, situated approximately 118½ miles west of London. The city is positioned in a basin-like hollow roughly 4 miles in diameter, where the river Avon—which forms the boundary between Somerset and Gloucestershire—meets the estuary of the Severn. The city's geography is marked by the Clifton Gorge, spanned by the Clifton Suspension Bridge, and it is served by the Great Western and Midland railways, with Temple Meads acting as the central station. As of 1901, the population was 328,945.

## Early History and Development
Bristol's greatness is attributed entirely to trade rather than military, religious, or feudal importance. While located near Roman and British camps, there is no evidence of settlements from those eras. A Saxon settlement began to grow around the year 1000 at the junction of the Avon and Frome rivers, marking the western limit of the Saxon invasion of Britain. Under Danish rule, specifically during the reign of Canute, Bristol became a market for English slaves and initiated a wool trade with Ireland.

By the time of the Domesday survey, Bristol was a royal borough governed by a king-appointed reeve, with a geld assessed at 110 marks. The existence of a mint at the time of the Conquest indicates the town had attained significant size, though it remained a member of the royal manor of Baston. Early fortifications were earthworks constructed by Geoffrey de Coutances, Bishop of Exeter. Following a rebellion against William II, the town and castle were granted to Robert FitzHamon. In 1119, the castle passed to Earl Robert of Gloucester via his marriage to Mabel, FitzHamon's daughter. Earl Robert strengthened the castle with masonry and used it as a base to harry the neighborhood and sell prisoners as slaves to Ireland during a rebellion against Stephen.

## Municipal Evolution and Charters
The transition from manorial to municipal organization was marked by several key charters. In 1172, Henry II granted a charter exempting burgesses from certain tolls and confirming liberties; that same year, the city of Dublin was granted to the men of Bristol as a colony. In 1190, a charter granted by John provided that burgesses could not be impleaded outside the walls, prohibited non-burgesses from selling corn, wool, leather, cloth, or wine in Bristol, and established burgage tenure.

A commune was established on the French model shortly after 1190, and in 1200, Robert FitzNichol became the first mayor. Unlike the reeve, the mayor was elected by the merchants of the merchant gild. The city's administration was further refined by a 1373 charter, which included Redcliffe within the town boundaries, made Bristol a county in itself with an elected sheriff, and established a council of forty. The town was divided into five wards, each with an alderman. A later charter in 1499 by Henry VII altered the corporation's basis by stipulating that aldermen be elected by the mayor and council. A formal charter of incorporation was granted by Charles II in 1664.

## Trade and Industry
Bristol's economy was driven by its connection to Ireland and the Baltic. It exported wool and developed a wine trade with southern France, while tanning and soap-making flourished. The 15th century marked a period of great prosperity, exemplified by William Canyng (1399–1474), a five-time mayor who conducted a massive cloth trade with the Baltic. During this era, cloth was also exported to Spain, France, and the Levant.

The Society of Merchant Venturers, whose records begin in 1467, grew rapidly to direct all foreign trade by 1500. In the 16th century, Bristol expanded its reach to the Canaries, Spain, and Spanish colonies in America, and participated in attempts to colonize Newfoundland. The city also engaged in the African slave trade, which flourished throughout the 17th century. In the 18th century, Joseph Fry (1728–1787) established a successful chocolate manufacturing firm, J.S. Fry & Sons, after purchasing a patent from William Churchman.

## Social and Modern History
Bristol was the center of the Methodist revival led by Wesley and Whitefield. During the 18th and early 19th centuries, the city was characterized by dense population, dirty streets, and violent riots in 1708, 1753, 1767, and 1831. The city was also besieged three times during the Civil War.

The city's prosperity declined at the start of the 19th century due to the rise of Liverpool, the abolition of the slave trade (which damaged the West Indian sugar trade), and high rates charged by the Bristol Dock Company (incorporated 1803). This decline was mitigated by the Bristol chamber of commerce (founded 1823) and the Municipal Reform Act of 1835. The new corporation purchased the docks in 1848 and later acquired the Avonmouth and Portishead docks (built 1877–1880) in 1884. This led to a trade revival and population growth, and in 1899, the chief magistrate became a lord mayor.
