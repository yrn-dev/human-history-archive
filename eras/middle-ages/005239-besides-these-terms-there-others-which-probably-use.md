# Terminology and Classification in Historical Contexts

The use of specific terms to categorize social classes, legal codes, urban centers, and professional roles varies significantly across different historical periods and cultures. These designations often reflect the underlying social structure, legal requirements, or the specific administrative needs of a civilization.

## Social Stratification in Early England
In early England, particularly within the kingdoms of Wessex and Mercia, society was structured into distinct classes defined by their *wergilds* (the value placed on a person's life). Three primary classes were identified by these amounts: the *twelfhynde* (1200 shillings), the *sixhynde* (600 shillings), and the *twihynde* (200 shillings). While similar classes likely existed in Northumbria, they may not have shared these specific names.

Across various kingdoms, including Kent, broader terms were utilized: *gesiðcund* referred to the two higher classes, while *ceorlisc* designated the lowest. However, the social system in Kent differed in character. In Kent, the *ceorlisc* class had a wergild of 100 shillings (composed of twenty silver coins called *sceattas*), and there was only one *gestiðcund* class with a wergild of 300 shillings. Additionally, Kent recognized three classes of *laetas* below the *ceorlisc*—likely corresponding to the *liti* or freedmen of continental laws—with wergilds of 80, 60, and 40 shillings. These *laetas* have no known analogue in other English kingdoms, though Welsh freemen of poorer classes had wergilds ranging from 60 to 120 shillings.

These classifications extended beyond wergilds to influence the value of a person's oath, the fines they were liable for, and the compensations they were entitled to receive for injuries.

## Rural and Dependent Classes in Ancient Greece
In the Dorian towns of the Argolic Aktê, a dependent rural population existed that was analogous to the Helots of Laconia. The terminology for this class varied by location:
*   **Sikyôn:** They were called *Korynêphori* (club men) or *Katônakophori*, the latter referring to a thick woollen mantle with a sheepskin sewn onto the skirt.
*   **Argos:** They were known as *Gymnêsii*, a term derived from their lack of regular arms or military panoply.
*   **Epidaurus:** They were called *Konipodes*, meaning "dusty-footed."

## Legal and Urban Nomenclature
The naming of legal codes and urban centers often evolved over time. The code of the emperor Leo, now known as the *Basilica*, underwent several title changes. It was originally titled *The Revision of the Ancient Laws* ([Greek: he anakatharsis ton palaion nomon]), then *he hexekontabiblos* (referring to its sixty books), and finally, before the end of the 10th century, it became *ho basilikos* or *ta basilika* (elliptical forms of "the Imperial Law" or "the Imperial Constitutions").

Regarding urban centers in Britain, the Romans established twenty-eight cities (*civitates*) and various fortresses (*castetta*). The English applied the term *ceaster* (from the Latin *castra*) or, less frequently, *burg* to both. While *burg* was used for any fortified dwelling-place, the term *urbs* served as a translation for *burg*.

## Professional and Religious Designations
In the Mongol Empire, specific terms identified specialized roles. *Balagachi* was the term for the door-keeper of the Khan's tent, while *Buluguchi* referred to hunters, specifically those who hunted sable. Additionally, in the *Yuen-shi*, falconers were called *Ying fang pu lie*, with a specific class among them termed *Bo-lan-ghi*.

In a religious and philosophical context, the term "atheism" (from the Greek *a-* and *theos*) literally denotes a belief system denying the existence of God. However, the term has been used ambiguously throughout history. It has served as a negative label used by orthodox adherents of a religion or sect to describe any belief that differs from their own. For example, Christians in the Roman Empire were called atheists due to their lack of idolatry, and Spinoza was persecuted as an atheist despite believing that God alone existed.
