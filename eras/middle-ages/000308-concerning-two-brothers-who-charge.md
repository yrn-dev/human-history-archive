# CONCERNING THE TWO BROTHERS WHO HAVE CHARGE OF THE KAAN’S HOUNDS

## Overview of the Masters of the Hounds
Within the administration of the Great Kaan, specific responsibilities were assigned to two brothers who held the charge of the Kaan’s hounds. This role is detailed in the historical accounts of the travels of Marco Polo, specifically in the chapter titled "Concerning the Two Brothers who have Charge of the Kaan’s Hounds." The archive notes that these individuals served as the Masters of the Hounds.

## Context of the Kaan's Hunting Establishments
The charge of the hounds existed within a broader system of hunting and game procurement. The Great Kaan enjoined his people to supply him with game and maintained a variety of animals for the chase, including wolves, leopards, and lions. These animals included the hunting leopard (also known as the cheeta), lynxes, and the Búrgút eagle. The tiger was also kept, though it was termed a "lion" by Polo. 

The management of these animals was part of the larger infrastructure used during the Emperor's hunting expeditions. These expeditions involved hawking establishments and the use of great tents, as well as the use of a litter by Kúblái.

## Diplomatic Missions of the Two Brothers
Beyond their roles in the Kaan's court, the Two Brothers were entrusted with a specific charge by the Great Kaan that required them to travel to the West. They traveled to Venice and subsequently to Acre, where they consulted with a Legate. 

The brothers' mission was delayed because they waited for a Pope to be made. During this period of waiting, they sought and received permission from the Legate to travel to Jerusalem. Their objective in Jerusalem was to obtain oil from the Lamp on the Sepulchre to bring back to the Great Kaan, as he had commanded.

Upon realizing that no Pope was being appointed, the Two Brothers decided that their return to the Great Kaan could be delayed no longer. Before departing Acre, the Legate provided them with letters for the Great Kaan. These letters served as testimony that the Two Brothers had acted in good faith to fulfill the Kaan's charge but were unable to do so because there was no Pope.
