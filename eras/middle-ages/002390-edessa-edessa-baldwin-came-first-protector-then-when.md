# Edessa

Edessa was a chief Latin principality in Syria, serving as the farthest Christian outpost in northern Syria beyond the Euphrates. Founded as the first principality of the Franks or Latins, it subsisted for fifty-four years before its eventual fall.

## Establishment and Baldwin I
The principality was established by Baldwin I, the brother of Godfrey of Bouillon. Originally a clerk in orders who held several prebends, Baldwin joined the first crusade in 1096. After accompanying his brother to Heraclea in Asia Minor, Baldwin followed Tancred into Cilicia, driven by political motives and jealousy. In September 1097, Baldwin wrested Tarsus from Tancred and established his own garrison there.

Following an invitation from an Armenian named Pakrad, Baldwin moved east toward the Euphrates and occupied Tell-bashir. He subsequently received an invitation from Thoros of Edessa. Baldwin first arrived in Edessa as a protector; however, following the assassination of Thoros in March 1098, Baldwin became his successor. During his rule from 1098 to 1100, Baldwin married an Armenian wife and acted as an intermediary between the Armenians and the crusaders. He successfully defended his position against a conspiracy of his own subjects in 1098 and against surrounding Mahommedan powers, from which he won Seruj (Sarorgia) and Samosata.

## Rule of Baldwin II
In 1100, Baldwin I was summoned to Jerusalem to succeed his brother Godfrey as king. He appointed Baldwin de Burg (Baldwin II), a nephew of both Godfrey and Baldwin I, as the count of Edessa. Baldwin II, who had previously helped Tancred occupy Bethlehem in 1099, conducted frequent forays against Mahommedan princes. During a large foray in 1104 alongside Bohemund, Baldwin II was defeated and captured at Balich.

While Baldwin II was in captivity, Tancred became the guardian of Edessa. Upon recovering his liberty at the start of 1108, Baldwin II struggled with Tancred to recover his principality, eventually regaining it in September 1108 and composing their dispute in 1109. For the following decade, Baldwin II ruled with success, though some sources, such as Matthew of Edessa, characterize him as avaricious and claim he plundered wealthy Armenians.

Baldwin II faced numerous attacks from Mosul and Mardin. In 1110, he was relieved during a siege of Edessa by Baldwin I. He also repelled an attack by Aksunkur of Mosul in 1114 and helped defeat Aksunkur at Danith in 1115. Baldwin II remained count until 1118, when he was made king of Jerusalem following the death of Baldwin I.

## Later Years and Fall
Following the reign of Baldwin II, the county was held by Joscelin, the grandson of Atho of Courtenay. Joscelin was attached to the standard of Baldwin II (referred to as Baldwin of Bruges) through a domestic alliance, as their mothers were sisters. Joscelin was eventually invested with the county of Edessa on both sides of the Euphrates. He replenished the territories with Syrian and Latin subjects and maintained the region's castles and magazines. Joscelin died as a soldier in a horse litter while leading his troops against Turkish invaders.

By the reign of Baldwin III of Jerusalem (who ascended in 1143), the position of the Franks in northern Syria was being fatally weakened by Zengi's attacks on Edessa and Greek attacks in Cilicia. Edessa fell to the Moslims in 1144, a loss that dealt a serious blow to the kingdom. This event led to the second crusade in 1147, which failed to recover the city, although it saved Antioch. In 1149, Baldwin III ceded Tellbashir, the final remnant of the county of Edessa, to Manuel of Constantinople in an effort to strengthen northern Palestine against Nureddin, the successor of Zengi.

## Historical Sources
The primary authority for the career of Baldwin I and the early history of Edessa is the *Historia Hierosolymitana* (also known as *Gesta Francorum Jerusalem expugnantium*) written by Fulcher of Chartres. Fulcher was a priest and chaplain who lived with Baldwin at Edessa and Jerusalem. Other perspectives on the early disputes between Baldwin and Tancred are found in the writings of Radulphus Cadomensis.
