# Bohemund IV and the Principality of Antioch

Bohemund IV was the younger son of Bohemund III and his second wife, Orguilleuse. His life and reign were characterized by a prolonged struggle for succession and territorial control involving the principality of Antioch and the county of Tripoli.

## Succession to Tripoli and Antioch
In 1187, Bohemund IV succeeded the last count of Tripoli, gaining possession of that county. However, his path to the principality of Antioch was contested. His father, Bohemund III, spent his final years attempting to decide whether the succession in Antioch should pass to his younger son, Bohemund, or to his grandson, Raymund Rhupen. Raymund Rhupen was the son of Bohemund III's eldest son, Raymund, and Alice, the daughter of the Armenian prince Rhupen.

Bohemund IV pursued his claim to Antioch with vigor, going so far as to evict his father from the city around 1199. Despite this, he was ousted by Leo of Armenia, who had become king by the grace of Emperor Henry VI. Bohemund III remained in possession of his principality until his death in 1201, at which point Bohemund IV succeeded him, excluding Raymund Rhupen.

## Conflict with Armenia
The dispute over the succession lasted for many years and required the attention of Pope Innocent III. Leo of Armenia continued to champion the cause of his great-nephew, Raymund Rhupen. During this period, Bohemund IV received support from the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and from the Templars, who held estates in Tripoli.

In contrast, Leo of Armenia was supported by the Hospitallers. Between 1210 and 1211, Leo appealed to both Emperor Otto IV and Innocent III. In 1216, Leo successfully captured Antioch and established Raymund Rhupen as its prince. This arrangement lasted less than four years; Bohemund IV regained possession of the city by the time Leo died in 1220. Following the death of Raymund Rhupen in 1221, Bohemund IV reigned over both Antioch and Tripoli without further challenge from the Armenian line.

## Later Reign and Relations
Bohemund IV's later years were marked by conflict with the Hospitallers, whom he viewed as enemies. This hostility led to his excommunication in 1230. His political alliances were similarly volatile; during the crusade of 1228–29, he first joined and then deserted Emperor Frederick II. As a result of this desertion, he was excluded from the operation of the treaty of 1229.

Bohemund IV died in 1233. Shortly before his death, he concluded a peace agreement with the Hospitallers, and Gregory IX released him from the excommunication he had incurred in 1230. He was succeeded by his son, Bohemund V, who was born to his wife Plaisance, the daughter of Hugh of Gibelet.
