# Sabine Baring-Gould

Sabine Baring-Gould (1834– ) was an English novelist and researcher who produced a wide array of works spanning fiction, history, religion, mythology, folk-lore, and travel. Born in Exeter on January 28, 1834, he graduated from Clare College, Cambridge. Following a period of travel, he entered the clergy, serving as the curate of Horbury, Yorkshire, in 1864. He later became the perpetual curate of Dalton in 1867 and the rector of East Mersea, Essex, in 1871.

In 1872, following the death of his father, Baring-Gould inherited the Lew Trenchard estate in North Devon, where his family had been established for nearly three centuries. In 1881, he exchanged his living in Essex for the rectory of Lew Trenchard.

## Literary and Academic Contributions

Baring-Gould began publishing his writings in 1854. His fictional works include several tales set in specific English regions, such as *Mehalah* (1880), set on the east coast of England; *John Herring* (1883), a tale of the west country; and *Broom Squire* (1896), a Sussex tale. Other novels include *Court Royal* (1886), *Red Spider* (1887), *The Pennycomequicks* (1889), and *Cheap Jack Zita* (1893).

Beyond fiction, Baring-Gould was noted for his research into antiquities, topography, and folk-lore. While his writing style was popular, it was characterized by real learning and serious research. His notable contributions to these fields include:
* *Book of Were-wolves* (1865)
* *Curious Myths of the Middle Ages* (1866)
* *Curious Survivals* (1892)

Additionally, he wrote numerous volumes of popular theology and sermons. Between 1871 and 1873, he served as the editor of *The Sacristy*, a quarterly review dedicated to ecclesiastical literature and art.
