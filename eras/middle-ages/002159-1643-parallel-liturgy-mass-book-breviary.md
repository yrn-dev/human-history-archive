# The Breviary

The Breviary (from the Latin *breviarium*, meaning epitome or abridgment) is a liturgical book containing the offices for the canonical hours, which constitute the daily service of the Roman Catholic Church. It serves as a guide for the daily recitation of prayers, hymns, and lessons.

## Nature and Contents
The Breviary is designed for use by every member of a religious order and every clerk in orders, who must read the allotted services for each day either privately or publicly. Reading these services aloud—using both the lips and the eyes—takes approximately two hours. In large churches, these services are typically grouped: Matins and Lauds occur around 7:30 A.M.; Prime, Terce (High Mass), Sext, and None around 10 A.M.; and Vespers and Compline at 4 P.M. Depending on the number of high masses and the amount of music, between four and eight hours may be spent in the choir.

Compared to the Anglican Book of Common Prayer, the Breviary is more comprehensive in its inclusion of hymns and lessons for every day of the year, but less comprehensive because it excludes the Eucharistic office (found in the Missal) and special offices for ordination, burial, marriage, and baptism (found in the Pontifical or Ritual).

The Roman Breviary is typically divided into four seasonal parts—winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Each part comprises:
1. The Psalter
2. *Proprium de Tempore* (the special office of the season)
3. *Proprium Sanctorum* (special offices of saints)
4. *Commune Sanctorum* (general offices for saints)
5. Extra Services

Introductory materials usually include general rubrics, a calendar, and tables for determining the date of Easter.

## Historical Evolution
In early Christian worship, the Bible provided all necessary materials, including the psalms and lessons. The Breviary evolved first through the separation of the Psalter into a choir-book. Initially, the leader of the choir or the bishop chose psalms as appropriate. By the 4th century, psalms began to be grouped together, a process accelerated by monastic practices of reciting all 150 psalms daily. To manage the time required, monks divided the day into hours and allotted a portion of the Psalter to each. In the 6th century, St Benedict established such an arrangement, likely based on an older, less skillful Roman division.

Over time, additions such as collects (short prayers), responses, and antiphons were added for those unable to improvise metrical compositions. By the 12th century, liturgical author Jean Beleth listed several necessary books for the canonical office, including the *Psalterium*, *Collectarium*, *Antiphonarium*, *Passionarius* (regarding martyrs), *Legendarius* (regarding saints), *Homiliarius* (Gospel homilies), *Sermologus* (sermons), the Old and New Testaments, and the works of the Fathers.

## Revisions and Standardization
Until the Council of Trent, bishops had the power to regulate the Breviary of their own diocese, and monastic communities maintained their own versions. Pope Pius V (1566–1572) made the Roman Breviary obligatory in all places, except for those that could prove their local versions had existed for at least 200 years. This led to the Roman version becoming nearly universal, though dioceses may still add offices for specially venerated local saints.

Notable revisions include:
* **Francis Quignonez (1536):** His revision was not accepted by Rome but served as a model for the Church of England's 1549 reform. The English Prayer-Book's morning and evening services are a simplification and condensation of Breviary offices, and some of its prefaces are free translations of Quignonez's work.
* **Pius V (1568):** Issued the *Breviarium Pianum* (Pian Breviary) after excluding Quignonez's version for being too modern and short.
* **Sixtus V (1588):** Introduced the revised Vulgate text.
* **Clement VIII (1602):** Revised the rubrics through Bellarmine and Baronius.
* **Urban VIII (1623–1644):** Altered the text of the hymns, which some view as injuring their historic worth and literary charm.

## Regional Variations and Art
The Sarum (or Salisbury) Breviary was widely used and particularly favored in Scotland as a protest against the jurisdiction of the church of York. Unlike modern Breviaries, which are usually printed in four volumes, Sarum editions never exceeded two parts. The first Sarum edition was printed in Venice in 1483, and the latest in Paris in 1557–1556.

In the Peninsula, the 9th to 12th centuries saw Mozarabic liturgies adorned with barbaric-style initial letters and interlaced designs, influenced by Moorish colors such as black, red, and yellow. By the 15th century, foreign artists were employed for illumination. A breviary created for Queen Isabella of Spain around 1497 exhibits a mix of French and Flemish styles, though it retains a national preference for black-tinted scrolls and foliage in the borders.

## Other References
The Breviary has also appeared in historical contexts outside of daily liturgy; for example, the Breviary of Liberatus (c. 14–19) is cited as a source for the general history from the death of Anastasius to the council of Chalcedon. Additionally, the work *A Parallel of the Liturgy with the Mass Book, the Breviary, the Ceremonial and other Romish Rituals* was published in London in 1661 by an author whose other works include *The Life of William (Laud) now Lord Archbishop of Canterbury Examined* (1643).
