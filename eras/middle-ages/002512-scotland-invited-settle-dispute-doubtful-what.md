# Scotland

## Political History and the Succession of 1290
Following the death of Margaret, known as the "maid of Norway" and granddaughter of King Alexander III, the Scottish throne became vacant in 1290. This vacancy led to a dispute over the crown between three candidates. The primary rivals were Robert Bruce, earl of Annandale, and John de Baliol (1249–1315). The conflict centered on the competing claims of the nearest of kin versus the eldest by descent. John de Baliol based his claim on his maternal grandmother, Margaret, who was the eldest daughter of David, earl of Huntingdon (a grandson of King David I).

King Edward I of England was invited to settle this dispute. While the rights of English kings over Scotland were doubtful, Edward demanded a formal recognition of his position as overlord when he met Scottish nobles at Norham in May 1291. This position was tacitly admitted by the nobles and acknowledged by the competitors, including Baliol, who agreed to abide by Edward's decision. A court consisting of twenty-four Englishmen and eighty Scotsmen was appointed to hear the case. Baliol argued based on the principles of feudal law for an indivisible inheritance. Consequently, Edward decided in Baliol's favor. 

Baliol was crowned king of Scotland at Scone on November 30, 1292, after swearing fealty to the English king. He performed homage to Edward at Newcastle and, in January 1293, released the English king from all obligations and promises made while Scotland was in his hands. However, relations deteriorated when a Scottish vassal brought a case to Edward as Baliol's overlord, leading to Baliol being summoned to the English court.

## Religious and Academic Figures
Robert Baillie (1602–1662) was a Scottish divine born in Glasgow, where he graduated in 1620. After studying divinity and being ordained, he served as a regent at the university before being appointed to the living of Kilwinning in Ayrshire in 1631. Baillie was a member of the Glasgow Assembly in 1638 and served as a preacher or chaplain accompanying the Scottish army and Leslie. 

In 1642, Baillie became a professor of divinity at Glasgow, and the following year he was one of five Scottish clergymen selected for the Westminster Assembly. In 1649, he served as one of the commissioners sent to Holland to settle the terms of admission for Charles II to the government and to invite him to Scotland. Baillie later became the principal of Glasgow University in 1661. He died in August of the following year, with his death likely hastened by his mortification regarding the firm establishment of episcopacy in Scotland. He is remembered for his *Letters*, which provide a lively historical picture of the period.

## Geography and Terminology
The term "Britain" is the anglicized form of the classical name for Scotland, Wales, and England, and is sometimes used to refer to the British Isles as a whole. The exact Celtic original of the name remains a matter of dispute. In the north of Scotland, the term "ayr" is used to describe a spit of sand that divides a river-mouth, pond, or lake from the sea.
