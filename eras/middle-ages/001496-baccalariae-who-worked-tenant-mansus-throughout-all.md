# Baccalarius

The term *baccalarius* (with the rare variant *baccalaris*) is derived from Medieval Latin and is related to the Italian *baccalare* and Old French *bacheler*. In its most general sense, the word refers to a young man. However, the term has a complex history of application, evolving through various meanings while consistently retaining an underlying idea of subordination.

## Origins and Agricultural Meaning
The term *baccalarius* was originally applied to the tenant of a *baccalaria*. A *baccalaria* (derived from *baccalia*, meaning a herd of cows, where *bacca* is a Low Latin variant of *vacca*) was presumably a grazing farm. In its early usage, a *baccalaria* was practically identical to a *vaselleria*, which refers to the fief of a sub-vassal.

As the size and character of the *baccalaria* shifted over different eras, the meaning of *baccalarius* changed accordingly. By the 8th century, the term was used to describe *rustici*—rural laborers—who worked for the tenant of a *mansus*. This application included both men and women, the latter being referred to as *baccalariae*.

## Evolution of the Term
Following its agricultural origins, the concept of subordination associated with the word led to its application in several other distinct social and professional categories:

### Ecclesiastical Use
The term was used to describe ecclesiastics of an inferior grade. Examples include recently appointed canons or young monks.

### Knighthood
In the context of chivalry, "knights bachelors" represented the lowest stage of knighthood. This category comprised two types of individuals:
* Poor vassals who lacked the financial means to take the field under their own banner.
* Knights who were too young to handle the dignity and responsibility associated with knights bannerets.

### Academic Use
In the 13th century, the term *baccalarius* or *baccalaureus* appeared at the university of Paris. This was part of a system of degrees established under the auspices of Pope Gregory IX. In this academic context, the term applied to scholars who were still *in statu pupillari*. This preliminary degree served as a requirement enabling the scholar to eventually proceed to the degree of master (*magister*), which was the only rank that entitled an individual to teach.

## The Mansus and Feudal Context
The *baccalariae* and *baccalarii* of the 8th century worked within a system involving the *mansus*. Under the Frankish kings, who maintained Roman administrative traditions, Gallo-Roman estates evolved into a feudal model between the 6th and 10th centuries. 

In this system, the estate was typically divided into the lord's domain (*dominicum* or *terra indominicata*) and various allotments known as *mansi*. These *mansi* were parcels of land distributed by lot to the cultivators of the domain. The tenants of these allotments held the land through a combination of personal service and labor on the domain, as well as payments in kind or money. These obligations were permanently fixed and passed to each new tenant along with the land, though they varied based on the needs of the estate and the size of the specific holding.
