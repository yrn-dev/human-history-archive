# Subordination of Interests and Members

The concept of subordination—whether applied to individual interests, organizational members, or political entities—appears across various historical, legal, and social contexts. These instances range from the ethical guidelines of social reform movements to the administrative structures of empires and the personal priorities of individuals.

## Ethical and Social Subordination
In the context of the Samaj, a specific ethical framework is established regarding the balance between collective and individual needs. The organization's primary objective is the improvement of the world's moral, intellectual, social, and physical conditions. To achieve this, the Samaj teaches that love and justice should serve as the guides for conduct, and that the good of others must be actively sought. 

Within this framework, a clear distinction is made between general and personal interests: in matters of general interest, members are required to subordinate themselves to the good of others. However, in matters of personal interest, members are encouraged to maintain their independence. This program of reform further includes the promotion of female education, the abolition of idolatry and caste, physical exercise and cleanliness, marriage reform, and abstinence from animal food and spirituous liquors.

## Legal and Administrative Subordination
In legal terminology, the prefix "by-" in "by-law" is used to denote something that is secondary or subordinate. A by-law is a regulation created by corporations, companies, boards, or councils—typically under statutory power—to ensure good government and the preservation of order within a specific jurisdiction. 

These regulations must meet several criteria to be valid: they must be reasonable, they cannot be retrospective, and they cannot contradict the general law of the land. When created under a statute, they generally require the sanction and approval of a confirming authority before they can operate, at which point they become as binding as enacted laws. For corporate bodies, by-laws must remain in harmony with the society's objects and cannot limit the duties or powers of its officers.

## Political and Military Subordination
Subordination is a recurring theme in the governance of ancient states and military command.

### Ancient Greece
In the history of Greece, the relationship between Athens and other city-states evolved from a confederacy into an imperial system. While Demosthenes envisioned Athens as a natural head of Greece—leading a free confederacy where members were bound by their own truest interests—the reality often shifted toward domination. Athens eventually became a despot city, treating its allies as dependent subjects without their active concurrence. This lack of a shared "all-pervading interest" or collective discussion led to the alliance becoming odious and burdensome.

Similarly, Greek confederacies dealt with "mediate" or subordinate dependencies. In certain agreements, imperial states agreed to forego the services or tribute of these subordinate dependencies if the dependencies were called upon to aid the general effort of the confederacy against a common enemy.

### Military and Imperial Command
In Japanese history, the figure Yoshisada provided guidance on the conduct of leadership. He admonished his family members that an officer in command of an army must respect the sovereign and treat subordinates with a combination of decision and clemency.

In the early 20th century, Japan's relationship with Korea involved a system of subordinate administration. While Japan assumed control of Korean foreign affairs and military direction, it maintained a resident-general in Seoul with subordinate residents throughout the provinces. In internal administration, Japan limited itself to advisory supervision, employing experts as counsellors whose advice the Korean Government could either accept or reject.

## Individual Subordination of Interest
Subordination also occurs on a personal level when an individual prioritizes one passion over all other concerns. An example is found in the life of Richard Henry Dana (1787-1879). Although he was educated at Harvard and admitted to the practice of law in 1811, he subordinated all other interests to his love of literature. This priority led him to join the Anthology Club in 1814 and contribute significantly to American literature through the *North American Review* and his own published poems and prose.
