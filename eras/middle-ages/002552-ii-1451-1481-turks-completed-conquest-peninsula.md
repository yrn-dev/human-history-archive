# The Turkish Conquest of the Peninsula and Asia Minor

The expansion of the Ottoman Turks during the 14th and 15th centuries resulted in the systematic conquest of the Balkan Peninsula and the consolidation of power across Asia Minor. This process involved the defeat of various Slavonic states, the annexation of Byzantine territories, and the establishment of a new socio-economic order under the Ottoman sultans.

## Conquest of the Balkan Peninsula
Turkish incursions into the peninsula began with raids that desolated Thrace and Macedonia. By 1340, the Turks had started ravaging the Maritza valley, subsequently capturing Philippopolis in 1362 and Sofia in 1382. The Bulgarian state faced steady decline; in 1366, the last Bulgarian tsar, Ivan Shishman III, became a vassal of Sultan Murad I. Following a three-month siege in 1393, Trnovo was captured, sacked, and burnt. The final remnant of Bulgarian independence vanished in 1396 with the fall of Vidin, where Ivan's brother, Strazhimir, had been established.

The fate of the peninsula was largely decided in 1389 at the field of Kossovo, where Sultan Murad I routed the combined forces of the Servians, Bosnians, and Croats under the Servian tsar Lazar. Further securing the region, Murad's son, Bayezid, captured Larissa and Salonica in 1395. Bayezid's victory over Sigismund of Hungary at Nicopolis in 1396 further sealed the peninsula's fate. Later, in 1424, John VII Palaeologus yielded the towns of the Struma valley to the Turks, and in 1428, Murad II took Salonica for the final time, resulting in the massacre of its inhabitants.

## Consolidation in Asia Minor
The Turkish presence in Asia Minor began earlier with the Seljuk Turks, who ravaged Cilicia and Cappadocia in 1067 and captured Nicaea in 1080. The Seljuks founded the empire of Rum, which at its peak included nearly all of Asia Minor and parts of Syria and Armenia. However, this empire passed to the Mongols after the sultan of Rum was defeated in 1243. Following the death of the last sultan of Rum in 1307, Turkoman governors in the western provinces asserted their independence, leading to a struggle for supremacy won by the Osmanli Turks of Brusa.

By 1400, Sultan Bayezid I controlled all of Asia Minor west of the Euphrates, though he was later defeated and imprisoned by Timur in 1402. After Timur's death, Osmanli supremacy was re-established. This process concluded under Mahommed II (1451-1481), who annexed Trebizond and Karamania and oversaw the abandonment of the remaining Italian trading settlements along the coast.

## The Reign of Mahommed II (1451-1481)
A pivotal moment in the Ottoman expansion occurred in 1453 when Sultan Muhammad II took Constantinople, utilizing a great number of guns to attack from the European side. This event caused intense excitement in Europe, though no crusade materialized.

Mahommed II also implemented significant administrative and land reforms. He developed a system of feudal tenure where large tracts of land were distributed among Ottoman chiefs. Each fief was required to furnish a specific number of armed warriors. Under this system, Christian peasant owners remained on the lands assigned to Moslem feudal lords and were required to pay a tithe.

Following the capture of Constantinople, Muhammad II turned his ambitions toward Rome. He captured and looted the Italian town of Otranto. It is probable that a vigorous attempt to conquer the Italian peninsula was only averted by his death in 1481, which occurred while he was preparing for the conquest of Italy.

## Social and Administrative Impact
The subject population under Ottoman rule faced deplorable conditions from the beginning. The country was policed by the janissaries, and many peasant proprietors were eventually reduced to serfdom, working as laborers on the *tchifliks* (farms) of Moslem beys. These conditions worsened during a period of anarchy in the 17th and 18th centuries coinciding with the decadence of central power.

Later efforts to improve the condition of the population were made in the latter half of the 17th century by the grand viziers Mustafa and Mehemet of the house of Koprülü. By the end of the 18th century, local governors had become practically independent; for example, Ismail Bey exercised a "beneficent despotism" at Serres with an army of 10,000 men, and Ali Pasha of Iannina controlled western Macedonia. Additionally, the Christian population suffered from Albanian incursions over two centuries. While the Hatt-i-Sherif of Gulhané (1839) and the Hatt-i-humayun (1856) proclaimed the equality of religions and races, these remained a "dead letter." The "Law of the Vilayets" (1864) failed to bring relief and deprived Christian communities of certain existing rights.
