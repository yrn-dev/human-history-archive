# Ethelfride of Northumbria

## Historical Context and Actions
Ethelfride of Northumbria is recorded as the perpetrator of a great massacre at Bangor-is-coed in 613. Following this event, the remaining priests fled the area. Their route of escape was via the road of the Rivals (*Yn Eifl*) hill, located in South Carnarvonshire, leading toward the island of Bardsey.

## The Kingdom of Northumbria
Northumbria was a kingdom located in the north of England. It was formed through the eventual union of two separate kingdoms: Bernicia and Deira. The districts north of the Humber were occupied by these two entities before their unification. 

During the seventh century, the monasteries within Northumbria became significant centers of learning and light. Due to the high concentration of monks and monasteries, this region of England was, for a period, far more advanced in civilization than the south. Notable figures associated with Northumbrian learning include the monk of Jarrow, Bede (known as the Venerable Bede, 673-735), who authored "The Ecclesiastical History of the English" and translated the Gospel of John into English.

## Regional Geography and Borders
The kingdom of Northumbria shared various frontiers. To the south, the boundary between Northumbria and the kingdom of Mercia was once marked by the line of Cheshire. In the north, the region of Bernicia (Northumbria north of the Tyne) is noted for having remained independent until a general submission to Edward in 924, appearing to have avoided Danish authority and rule.

Later in history, the territory of Northumbria was subject to external conflicts. For example, Kenneth II, king of Alban, attacked Eadulf, the earl of the northern half of Northumbria, ravaging his entire territory and capturing the earl's son.

## The Destination of the Refugees: Bardsey
The priests fleeing Ethelfride's massacre sought refuge on Bardsey (meaning "Bards' Island" or *Ynys Enlli*), an island situated at the northern extremity of Cardigan Bay in North Wales. The island is approximately 2-1/2 miles long and 3/4 miles broad, covering some 370 acres.

Bardsey was made famous as a sanctuary by St Dubricius, who died there in 612. The island served as a burial place for monks whose friends could afford to transport their bodies there, with all the great abbeys of England sending their quota. The island features a large cemetery of about 3 to 4 acres containing close-set, rough graves with slabs. Access to the island was facilitated by roads running from the north, east, and south, which featured monks' wells placed at intervals of 7 to 9 miles.
