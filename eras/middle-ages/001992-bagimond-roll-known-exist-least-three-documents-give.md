# Bagimond's Roll

## Origins and Purpose
Bagimond's Roll originated from a tax imposed by the council of Lyons in 1274. This tax required a tenth part of all church revenues to be paid over the following six years to provide relief for the Holy Land. In Scotland, the responsibility for collecting this tax was entrusted by Pope Gregory X to Master Boiamund de Vitia, a canon of Asti, who is more commonly known as Bagimund.

## Assessment and Conflict
The roll of valuation created by Boiamund served as the foundation for ecclesiastical taxation for several centuries. Boiamund sought to innovate the assessment process by basing the tax on the true value of the benefices at the time of the assessment, rather than relying on old conventional valuations. This proposal was met with objection from the clergy of Scotland. In August 1275, the clergy held a council at Perth and successfully persuaded Boiamund to return to Rome to convince the pope to revert to the older method of taxation. However, the pope insisted that the tax be collected according to the true value, and Boiamund subsequently returned to Scotland to oversee the collection.

## Surviving Fragments and Records
While no contemporary copy of Bagimond's Roll is known to exist, a fragment is preserved at Durham in a form very similar to the original. This fragment was printed by James Raine in *Priory of Coldingham*. The document features two columns: one listing the real values and another listing the tenth parts of each benefice within the archdeaconry of Lothian.

It is noted that the taxation described in this specific fragment does not refer to the tenth collected by Boiamund himself. Instead, it refers to a tenth of all ecclesiastical property in Ireland, Wales, Scotland, and England granted to Edward I of England by Pope Nicholas IV in 1288. Consequently, the fragment is considered supplementary to the *Taxatio Ecclesiastica Angliae et Walliae*.

## Later Usage
Despite the lack of a contemporary copy, the original roll continued to influence later records. At least three documents from the 16th century provide particulars regarding the taxation of the Church of Scotland based upon the original roll.
