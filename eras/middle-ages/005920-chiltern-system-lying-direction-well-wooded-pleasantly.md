# Buckinghamshire

Buckinghamshire (abbreviated Bucks) is a south midland county of England with an area of 743.2 sq. m. It is bounded to the north by Northamptonshire, to the east by Middlesex, Hertfordshire, and Bedfordshire, to the south by Berkshire and for a short distance by Surrey, and to the west by Oxfordshire.

## Physical Geography and Hydrography
The county is divided between the basins of the Thames and Ouse rivers. The Thames forms the entire southern boundary, and the county includes the upper part of this river. The Ouse, in its uppermost course, forms part of the north-western boundary and a short stretch of the north-eastern boundary; it passes through the towns of Olney, Newport Pagnell, Wolverton, Stony Stratford, and Buckingham. The Ouzel serves as a principal tributary to the Ouse within the county.

The terrain varies by region. To the north-west of Buckingham, and both east and west of the Ouzel, the land rises in gentle undulations to nearly 500 ft. North of the Thames valley, isolated hills such as Muswell Hill and Brill Hill exceed 600 ft. The hilliest region is the south, which is occupied by part of the Chiltern system. These hills run generally from south-west to north-east, reaching heights of nearly 900 ft. within the county. The crest-line of the Chilterns crosses the county at its narrowest point—a line not exceeding 11 m. in length above the towns of Wendover and Prince's Risborough. South of this line, the land is predominantly hilly, well wooded, and diversified with narrow vales watered by the Chess, Misbourne, and Wye streams.

## Geology
The geological composition of the county is split between the northern and southern halves. The northern half consists of Jurassic strata, while Cretaceous rocks predominate in the southern half, except for the south-eastern corner, which is covered by Tertiary beds. The rocks generally dip south-easterly, with the oldest rocks located in the north.

Key geological features include:
*   **Oolitic series:** Represented by the Great Oolite, with limestones quarried for building stones at Brock, Thornborough, Westbury, and Whittlewood Forest.
*   **Cornbrash:** A series of thin clays and rubbly limestones running by Newport Pagnell, Buckingham, Berehampton, and Tingwick; it is quarried at Wolverton for road metal.
*   **Clays:** The vale of Aylesbury contains the Kimmeridge Clay, Oxford Clay, and Gault. A broad band of bluish Gault clay extends north-easterly across the county from Towersey.
*   **Portland beds:** These follow a south-west to north-east direction from Aylesbury through Thame and are quarried for fossils and building stone.
*   **Other deposits:** Lower Greensand at Hartwell has been worked for glass-making sands, and phosphatic nodules have been found at Brickhill and Towersey.

The beech tree is the predominant species in the woods. William Camden, writing around 1585, suggested the county's name derives from the Anglo-Saxon word for beech (*boc*). A remnant of ancient forest, known as Burnham Beeches, is preserved as public ground in the south.

## History
The district was reached by the West Saxons in 571 as they pushed north along the Thames valley. It was later included in Mercia during the consolidation of that kingdom under Offa. Following the submission of Mercia to the Northmen, the area became part of the Danelaw. During the 10th century, the region suffered frequent Danish raids, leaving behind numerous earthworks and barrows, particularly in the vale of Aylesbury.

The Chiltern district was historically described as an impassable forest inhabited by wild beasts and robbers. During the reign of Edward the Confessor, Leofstan, the 12th abbot of St Albans, cleared large tracts of wood and granted the manor of Hamstead to a knight and two soldiers to stop the robbers' depredations. This necessity led to the appointment of a steward of the Chiltern Hundreds, an office that eventually became a sinecure. The district was not fully disforested until the reign of James I.

## Administration
The shire of Buckingham originated during the reign of Edward the Elder through the division of Mercia. It was likely formed by aggregating pre-existing hundreds around the county town, resulting in irregular boundary lines. 

While the Domesday survey recorded eighteen hundreds, these were eventually reduced to eight. Three of these—Stoke, Burnham, and Desborough (the Chiltern hundreds)—remain unaltered in name and extent. Others were formed by uniting three ancient hundreds; for example, Aylesbury is referred to as "the three hundreds of Aylesbury." Except for Buckingham and Newport, the hundreds retain their Domesday names. Until the era of Queen Elizabeth, Buckinghamshire shared a common sheriff with Bedfordshire, and the shire court was held at Aylesbury.
