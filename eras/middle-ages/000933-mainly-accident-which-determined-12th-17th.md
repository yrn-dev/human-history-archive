# The Influence of Avicenna in European Medical Study

## Academic Dominance and the Role of Accident
From the 12th to the 17th century, it was mainly accident that determined Avicenna should serve as the guide of medical study within European universities. During this period, his influence eclipsed that of other figures such as Avenzoar, Ali ibn al-Abbas, and Rhazes. Avicenna's prominence was such that his primary work, the *Canon*, continued to be utilized as a text-book at the universities of Montpellier and Louvain until approximately the year 1650.

## Comparison with Predecessors
The work of Avicenna is not essentially different from that of his predecessors, Ali and Rhazes. All three figures presented the doctrine of Galen, and by extension the doctrine of Hippocrates, which had been modified by the system of Aristotle. However, Avicenna's *Canon* is distinguished from Rhazes' *Summary* (also known as the *Al-Hawi* or *Continens*) by a greater degree of method. This methodological superiority is attributed perhaps to Avicenna's logical studies, a quality that earned him the surname "Prince of the Physicians."

## Structure and Content of the Canon
The *Canon* consists of five books:
*   **Books One and Two:** These sections treat hygiene, pathology, and physiology.
*   **Books Three and Four:** These sections deal with the methods used for treating disease.
*   **Book Five:** This section describes the preparation and composition of remedies, containing some of Avicenna's personal observations.

In terms of medical theory, Avicenna introduced the four causes of the Peripatetic system. While he is noted for being ample in the enumeration of symptoms—a trait shared with his countrymen—he is said to be inferior to Ali in the fields of surgery and practical medicine. Additionally, Avicenna claimed no special knowledge regarding botany or natural history.

## Critical Reception and Evaluation
The *Canon* has been viewed with varying degrees of appreciation across different eras. Some have regarded the work as a treasury of wisdom, while others, such as Avenzoar, considered it useful only as waste paper. In modern times, the text has been criticized more than it has been read. Critics point to the book's "vice" as being an over-subtlety in the discrimination of diseases and an excessive classification of bodily faculties.
