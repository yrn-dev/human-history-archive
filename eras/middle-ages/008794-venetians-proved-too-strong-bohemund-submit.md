# The Bohemunds of Antioch and Tripoli

The history of the Bohemunds is inextricably linked to the governance of the principality of Antioch and, from the time of Bohemund IV., the county of Tripoli. This lineage of Norman leaders played a central role in the First Crusade and the subsequent establishment of Christian principalities in the East.

## Bohemund I and the First Crusade
Bohemund I (c. A.D. 1058–1111), born Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, the *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*. Early in his career, between 1080 and 1085, he served his father during an attack on the East Roman empire. During Guiscard's absence from 1082 to 1084, Bohemund commanded the Normans and penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly, though he was repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early conflict with Alexius significantly influenced his future trajectory.

Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a conflict arose between Bohemund and his younger half-brother, Roger, who had succeeded to the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This dispute was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions. In 1096, while attacking Amalfi, Bohemund was inspired by the passing crusaders. Seeking to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* and carve out an eastern principality, he led a Norman army across the Adriatic to Constantinople. Upon his arrival in April 1097, he performed homage to Emperor Alexius. Bohemund became the real leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch, guiding the host across Asia Minor.

## Bohemund II and III
Bohemund II (1108–1131), the son of Bohemund I and Constance of France, arrived in Antioch from Apulia in 1126. He married Alice, the daughter of Baldwin II, in 1127. After participating in an attack on Damascus with Baldwin II, Bohemund II was defeated and killed in 1131 by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo on his northern frontier.

Bohemund III succeeded his mother, Constance (daughter of Bohemund II), in the principality of Antioch in 1163. In 1164, while serving as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem during Amalric I's expedition to Egypt, he was defeated and captured by Nureddin at Harenc. He was ransomed by his brother-in-law, Emperor Manuel, and returned to Antioch with a Greek patriarch. Bohemund III's later years were marked by familial strife regarding his succession. He had two sons by his second wife, Orguilleuse: Raymund and Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV). After Raymund died in 1197, a dispute emerged between Raymund's son, Raymund Rhupen, and Bohemund IV. Leo of Armenia championed Raymund Rhupen's claim and even captured Bohemund III at Gastin in 1194. Bohemund III died in 1201 while still in possession of his principality.

## Bohemund IV and V
Bohemund IV became the count of Tripoli in 1187 and succeeded his father in Antioch in 1201. His succession was contested for many years by Raymund Rhupen, with Leo of Armenia supporting the latter. This dispute involved the attention of Innocent III and the support of different military orders; Bohemund IV was supported by the Templars and the Greek inhabitants of Antioch, while Leo and Raymund Rhupen were supported by the Hospitallers. Although Leo captured Antioch in 1216 and installed Raymund Rhupen, Bohemund IV regained control before Leo's death in 1220. Bohemund IV remained a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, leading to his excommunication in 1230, though he was released from this by Gregory IX before his death in 1233.

Bohemund V succeeded his father in 1233 as prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. He maintained an alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia. He connected the history of Antioch with Cyprus through his 1225 marriage to Alice, the widow of Hugh I. Bohemund V resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving the governance of Antioch to a commune and its bailiff. He died in 1251.

## Bohemund VI and VII
Bohemund VI (born 1237), son of Bohemund V and Luciana, succeeded his father in 1251. He was knighted by St Louis in 1252. He established himself in Antioch and recognized his nephew, Hugh II, as king of Jerusalem in 1257. To counter the Egyptian sultan, Bohemund VI allied himself with the Mongols. However, he lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268 and died in 1275 as only the count of Tripoli.

The lineage ended with Bohemund VII, son of Bohemund VI and Sibylla of Armenia. He succeeded to the county of Tripoli in 1275. His short reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars, and in 1287, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue in 1287. With the capture of Tripoli shortly thereafter, the county became extinct.
