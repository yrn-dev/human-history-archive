# Bohemund I and the First Crusade

Bohemund I (c. A.D. 1058–1111), also known by the first name Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, the *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*. Born to Guiscard's early marriage before 1059, Bohemund was destined by his father for the throne of Constantinople. His early military career was defined by his service under Guiscard during a major attack on the East Roman empire between 1080 and 1085. During Guiscard's absence from 1082 to 1084, Bohemund commanded the Normans and penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly, though he was eventually repulsed by Alexius Comnenus.

## Rise to Power and the Crusade
Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a conflict erupted between Bohemund and his younger brother, Roger, who had succeeded to the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This war was eventually resolved through the mediation of Urban II, resulting in Bohemund being awarded Otranto and other possessions.

In 1096, while Bohemund and his uncle, the count of Sicily, were attacking Amalfi, bands of crusaders began passing through Italy toward Constantinople. Bohemund was seized by the zeal of the movement. He also viewed the First Crusade as an opportunity to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* (drive to the East) and hoped to establish an eastern principality for himself. He assembled a Norman army, described as perhaps the finest division in the crusading host, and crossed the Adriatic.

## Leadership from Constantinople to Antioch
Bohemund entered Constantinople in April 1097. Despite his previous hostilities toward Alexius Comnenus, he maintained a "correct" attitude and did homage to the emperor. While he may have attempted to negotiate for a principality at Antioch, he received little encouragement from Alexius.

From the point of departure at Constantinople to the arrival at Antioch, Bohemund served as the real leader of the First Crusade. His leadership is credited as a primary reason the First Crusade successfully crossed Asia Minor. The fighting forces of the crusade were essentially Norman in spirit and leadership; they stormed Nicæa and marched toward Antioch, where they conducted a siege that lasted one year.

## Legacy and Succession
Bohemund's career is documented in the *Alexiad* of Anna Comnena and the *Gesta Francorum*, the latter written by one of his followers. He married Constance, the daughter of Philip I of France. Their son, Bohemund II (1108–1131), later succeeded to the principality of Antioch. Bohemund II arrived in Antioch in 1126 and married Alice, the daughter of Baldwin II of Jerusalem. He was eventually slain in 1131 by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo on his northern frontier.

The history of the Bohemunds became synonymous with the history of the principality of Antioch and, later, the county of Tripoli. Subsequent leaders included Bohemund III, who was captured by Nureddin in 1164; Bohemund IV, who reigned in both Antioch and Tripoli and was a determined enemy of the Hospitallers; Bohemund V, who connected Antioch's history with Cyprus through marriage; and Bohemund VI, who allied with the Mongols before losing Antioch to Bibars in 1268. The line continued to Bohemund VII, who died without issue in 1287, coinciding with the capture of Tripoli and the extinction of the county.
