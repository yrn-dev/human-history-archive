# Recognition of Titles and Imperial Sanction

The process of recognizing titles and the role of imperial sanction vary across different historical and regional contexts, ranging from the constitutional struggles of the Bohemian crown to the hereditary and legal frameworks of the Japanese empire and the evolution of the Habsburg monarchy.

## The Bohemian Crown and Succession
In the 15th century, the Bohemian throne was the subject of significant dispute regarding whether the crown was hereditary or elective, a question that remained undecided until 1627. Following the death of King Sigismund in 1437, he was succeeded by Albert of Austria. After Albert's reign of less than two years, the nobility split into two factions: a national party led by George of Podebrad and a Roman or Austrian division led by Ulrich of Rosenberg.

These parties eventually reached an agreement called the "Letter of Peace" (_list mirny_). Under this agreement, signatories pledged to recognize the Compacts and support John of Rokycan as the archbishop of Prague, despite the Church of Rome's refusal to recognize him. Simultaneously, the national party withdrew the candidature of Prince Casimir of Poland to allow for the succession of Albert's heir. On February 22, 1440, Queen Elizabeth gave birth to Ladislas. The Bohemians formally acknowledged Ladislas as their king after the crown had been declined by Albert, duke of Bavaria. Ladislas remained in Austria under the guardianship of his uncle, Frederick, duke of Styria, who later became emperor Frederick III.

## Imperial Titles in the Habsburg Monarchy
The designation "empire of Austria" was officially adopted in 1804 when Francis II, the last of the Holy Roman emperors, proclaimed himself Francis I, emperor of Austria. This move was intended to prevent the House of Habsburg from being viewed as inferior to the Bonapartes should the Holy Roman Empire collapse or if Napoleon were elected as successor to the throne of Charlemagne. In 1806, the title "emperor of Austria" replaced "Imperator Romanorum semper Augustus."

This title was often viewed as a purely formal designation that did not accurately reflect the relationship between the Habsburg sovereigns and their states. Slavs and Magyars resisted the style because it implied the superiority of German elements and ignored their national rights. By 1871, following the proclamation of the German empire and a struggle with Prussia in 1866, the title of emperor of Austria was stripped of its broader significance, becoming purely territorial. Furthermore, a 1867 compact with Hungary meant the title no longer fully represented the emperor's relation to all his dominions.

## Titles and Law in Japan
In Japan, the sanctioning of titles was often governed by strict codes and the influence of powerful families. For example, when Emperor Shomu desired to raise his consort, Asuka, to the position of Empress, he faced opposition from Prince Nagaya, the minister of the Left. Prince Nagaya argued that neither law nor precedent sanctioned the nomination of a lady from the Shimbetsu class to the rank of Empress, citing the explicit requirements of the Daiho code, which mandated that an Empress be chosen from among Imperial princesses.

Additionally, the Fujiwara family exerted significant influence over the imperial house. At the recognized instance of the Fujiwara, emperors sometimes abdicated the throne to their heirs, retiring into religious life. These retired sovereigns took the secular title of Da-joko (Great ex-Emperor) and the ecclesiastical title of Ho-o (pontiff).

Regarding the nature of authority, the Japanese polity held the cardinal tenet that the Imperial line must remain unbroken to all eternity and that "the King can do no wrong." This contrasted with Chinese theories of governance, which recognized the right to overturn tyrants. In Japan, the sovereign reigned by virtue of lineage, and the hereditary nobles held their positions based on descent.

## Other Imperial and Ducal Titles
Following the fall of Constantinople in 1453, the grand duke of Moscow, Ivan the Great (1462-1505), claimed the Byzantine throne as its heir and adopted the Byzantine double-headed eagle. His grandson, Ivan IV (Ivan the Terrible), later assumed the imperial title of Cæsar (Tsar).

In the British peerage, dukes rank below princes and princesses of the blood royal and the archbishops of Canterbury and York. While a herald may proclaim a duke as "most high, potent and noble prince," they are not recognized as equals in blood to the crowned or mediatized dukes of the continent. A non-royal duke in the British peerage is styled "the Most Noble" and addressed as "Your Grace."
