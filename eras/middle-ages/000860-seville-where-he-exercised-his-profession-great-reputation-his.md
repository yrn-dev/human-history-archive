# Avenzoar

## Biography and Professional Standing
Avenzoar, also known as Abumeron (Ab[=u] Merw[=a]n 'Abdal-Malik ibn Zuhr), was an Arabian physician who flourished at the beginning of the 12th century. Born in Seville, he practiced his profession there and achieved a great reputation. Avenzoar came from a lineage of medical practitioners, as his ancestors had been celebrated as physicians for several generations. This tradition of excellence continued with his son, who was regarded by Arabians as being even more eminent in the profession than Avenzoar himself.

Avenzoar was a contemporary of Averroes. According to the accounts of Leo Africanus, Averroes learned physic from Avenzoar and attended his lectures.

## Medical Philosophy and Approach
In his professional orientation, Avenzoar aligned more closely with the Rational School, or the Dogmatists, than with the Empirics. He held a great admiration for Galen. In his written works, Avenzoar expressed emphatic protests against the use of superstitious remedies provided by astrologers as well as against quackery.

## Contributions to Anatomy and Treatment
Avenzoar demonstrated significant knowledge of anatomy through his detailed description of an abscess and inflammation of the mediastinum, which occurred in his own person. He was able to provide a diagnosis to distinguish this condition from common pleuritis, as well as from dropsy and abscess of the pericardium.

He also developed ingenious methods for treating cases of palsy of the gullet or obstruction. His proposed treatments included:
*   The use of nutritive enemata, which he strongly recommended.
*   The introduction of food into the stomach via a silver tube, a method he spoke of more favorably than others.
*   The placement of the patient in a tepid bath of nutritious liquids to support strength through cutaneous imbibition, though he did not recommend this specific approach.

## Professional Observations and Writings
The writings of Avenzoar indicate that, during his time, the roles of the apothecary, surgeon, and physician were already viewed as distinct professions. 

He authored a book titled *The Method of Preparing Medicines and Diet*. This work was translated into Hebrew in 1280 and subsequently translated into Latin by Paravicius. Paravicius's version was first printed in Venice in 1490 and has since been published in several editions.
