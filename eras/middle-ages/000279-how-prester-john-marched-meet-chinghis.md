# The March of Prester John to Meet Chinghis Kaan

The conflict between Chinghis Kaan and Prester John was the culmination of a deteriorating relationship between the leader of the Tartars and the prince. The tension escalated from political friction to an open military confrontation that resulted in a massive clash of forces on the plain of Tanduc.

## Origins of the Conflict
The roots of the hostility lay in the Tartars' desire for independence and a personal dispute between the two leaders. Originally, the Tartars were under the authority of Prester John, but as their population multiplied, Prester John feared the trouble they might cause. He attempted to distribute the Tartars across various countries via one of his Barons. In response, the Tartars revolted, ceased paying tribute, and migrated across a desert to a distant northern region to escape his influence.

Later, in the year of Christ 1200, Chinghis Kaan—who had been chosen King of the Tartars in 1187 and had already conquered eight provinces—sent an embassy to Prester John requesting his daughter's hand in marriage. Prester John reacted with rage, viewing the request as impudence. He informed the envoys that Chinghis was his "liegeman and serf," stating he would rather set his daughter on fire than marry her to him, and labeled Chinghis a "rebel and traitor" who deserved death.

## Mobilization of Forces
Upon receiving this "brutal message," Chinghis Kaan was seized with rage and vowed to take revenge for the insult, declaring that Prester John would soon learn if he were a serf or not. Chinghis mustered a host of unprecedented size, sending word to Prester John to prepare his defenses.

Prester John initially treated the threat as a "jest and a trifle," claiming that the Tartars were "no soldiers." However, he eventually marshaled his own forces, assembling a host composed of many different nations, which was described as a "world’s wonder." His goal was to capture and put Chinghis to death.

## The Advance to Tanduc
Chinghis Kaan marched his host to a vast and beautiful plain called Tanduc, which belonged to Prester John. The multitude of the Tartar army was so great that it was impossible to number them. Chinghis rejoiced upon learning that Prester John was approaching, as he considered the plain an ample and fine battle-ground.

Prester John advanced with all his forces and reached the plain of Tanduc, pitching his camp opposite that of Chinghis Kaan. The two camps were separated by a distance of 20 miles. Both armies remained at rest for two days to ensure they were heartier and fresher for the impending battle.

## Divination and the Christian Astrologers
While the armies were stationed at Tanduc, Chinghis Kaan summoned his astrologers, including both Saracens and Christians, to predict the outcome of the battle. While the Saracens were unable to provide a true answer, the Christian astrologers performed a divination using a cane.

They split a cane lengthwise and placed the two halves apart, naming one "Chinghis Kaan" and the other "Prester John." After reading a Psalm from the Psalter and performing incantations, the piece of cane bearing the name of Chinghis Kaan moved without being touched and climbed on top of the piece representing Prester John. This was interpreted as a sign that Chinghis Kaan would be victorious. Delighted by this result, the Kaan thereafter treated the Christians with great respect.

## The Battle and Aftermath
Following the two days of rest, the two hosts engaged in a desperate combat described as the greatest battle ever seen. Although the number of slain on both sides was very great, Chinghis Kaan obtained the victory. Prester John was slain during the battle. Following this victory, Prester John's kingdom passed day by day into the hands of Chinghis Kaan until the entire territory was conquered.
