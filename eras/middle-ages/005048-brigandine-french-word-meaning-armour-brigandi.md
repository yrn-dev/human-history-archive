# BRIGANDINE

## Definition and Composition
Brigandine is a term derived from a French word meaning the armour designed for the *brigandi* or *brigantes*, who were light-armed foot soldiers. During the Middle Ages, this specific type of armour for a foot soldier consisted of a padded tunic made from materials such as leather or canvas. This tunic was lined with rings of iron or scales that were sewn closely together.

## The Brigantes
The term *brigantes* is Celtic for "free, privileged" or "mountaineers." They were a people residing in northern Britain, occupying a territory that spanned from the Belisama (identified by some as the Ribble and by others as the Mersey) in the west to the mouth of the Abus (Humber) in the east. Their lands extended north to the Wall of Antoninus and encompassed Durham, Westmorland, Cumberland, most of Yorkshire, and the entirety of Lancashire, as well as a portion of Northumberland. The chief town of the Brigantes was Eburacum (York).

The Brigantes first encountered the Romans during the reign of Claudius, at which time they were defeated by Publius Ostorius Scapula. Although they submitted to Petillius Cerealis under the reign of Vespasian, they were not fully subdued until the era of Antoninus Pius. Inscriptions have identified a god named Bergans (or Brigans) and an eponymous goddess named Brigantia. Additionally, a branch of the Brigantes settled in the south-east corner of Ireland near the river Birgus (Barrow).
