# Stafford and the Duchy of Buckingham

The history of the Stafford family is closely linked to the evolution of the earldom and duchy of Buckingham. While the origins of the earldom are obscure, some charter evidence suggests it existed under William Rufus. Odericus Vitalis identifies Walter Giffard, lord of Longueville in Normandy, as the first earl, describing him as "Comes Bucchingehamensis" in 1097 and 1102. Following the death of the 2nd earl, Walter Giffard, in 1164, the title was assumed by Richard de Clare, earl of Pembroke, through his wife Rohais, sister of Walter Giffard I; the title died with Richard in 1176.

## Transition to the Stafford Line
The title was revived in 1377 when Thomas of "Woodstock," duke of Gloucester, was created earl of Buckingham during the coronation of Richard II. After Thomas's death, the title of Gloucester went to Thomas le Despenser, while his son Humphrey held only the earldom of Buckingham. Upon Humphrey's death, his sister Anne became countess of Buckingham in her own right. Anne married Edmund Stafford, earl of Stafford. Following her death in 1438, the title passed to her son, Humphrey Stafford, earl of Stafford, who was created duke of Buckingham in 1444.

## Henry Stafford, 2nd Duke of Buckingham
Henry Stafford (1454–1483) was the son of Humphrey Stafford (killed at the first battle of St Albans in 1455) and grandson of the 1st duke, Humphrey (killed at Northampton in 1460). Both father and grandfather fought for Lancaster. Henry's lineage connected him to the royal blood on both sides: his grandmother was Anne, daughter of Thomas, duke of Gloucester (the youngest son of Edward III), and his mother was Margaret, daughter of Edmund Beaufort, 2nd duke of Somerset.

Recognized as duke in 1465, Henry married Catherine Woodville, sister of the queen, in 1466. He was made a knight of the Garter in 1474 and served as high steward at the trial of George, duke of Clarence, in 1478. Following the death of Edward IV, Henry became an ally of Richard, duke of Gloucester. He assisted Richard in obtaining possession of the young king and was rewarded with the offices of chamberlain and justiciar of North and South Wales, as well as constable of the royal castles in the Welsh Marches. 

On June 24, 1483, Henry played a prominent role in the deposition of Edward V, delivering an eloquent speech at the Guildhall urging citizens to accept Richard as king. He served as chamberlain at Richard's coronation and was subsequently confirmed as constable of England and in his powers in Wales. However, in early August, Henry withdrew to Brecon, possibly due to desires for greater rewards or ambitions for the crown.

## Later Successors and the Barony of Stafford
Henry's eldest son, Edward (1478–1521), eventually succeeded him as the 3rd duke after an attainder was removed in 1485. Edward served as lord high constable at the start of Henry VIII's reign but was executed for treason on May 17, 1521, following opposition to Wolsey. The title was forfeited upon his attainder.

Edward's only son, Henry (1501–1563), who had been styled earl of Stafford, was granted his estates back in 1522. In 1547, parliament restored him in blood with the title of Baron Stafford. This line became extinct with Roger, 5th Baron, in 1640. In that same year, the barony of Stafford was granted to William Howard (1614–1680), who was created Viscount Stafford and later beheaded in 1680. His son was created earl of Stafford in 1688, a title that became extinct in 1762. In 1825, the House of Lords established the descent of the 1640 barony in the person of Sir G.W. Jerningham.
