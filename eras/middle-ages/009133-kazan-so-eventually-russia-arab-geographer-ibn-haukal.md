# Bolgari and the Transition to Kazan and Russia

## The City of Bolgari
Bolgari (also referred to as Bolghar or Velikij Gorod in Russian annals) was a significant town located approximately four miles from the left bank of the Volga, at 55 degrees North latitude. It is generally considered to have been the capital of the Bulgarians, a people of Turkish origin who established themselves in that part of Europe between the 5th and 15th centuries. The Bulgars of the Volga may have assimilated Slavonian and Finnish elements over time.

The site is marked today by a village where ruins of old towers and walls survive. Archaeological excavations have uncovered numerous *kurgans* (burial-mounds) containing weapons, utensils, implements, and silver and copper coins. These finds include inscriptions in Turkic, Armenian (dated 557, 984, and 986), and Arabic (dated 1222–1341), with some Arabic inscriptions appearing in Kufic, an epigraphic style. Antiquities collected from the site in 1722 are currently preserved in museums in St Petersburg, Moscow, and Kazan.

## Political History and Decline
The city served as a center for a powerful kingdom that existed from the 5th to the 15th century, spanning the middle Volga and covering territories in North Astrakhan, Saratov, Simbirsk, Samara, and possibly Perm. In the 5th century, the Bulgars conducted raids against the Greeks and attacked the Russians in the Black Sea prairies. 

The city's stability was interrupted by several waves of conquest. It was destroyed by the Mongols in 1238 (some sources cite 1225) and again by Tamerlane in the early 14th century. Following these destructions, Bolgari served as the capital for the khans of the Mongol Golden Horde. By the second half of the 15th century, the city became part of the principality of Kazan. After the fall of Kazan, the territory was annexed to Russia. As Bolgari lost its political and commercial importance, the city of Kazan practically took its place as the regional representative.

## Economy and Trade
Trade was the chief occupation of the Volga Bulgars, with significant commerce conducted with the Russians, the Khazars, Persia, India, and Biarmia (the Urals). Their primary riches included wax, nuts, leather, wool, and furs. 

Bolghar was particularly famous for the production of "Russia leather" (referred to as *Camut* or *Borgal*), a material traditionally said to have been invented by the people of Bolghar. This leather—specifically horse-leather lined with wolf-skin—became a staple article of trade across Asia. The manufacture of this leather remains one of the staple trades of Kazan. Other exports from the region included honey, peltry, and fossil ivory.

## Religious and Cultural Development
The Bulgars were converted to Islam in 922. Prior to this, it is suggested they may have professed Christianity. Following their conversion, they began constructing forts and stone houses, the latter of which were built by Arabian architects. Before this period, Ibn Foslan observed that the population was not entirely nomadic and utilized wooden houses.

The city was a point of interest for Arab travelers and geographers. Old Arab writers viewed the area as nearly the limit of the habitable world, recording wonders regarding the extreme cold and the brief duration of summer nights.

## Ibn Haukal and Arab Geographers
The Arab geographer Ibn Haukal, active between 942 and 970, provided significant documentation of the region. He stated that near the end of the 10th century, Bolgari was a place of 10,000 inhabitants. Ibn Haukal is recognized as one of the foremost Arabian geographers whose materials aided in the compilation of maps, although the maps of his era are described as rudimentary. 

Beyond his accounts of the Volga region, Ibn Haukal's work extended to other geographies; he recorded and mapped the citadels, bazaars, and caravanserais of trading towns in the delta of the Makran region, such as Armel (Armabel).

Other Arabian historians who visited the kingdom beginning in the 10th century include Abu Abdallah Harnati, Abul Hamid Andalusi, and Ibn Foslan. A history of Bolgari, the *Tarikh Bulgar*, is said to have been written in the 12th century by Yakub Ibn Noman, an Arabian cadi of the city, though this text has not been discovered.
