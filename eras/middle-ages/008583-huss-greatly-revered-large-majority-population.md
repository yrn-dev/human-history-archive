# John Huss

John Huss was a learned Czech religious figure and rector of the university of Prague whose teachings and subsequent execution sparked significant religious and civil conflict in Bohemia. His work was influenced by the doctrines of the Oxford teacher Wycliffe, upon which Huss based a series of lectures delivered at the university of Prague around 1396.

## Religious Context in Bohemia
The environment in Bohemia had been primed for church reform during the reign of King Charles, who granted protection to several priests favoring such causes. Pious priests, including Waldhauser and Milic, had previously attacked the immorality of the clergy, weakening the hold of the Church of Rome. This alienation was further increased by the church schism, during which rival pontiffs engaged in medieval theological strife. Consequently, almost the entire Bohemian nation espoused the cause of Huss.

## Writings and Language
Huss contributed significantly to the development of the Bohemian language, writing in both Latin and his native tongue. His most important Bohemian work, written in 1412, is the *Vyklad viry, desatera Boziho prikazani, a patere* (exposition of the creed, the ten commandments and the Lord's Prayer). Other notable Bohemian works include:
* *Postilla* (a collection of sermons)
* *O poznani cesty prave k spaseni* (the true road to salvation)
* *O svatokupectvi* (on simony)
* A large collection of letters, including touching writings composed while in prison.

Huss is considered a successor to the "forerunners of Huss," such as Thomas of Stitny (c. 1331-1401), who had previously written on religious and philosophical subjects in the national language.

## Trial and Execution
In 1412, the church excommunicated Huss due to his teachings. During the Council of Constance (1414-1418), which sought to reunite Christendom and end the Great Schism, Huss was decoyed to the council under a promise of safe conduct provided by Sigismund, king of the Romans and brother of Wenceslas.

During his trial for heresy, Huss was ordered to recant certain opinions. He refused to do so unless he was convinced of his error by logical argument or the authority of Scripture. When told it was his duty to recant regardless of conviction if required by superiors, he maintained his refusal. Despite the Emperor's safe conduct, he was declared a heretic and burnt alive on July 6, 1415. He is described as a martyr for the free conscience and free intelligence of mankind.

## Aftermath and the Hussite Wars
The execution of Huss led to an immediate outbreak of civil war in Bohemia, as he was greatly revered by the large majority of the population. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. This document protested his execution, describing Huss as a "good, just and catholic man" who had been known for many years by his fame, conduct, and life, and who had been convicted of no offence. 

The nobles further declared that anyone claiming heresy existed in Bohemia were "sons of the devil" and "liars," stating they would defend the law of Jesus Christ and its humble preachers at the cost of their blood. This protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church and marked the beginning of the Hussite wars.

## The Hussite Movement
The resulting anti-Roman or Hussite movement was largely democratic, which led to the prevalence of the national language over Latin in the period's writings. The movement split into different factions:
* **The Taborites:** The extreme party whose writings were entirely destroyed by the Jesuits after the battle of the White Hill in 1620.
* **The Calixtines (or Utraquists):** The more moderate party. Some of their works survived, including *Lives of the Priests of Tabor* and *Of the Great Torment of the Holy Church*, both of which were hostile toward the Taborite community.

The influence of Huss's spirit persisted into later centuries; for example, in 1517, Martin Luther refused to recant his views unless convinced by Scripture or logical argument, an act described as being entirely in the spirit of Huss.
