# Legend of Good Women

The *Legend of Good Women* is a work by Chaucer that served as a literary influence for later English authors, most notably Osbern Bokenam.

## Influence on Osbern Bokenam
Osbern Bokenam (1393?–1447?), an English author and "Suffolke man" known as frere Austyn of Stoke Clare, wrote a series of thirteen legends focusing on holy women and maidens. Bokenam identified himself as a follower of Lydgate and Chaucer, and he specifically had Chaucer's *Legend of Good Women* in mind when composing his own works. 

Bokenam wrote his poems plainly in the Suffolk speech, justifying his lack of poetic decoration by stating that the "finest flowers" had already been plucked by authors such as Gower, Lydgate, and Chaucer. His legends were primarily written in stanzas of seven or eight lines, with nine of the thirteen legends featuring prologues.

## Sources and Composition of Bokenam's Legends
While influenced by Chaucer, Bokenam's chief source—though not his only one—was the *Legenda Aurea* written by Jacobus de Voragine, the archbishop of Genoa, whom Bokenam refers to as "Januence." 

Bokenam's works included dedications to various individuals:
* The first legend, *Vita Scae Margaretae, virginis et martiris*, was written for Thomas Burgh, a monk at Cambridge.
* Other legends were dedicated to pious ladies who wanted the history of the saints who shared their names.

A unique copy of Bokenam's work is preserved in the Arundel MS. 327 at the British Museum. This manuscript was finished in 1447 and presented by the scribe, Thomas Burgh, to an unnamed convent for the purpose of remembering him and his sister, Dame Betrice Burgh.

## Later Editions and Related Texts
Bokenam's poems were edited in 1835 for the Roxburghe Club under the title *Lyvys of Seyntys*. They were also edited by Dr. Carl Horstmann in 1883 as *Osbern Bokenams Legenden* within E. Kolbing's *Altengl. Bibliothek*, vol. i. 

Both of these editions contain a dialogue in English and Latin sourced from Dugdale's *Monasticon Anglicanum*. This dialogue takes place at the grave of Dame Johan of Acres between a "Frere answerynge" and a "Secular asking," and it details the lineal descent of the lords of the honour of Clare from 1248 to 1356.
