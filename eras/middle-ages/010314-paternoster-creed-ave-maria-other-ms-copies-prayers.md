# Paternoster, Creed, Ave Maria and MS. Copies of Prayers

The production and distribution of devotional texts, including the Paternoster (the Lord's Prayer), the Creed, and the Ave Maria, have historically been driven by the spread of Christianity and the subsequent demand for sacred books, missals, and volumes for private and church use.

## Trade and Production in London and Oxford
Prior to the Reformation, the sale of devotional materials was a established trade in London. Text writers and stationers (_stacyoneres_) operated in the neighborhood of St Paul's, selling various copies of prayers, including the Paternoster, Creed, Ave Maria, and the ABC. In 1403, these stationers formed a gild. Some of these traders maintained stalls or stations built directly against the walls of the cathedral.

A similar system of book supply existed at the university in Oxford. "Sworn" university stationers, such as John More, held a significant monopoly on the supply of books to pupils. Because many students could not afford to purchase books and instruction was primarily oral, a large number of books constantly changed hands. The university implemented statutes in 1373 to protect this lawful business from non-sworn booksellers, enacting that no bookseller other than two sworn stationers (or their deputies) could sell any book exceeding half a mark in value.

## The Disciplina Arcani
In the early Christian church, the communication of the Lord's Prayer and the Creed was treated as a solemn rite. This practice, known as the _Disciplina arcani_, required candidates to learn the Creed by heart without writing it down. According to the instructions of Cyril of Jerusalem and Clement of Alexandria (before the year 200), these texts were not to be divulged to unbaptized persons or pagans.

This tradition of secrecy mirrored pagan mysteries, where sacred objects were only viewed after scrupulous purifications. By the 3rd century, this tendency toward mystification extended to hiding the gospels. While some suggest this was a "make-believe" or rhetorical exercise by 4th and 5th-century Fathers to out-trump pagan _Mystae_, the practice was akin to primitive religious scruples against revealing powerful names or magic formulas to the profane.

## Literary and Musical Adaptations
Devotional prayers and creeds have frequently been incorporated into literary and musical works. The poet Dante produced a collection known as the _Canzoniere_ (with the best edition published by Fraticelli), which includes paraphrases in _terza rima_ of the Credo, the Ave Maria, the Lord's Prayer, the ten commandments, and the seven sacraments.

In the realm of music, the Bohemian composer Karel Bendl (1838–1897) composed two songs to the Ave Maria, in addition to other choral works and masses.

## Other Historical References
Devotional prayers have appeared in various other contexts throughout history:
* **Bell Inscriptions:** Church bells were often cast with monkish Latin inscriptions. These included prayers to the Virgin or for the soul of the donor. For example, a bell named Maria was inscribed "Sum Rosa pulsata mundique Maria vocata" (I, Maria, am struck and called the Rose of the world).
* **Personal Accounts:** The English poet John Lydgate (c. 1370–c. 1451) described his youth in his _Testament_, depicting himself as a boy who "threw creed and paternoster at the cock."
* **War-time Devotion:** During war, spiritual cravings led to the printing of sermons in tens and hundreds of thousands of copies for distribution in trenches, where both Catholics and Protestants utilized prayer as a spiritual weapon.
