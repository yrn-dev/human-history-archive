# Hussites

The Hussites were a religious and national movement in Bohemia that emerged in the early 15th century. The movement was characterized by a struggle between "priest and anti-priest," resulting in a series of religious wars that contributed to the breaking-up of Christendom.

## Origins and the Trial of John Huss
The roots of the conflict lay in a weakening of the Roman Church's hold on Bohemia during the reign of King Charles, as pious priests like Waldhauser and Milic attacked the immorality of the clergy. This alienation was further increased by the church schism, during which rival pontiffs engaged in theological strife. Consequently, nearly the entire Bohemian nation supported the cause of John Huss, who preached Wycliffism at Prague starting in 1398. While King Wenceslas was irresolute regarding these disputes, Queen Sophia openly favored Huss, serving as his confessor for a time.

Huss traveled to the council of Constance under a letter of safe conduct provided by Sigismund, king of the Romans and brother to Wenceslas. However, he was declared a heretic and burnt on July 6, 1415. Jerome of Prague, a colleague of Huss, was burnt the following year.

## The Hussite Wars and Crusades
The execution of Huss led to immediate civil war in Bohemia. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. In this document, they defended Huss as a "good, just and catholic man" and declared that those claiming heresy existed in Bohemia were "liars, vile traitors and calumniators." This protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church.

In 1419, a Hussite insurrection began. In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade to destroy the Hussites, Wycliffites, and other heretics in Bohemia. This attracted soldiers of fortune from across Europe. Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites—who conducted their affairs on extreme democratic lines—repelled the crusaders. Although the crusaders beleaguered Prague, they failed to take the city. Sigismund, who claimed the Bohemian crown, occupied the castle of Prague on June 30, 1420, viewing the city as a heretical community because its inhabitants accepted other evangelical truths and used the sacred chalice. However, the Taborites, led by Ziska, repulsed an attack on the Vitkov hill, and Sigismund was further defeated at the battle of the Vysehrad on November 1, 1420.

A total of five crusades were launched against the Hussites. The second, third, and fourth all failed. Internal dissensions eventually weakened the Hussites, allowing a fifth crusade to cross the frontier in 1431 under Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg. The Hussites eventually came to terms with the church in 1436.

## Internal Divisions and Factions
The Hussite movement was split into different factions:
*   **Utraquists (or Calixtines):** The more moderate wing of the movement.
*   **Taborites:** The extreme Hussite party.
*   **Bohemian Brethren:** A later group of extreme Hussites.

The conflict also manifested in urban violence. In Kuttenberg, a town with a fanatically Catholic and mainly German mining population, reprisals for Hussite outrages in Prague led to the burning, beheading, or throwing of Hussites into disused mine shafts; approximately 1,600 people are said to have perished. In 1420, Emperor Sigismund used Kuttenberg as a base to attack the Taborites, but Zizka took the city.

## Later Developments and Legacy
The Hussite movement emphasized the use of the national language over Latin in its writings. However, much of this literature was systematically destroyed by the Jesuits after the battle of the White Hill in 1620, including all writings of the Taborites. Some works by the Utraquists and Archbishop John of Rokycan's *Postilla* survived.

In the late 15th century, the struggle continued through political succession. George of Podebrad's successor, Prince Vladislav of Poland (elected May 27, 1471), promised to maintain the "Compacts" and did not prevent Bohemians from receiving communion in both kinds. Despite this, Vladislav's policy was generally reactionary. He appointed government officials in Prague from the section of the Utraquist party closest to Rome and oversaw the severe persecution of the Bohemian Brethren. This led to serious riots in Prague, where advanced Hussites stormed three town halls, and nobles of the same faith formed a league to protect themselves. Peace was temporarily restored following a meeting of the estates at Kutna Hora in 1485.
