# Bretone

The term *Bretone* (also appearing as *Bretoun* or *Britton*) refers to an early legal summary of the laws of England written in the French tongue. The work is purported to have been written by the command of King Edward I.

## Authorship and Origins
The authorship and origin of *Britton* have been the subject of significant dispute. One theory attributes the work to John le Breton, the bishop of Hereford, based on a passage found in some manuscripts of the history of Matthew of Westminster. However, this theory faces difficulties because the bishop of Hereford died in 1275, while the text of *Britton* contains allusions to statutes passed after that date. Specifically, the work references the statute *Quia emptores terrarum*, which was passed in 1290.

An alternative opinion held by Selden suggests that the book was a royal abridgment of the great work on the customs and laws of England written by Henry de Bracton, the last of the chief justiciaries. Selden noted that Bracton's name was sometimes spelled as "Bretton" or "Bratton" in the fine Rolls. Despite this theory, the two works differ in their arrangement, and only a small portion of Bracton's work is incorporated into *Britton*.

## Historical Manuscripts and Records
An early 14th-century manuscript of the work, which was once owned by Selden and is currently held in the Cambridge university library, titles the work *Summa de legibus Anglie que vocatur Bretone*. 

The work is also mentioned in the will of Andrew Horn, who served as the learned chamberlain of the city of London. In 1329, Horn bequeathed "a book called Bretoun" to the chamber of the Guildhall, along with another volume titled *Mirroir des Justices*.
