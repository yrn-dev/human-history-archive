# Notaries and Legal Practitioners in History

The role of the notary and the advocate has evolved across different eras and jurisdictions, serving as essential components of the judicial and administrative systems in Europe and the Mediterranean. These roles have ranged from drafting legal pleas and validating wills to serving in high-ranking diplomatic and ecclesiastical positions.

## The Notary in Legal and Civil Administration
In certain historical legal systems, the notary played a critical role in the drafting of legal documents. In cases involving lawsuits, while the plaintiff preferred their own plea, the plea was required to be in writing; the notary assisted in the drafting of these documents. 

In Italy, by the middle of the 13th century, the presence of a notary was considered sufficient to make a deed valid. During this period, it became common for deeds—including wills—to lack the signatures of witnesses or the parties involved, relying instead on the signature of the notary. Notaries utilized a "Tabellionato," which was a fanciful, distinctive monogram adopted by each individual notary, placed before their name. While the use of marks or signs manual generally declined after the 12th century in favor of seals, the *signum manuale* persisted among notaries.

## Notaries and Advocates in the Papal Court and State
The professional training of advocates and notaries often led to significant political and religious influence. Benedetto Gaetano, who became Pope Boniface VIII (reigned 1294–1303), studied civil and canon law in Italy and possibly Paris. After receiving appointments to canonicates in France and Todi in June 1260, he served as an advocate and subsequently as a notary at the papal court. His career progressed through diplomatic missions and the Sacred College, eventually leading to his election as pope in Naples on December 24, 1294.

Similarly, the Dominican pope Benedict XI (reigned 1303–1304) was the son of a notary. Born in 1240 at Treviso, he rose through the Dominican order to become its general in 1296 before being elected pope on October 22, 1303.

In France, legal practitioners often transitioned into high state offices. Jerome Bignon (1589–1656), a Parisian lawyer, served as advocate-general to the grand council and later as advocate-general to the parlement of Paris in 1626. He also served as a councillor of state and was eventually appointed by Richelieu to manage the royal library in 1642.

## Legal Reform and the French Parlement
During the late 18th century, the role of the advocate was central to the political upheavals in France. Adrien Duport (1759–1798), a Parisian politician and learned jurist, was an influential advocate in the parlement. He became a prominent figure in opposition to ministers Lomenie de Brienne and Calonne. 

Following his election to the states-general in 1789, Duport contributed to the organization of the French judiciary during the Constituent Assembly. In a notable report dated March 29, 1790, he advocated for the implementation of trial by jury, although he was unsuccessful in establishing the jury system for civil cases. Duport later served as the president of the criminal tribunal of Paris before his eventual exile and death in Switzerland.
