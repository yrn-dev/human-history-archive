# Machicolations and Fortified Walls

In medieval architecture, machicolations served as a critical defensive feature of fortifications. A machicolation is defined as an opening located between a wall and a parapet. This structure is created by corbelling out the parapet, which allows defenders to throw stones, melted lead, and other materials down upon assailants positioned below.

## Architectural Components
The construction of machicolations relies on the use of corbels. A corbel is a piece of stone that juts out from a wall to support a super-incumbent weight. In the context of English and French castles, the corbels used to carry machicolations typically consisted of four courses. This differs from corbels used for balconies in France and Italy, which were sometimes richly carved and of great size, or those used for corbel tables, which are projecting moulded string courses.

## Examples in French Fortifications
Several cities in south-eastern and south-western France retain examples of these defensive structures:

*   **Avignon:** The city is encircled by ramparts built by the popes during the 14th century. These walls are described as being of great strength and are surmounted by machicolated battlements. The fortification system is further strengthened by thirty-nine massive towers and several gateways, three of which date from the 14th century.
*   **Carpentras:** Among the remnants of the town's old fortifications, the Porte d'Orange survives as a gateway surmounted by a fine machicolated tower.
*   **Cahors:** This city features the Pont Valentré, an early 14th-century structure regarded as the finest fortified bridge of the middle ages in France. The bridge is defended by high machicolated towers at both ends, with a less elaborate tower surmounting the center pier.

## Other Fortified Structures
While machicolations are prominent in French medieval examples, other historical sites demonstrate varying levels of wall strength and fortification:

*   **Bijapur:** In British India, the citadel built by Yusuf Adil Shah is noted for being of great strength and constructed from massive materials. It is encompassed by a ditch 100 yards wide. The surrounding fort, completed by Ali Adil Shah in 1566, features a wall 6 meters in circumference, ranging from 30 to 50 feet in height. This wall is strengthened by ninety-six massive bastions and ten additional bastions at the gateways, with a battlemented curtained wall approximately 10 feet high running between the bastions.
*   **Cairo:** The city's three great gates—Bab-en-Nasr, Bab-el-Futuh, and Bab-Zuwela—are characterized by simple and massive designs that rely on fine ashlar masonry for their effect.
*   **Sarai:** In the empire of Kipchak, the Khan's Palace was a great edifice encompassed by a wall flanked with towers.
