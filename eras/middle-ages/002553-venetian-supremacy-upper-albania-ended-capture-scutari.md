# Venetian Presence and Transitions in Albania and Epirus

The historical trajectory of the regions now known as Albania and Epirus is characterized by successive waves of migration, the rise and fall of local kingdoms, and the shifting dominance of external powers, including the Venetians and the Turks.

## Early Ethnographic and Political Foundations
The ethnographic composition of the Balkan Peninsula was significantly altered beginning in the 3rd century A.D. by a great Slavonic immigration that continued for four centuries. During this period, the Byzantine empire held nominal or actual possession of regions south of the Danube. While much of the native Thraco-Illyrian population in the interior became romanized and spoke Latin, the aboriginal Illyrian element in Albania maintained its ancient language in the mountains, eventually forcing back the immigrant race.

In the 7th century, Serbo-Croats invaded northern Albania and other north-western regions, assimilating or expelling the Illyrian population. Earlier, in the third century B.C., an Illyrian kingdom existed on the Adriatic coast with its capital at Skodra (Scutari), which gained prominence through reckless piracies.

## Regional Geography and Tribal Structure
The territory of Albania is historically divided into distinct regions: Upper, Middle, and Lower Albania (the latter also referred to as Epirus). Upper Albania is characterized by impracticable territory and narrow means of habitation. Because of these geographical difficulties, many resident tribes in Upper Albania were considered free and paid no tribute to the Turkish government, as the cost for Pachas to extort such payments exceeded the potential gain. 

Middle Albania does not present the same geographical difficulties, and consequently, no such exemptions from tribute were allowed. The tribes of these regions, particularly the Illyrians, were historically described as poor, fierce, and formidable in battle, often selling their military service for hire.

## Venetian and Turkish Transitions in Epirus
The political control of Epirus underwent several shifts following the Latin capture of Constantinople in 1204. The region was held by various figures, including Michel Angelus Comnenus and his brother Theodore. By the beginning of the 15th century, Charles II Tocco, lord of Cephalonia and Zante, was recognized as the Despot of Epirus by Emperor Manuel Comnenus, though his family lost this possession to Murad (Amurath) II in 1431.

In 1443, Scanderbeg, the king of Albania, seized a considerable portion of Epirus. Following Scanderbeg's death, the territory fell under the power of the Venetians. Eventually, control passed from the Venetians to the Turks, under whose dominion the region remained.

## Strategic Ports and Urban Decline
Durazzo (ancient Epidamnus and Dyrrachium) serves as a primary example of the region's urban evolution. Located about 50 miles south of Scutari on the Bay of Durazzo, it functioned as a seaport and the capital of the sanjak of Durazzo. Despite its history as a seat for a Greek metropolitan and a Roman Catholic archbishop, the city experienced significant decline. Its Byzantine citadel fell into ruins, its walls became dilapidated, and its once-safe harbor gradually silted up. By 1900, its population was approximately 5,000, with its economy relying on the export of skins, pottery, wheat, barley, and olive oil.

## Broader Venetian-Ottoman Conflicts
The Venetian struggle for influence in the region was part of a wider conflict with the Ottoman Empire. In 1466, the Venetians occupied Athens but were unable to capture the Acropolis. Later, in September 1687, under Francesco Morosini, the Venetians attacked Athens again. During the bombardment of the Acropolis, a powder magazine in the Parthenon exploded, causing the building to be rent asunder. The Venetians used materials from ancient edifices to repair the walls before withdrawing the following year when the Turks set fire to the city.

At sea, the Venetians and Turks continued to clash. A Venetian victory off Chios in May 1657 dealt a severe blow to Turkish sea-power, though the Ottomans later restored their prestige through victories in the Dardanelles and the capture of Tenedos on August 31.
