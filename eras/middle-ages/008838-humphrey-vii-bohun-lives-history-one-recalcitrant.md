# The Bohun Family

The Bohuns were a family of significant importance in English history during the 13th and 14th centuries. The family name originated from a village located in the Cotentin, situated between the estuary of the Vire and Coutances. While the family arrived in England at or shortly after the Norman Conquest, the details of their early history in the country remain obscure.

## Rise to Prominence
The foundation of the family's greatness is attributed to Humphrey III, who served as a *dapifer* (steward) in the royal household during the later years of Henry I. Humphrey III married the daughter of Milo of Gloucester. During the reign of Stephen, he occupied an ambiguous position, initially siding with the king before shifting his support to the empress. He remained loyal to Henry II through various changes and fought against the rebels of East Anglia at Farnham in 1173. Humphrey III lived until 1187.

Humphrey III outlived his eldest son, Humphrey IV, and was succeeded in the family estates by his grandson, Henry. Henry's mother, Margaret, was the sister of William the Lion, creating a connection to the royal house of Scotland that likely aided Henry in obtaining the earldom of Hereford from John in 1199.

## The Marcher Barons and Political Conflict
From the time of Henry, the Bohuns became leading figures among the Marcher barons, as their lands were primarily located on the Welsh Marches. Henry de Bohun was one of the twenty-five barons elected by his peers to enforce the terms of the Great Charter. However, during the subsequent civil war, he fought alongside Louis and was captured at the battle of Lincoln in 1217. He died on a pilgrimage on June 1, 1220, after taking the cross.

His son and heir, Humphrey V, initially returned to a path of loyalty. Before 1239, he was permitted to inherit the earldom of Essex from his maternal uncle, William de Mandeville. By 1258, however, Humphrey V shifted his allegiance from the royal cause to the baronial cause, serving as a nominee of the opposition on the committee of twenty-four appointed during the Oxford parliament to reform the administration. He eventually returned to royal allegiance following the alliance between Montfort and Llewelyn of North Wales.

## Later Influence and the Earldom of Hereford
The Bohuns held the earldom of Hereford and Essex, and with these titles came the office of Lord High Constable. This office, originally the master of the horse and commander of the royal armies, had been granted as a grand serjeanty to Milo of Gloucester by the empress Maud and passed to the Bohuns via his heiress.

In the late 13th and early 14th centuries, the family continued to engage in political struggles against the monarchy. Humphrey Bohun, earl of Hereford, collaborated with the earl of Norfolk to prevent the collection of an aid for war and forced King Edward I to confirm the charters in 1297 and again in 1301. Regarding this period, the historian Stubbs characterized Bohun and Bigod as "degenerate sons of mighty fathers," suggesting they were greater in their opportunities than in their patriotism.

In the 14th century, Humphrey Bohun, earl of Hereford, became involved in a dispute over lands in Glamorganshire. He supported the Mowbrays in their conflict against the younger Hugh le Despenser. This dispute was part of a broader hostility among English barons toward the Despensers, which eventually led Edward II to consent to the banishment of the Despensers in 1321.

## Legacy and Succession
The office of Lord High Constable eventually descended from the Bohuns to the Staffords, dukes of Buckingham, through a coheiress of the Bohuns. This office remained with the Staffords until the reign of Henry VIII, when it merged into the crown following the attainder of Edward Stafford, the third duke of Buckingham.

Additionally, the Bohun family held manors in Essex. One such manor provided the title "Lord Rochford," which was used by Thomas, the 7th earl (of the Wiltshire/Irish line), after the statute of attainder against him was annulled by the first parliament of Henry VII. Thomas died without male issue in 1515, leaving two daughters and co-heirs: Anne, who married Sir James St. Leger, and Margaret, who married Sir William Boleyn of Blickling. Margaret was the mother of Sir Thomas Boleyn, who was created earl of Wiltshire and Ormonde in 1529.
