# Estate Acquisition and Land Ownership in World History

The acquisition and management of great estates have played significant roles in the political and social structures of various civilizations, often serving as a source of power, familial wealth, or a catalyst for conflict.

## Papal Estates and the Colonna Conflict
During the papacy of Boniface VIII (who served from 1294 to 1303), the pursuit of land for familial benefit became a point of contention. Born into a noble family at Anagni, Boniface VIII was characterized by avarice and arrogance, asserting the lordship of the papacy over all global kingdoms. His attempt to build up great estates for his own family resulted in the Colonna becoming most of his enemies.

## Land Tenure in Post-Conquest England
Following the Conquest, the redistribution of estates saw very few Englishmen retain land of any importance. Notable chief landowners during this period included Odo, the bishop of Bayeux, and Walter Giffard, the first earl of Buckingham. In Derbyshire, the Domesday Survey identified Henry de Ferrers as the greatest landholder, owning nearly the entire modern hundred of Appletree. However, these estates were later forfeited by Robert, earl of Derby, during the reign of Henry III. 

Other significant holdings included the "Honour of Peverel," held by William Peverel; these possessions were forfeited to the crown in 1155 after the younger Peverel was disinherited for poisoning the earl of Chester. Despite these trends, a few families maintained unbroken lines of descent from Domesday tenants, including the families of Gresley, Shirley, and the Hampdens. Elfin, an under-tenant of Henry de Ferrers, also retained considerable property and became the ancestor of the Brailsford family in Derbyshire.

## Japanese Estates and the Bakufu System
In Japan, the concept of great estates (shoen) and manors was central to the social order. The Fujiwara family exerted significant influence, and later, the followers of Yoritomo acquired estates. However, the transition of society during the Hojo rule led to economic instability for some veterans of the Bakufu. These individuals, unable to gain riches through military action during peaceful times, were forced to mortgage their estates to fund extravagant lifestyles involving rich garments, fine buildings, and costly banquets. 

Between 1252 and 1330, the Bakufu issued edicts eight times to curb these excesses, prohibiting expensive garments and vetoing costly residences, though these attempts had only partial success. Other figures associated with great estates include Sasaki Takayori (d. 1520) and the estates of Sasaki Mochikiyo.

## Private and Reclaimed Estates
Land ownership has also evolved through private purchase and engineering. In Scotland, the estate containing Balmoral Castle originally belonged to the Farquharsons of Inverey. It was acquired by Sir Robert Gordon, and the lease was later disposed of in 1848 to the prince consort, who purchased the entire estate in 1852 and bequeathed it to Queen Victoria.

In Italy, the reclamation of the Lago di Fucino bed created new agricultural land. After a company initially undertook the drainage, the rights were purchased in 1854 by Prince Giulio Torlonia, a Roman banker. Torlonia funded the project, and by 1876, the lake was drained at a cost of some L1,700,000. The resulting reclaimed area, measuring 12-1/2 miles long and 7 miles broad, is cultivated by families from the Torlonia estates.

## Domestic Estate Management
On a smaller scale, the acquisition of estates served as a means of financial stability. Martin Luther, who married Catherine von Bora in 1525, initially faced straitened circumstances. However, through gifts from princes and his own salary, Luther was able to purchase the small estate of Zulsdorf from his wife's brother. Catherine von Bora contributed to the estate's viability through diligent housekeeping, which included stocking a fishpond, renting other gardens, farming, and keeping poultry and pigs.
