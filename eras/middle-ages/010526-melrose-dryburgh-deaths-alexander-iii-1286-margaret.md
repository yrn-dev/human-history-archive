# Melrose, Dryburgh, and the Deaths of Alexander III and Margaret

## The Border Abbeys of Melrose and Dryburgh
During the 12th century, several abbeys were established in the border regions of Scotland, including those at Jedburgh, Kelso, Melrose, and Dryburgh. These institutions were situated in a region that experienced strife and lawlessness at frequent intervals over a period of 1,500 years. 

Melrose Abbey is noted as the resting place of the heart of Robert Bruce, although his bones are interred at Dunfermline Abbey. The border abbeys, including Melrose and Dryburgh, suffered significant damage during English expeditions in 1544 and 1545, which resulted in their destruction.

Dryburgh is also associated with later Scottish nobility; David Steuart Erskine, the 11th earl of Buchan (1742–1829), an antiquary and founder of the Scottish Society of Antiquaries in 1780, died at his residence in Dryburgh in April 1829.

## The Death of Alexander III
Alexander III, King of Scotland, died in March 1286. His death, and the subsequent lack of a direct heir, created a political vacuum that plunged the country into wars of independence and succession. The narrative poem *The Brus* opens its account of Scottish history at the time of Alexander III's death in 1286.

Alexander III is buried in Dunfermline Abbey, where he lies alongside his first wife and their sons, Alexander and David. Earlier in his reign, in 1255, Alexander III had been liberated from the Comyns by a party led by Patrick Dunbar, the 7th earl, who was subsequently named guardian of the king and queen by the Treaty of Roxburgh that same year.

## Margaret the Maid of Norway
Following the death of Alexander III, the crown was intended to pass to his granddaughter, Margaret, known as the "Maid of Norway." Margaret was the daughter of King Eric II of Norway and the daughter of Alexander III. In 1284, the estates of Scotland had decided that the crown should pass to her if Alexander died childless. Upon his death in March 1286, Margaret became the titular queen of Scotland.

The English King Edward I closely monitored Scottish affairs, and in 1289, a marriage was arranged between the infant queen and Edward's son (the future Edward II). Margaret sailed from Norway toward Scotland but died around the end of September 1290 after reaching the Orkneys. This event was officially communicated in a letter dated October 7, 1290.

The death of Margaret in 1290, following the death of Alexander III in 1286, further contributed to the instability of the country, leading to prolonged periods of border disturbance until the union of the crowns in 1603. Some mystery followed her passing; around the year 1300, a woman from Leipzig claimed to be Queen Margaret, but this individual was burned as a witch at Bergen.
