# Border Families and Related Names

## The Border Families of the Anglo-Scottish Marches
Between the 12th century and the union of the crowns in 1603, the border regions between England and Scotland were characterized by frequent disturbance, strife, and lawlessness. While the area saw major military engagements—such as the battles of Halidon Hill (1333), Flodden (1513), and Ancrum Moor (1544)—much of the instability was driven by family feuds and raids.

Among the families responsible for fomenting these raids and feuds were the Armstrongs, Eliots, Grahams, Johnstones, and Maxwells. These activities led to several serious encounters, most notably the combat at Dryfe Sands in 1593, the Raid of Reidswire in 1575, and the encounter at Arkenholme (Langholm) in 1455. To counter the threat posed by these families, as well as other "mosstroopers" and "freebooters" who remained a menace until the 17th century, castles were constructed on both sides of the border for defense and to overawe the lawless elements.

## Graham: Military and Political Activity
In the late 17th century, an individual named Graham operated in Scotland as a soldier, judge, prosecutor, and spy. Following the murder of Archbishop Sharp in 1679, Graham was tasked with pursuing rebels near Glasgow. On June 1, he attacked Covenanters at Drumclog; however, the royal cavalry was routed by a Covenanter charge, and Graham only narrowly escaped. This event marked the beginning of an open rebellion.

Graham's political career involved a bitter quarrel with Sir John Dalrymple. While Dalrymple was condemned to imprisonment and a fine for interfering with Graham's proceedings, Graham was acquitted by the privy council of charges regarding oppression and exaction. In December 1682, Graham was appointed colonel of a new Scottish regiment. By May 1683, he was nominated to the privy council of Scotland, having successfully obtained a royal grant for property previously belonging to the earl of Lauderdale (Maitland of Hatton).
