# Auvergne and the Plantagenet Suzerainty

## Geography and Early History
Auvergne was formerly a province of France, corresponding to the departments of Puy-de-Dome and Cantal, as well as the arrondissement of Brioude in Haute-Loire. The region is characterized by fertile valleys, such as the Limagne, vast pasture-lands, numerous medicinal springs, and mountains of volcanic origin, including Mont Dore, Puy de Dome, and Plomb du Cantal. The population of the region has retained strongly-marked Celtic characteristics into the present day.

In the era of Caesar, the region was home to the Arverni, a powerful confederation of Gauls. Their most famous chieftain, Vercingetorix, fought against the Romans. During the Roman Empire, the district was known as Arvernia and formed part of Prima Aquitania. It subsequently shared the fortunes of Aquitaine during the Carolingian and Merovingian periods.

## The Countship and Plantagenet Influence
Auvergne served as the seat of a separate countship prior to the end of the 8th century, with William the Pious becoming the first hereditary count in 886. The political status of the countship shifted significantly through marriage; when Eleanor of Aquitaine married Henry Plantagenet, the countship passed under the suzerainty of the kings of England.

However, this transition coincided with a division of the domain caused by a family quarrel. William VII, known as the Young (1145-1168), was despoiled of a portion of his domain by his uncle, William VIII, known as the Old. This action was supported by Henry II of England. Consequently, William VII retained only the region bounded by the Coux and the Allier. By the end of the 13th century, this specific district became known as the Dauphine d'Auvergne.

## Royal Intervention and the Four Lordships
The internal conflict between the counts of Auvergne led to the intervention of Philip Augustus, the king of France. Philip Augustus succeeded in taking possession of a large part of the country, which was then annexed to the royal domains under the name Terre d'Auvergne. During this process, Robert I, the bishop of Clermont (1195-1227), was granted the lordship of the town of Clermont as the price for his concurrence with the king. This lordship later became a countship. These events established the four great historic lordships of Auvergne.

## Later Administrative Transitions
The Terre d'Auvergne first served as an appanage for Count Alphonse of Poitiers between 1241 and 1271. In 1360, King John II erected the territory into a duchy in the peerage of France (duche-pairie) for his son, John. The title subsequently passed to the house of Bourbon in 1416 through John's daughter.
