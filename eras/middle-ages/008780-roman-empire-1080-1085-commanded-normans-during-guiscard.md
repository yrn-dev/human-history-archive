# Norman Attacks on the Eastern Roman Empire (1080–1085)

The period between 1080 and 1085 was marked by a significant military offensive launched by the Normans against the Eastern Roman Empire. Led by Robert Guiscard, the Duke of Apulia and Calabria, this campaign sought to expand Norman influence eastward, driven by Guiscard's ambition to create or seize a position of power within the Roman empire of the East.

## The Ambitions of Robert Guiscard
Robert Guiscard, who had entered Italy as a pilgrim adventurer and begun his career as a brigand in Calabria, eventually established himself as a powerful leader. Having conquered Sicily from the Saracens between 1060 and 1090, Guiscard found the possessions of Calabria and Apulia inadequate for his ambitions. 

To provide a justification for his invasion, Guiscard utilized a pretender who claimed to be Michael, the expelled emperor. This individual, described by both Greeks and Latins as an impostor—possibly a monk or a palace domestic—was presented at Salerno with imperial titles. Pope Gregory VII supported this effort, exhorting Catholics to fight for the restoration of this figure. Guiscard used the pretender to give a "decent color" to his military arms, intending for the impostor to fade into obscurity once the conquest was achieved.

## The Invasion and the Siege of Durazzo
The Norman campaign began with a crossing from Brindisi to Epirus. Before the general embarkation, Guiscard dispatched his eldest son, Bohemund, with fifteen galleys to survey the coast, secure a harbor near Vallona, and threaten the Isle of Corfu. The ease with which these forces landed highlighted the decay of Greek naval power.

Guiscard then led his fleet and army from Corfu to the siege of Durazzo, a city regarded as the western key of the empire. Durazzo was defended by George Palaeologus, a patrician, and a garrison composed of Macedonians and Albanians. The invasion was plagued by misfortune; a sudden storm of wind and snow shattered masts and oars, causing shipwrecks and the loss of most provisions. Guiscard was forced to halt for seven days on an adjacent cape to recover his losses and revive his soldiers' spirits. Despite these setbacks, Guiscard successfully captured Durazzo in 1082.

## The Role of Bohemund and the Comnenian Response
During the campaign, Bohemund served under his father and commanded the Norman forces during Guiscard's absences between 1082 and 1084. During this time, Bohemund penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly. However, he was eventually repulsed by Alexius Comnenus, the founder of the Comnenian dynasty. This early hostility between Bohemund and Alexius would later influence the course of the First Crusade.

The Eastern Roman Empire, under Alexius, was facing a "double combat," being attacked by the Turks in the east and the Normans in the west. To counter the Norman threat, Alexius assembled an army of 70,000 men within six months, drawing levies from Asia and Europe, from the Black Sea to the Peloponnesus. The strength of this army included the Varangians (Scandinavian guards), which had been augmented by English and Danish exiles fleeing Norman oppression in the British Isles.

## Conclusion of the Campaign and Aftermath
The first Norman attack upon the Empire of Byzantium ultimately ended when the pressure of affairs in Italy recalled Robert Guiscard. This withdrawal left the way open for the rule of the Comnenian dynasty from 1081 to 1204. 

In Italy, Guiscard's activities continued; in 1084, he besieged and sacked Rome. His army during this period included a large contingent of Sicilian Moslems. Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a conflict arose between his sons. While Guiscard had destined Bohemund for the throne of Constantinople, the duchy of Apulia and Calabria was succeeded by his younger son, Roger, who had been born "in the purple" to a Lombard princess, Sicelgaeta. This war was eventually settled through the mediation of Pope Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions.
