# The Foreign Title of Baron

The title of baron, while sharing a common origin across the European continent, has evolved to imply ranks and statuses that vary significantly between different countries and differ from its connotation within the United Kingdom.

## Etymology and Early Development
The term originates from the word *baro*, which in the Salic and other "barbarian" laws—such as the *Lex Aleman.*—simply meant "man." This original sense was preserved for a long period in the meaning of "husband," as seen in the Assize of Jerusalem. Over time, the meaning shifted to describe a "strong or powerful man," and eventually became a general term for a "magnate."

## Evolution in France
In 12th-century France, the term *barones* was introduced in a more restricted sense. It was applied to lords who possessed an important fief that was held of a single overlord and subject to the rule of primogeniture, meaning the fief was not liable to be divided. This category occasionally included first-rank ecclesiastical lordships.

By the 13th century, the Register of King Philip Augustus listed the *barones regis Francie* as vassals of the second rank, positioned next to counts and dukes holding in chief. Toward the end of that century, the title indicated that the bearer held his principal fief directly from the crown, making the title more important than that of count, as many counts were merely mediate vassals. Consequently, kings granting a countship or duchy as an apanage to sons or brothers used the phrase *in comitatum et baroniam*.

However, the title's relative importance declined after this period. By the 14th century, when the feudal hierarchy became stereotyped, barons were ranked below both viscounts and counts, regardless of the fact that some barons possessed more power and land than many counts. Until the 17th century, the title required the holder to possess a territorial barony. It was Louis XIV who eventually cheapened the title by creating numerous barons via royal letters.

## Development in Central Europe and Italy
Similar developments occurred in Italy and other European nations. In Austria and Germany, the term *barones* was used for *liberi domini* in medieval Latin documents. It was not until the 17th century, possibly influenced by the court of Versailles, that "Baron" began to be used as an equivalent to the old German *Freiherr* (free lord of the Empire).

The style *Freiherr* (*liber dominus*) originally implied a dynastic status. Some *Freiherren* held countships without adopting the title of count; when the more prominent among them did take the title of count, the *Freiherren* became an inferior class of nobility. In the 16th century, Charles V began the practice of conferring the title *Freiherr* through imperial letters. This practice was adopted by many princes of the Empire through special imperial concessions and is now used by all German sovereigns.

In these regions, the custom of all children inheriting their father's title has made the rank of Baron common and removed the requirement of territorial possession. Nevertheless, it still signifies considerable privilege and social status in societies that maintain a sharp distinction between the "nobles" (*Adeliger*) and the common population.

## The Title in Japan
The title of baron (*Dan*) was introduced to Japan in 1885 based on the European model. It serves as the lowest of five titles of nobility. While it was originally granted to the least important class of territorial nobles, it is also bestowed as a title of honor without requiring territorial possession.

## Use in the United Kingdom
Foreign titles of baron are occasionally held by English subjects, though such titles confer no precedence within the United Kingdom. Examples of such titles include:
* **Russian:** Baron Dimsdale (1762)
* **German:** Baron Stockmar and Baron Halkett (Hanoverian)
* **Austrian:** Baron Rothschild (1822) and Baron de Worms
* **Italian:** Baron Heath
* **French:** Baron de Teissier
* **French-Canadian:** Baron de Longueil (1700)
* **Dutch:** Baron Mackay (Lord Reay)

## Other Uses of the Term
In heraldry, the term "Baron and Feme" refers to the practice of placing the coats-of-arms of a husband and wife per pale in a single escutcheon, with the man's arms on the dexter side and the woman's on the sinister. This specific arrangement is used when the woman is not an heiress.

Historically, "Barons of the Exchequer" were six judges (one chief baron and five puisne barons) responsible for justice in revenue matters between the king and his subjects. This title became obsolete in 1875 when the court of exchequer merged into the High Court of Judicature.
