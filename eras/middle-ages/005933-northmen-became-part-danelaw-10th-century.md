# The Northmen

The Northmen were Nordic tribes originating from the regions around the Baltic and North Seas. Characterized as bold seamen and pirates, they were only slowly and reluctantly converted to Christianity. These peoples, who viewed the world through the lens of valiant adventure, developed advanced shipbuilding skills and engaged in wide-ranging maritime exploration. Their travels took them as far as the icy shores of Greenland and, by the ninth century, led to the establishment of settlements in America.

## Expansion into Russia and the East
The Northmen pushed eastward, navigating the Baltic and ascending Russian rivers into the desolate central lands. They established principalities in Russia and were the first people to be called Russians. One of their earliest settlements in this region was Novgorod the Great; around 850, a Northman named Rurik became the ruler of Novgorod and Kieff.

From these positions, the Northmen reached the Caspian and Black Seas. Their fleets threatened Constantinople on several occasions, specifically in 865 and 904. The descendants of these Northmen, including the kindred Swedes, were drawn toward the Mediterranean by the magnetism of a great southern city they called "Miklagård" (Michael’s court) or Micklegarth, which they imagined as a rich city combining elements of Rome and Byzantium.

## Conquests in England and the Danelaw
In England, the Northmen followed the earlier migrations of the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes. In the early ninth century, England was a Christianized Low German country under King Egbert. However, the Northmen eventually wrested half of the kingdom from Egbert's successor, Alfred the Great. This resulted in the Treaty of Alfred and Guthrum the Dane in 886, which established the Danes in the Danelaw.

The district of Buckinghamshire provides a specific example of this transition. Originally reached by the West Saxons in 571 and later included in the kingdom of Mercia under Offa, Buckinghamshire became part of the Danelaw following the submission of Mercia to the Northmen. Throughout the 10th century, the region suffered frequent ravages by the Danes. Evidence of these struggles survives in numerous earthworks and barrows, particularly in the vale of Aylesbury, which was likely one of the best protected and richest Saxon settlements of that era.

By 1013, a Danish King named Canute reigned over England, Denmark, and Norway. Under Canute and his sons, it appeared that a great confederation of Northmen might be established, and by 1016, they had become masters of the entire land.

## Activities in France and Brittany
Northmen activity was also prominent in France and Brittany. In 912, Rolf the Ganger established himself in the north of France, creating the state of Normandy. The inhabitants of this region eventually learned to speak French. Later, in 1066, a third wave of these people flowed from Normandy into England under William, Duke of Normandy, known as William the Conqueror.

In Brittany, the end of the 9th century and the beginning of the 10th century were marked by Northmen invasions. While they were driven back on several occasions by Salomon (who died in 874) and Alain, count of Vannes (who died in 907), it was Alain Barbetorte (who died in 952) who finally achieved a decisive victory over them.

## Culture and Legacy
The Northmen's culture was rooted in adventure, often involving the hunting of whales, bears, and walruses. In the 10th and 11th centuries, many of their sagas began to be recorded in Iceland. 

From a historical standpoint, the various waves of these people—including the Danes and the Normans—are viewed as the same Nordic stock. This stock also included the Goths, who had previously moved from the Baltic to the Black Sea, eventually splitting into the Visigoths and Ostrogoths.
