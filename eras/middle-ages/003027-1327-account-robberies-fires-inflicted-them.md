## English Governance and the Borough of Bamburgh
In 1327, Edmund Plantagenet, the youngest brother of Edward II, served as a member of the council upon the accession of Edward III to the throne. Edmund had previously fought in France and Scotland and had remained a steady supporter of his brother, Edward II, until the final acts of the king's life in 1326.

During this same period, the town of Bamburgh, which had developed around an ancient castle, faced significant hardship. The burgesses of Bamburgh had previously been granted their town by Henry III via a charter in 1254-1255, subject to an annual fee farm rent of 26 marks. However, in 1327 (as well as in 1318), the burgesses were acquitted of this rent. This remission was granted on account of the fires and robberies inflicted upon them by the Scots.

## Intellectual and Religious Figures
In the field of classical scholarship, the monk of Constantinople, Maximus Planudes, was active during a period spanning 1327 to 1353. Planudes is credited with translating several major works from Latin into Greek, including the *Heroides* and *Metamorphoses* of Ovid, as well as the *Somnium Scipionis* and Cæsar’s *Commentaries*.

In the religious history of the period, 1327 marks the death of Asher ben-Yehiel (also known as Rosh) in Toledo. Born around 1250 in the Rhine district, Asher was a rabbi and codifier who fled to Spain to escape persecutions inflicted on the German Jews during the 13th century. A disciple of Meir of Rothenburg, Asher was characterized by austere piety and a profound focus on the Talmud, while remaining a determined opponent of the study of philosophy. Between 1307 and 1314, he compiled a *Compendium* of the Talmud, which was noted for its simplicity and its deference to German authorities, eventually superseding the work of 'Al-phasi.

In later English common law, courts had the jurisdiction to sentence individuals to whipping for misdemeanors or petty larceny. By the 19th century, statutory authority governed these punishments. For example, the Larceny Act of 1861 authorized whipping for those guilty of robbery with violence.
