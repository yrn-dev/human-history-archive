# Islam in Sind and the Arab Conquest

## Historical Conquest of Sind
The establishment of Islam in Sind is linked to the early Arab conquests of the 8th century. Specifically, the Arabs conquered Sind as early as 712. This expansion was marked by the victorious march of Mahommed Kasim, who is credited with the destruction of Armabel (likely the site of Karia Pir) around 710. Prior to this influx of Arabs, Sind was ruled by the Brahman, Chach. 

Following these initial conquests, the influence of Moslem rule expanded across northern India. By the end of the 11th century, the entirety of northern India was in Moslem hands. This era of governance was later divided into two primary periods: the Turki period (1200–1526), which consisted of various dynasties of mixed Iranian and Turki race, and the subsequent Mogul empire.

## Religious Predominance and Demographics
Since the earliest Arab conquests in the 8th century, Islam has been the predominant religion in Sind. The reach of the faith extended further west; the population of Asia west of the Sutlej river is described as being wholly Mahommedan, with the exception of some small areas in Syria and Asia Minor where Christians predominate. 

While Islam became the majority faith in Sind, the religious landscape of the broader region remained varied. From the borders of the Sind desert and the river Sutlej eastward to Ceylon and Burma, the majority of the population remained Hindu or Brahminical, although Mahommedans were often numerous and occasionally formed a majority in certain places. In contrast to the Maratha country, which was not conquered by Mussulmans until a later period and was not as thoroughly occupied, Sind—along with the Punjab and the Gangetic valley—experienced a more thorough occupation.

## Cultural and Linguistic Influence
The spread of Islam brought with it a distinct style of civilization and art. A primary feature of this influence was the introduction of the Arabic alphabet. In several Moslem nations, including those speaking Persian, Turkish, and Hindustani, a large portion of the vocabulary was borrowed from Arabic.

In northern India, this resulted in the development of Urdu or Hindustani, a mixed language used by the Mahommedan conquerors. This language utilizes the Persian character for writing and is written from right to left. This contrasts with other northern Indian languages, which are Sanskritic in vocables and written from left to right. In Sind specifically, the chief language spoken is Sindhi. West of the Indus, dialects shift closer to Persian, which in turn meets Arabic and Turki further west.

## Core Teachings of Islam
Islam is described as a prophetic religion characterized by an uncompromising monotheism and a simple faith in the fatherhood and rule of God, remaining free from theological complications. The faith emphasizes a complete detachment from temples and sacrificial priests, serving as a safeguard against a return to blood sacrifices.

The text of the Koran defines the pilgrimage to Mecca as being ceremonial and limited in nature. Furthermore, the religion insists upon the perfect equality and brotherhood of all believers before God, regardless of their status, origin, or colour. To prevent the deification of the prophet after his death, Muhammad took every precaution to ensure the faith remained centered on the divine.
