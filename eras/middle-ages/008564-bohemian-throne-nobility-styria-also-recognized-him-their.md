# The Bohemian Throne

The history of the Bohemian throne is characterized by periods of internal struggle, shifts between elective and hereditary succession, and the eventual permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty. These dynamics were often influenced by the power of the nobility and the religious conflicts between the Church of Rome and various Protestant movements.

## Early Sovereignty and the Reign of Premysl Ottakar II
The royal title of the Bohemian sovereigns was continued uninterruptedly starting from 1198, following the coronation of Premysl Ottakar, who became the undisputed ruler of Bohemia in 1197. His successor, Wenceslas I (1230–1253), took the throne without opposition, though his final years were marked by internal discord involving his son, Premysl Ottakar II.

Premysl Ottakar II is regarded as one of Bohemia's greatest kings. During his father's lifetime, he acquired the archduchies of Austria. Around the time he ascended the Bohemian throne, the nobility of Styria also recognized him as their ruler. His territorial expansions led to repeated conflicts with Hungary, culminating in a decisive victory over King Bela in the battle of Kressenbrunn in 1260. Following this, Ottakar's power reached its peak as he obtained possession of Istria, Carinthia, and parts of northern Italy. His dominions stretched from the Adriatic to the Giant Mountains in Bohemia. Due to his military strength and immense wealth, contemporaries referred to him as "the man of iron" and "the man of gold," respectively. To counter the power of the Bohemian nobility, Ottakar encouraged the immigration of Germans into his lands.

## The Habsburg Transition and Territorial Loss
The expansion of the Bohemian crown under Ottakar ended following the election of Rudolph, count of Habsburg, as king of the Romans in 1273. Rudolph challenged Ottakar's titles to three duchies. After being placed under the ban in July 1275, Ottakar submitted to Rudolph in November 1276, renouncing the duchies of Styria, Carinthia, and Austria. Rudolph eventually invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Austria and Styria on December 27, 1282.

## Succession Disputes and the National Party
For centuries, the nature of the Bohemian throne remained a point of contention, as it was not decided until 1627 whether the crown was hereditary or elective. Following the death of King Sigismund in 1437, he was succeeded by his son-in-law, Albert of Austria. After Albert's short reign, the nobility split into two factions: a national party led by George of Podebrad and a Roman or Austrian division led by Ulrich of Rosenberg. These parties eventually reached an agreement called the "Letter of Peace" (_list mirny_), which paved the way for the succession of Albert's heir, Ladislas, born in 1440.

Later, George of Podebrad attempted to establish a national dynasty but eventually offered the succession to Vladislav of Poland. Vladislav was elected at Kutna Hora on May 27, 1471, a victory for the national party due to the Polish envoys' promise to maintain the Compacts. Vladislav's reign involved a struggle with Matthias of Hungary, which ended with a treaty at Olmutz in 1478 that secured Bohemia for Vladislav.

## Permanent Habsburg Accession and Religious Conflict
The permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne occurred on October 23, 1526, when twenty-four electors chose Archduke Ferdinand of Austria (later Emperor Ferdinand I) as king. Ferdinand sought to establish a hereditary right for his dynasty, notably substituting a charter in 1541 to claim he was recognized as king via the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna, rather than by election.

The 16th and 17th centuries were marked by intense religious friction. In 1575, King Maximilian faced demands from the Bohemian estates to recognize the "Confession of Augsburg." While Maximilian refused to include the resulting _Confessio Bohemica_ among the kingdom's charters, he did exempt Lutherans and advanced Utraquists from the jurisdiction of the consistory.

By the early 17th century, the throne was held by Matthias. Because Matthias was childless, he sought to secure the succession for his cousin, Ferdinand, duke of Styria. Ferdinand was a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome and a persecutor of Styrian Protestants. Despite this, the estates were persuaded to accept him as heir, and he was crowned in Prague on June 17, 1617. This succession was opposed by Bohemian Protestants led by Count Thurn, precipitating a conflict that began in May 1618.

## Later Habsburg Rule and the Pragmatic Sanction
In the 18th century, the possibility of the extinction of the Habsburg male line led the estates of Bohemia to sanction female succession on October 16, 1720, through the Pragmatic Sanction. This recognized the indivisibility of the Habsburg realm in favor of Archduchess Maria Theresa. Upon the death of Emperor Charles VI, Charles, elector of Bavaria, challenged this claim and occupied Prague in 1741, receiving oaths of allegiance from many nobles. However, Maria Theresa eventually recovered Bohemia. Under her and her son Joseph II, the autonomy of Bohemia was further limited, including the 1749 abolition of separate Bohemian law-courts and the appointment of an Austro-Bohemian chancellor.
