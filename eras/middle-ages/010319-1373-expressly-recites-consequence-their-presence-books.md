# Books and Recitation in Human History

The history of the written word and the oral tradition of recitation reveals a complex evolution of how knowledge, sacred texts, and literature were preserved and distributed across different civilizations.

## The Trade and Regulation of Books in Medieval England
The spread of Christianity generated a significant demand for sacred texts, including the Gospels, as well as devotional volumes such as missals for private and church use. In 671, Benedict Biscop, the founder of the abbey at Wearmouth in England, imported a cargo of books from France, some of which he had purchased.

By the period preceding the Reformation, the sale of manuscript copies of prayers—such as the Creed, Ave Maria, Paternoster, and the ABC—was centered around St Paul's in London. In 1403, the stationers (or text writers) who sold these works formed a gild, with some maintaining stalls built directly against the walls of the cathedral.

In Oxford, the supply of books was largely controlled by "sworn" university stationers who held a monopoly. One such individual, John More, provided books to pupils and also functioned as a pawnbroker. Because many students could not afford to purchase books and instruction was primarily oral, books frequently changed hands. However, the presence of non-sworn booksellers led to the passage of a statute in 1373. This law expressly recited that because of these unauthorized sellers, owners were being cheated and books of great value were being sold and carried away from Oxford, thereby depriving sworn stationers of their lawful business. Consequently, it was enacted that no bookseller, other than two sworn stationers or their deputies, could sell any book valued at more than half a mark.

## Classical Recitation and the Homeric Poems
In ancient Athens, the recitation of Homeric poems was mandated by law for every occasion of the Panathenaea, a fact cited as a particular glory of the city by the orator Lycurgus. While the Platonic dialogue *Hipparchus* attributes the introduction of this custom to Hipparchus, son of Peisistratus, this is considered part of a historical romance. Other accounts, such as those from Diogenes Laertius, suggest that Solon established the law requiring poems to be recited "with prompting" (*ex hypoboles*).

To ensure a proper order of recitation and prevent rhapsodists from choosing only their favorite or "show" passages, the poems were divided into parts. A person was charged with giving the rhapsodists their cue (*hypoballein*), requiring them to take each other up in order. There is scholarly debate regarding the nature of these recitations prior to Solon; some, like Ritschl, argue that the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* were only recited in parts without a fixed order until Peisistratus, with the help of others like Onomakritus, settled the order and bound the poems into a whole.

## Literary and Religious Figures of the 14th Century
The 14th century saw the intersection of biography, romance, and religious devotion. Griselda, a heroine of romance said to be the 11th-century wife of Walter, marquis of Saluces, became a subject of significant literature. Boccaccio included her story in the *Decamerone* around 1350, and Petrarch related it in a Latin letter in 1373. This version by Petrarch served as the basis for later works, including Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* and various plays.

During this same era, Saint Bridget of Sweden (c. 1302–1373) exerted considerable religious influence. The daughter of Birger Persson, she founded the order of St Saviour (the Bridgittines), with a principal house at Vadstena. Bridget recorded "revelations" and visions, which were translated into Latin by her confessor Peter and Matthias, a canon of Linköping. After traveling to Rome around 1350 to seek authorization for her order and to elevate the moral tone of the age, she remained there until her death on July 23, 1373, following a pilgrimage to Jerusalem earlier that year.

## Teachings of the Qur'an on the Book and Recitation
The Qur'an presents the Book as a divine revelation and provides specific commands regarding its recitation and the interaction with other scriptures. It instructs the recipient to recite what has been revealed of the Book of the Lord, asserting that no one can alter His words and that there is no refuge besides Him. Recitation is linked to the establishment of regular prayer, which is taught to restrain individuals from evil, indecency, and unjust deeds.

The text emphasizes that the remembrance of Allah is the greatest thing in life and warns against following those whose hearts have been made heedless of this remembrance in pursuit of the pomp and glitter of worldly life. Regarding the "People of the Book," the text commands that disputes with them be handled with better means, unless they inflict injury. It teaches a belief in the revelation sent to the current messenger as well as that which came down previously, stating that "Our Allah and your Allah is one." Those who were given the Scripture before the current revelation and believe in it are described as believers who, when the truth is recited to them, acknowledge it as the truth from their Lord. Such individuals are promised a double reward for their steadfastness, their practice of repelling evil with good, and their charity.
