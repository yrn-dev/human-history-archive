# Lakes of Asia and Scotland

## Asian Lake Systems
The lakes of Asia are numerous and vary significantly in scale, ranging from highland lochs and indefinitely extended swamps in Persia to vast inland seas such as Lakes Baikal and Balkash. Many of these bodies of water are situated at high elevations—with Lake Victoria, at 13,400 ft., being likely the most elevated—and are considered vestiges of an ancient period of glaciation. These specific lakes generally show signs of a gradual decrease in size.

Other Asian lakes are relics of earlier geological periods. These occurred when land areas, recently upheaved from the sea, were spread at low levels and experienced alternating inundations of fresh and salt water. Typical examples include the Helmund *hamuns* and Lop Nor. Along with the plateau *hamuns* of Persia and south-west Baluchistan, these lakes change their extent and form from season to season, and many contain saline deposits from underlying strata. In the Persian desert, the *kavirs* (salt depressions) are more frequently widespread deposits of salt and mud than areas covered by water.

## Lake Baikal
Lake Baikal is surrounded by lofty mountains, with the exception of the delta of the Selenga. Its geography is defined by several mountain ranges: the Barguzin range impinges obliquely in the east, and the Ulanburgasu mountains intrude into the Selenga delta. To the south, the Khamar-daban border-ridge fringes the lake with steep cliffs, featuring a summit 5,300 ft. above the lake level. The north-west shore is struck diagonally by the Baikal and Onot ridges (also Primorskiy), while an unexplored Alpine complex of mountains rises on the north-east shore. A deeply-ravined highland exists between the Angara and the Irkut.

The lake is fed by over 300 streams, primarily short mountain torrents. Major inflows include the Selenga on the south-east, the Barguzin on the east, and the Upper Angara at the north-east extremity. The only outflow is the lower Angara, which exits through a rocky cleft on the west shore. While the Irkut once reached Lake Baikal, it now abandons the broad valley leading to the lake near its south-west extremity, moving north through a narrow mountain gap to join the Angara at Irkutsk.

## Fauna of Lake Baikal
The fauna of Lake Baikal is original and richer than previously assumed. Although hypotheses regarding direct communication between the lake and the Arctic Ocean during the Tertiary or Post-Tertiary ages remain unproven, the lake contains a seal (*Phoca baikalensis*) akin to those found in Spitsbergen, as well as polychaetes, marine sponges, a marine mollusc (*ancilodoris*), and some marine gammarids. 

The lake's waters are swarming with fish, including *salmonidae* and sturgeons. The chief product of the fisheries is the herring (*Salmo omul*), though catches have notably decreased over the last forty or fifty years. Plankton is very abundant. Additionally, the small Lake Frolikha, which communicates with Baikal via the Frolikha river near the northern extremity, contains a peculiar species of trout (*Salmo erythreas*) not found elsewhere. While zoological groups are relatively poor, there is a great wealth of species within those groups; for instance, there are as many as 300 species of gammarids. Those living at depths of 330 to 380 fathoms exhibit abyssal characters similar to deep-sea ocean fauna.

## Scottish Lochs
In Scotland, various lochs characterize the landscape across different counties. In mid-Argyllshire, Loch Awe is the longest freshwater lake in Scotland, extending 23 m. from Ford to Kilchurn Castle. It sits 116 ft. above the sea and has an area of nearly 16 sq. m. The lake's breadth varies from 1/3 of a mile to 3 m. at its upper end, where it forms a crescent. The river Awe issues from the north-western horn of the loch.

In Dumbartonshire, the largest and most magnificent inland lake is Loch Lomond. The boundary between Dumbarton and Stirling shires runs through the lake from the mouth of Endrick Water to a point opposite the isle of Vow. Other water bodies in this region include Loch Sloy, a long, narrow lake 812 ft. above the sea, and the Gareloch and Loch Long, which are salt-water inlets.

In Perthshire, Loch Tay is located near the village of Killin at its south-western extremity. The Dochart river, issuing from Loch Dochart, flows north-easterly for 13 m. before falling into Loch Tay. In the district of Badenoch in south-east Inverness-shire, the principal lakes are Loch Ericht and Loch Laggan.

## Historical Context
In the beginning of the thirteenth century, Jengis Khan began to consolidate his power among the nomads in the region located between Lakes Baikal and Balkash. During this same period, in 1206, a former slave and governor named Kutub established a separate Moslem state of Hindustan in Delhi, after Northern India had been part of the Khivan empire.
