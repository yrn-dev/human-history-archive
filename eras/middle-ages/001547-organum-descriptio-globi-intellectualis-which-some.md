# Organum

The *Organum* (specifically the *Novum Organum*, published in 1620) is a celebrated work by Francis Bacon that outlines a new method of inquiry. This method is characterized by Bacon as possessing mechanical simplicity and absolute certainty. The *Organum* is a central component of Bacon's broader project, the *Instauratio* (or *Instauration of the Sciences*), specifically forming part of the third division of that system.

## Relationship with Natural History

Bacon maintained that the *Organum* and "Natural History" are correlative and together form the two necessary sides of a true philosophy. He argued that the new method described in the *Organum* would be valueless and inapplicable if it were not supplied with materials that had been duly presented and collected. Specifically, a competent natural history of the *Phaenomena Universi* is required as the *basis totius negotii* (the basis of the whole business).

In the preface to the third part of the *Instauratio*, Bacon stated that even if the *Organum* were completed, it would not significantly advance the *Instauration of the Sciences* without the *Natural History*. Conversely, he believed the *Natural History* without the *Organum* would advance the project "not a little." 

Bacon's vision of a true natural history was one that consisted exclusively of canons, observations, connections, and instances. In a letter to Jean Antoine Baranzano, Bacon addressed the difficulty of procuring a multitude of instances, asserting that such a store of instances must be procured or the business must be abandoned, as all other paths are impassable. He further noted that the mode of experimenting upon "experiments of light" would reduce the number of instances required, though he remained indifferent to whether the description of these instances filled six times as many volumes as the history written by Pliny.

## Associated Works and Tracts

Several of Bacon's minor philosophical writings and tracts relate to the substance and development of the *Organum*:

*   ***Cogitata et Visa***: Dating from 1607, this work is considered one of the most important minor writings and contains the substance of the first book of the *Organum* written in sonorous Latin.
*   ***Redargutio Philosophiarum***: Composed around 1608 or 1609, this oration contains much of the detail that later appears in the *Novum Organum* (book i) regarding the *Idola Theatri*.
*   ***Parasceve***: This tract is appended to the *Novum Organum* and provides a short introductory sketch of the requirements for a natural history.
*   ***Descriptio Globi Intellectualis***: This work is intermediate between the *Advancement of Learning* and the *De Augmentis*; it provides a detailed general classification of the sciences.

Other works intended to form portions of the necessary natural history include the *Sylva Sylvarum* (an extensive collection of observations and facts), as well as *Historia Ventorum*, *Historia Vitae et Mortis*, and *Historia Densi et Rari*.

## Influence and Legacy

Bacon is regarded by many as the father of the most characteristic elements of English psychological speculation, having "rung the bell which called the wits together." His influence is noted in both natural science and philosophy:

*   **Psychology**: The fundamental principle of Locke's *Essay*—that reflection and sensation produce all ideas—is briefly stated in the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*.
*   **Ethics**: Bacon is seen as a forerunner of the empirical method in ethics. The inductive method discussed in the *Novum Organum*, along with the spirit of the *De Augmentis* (book vii), serves as the root for theories that construct moral codes through the inductive examination of actions and human consciousness. Utilitarianism is cited as a natural result of applying the Baconian experimental method to the phenomenon of conduct.

However, some evidence suggests this influence was not immediate; for example, Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, made no critical reference to Bacon or Baconian induction in his works.
