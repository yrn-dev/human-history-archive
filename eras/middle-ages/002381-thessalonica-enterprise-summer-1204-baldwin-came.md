# Thessalonica

Thessalonica (also referred to as Saloniki) was a strategic city in the East Roman Empire that became a focal point of political contention and territorial division following the Fourth Crusade in 1204. Its conquest and subsequent administration were central to the organization of the Latin Empire of Romania.

## The Conflict of 1204
Following the capture of Constantinople in 1204, the Latin Empire of Romania was established on feudal principles. Baldwin I, the elected emperor, held direct control over Constantinople, its adjacent European and Asiatic regions, several outlying districts, and islands such as Chios, Lesbos, Tenos, and Lemnos. However, to secure the empire, it was necessary to break Greek resistance in Thrace and secure Thessalonica.

In the summer of 1204, this objective led to a collision between Emperor Baldwin and Boniface of Montferrat. Boniface, who had been a rival candidate for the imperial crown, was designated to receive a large territory in Macedonia and hold the title of king of Saloniki. Boniface opposed Baldwin's proposal to march toward Thessalonica, as he hoped to maintain a kingdom independent of the empire and avoid performing homage. This political rivalry was further aggravated by existing antagonisms between the Lombards and the Flemings.

The dispute escalated to the point where civil war seemed inevitable; while Baldwin insisted on the march to Thessalonica, Boniface laid siege to Hadrianople, a city where Baldwin had already installed a governor. The conflict was eventually resolved through the mediation of the count of Blois and Henry Dandolo, the doge of Venice. Under the resulting agreement, Boniface was appointed commander of the forces destined for the conquest of Greece and received Thessalonica as a fief from the emperor.

## The Kingdom of Thessalonica and Latin Rule
Under the rule of Boniface of Montferrat as king of Thessalonica, the city served as a center of Latin power in the region. The influence of the kingdom extended to other territories; for example, Boniface granted the lordship of Athens to Otho de la Roche, granting him the title of Megaskyr (great lord).

The broader Latin presence in the region was part of a larger movement where the Latin Church turned upon the Greek Empire. During this period, the Latin and Greek Churches were declared reunited, and Latin emperors ruled as conquerors in Constantinople from 1204 until 1261.

## Decline of Latin Influence
The stability of the Latin conquests was challenged shortly after their establishment. During the winter of 1204–1205, the Franks pursued conquests in Bithynia. However, in February 1205, the Greeks in Thrace revolted with the support of King John (Kaloyan) of Bulgaria. This led to the expulsion of the garrison at Hadrianople. In April 1205, the crusader forces, including Emperor Baldwin, were utterly defeated by the Bulgarian army. This defeat resulted in the death of the count of Blois and the capture of Emperor Baldwin, who died in captivity.

By the time of Baldwin II (who ruled from 1228 to 1261), the Latin Empire had declined significantly while Greek power advanced. The realm governed by Baldwin II was reduced to little more than Constantinople. The era of Latin predominance in the region ended in 1261 when Michael Palaeologus captured Constantinople, allowing the Greek world to shake itself free from Roman predominance.
