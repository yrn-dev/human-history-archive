# Habsburg Rule and the Administration of Bohemia

The house of Habsburg was a growing and acquisitive family with possessions across Germany. Their ascent to imperial power began in 1273 when Rudolf of Habsburg was elected the first Habsburg Emperor (or German king) following the extinction of the Hohenstaufen house. This distinction eventually became practically hereditary within the family.

## Early Territorial Expansion
Upon his election, Rudolf of Habsburg focused on the power of Ottakar, who menaced the German throne. After the imperial court declared against Ottakar and placed him under the ban in July 1275, Ottakar submitted in November 1276, renouncing the duchies of Carinthia, Styria, and Austria. While these lands were initially administered by Rudolf as head of the Empire, he sought to make them hereditary possessions for his family and moved the Habsburg headquarters from the Rhine to the Danube. On December 27, 1282, Rudolf invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Styria and Austria.

The Habsburgs also expanded their influence into Switzerland. In 1291, they purchased Lucerne from the abbey of Murbach. However, this acquisition, along with the desire of the men of Uri, Schwyz, and Unterwalden to avoid Habsburg rule, led to the foundation of the Swiss Confederation in 1291. Lucerne became the fourth member of this confederation in 1332, though it did not fully remove all traces of Habsburg domination until after the victory of Sempach in 1386.

## Habsburg Rule in Bohemia
The Habsburgs maintained a long-term plan to establish Bohemia as a hereditary kingdom and to curtail its ancient constitution as far as possible. Following a victory at Muhlberg that crushed German Protestantism, Ferdinand arrived in Prague on July 20 with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries. While he treated nobles and knights with forbearance, he dealt severely with the towns, specifically Prague. Ferdinand declared that ancient municipal privileges would be revised, which resulted in a broad confiscation of lands belonging to municipalities. He further imposed state officials—town-captains in Prague and town-judges elsewhere—who exercised almost unlimited control over municipal affairs. Additionally, the Bohemian Brethren were persecuted, and their bishop, Augusta, was imprisoned for many years.

Bohemia later became a primary theater of the Thirty Years' War. The country was occupied by Saxons in 1631 and frequently pillaged by Swedish troops; the war concluded in 1648 with the Swedish general Konigsmark taking part of Prague. Despite Swedish efforts, imperial representatives remained inflexible regarding toleration for Bohemian Protestants.

## Succession and Centralization
In 1720, the estates of Bohemia recognized the Pragmatic Sanction, which proclaimed the indivisibility of the Habsburg realm and sanctioned female succession to the throne. This favored Archduchess Maria Theresa. Upon the death of her father, Emperor Charles VI, Maria Theresa faced opposition from Charles, elector of Bavaria, who invaded Bohemia and occupied Prague in 1741. Maria Theresa eventually recovered Bohemia and other Habsburg lands.

Under Maria Theresa and her son Joseph II, internal administration was altered to further limit Bohemian autonomy. A 1749 decree abolished separate Bohemian law-courts, and an Austro-Bohemian chancellor was later appointed to control the administration of both Bohemia and the German domains of the house of Habsburg.

## The Austrian Empire and Constitutional Struggles
In 1804, Francis II proclaimed himself Francis I, Emperor of Austria. This title was adopted to prevent the house of Habsburg from being relegated to a position inferior to the Bonapartes if the Holy Roman Empire collapsed. The title "Emperor of Austria" officially replaced "Imperator Romanorum semper Augustus" when the Holy Empire ended in 1806. This title was often resented by Slavs and Magyars, who felt it ignored their national rights and implied German superiority.

Following the Italian campaign of 1859, absolutism became untenable. An attempt was made to establish a constitutional system; a decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, which was initially welcomed in Bohemia. However, a subsequent decree on February 26, 1861, created a central parliament in Vienna with extensive powers and an electoral system biased toward Germans. The Bohemians left this parliament in 1863, arguing it encroached upon the power of the diet of Prague.

The Austro-Prussian war of 1866 led to a treaty signed at Prague on August 23, which excluded all Habsburg-ruled lands from Germany. While Hungary obtained complete independence through a compact in 1867, the new constitution for the German and Slavic parts of the empire maintained centralization. The Bohemians energetically opposed this constitution.
