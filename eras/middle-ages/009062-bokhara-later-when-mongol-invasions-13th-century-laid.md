# Bokhara

Bokhara, known to the ancients as Sogdiana, is a city in central Asia that has served for ages as a primary center of religious life and learning. Historically, it was too far east to be brought under Roman dominion, though it was deeply involved in the various bloody revolutions of Asia. The foundation of the capital is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, the region formed part of the Seleucidae empire and shared the fortunes of Bactria. Later, the nomad Yue-chi moved into the valley of the Oxus from the east, becoming a settled power; the dominions of their king, Kadphises I (who is believed to have ascended the throne around A.D. 45), extended from Bokhara to the Indus.

## Religious and Cultural Evolution
The district was later reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand. Islam was introduced shortly after the Arab conquest of Persia (640-642) and quickly became the dominant faith. During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the region—then called Sughd—was one of the most flourishing districts of central Asia. It was also known as Ma wara 'l Nahr (or Ma-vera-un-nahr), a term translated as Transoxiana. While Samarkand generally served as the seat of government, Bokhara earned a high reputation as a seat of learning and religion. In the middle ages, the mysticism that took hold in Persia also spread to Bokhara.

## Mongol Invasions and Political Shifts
At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah of the Seljuk dynasty subdued the country watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus. In 1216, Bokhara was subdued by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm, but Jenghiz Khan wrested control from him in 1220. The region was wasted by the fury of this conqueror, though it recovered some prosperity under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent and humane. 

During the Mongol invasions of the 13th century, while other Moslem cities and Samarkand were laid waste, Bokhara remained independent. Consequently, it continued to serve as a chief seat of Islamitic learning. The Mongol Khans also resided in the valley of the Ili in the Kulja territory, which Jenghiz Khan had conquered in the 13th century. The posterity of Ogdai Khan maintained possession of the region until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand.

## Trade and Economy
Bokhara is the most important trading town in central Asia and serves as the region's principal book-market. Bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan visit the city to purchase rare books. While many rich *madrasa* libraries were lost to conflagrations, confiscated by emirs, or scattered, treasures of literature remain concealed in private libraries. 

The city's bazaars are centers for the sale and manufacture of carpets, metal wares (particularly copper), silk stuffs, and Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins.

## Population and Geography
Estimates of the population vary; Russian travellers suggest it does not exceed 50,000 or 60,000, while other estimates place the population between 75,000 and 100,000. The inhabitants include a large and ancient colony of Jews. 

Near the railway station, approximately 8 miles from the city, is a rapidly growing Russian town called New Bokhara, or Kagan. This town, with a population of 2,000, is situated on territory ceded by the emir.
