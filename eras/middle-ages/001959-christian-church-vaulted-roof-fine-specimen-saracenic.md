# Christian Churches

The history and architecture of Christian churches across various regions demonstrate a wide range of styles, influences, and fates, from the construction of great cathedrals in Europe to the establishment of churches in the East.

## Churches in the East and Central Asia
In the city of Campichu, the capital of the province of Tangut, Christians are among the inhabitants alongside Saracens and Idolaters; the city contains three very fine Christian churches. In Samarkand, the city served as a bishopric and one of the Metropolitan Sees of the Nestorian Church since the beginning of the 8th century. Prince Sempad, High Constable of Armenia, noted in 1246 or 1247 that many fine, ancient, and lofty churches of good architecture existed throughout the East, though many had been spoiled by the Turks.

In other regions, the relationship between Christians and Saracens has been marked by conflict over architectural elements. In one instance, after the brother of the Great Kaan, Sigatay, became a Christian, a great church was built in his honor and named after John the Baptist. The Christians used a fine stone belonging to the Saracens as a pedestal for a column in the middle of the church to support the roof. Following Sigatay's death, the Saracens demanded the return of the stone. Despite the Christians' offer to pay a large sum of money, the Saracens refused, and the Prince eventually ordered the Christians to either satisfy the Saracens with money or surrender the stone within three days.

## Architectural Features and Saracenic Influence
Christian church architecture has frequently incorporated diverse structural elements, including various types of vaulting and domes. In Sicily, strong Saracenic influence is evident in the use of simple versions of stalactite pendentives for squinches, with the church of San Giovanni-dei-Leprosi (A.D. 1072) serving as the earliest example; these domes are ovoid in section. 

In other locations, the relationship between Christian sites and Saracenic architecture is found in the reuse of sites. In Bagdad, the khan el-Aurtmeh, which dates from A.D. 1356, is said to occupy the site of an ancient Christian church. This building features a vaulted roof described as a fine specimen of Saracenic brickwork. Additionally, in the town of Baiburt in Asiatic Turkey, the remains of a Christian church are located on an isolated mass of rock on the left bank of the Churuk river, alongside a mosque, a vaulted chamber, and two covered staircases.

## European Cathedrals and Churches
Across Europe, Christian churches exhibit a variety of regional styles:

*   **Italy:** In Bari, the cathedral of S. Sabino (begun 1035, completed 1171) preserves its original dome and campanile. The church of S. Nicola, founded in 1087, features an interior divided into three naves by columns with galleries over the aisles, though the vaulting of its crypt has been covered with modern stucco. In central Italy, the cathedral of Pisa features an elliptical dome.
*   **England:** In Bristol, the cathedral features a modern nave (1877) with curious skeleton-vaulting in the aisles. The chapter-house is noted for its Byzantine exuberance of decoration and is considered one of the finest Norman chambers in Europe. In Bury St Edmunds, St Mary's church, built in the early 15th century, is known for its beautifully carved roof.
*   **France:** In the Département of the Somme, one church featured side-aisles with five separate roofs at right-angles to the great nave, with vaulting attributed to Jean Vaast, an architect of Beauvais Cathedral. However, this church was almost entirely destroyed in 1918, resulting in the loss of the pulpit, tower, and vaulting.
