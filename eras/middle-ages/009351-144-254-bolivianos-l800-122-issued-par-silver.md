# Finance and Currency of Bolivia

## Public Debt and Treasury Issues
The acknowledged public debt of Bolivia is considered comparatively small. Following the war with Chile, the country held an indemnity debt owed to citizens of that republic totaling 6,550,830 bolivianos. This debt was nearly liquidated by 1904, at which time Chile assumed the unpaid balance, leaving it as Bolivia's sole foreign debt.

By 1905, the internal debt of the country amounted to 6,243,270 bolivianos (L546,286), a figure that included 1,998,500 bolivianos in treasury bills. Additionally, in 1903, the government authorized the issuance of treasury notes for the National Territory and the department of Beni totaling one million bolivianos (L87,500). To ensure the redemption of these notes, 10% of the customs receipts from these two districts are set aside.

## Currency and Coinage
The paper currency of the republic is not part of the public debt, as bank-notes are issued by four private banks. As of June 30, 1903, the official estimate of paper currency in circulation was 9,144,254 bolivianos (L800,122), which was issued on a par with silver.

The national coinage consists of copper, nickel, and silver. The denominations are as follows:
*   **Silver:** 1 boliviano (or 100 centavos), and 50, 20, 10, and 5 centavos.
*   **Nickel:** 10 and 5 centavos.
*   **Copper:** 2 and 1 centavos.

The Potosi mint produces approximately 1,500,000 bolivianos in silver coins annually. By law, silver mining companies must send 20% of their product to the mint. However, because paper currency is cheaper, silver bolivianos are rarely seen in circulation. To prevent the exportation of silver coins, the fractional denominations have been slightly debased.

## National Revenue and Expenditure
Information regarding Bolivia's finances is drawn from estimates presented to congress by cabinet ministers, as itemized returns of expenditures and receipts are not published. Taxation is not viewed as heavy, and expenditures are not large.

For the years 1904 and 1905, based on a rate of 21 pence per boliviano, the estimated finances were:
*   **1904:** Revenue of L632,773:15s. and expenditure of L748,571:10s.
*   **1905:** Revenue of L693,763:17:6 and expenditure of L828,937:19:9.

Revenues are primarily sourced from export duties on minerals (excluding silver), rubber, and coca; excise taxes on sugar, tobacco, wines, and spirits; duties and fees on imports; general and mining taxes; taxes on stock company profits; stamp taxes; postal and telegraph revenues; and fees for patents and licences.

While Bolivia is sometimes referred to as a "free-trade country" due to moderate nominal import duties, this is considered a misnomer. In addition to *ad valorem* import rates of 10% to 40%, the system includes a 1-1/2% consular fee for registering invoices over 200 bolivianos, a consumption tax of 10 centavos per quintal (46 kilogrammes), fees for viseing certificates for merchandise in transit, import and consumption taxes from municipalities and departments, and special "octroi" taxes on monopoly-controlled goods such as tobacco and spirits.

## Government Appropriations and Local Finance
National expenditures are primarily directed toward public works, official salaries, military expenses, interest on public debt, justice, public instruction, and support for missions and the church. In 1905, appropriations were allocated as follows:
*   **Government and fomento:** 2,021,428 bolivianos.
*   **War:** 2,081,119 bolivianos.
*   **Justice and public instruction:** 1,878,941 bolivianos.
*   **Finance and industry:** 1,462,259 bolivianos.

At the departmental level, revenues—derived from mining grants, land taxes, excise, tithes, inheritance taxes, stamp taxes, tolls, and national treasury subsidies—were estimated at 2,296,172 bolivianos in 1903, with expenditures totaling 2,295,791 bolivianos. These funds primarily supported the Church, public instruction, police, justice, and public works.

Municipal revenues in 1902 aggregated 2,317,670 bolivianos, with expenditures exceeding that amount by 61,510 bolivianos. Municipal income is derived from ground rents, leases, a lighting tax, market and consumption taxes, cemetery fees, tolls, licences, personal taxes, and taxes on skins and hides. The archive notes a multiplication of trade taxes reminiscent of the colonial *alcabala* tax, which increases the cost of goods and restricts commerce.
