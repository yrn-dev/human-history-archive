# Buckinghamshire

Buckinghamshire (abbreviated *Bucks*) is a south midland county of England. It is bounded to the north by Northamptonshire, to the east by Bedfordshire, Hertfordshire, and Middlesex, to the south by Berkshire and (for a short distance) Surrey, and to the west by Oxfordshire. The county covers an area of 743.2 square miles.

## Geography and Physical Character
The county is divided between the basins of the rivers Thames and Ouse. The Thames forms the entire southern boundary, and the county includes the upper part of this river. The Ouse, in its uppermost course, forms part of the north-western boundary and passes through the towns of Wolverton, Stony Stratford, Buckingham, Newport Pagnell, and Olney before forming a short stretch of the north-eastern boundary; its principal tributary within the county is the Ouzel.

The landscape varies significantly between the north and south. To the north-west of Buckingham, the land rises in gentle undulations to nearly 500 feet, with isolated hills such as Muswell Hill and Brill Hill exceeding 600 feet. The south is the hilliest region, occupied by the Chiltern system, which runs from south-west to north-east and reaches heights of nearly 900 feet. This hilly region is well-wooded and characterized by narrow vales watered by the Chess, Misbourne, and Wye streams. The beech tree is predominant here; William Camden suggested around 1585 that the county's name derives from the Anglo-Saxon word *boc* (beech). A remnant of ancient forest, Burnham Beeches, is preserved as public ground.

## History
The district was reached by the West Saxons in 571. It was later included in Mercia under Offa and subsequently became part of the Danelaw following the submission of Mercia to the Northmen. During the 10th century, the county suffered from Danish raids, particularly in the vale of Aylesbury. In contrast, the Chiltern district was once an impassable forest inhabited by wild beasts and robbers. To combat this, Leofstan, the 12th abbot of St Albans, cleared large tracts of wood during the reign of Edward the Confessor. This necessity led to the appointment of a steward of the Chiltern Hundreds, an office that eventually became a sinecure. The district was not fully disforested until the reign of James I.

The shire of Buckingham originated during the reign of Edward the Elder through the division of Mercia. At the time of the Norman invasion, the area was likely part of the earldom of Leofwine, son of Godwin. Following the Battle of Hastings, the county faced sweeping confiscations. Due to its proximity to London, Buckinghamshire was involved in many national events. In 1216, during the war between King John and his barons, Falkes de Bréauté captured and demolished Hanslape Castle, which had been held by William Mauduit. The county was also severely impacted by the Black Death, which almost entirely depopulated districts such as Winslow.

During the English Civil War, Buckinghamshire joined a mutual defence association supporting parliament, maintaining garrisons at Brill and Aylesbury. However, royalist troops briefly garrisoned Newport Pagnell, and King Charles I established his headquarters at Buckingham in 1644.

## Economy and Industry
Historically, Buckinghamshire has been an agricultural county famed for its cattle and corn. The vale of Aylesbury was noted for producing England's biggest bodied sheep, and the phrase "Buckinghamshire bread and beef" became an old proverb. 

Manufacturing was primarily centered on lace-making, introduced by Fleming refugees fleeing the Alva persecution. This became a highly profitable industry, particularly in Olney and Newport Pagnell; the parish of Hanslape reportedly earned £8,000 to £9,000 annually from it. However, the trade decayed due to the monopolies of James I, leading to a 1623 petition to the high sheriff regarding the people's distress. Additionally, a straw-plait industry introduced during the reign of George I formerly employed a large portion of the population. In the modern era, Wolverton became the site of the London & North-Western railway carriage works.

## Administration and Population
The ancient county area is 475,682 acres, while the administrative county is 479,358 acres. The population grew from 185,284 in 1891 to 195,764 in 1901. The county is divided into eight hundreds; three of these—Burnham, Stoke, and Desborough—constitute the "Chiltern Hundreds." The hundred of Aylesbury is known as the "three hundreds of Aylesbury."

The county town is Buckingham (pop. 3,152), while the largest municipal borough is Chepping Wycombe (also known as High or Chipping Wycombe, pop. 15,542). Other urban districts include Aylesbury, Slough, Chesham, Fenny Stratford, Newport Pagnell, Marlow, Eton, Linslade, and Beaconsfield. Lesser market towns include Amersham, Olney, Stony Stratford, Prince's Risborough, Wendover, Ivinghoe, and Winslow.

Administratively, the county is in the midland circuit with assizes held at Aylesbury. It contains 230 civil parishes. Parliamentarily, the county is divided into three divisions—Northern (Buckingham), Mid (Aylesbury), and Southern (Wycombe)—each returning one member. This follows a reduction from fourteen members in 1603 to eleven in 1832, and finally to three in 1885.

## Religion and Antiquities
The county is almost entirely within the diocese of Oxford, having been transferred from the Lincoln diocese in 1837. The most notable educational institution is Eton College, a public school founded by Henry VI.

Ecclesiastical architecture includes the church of St Mary in Aylesbury, which features a pre-Norman crypt and a 17th-century clock-turret. Other notable churches include Stewkley (fine Norman work), Wing (pre-Norman workmanship), and the Early English style of Haddenham and Chetwode. Perpendicular architecture is found in the churches of Hillesden and Maids Moreton, as well as the Eton College chapel.

Monastic remains are limited. Notley Abbey (1162) is noted for its picturesque location. Medmenham Abbey, a Cistercian foundation from 1201, became famous in the 18th century as the meeting place for Sir Francis Dashwood's "Franciscans" (or "Hell-Fire Club"), which included John Wilkes and Bubb Dodington.
