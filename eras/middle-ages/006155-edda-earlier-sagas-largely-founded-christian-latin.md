# The Edda and Early Sagas

The Edda and the early sagas constitute a significant body of Scandinavian literature, encompassing mythical narratives, heroic poetry, and historical accounts. These works serve as primary sources for the study of Old Norse philology, folklore, and the primitive sentiments of the Teutonic and Scandinavian races.

## The Edda and its Versions
The archive identifies different forms of the Edda. The *Elder Edda* (also referred to as *Norroen Fornkvoedi*) consists of epical fragments of Icelandic composition preserved in the *Codex Regius*, a manuscript discovered in the 17th century. These fragments are characterized as shorter and more episodical than lost Teutonic specimens and were written in alliterative verse.

The *Prose Edda*, attributed to Snorro Sturleson, includes genealogical tables and serves as a logographic record. Snorro is noted as being more faithful and less unfriendly to the old religion than other logographers of the ancient Scandinavian sagas. However, the *Prose Edda* also contains warnings advising Christian readers not to believe in ethnic deities or base their faith on such narratives.

## Theories of Origin and Influence
There are conflicting views regarding the origins of the Edda and the early sagas. Sophus Bugge, a Norwegian philologist and professor of comparative philology and Old Norse, maintained that the earlier sagas and the songs of the *Edda* were largely founded on Latin and Christian traditions. According to Bugge, these influences were imported into Scandinavian literature via England.

Conversely, other analyses suggest that the *Edda* represents a purely heathen version of these stories. For example, while the German epic *Niebelungen Lied* presents its characters and circumstances as Christian, the *Edda* shows no trace of anything other than heathenism. This version is seen as a reflection of the subjective mythical world of the race that sang them, relating to a common fund of fabulous adventures and superhuman personages.

## Mythical Figures and Narratives
The *Edda* and related sagas, such as the *Volsunga Saga*, feature mythical characters including Sigurd, Brynhilde, Gudrun, and Atle. These figures are celebrated by both German epic poets and Scandinavian Scalds, though with various additions. In the family of the Volsungs, a curse of destiny leads to ruin despite the characters' preëminent personal qualities—a narrative pattern that shows a remarkable analogy to Grecian mythical narratives, such as the stories of the Labdakids of Thêbes and Achilles.

Odin is a central figure in these texts, appearing in various roles. He is described as a god of war, a magician, a high priest of heathenism, and the great legislator of Scandinavia. Some accounts characterize him as an evil dæmon, while others describe him as a human who propagated a faith and eventually wounded himself in nine mortal places to prepare a feast of heroes in the palace of the God of war. Thor is also mentioned, appearing in some accounts as an evil dæmon.

## The Sagas and Heroic Literature
In the Middle Ages, a new heroic literature was developed in Iceland. The Sagas are described as a reduction to prose of the epics concerning the warlike history of the North, effectively replacing archaic Icelandic epics such as the poem *Atlamal*. 

Various sagas are preserved in collections such as *Heimskringla*, which includes the *Saga of Olaf Haraldson* (the longest and most finished of the sagas in that collection), the *Saga of King Harald Grafeld and of Earl Hakon son of Sigurd*, and *Hakon the Good's Saga*. These texts often intertwine the transition from the old religion to Christianity. For instance, Earls Eirik and Svein were baptized into the "true faith" but continued to uphold the old laws and customs of the land. Similarly, King Hakon is noted as a Christian who favored the old religion and spared the temples of Odin, resulting in him being assigned a place in Valhal.
