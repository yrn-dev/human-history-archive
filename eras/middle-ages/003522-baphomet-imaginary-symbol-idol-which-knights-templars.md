# BAPHOMET

Baphomet is described as an imaginary idol or symbol. It is associated with the Knights Templars, who were accused of worshipping this figure during their secret rites.

## Etymology and Interpretations
The origin of the term "Baphomet" is believed to be a corruption of the name *Mahomet*. This connection is evidenced by several medieval Latin poems in which Mahomet is referred to by this name.

Alternative interpretations of the word have been proposed by J. von Hammer-Purgstall in his works *Die Schuld der Templer* and *Mysterium Baphometis relevatum, &c.* Hammer-Purgstall argued that the term signifies the baptism of fire, or the baptism of *Metis*. Based on this interpretation, he linked the symbol to the impurities associated with the Gnostic Ophites. To support this claim, Hammer-Purgstall pointed to the architectural decorations found within the churches of the Templars.

## Academic Criticism
The arguments presented by Hammer-Purgstall were subjected to elaborate criticism in the *Journal des Savans* during March and April of 1819. This critique was authored by M. Raynouard, who was a well-known defender of the Knights Templars. Further references to these matters can be found in note 15 of chapter one of Hallam's *Middle Ages*.

## Context of the Knights Templars
The Knights Templars were a crusading military religious order, similar to the Hospitallers and the Spanish orders of Calatrava and Alcantara. While the origins of knighthood on the European continent remain obscure in many important details, the Templars are categorized among the more important religious orders of chivalry.

The order's history involved various geopolitical conflicts and alliances. In Tripoli, the Templars were established and encountered trouble during the reign of Bohemund VII, who died in 1287. Additionally, the Templars were noted for refusing to join certain enterprises due to a jealousy of the Hospitallers or a hatred for the perfidious breach of treaties.

## Related Allegations of Idolatry
The charges of idolatry against the Templars have occasionally been linked by some authors to broader Eastern heretical opinions. Some theories suggest a connection to Manichee or Gnostic standards, proposing that certain animals used as standards—such as the goat (representing the Devil or Typhon) and the goose (representing the "divine Son" in Egyptian symbols)—reflected a belief system of opposing principles of good and evil. These theories suggest that a portion of the crusading invaders may have been infected with Gnostic or Manichee idolatry, though such explanations are characterized as wild or subtile.
