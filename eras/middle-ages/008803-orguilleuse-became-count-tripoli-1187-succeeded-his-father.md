# Bohemund IV and the House of Antioch

## Succession to Tripoli and Antioch
Bohemund IV was the younger son of Bohemund III and his second wife, the princess Orguilleuse. In 1187, Bohemund IV became the count of Tripoli, succeeding the previous count in the possession of that county. Following the death of his father, Bohemund III, in 1201, Bohemund IV succeeded him in the principality of Antioch. This succession occurred to the exclusion of Raymund Rhupen.

## Conflict over the Principality
The succession of Bohemund IV was the subject of a dispute that lasted for many years. Raymund Rhupen was championed by Leo of Armenia, who was his great-uncle. Leo had previously attempted to force Bohemund III to cede Antioch in 1194, capturing him at Gastin. Bohemund IV pursued his own claim to the principality with vigor, and around 1199, he evicted his father from Antioch. However, he was subsequently ousted by Leo, who had become king of Armenia by the grace of Emperor Henry VI.

The conflict occupied the attention of Pope Innocent III for a significant period. Bohemund IV was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and by the Templars, who held estates in Tripoli. Conversely, Leo of Armenia was supported by the Hospitallers and appealed to both Emperor Otto IV and Pope Innocent III between 1210 and 1211.

In 1216, Leo captured Antioch and established Raymund Rhupen as its prince. This arrangement lasted less than four years; by the time Leo died in 1220, Bohemund IV was once again in possession of the city. Following the death of Raymund Rhupen in 1221, Bohemund IV reigned in both Antioch and Tripoli until his own death.

## Relations with the Church and Empire
During his reign, Bohemund IV proved to be a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, a stance that led to his excommunication in 1230. His political alliances shifted during the crusade of 1228–29, during which he first joined and then deserted Emperor Frederick II. As a result, he was excluded from the operation of the treaty of 1229. Before his death in 1233, Bohemund IV concluded a peace with the Hospitallers, and Pope Gregory IX released him from the excommunication of 1230.

## Lineage and Legacy
Bohemund IV married Plaisance, the daughter of Hugh of Gibelet. Their son, Bohemund V, succeeded his father in 1233 as the prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. Bohemund V continued his father's alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia until 1251.

The lineage continued through Bohemund V's son, Bohemund VI (born 1237), who succeeded his father in 1251. Bohemund VI established himself in Antioch in 1252, leaving Tripoli to its own administration. He eventually lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268. Upon Bohemund VI's death in 1275, he was only the count of Tripoli. He was succeeded by his son, Bohemund VII, who reigned during a troubled period marked by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli. Bohemund VII died without issue in 1287, and the county of Tripoli became extinct shortly thereafter when it was captured.
