# John Baliol and the Baliol Family

The Baliol family played a significant role in the history of Scotland and England, rising from Norman origins to the Scottish throne. The family's presence in England began with Guy (or Guido) de Baliol, a Norman baron who held the fiefs of Vinoy, Harcourt, Dampierre, and Bailleul in Normandy. After arriving in England with William the Conqueror, he received lands in the north of England from William II.

## The Establishment of Barnard Castle
A descendant of Guy—either his son or grandson—named Bernard (or Barnard) de Baliol, established a fortress in Durham known as Castle Bernard (later Barnard Castle). Around the middle of the 12th century, Bernard granted a charter to the residents of the town growing outside the castle walls, making them burgesses with privileges equivalent to those of Richmond in Yorkshire. 

Bernard was active in the civil wars of King Stephen, fighting in the battle of the Standard in August 1138 and becoming a prisoner at the battle of Lincoln in February 1141. Records suggest a younger Bernard de Baliol followed, who confirmed his father's charters to the burgesses and took William the Lion, king of Scotland, prisoner in July 1174. The lordship and castle remained with the Baliols through subsequent generations, including Eustace and Hugh, until the late 13th century.

## John de Baliol (d. 1269)
The family's wealth and influence grew significantly under John de Baliol (d. 1269), son of Hugh. He married Dervorguila, the daughter of Alan, earl of Galloway. Through this marriage, he is said to have held one half of the lands in Galloway and thirty knights' fees in England. He resided at the castle of Buittle (or Botel). 

John served as one of the regents of Scotland during the minority of Alexander III, though he was deprived of this office and had his lands forfeited for treason in 1255. He later fought for Henry III against Simon de Montfort and was captured at the battle of Lewes in 1264. In 1263, he established several scholarships at Oxford; following his death, his widow founded a college in the family's name.

## John Baliol, King of Scotland
The son of John de Baliol and Dervorguila, also named John de Baliol (1249–1315), became the head of the family in 1278. He held extensive lands across Scotland, France, and England. He rose to prominence in 1290 when the Scottish throne became vacant following the death of Margaret, the "maid of Norway."

Baliol claimed the crown through his maternal grandmother, Margaret, the eldest daughter of David, earl of Huntingdon. His primary rival was Robert Bruce, earl of Annandale. King Edward I of England was invited to settle the dispute. After demanding and receiving a formal recognition of his position as overlord of Scotland from the Scottish nobles and the candidates, Edward I appointed a court of twenty-four Englishmen and eighty Scotsmen to decide the matter. Based on the principles of feudal law regarding indivisible inheritance, the court decided in Baliol's favor.

John Baliol was crowned king of Scotland at Scone on November 30, 1292. He swore fealty and did homage to Edward I at Newcastle. However, relations deteriorated when Scottish vassals appealed to Edward I as Baliol's overlord. Eventually, Scottish barons induced Baliol to break his bond with the English king.

## Forfeiture and Aftermath
In 1296, the English army sacked the town of Berwick, and John Baliol surrendered to Antony, bishop of Durham, at Brechin. Consequently, John Baliol forfeited his English estates, including the lordship of Gainford and Barnard Castle.

The forfeiture of Barnard Castle led to a dispute over ownership. Antony, bishop of Durham, seized the castle, claiming it fell within his palatinate of Durham. However, Edward I denied the bishop's rights and instead granted the town and castle to Guy Beauchamp, earl of Warwick. The Beauchamp descendants held the property until it passed to the crown through the marriage of Richard III, duke of Gloucester, to Anne Nevill.

## Cadet Branches and Legacy
A cadet branch of the family descended from Ingelram, a son of the younger Bernard de Baliol. Ingelram's son, Henry, became chamberlain of Scotland around 1223. His probable son, Alexander de Baliol, also served as chamberlain and was involved in the treaties of Brigham (1290) and Salisbury (1289). Alexander may have been imprisoned alongside King John Baliol around 1296, after which he fought for Edward I in Scotland. The family's presence in Scottish records ended with Thomas, son of Alexander, who sold the estate of Cavers to William, earl of Douglas, in 1368.

A dubious tradition suggests that the family name became so discredited due to the conduct of Edward and John Baliol that some family members abandoned the name in favor of "Baillie."
