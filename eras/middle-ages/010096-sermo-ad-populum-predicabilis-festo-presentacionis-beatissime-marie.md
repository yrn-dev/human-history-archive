# Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie

The *Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie Semper Virginis* is a historical work of significant importance to the study of early printing and bibliography. It is recognized as the first known instance of a dated title-page in the history of printed books.

## Bibliographic Significance
In the early period of printing, the general appearance of printed copies was very similar to manuscripts written in formal book-hand, particularly when the book was printed in the same district where the manuscript originated. During this era, printers typically based their type on handwriting that scribes considered appropriate for the specific class of work. 

Standard early printed works generally lacked title-pages and often contained no statement regarding where, when, or by whom the book was produced. Decorative elements—such as paragraph marks, initial letters, headlines, chapter headings, and illustrations—were typically added by hand in styles that mirrored the decorations found in the original manuscripts. When production information was provided, it was reserved for a "colophon," a paragraph located at the end of the book. Printers often accompanied the colophon with a device, such as a fanciful design or the arms of the printer or the town of production, which sometimes served as the only identification of the printer's name.

## The First Dated Title-Page
The *Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie Semper Virginis* broke from this tradition. The work features a nine-line paragraph on an otherwise blank page, which serves as the first dated title-page known to history. This page provides the title of the book, includes words of praise for the work, and lists the date 1470 in roman numerals. Additionally, the title-page contains a reference directing the reader to further information on the following page.

## Production and Context
The book was printed in Cologne by Arnold ther Hoernen. This development preceded other notable advancements in title-page design; for example, six years after the publication of the *Sermo*, Erhard Ratdolt and his partners in Venice printed a Latin calendar that included their names, the date, and descriptive verses on the title-page, surrounded by a four-piece border.

Despite the precedent set by the *Sermo*, the use of title-pages remained inconsistent for another twenty years. When they were employed, they usually consisted only of a short title, occasionally accompanied by a printer's or publisher's device or a woodcut. During this period, ornamental borders and decorations were more frequently applied to the first page of the actual text rather than a separate title-page.
