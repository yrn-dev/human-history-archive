# Chester

## Roman Origins and Military Function
Chester, known in Roman times as Deva, served as one of the three great legionary fortresses of Roman Britain. It was the base for the Twentieth Legion, which, along with the Second Legion at Isca Silurum (Caerleon-on-Usk) and the Ninth Legion at Eburacum (York), comprised a heavy infantry force of approximately 15,000 men. These legionary fortresses were designed as large rectangular enclosures covering 50 to 60 acres. Traces of these original Roman walls are still visible today in the lower courses of the north and east town-walls of Chester.

Unlike York, Chester remained purely military in nature, and there is no evidence to support the notion that it served as the seat of a Christian bishopric during the 4th century. As a strategic hub, Chester was the starting point for several military roads. One road led from Chester past a fort at Caer-hyn to Carnarvon (Segontium), while another complex route passed up the western coast, with forts located at Manchester (Mancunium or Mamucium), Ribchester (Bremetennacum), Brougham Castle (Brocavum), and Old Penrith (Voreda).

## Post-Roman Period and Early Middle Ages
Following the Roman era, the history of many British cities became obscure. According to Gildas, Roman Britain contained twenty-eight cities (*civitates*) and various fortresses (*castetta*). While some cities like London and Canterbury recovered their importance within two centuries after the invasion, Chester experienced a significant decline. The city appears to have been deserted for three centuries following its destruction early in the 7th century.

The term *ceaster* (derived from the Latin *castra*) was applied to both Roman cities and fortresses. In the early period, there is little evidence of towns existing other than those of Roman origin. It was not until the end of the 9th century, with the defensive systems initiated by King Alfred and the establishment of Danish settlements, that a new series of fortified towns emerged, forming the basis for medieval boroughs.

## Legal Customs and Administration
In the medieval period, Chester was associated with specific legal privileges. An ancient manuscript concerning the earls of Chester indicates that the bailiffs or serjeants of the earls possessed the authority to behead thieves or any malefactor. This practice involved the presentation of the heads of felons at the castle of Chester. This practice, referred to in a roll of 3 Edward II as the "custom of Cheshire," also appeared to be attached to the barony of Malpas.

The influence of the earldom extended to other regions; for example, at the time of the Domesday Survey, Macclesfield formed part of the lands of the earl of Chester.

## Sporting Traditions and the Roodee
By the 17th century, Chester had developed a tradition of horse racing on the Roodee. In 1609, Robert Ambrye, a former sheriff of the city, established races on St George's day. The prizes for these events were three silver bells of good value. These functioned as challenge cups, where the winning horse kept the best bell and the sweepstake money until the following year.

These challenge bells remained the standard prize until 1623 or 1624. At that time, John Brereton, the mayor of Chester, altered the racing course to require horses to run five times around the Roodee. He also changed the prize to a "free bell" valued at £8 or £10, which was a permanent presentation rather than a challenge prize. In the 19th century, racing continued to evolve, with the establishment of the Chester Cup in 1824.
