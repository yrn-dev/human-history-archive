# The Succession of the Grand Kaans and the Reign of Chinghis Kaan

## The Rise and Reign of Chinghis Kaan
In the year of Christ's Incarnation 1187, the Tartars chose a king named Chinghis Kaan. Described as a man of great valour, ability, and eloquence, he was recognized as Lord by all the Tartars in the world. Upon his ascension, he organized a vast multitude of people and equipped them with spears, arrows, and other arms to begin a campaign of conquest. He successfully conquered eight provinces; during these conquests, he protected the people and their property, establishing his own men in the regions alongside the local populations. This noble treatment led the conquered peoples to become his devoted followers.

By the year 1200, Chinghis Kaan sought to expand his influence further and sent an embassy to Prester John to request his daughter's hand in marriage. Prester John reacted with anger, viewing Chinghis Kaan as a rebel, traitor, and serf, stating he would rather set his daughter on fire than marry her to him. This brutal message provoked Chinghis Kaan to muster a host of unprecedented size to seek revenge.

## The Conflict with Prester John
The two forces eventually met on the plains of Tanduc. Before the engagement, Chinghis Kaan consulted astrologers of both the Saracen and Christian faiths to determine the outcome of the battle. While the Saracens could not provide a true answer, the Christians performed a divination using a split cane. They assigned one half of the cane to Chinghis Kaan and the other to Prester John. After reading a Psalm and performing incantations, the piece representing Chinghis Kaan moved on its own to sit atop the piece representing Prester John. Greatly delighted by this accuracy, the Kaan thereafter treated Christians with great respect.

Following two days of rest, the two hosts engaged in a desperate combat described as the greatest battle ever seen. Although casualties were heavy on both sides, Chinghis Kaan emerged victorious and Prester John was slain. Consequently, Prester John's kingdom gradually passed into the hands of Chinghis Kaan until it was entirely conquered.

## Death and Burial Customs
Chinghis Kaan continued his conquests for six years following the battle with Prester John, taking many strongholds, cities, and provinces. His reign ended when he was shot in the knee with an arrow while attacking a castle called Caaju, a wound from which he died.

The archive records a specific funerary tradition for the Grand Kaans and the descendants of Chinghis Kaan. Regardless of where a sovereign dies—even if the location is a 100 days' journey away—the body must be carried to a mountain called Altay to be interred with his predecessors.

## The Succession of the Grand Kaans
Following the death of the first Lord, the sovereignty of the Tartars passed through a succession of leaders. The order of reign was as follows:
1. Chinghis Kaan
2. Cuy Kaan
3. Batuy Kaan
4. Alacou Kaan
5. Mongou Kaan
6. Cublay Kaan

Cublay Kaan, the sixth in the imperial lineage, ascended to the throne in the year of Christ 1256. His claim was disputed by kinsmen and brothers, but he maintained the throne through his valour and his direct descent from the imperial line. By the year 1298, Cublay Kaan had reigned for forty-two years and was approximately eighty-five years of age. 

Cublay Kaan is described as being more potent than any of the five predecessors combined. It is asserted that the combined power of all the Christians in the world, including their Kings and Emperors, along with the Saracens, would not equal the power of Cublay Kaan, who serves as the Lord of all the Tartars of the Levant and the Ponent. Although he was a gallant soldier and captain before his ascension, he only personally led his armies to war once after becoming throne, in the year 1286.

## Imperial Lineage and Heirs
The Great Kaan (Cublay) had four wives, with whom he fathered twenty-two male children. The eldest was named Chinkin, named in honor of Chinghis Kaan. Although Chinkin was intended to reign after his father, he died, leaving behind a son named Temur. Temur is designated to be the Great Kaan and Emperor upon his grandfather's death, as he is the child of the eldest son. Additionally, the Great Kaan fathered twenty-five sons by concubines, all of whom are described as valiant soldiers and great chiefs. Among the children born to the four lawful wives, seven serve as governors or kings of vast provinces.
