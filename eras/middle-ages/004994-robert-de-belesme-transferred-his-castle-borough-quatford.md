# Robert de Belesme and the Development of Bridgnorth

## Transfer of the Castle and Borough
Robert de Belesme, the Earl of Shrewsbury, is credited with a significant shift in the early development of Bridgnorth. He transferred his castle and borough from Quatford to Bridgnorth. Prior to this move, Robert de Belesme had originally founded a college at Quatford, which was subsequently moved to Bridgnorth. The site at Quatford, located one mile south-east of the town, contains the traces of a castle dating from 1085 that once dominated the ancient Forest of Morf.

## Biography of Robert de Belesme
Robert de Belesme (fl. 1100) was a powerful and independent feudal noble who served as the Earl of Shrewsbury. He inherited the fief of Shrewsbury from his father, a companion of the Conqueror, and the fief of Belesme from his mother, Mabel Talvas. These were march-fiefs designed to guard England from the Welsh and Normandy from Maine.

Described as a tall and strong man, Robert was an able military engineer and was noted for being persuasive, eloquent, and daring in battle. However, he was also characterized as a systematic rebel, a traitor, and an oppressor of the weak who was greedy for land and savagely cruel. His political history was marked by frequent instability:
* In 1077, he supported a rebellion against the Conqueror.
* In 1088, he was an accomplice in an English conspiracy against Rufus.
* In 1098, he succeeded his brother Hugh in the earldom of Shrewsbury.
* In 1102, he revolted against Henry I.

Following his revolt in 1102, Robert was deprived of his English estate and banished. Although he remained at large in Normandy for a time and betrayed the cause of Robert at Tinchebrai, he was eventually imprisoned for life by Henry I in 1112.

## Evolution of Bridgnorth
The early history of Bridgnorth began in 912 when Æthelfleda, lady of the Mercians, raised a mound there as part of an offensive policy against the Danes of the five boroughs. Following the Conquest, William I granted the manor of Bridgnorth to Earl Roger of Shrewsbury, the father of Robert de Belesme.

After Robert de Belesme's attainder in 1102, Bridgnorth became a royal borough. The town's burgesses were granted various privileges over the following centuries:
* **Henry I:** It is probable that he granted the burgesses certain privileges, which were later confirmed by Henry II.
* **King John:** In 1215, he granted the burgesses freedom from toll throughout England, with the exception of the city of London.
* **Henry III:** In 1227, he conferred new liberties, including a hanse with a gild merchant.
* **Henry VI:** He granted additional privileges, including the assize of ale and bread.

Bridgnorth was officially incorporated by James I in 1546. In terms of political representation, the burgesses returned two members to parliament starting in 1295; this continued until 1867, when the number was reduced to one member, before the town was finally disfranchised in 1885.

## Geography and Town Features
Bridgnorth is a municipal borough and market town in Shropshire, located 150 miles north-west by west from London. The town is divided by the river Severn into a lower town on the left bank and an upper town on the right bank, which are connected by a steep line of rail. The upper town is situated on a rock rising 180 feet from the river. On the summit of this rock stands the tower of the old castle, which leans approximately 17° from the perpendicular.

The town is characterized by picturesque half-timbered domestic buildings, a style seen in the town hall (built in 1652) and a house dated 1580. Educational and religious institutions include a grammar school founded in 1503, a college of divinity, a blue-coat school, and a literary institute. The town's two parish churches are St Mary's, built in the classic style of the late 18th century, and St Leonard, which was practically rebuilt in 1862 and was formerly collegiate.
