# Ring-Sangs, Dances, Ledes, and Rounds

The terms "ring-sangs, dances, ledes, and rounds" refer to forms of lyrical and musical expression often associated with communal activity, folk-lore, and ritual. These expressions frequently combine vocal performance with physical movement, serving various social, celebratory, and religious functions across different cultures and eras.

## Nature and Definition of Ring-Sangs
Ring-sangs are defined as dancing songs or ballads. An example of a piece that was likely danced to is *Young Tamlane*, which possessed an appropriate air for such a purpose. These forms of music are often linked to the institution of dances, a connection that is still observed in warmer and pleasanter climates. It is conjectured that ballads may have originated from these dancing institutions.

The legends that form the basis of these ballads are found across a vast geographical range, from the White Sea to Cape Matapan, suggesting they are part of a stock of primitive folk-lore. These legends possess an immemorial antiquity, as do the lyrical choruses used for their improvised musical rendering. Some of these ballads are considered distinctly popular and impersonal in their origin.

## Historical and Cultural Examples of Dance-Songs
Evidence of singing during dances appears in various historical accounts:
*   **Scotland:** The chronicler Fabyan notes that a song of triumph over Edward II was sung "in dances" to the carols of minstrels and maidens. Similarly, the *Complaynt of Scotland* describes shepherds and their wives singing "melodi sangs" before beginning to dance in a ring.
*   **Ancient Greece:** In the Attic tradition, a "circular or cyclic chorus" specifically referred to a group that sang and danced around an altar, with particular reference to dithyrambic choruses dedicated to Bacchus.
*   **Alexandria:** Epiphanius describes a celebration of the Birth of the Aeon at the Temple of the Maiden (Kore). Votaries sang hymns to flute music until dawn, then carried a wooden idol of Kore seven times around a central shrine accompanied by hymns, dances, and flute-playing.

## Relationship to Epic Poetry and Other Forms
There are noted resemblances between popular ballads and early epic poetry, such as the French *epopees* and the works of Homer. These similarities include a directness and simplicity of style, the use of recurring epithets (such as "salt sea," "green grass," and "shadowy hills"), the repetition of speeches, and a "barbaric profusion" in the description of silver and gold. Furthermore, it is suggested that Greek drama evolved from the sacred choruses of village vintagers. However, these similarities do not imply that the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* are merely collections of ballads; they are regarded as the highest form of artistic epic.

## Poetry, Music, and Bravery
In some cultures, music and song were intentionally designed to inspire courage and prowess. In Laconian poetry, songs were written in plain, unaffected language on noble subjects, such as panegyrics for those who died for their country or reproaches for cowards. 

During festivals, three different choruses—consisting of old men, young men, and children—would sing in succession, with each group claiming superiority in strength or youth. This connection between poetry and bravery is echoed in the writings of Pindar and Terpander, who noted that in Lacedaemon, song and dance were honored on festal nights and that "the sword with song full well combines."

## Other Lyrical and Verse Traditions
Beyond the communal dance-songs, other forms of verse and song served specific social functions:
*   **Old English Poetry:** Poetry such as *Beowulf* was characterized by alliteration, bold compounds, and metaphorical expressions not found in prose.
*   **Norse Verse:** In the *Heimskringla*, figures such as Sigvat and Thormod used song and verse to recount travels, express grievances, or praise the bravery of leaders. For instance, Thormod sang verses praising King Olaf's courage at Stiklestad, while Sigvat used song to petition the king for the remission of harbour-dues for poor men.
