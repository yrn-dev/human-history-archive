# Jerusalem in the 7th Century

During the 7th century, Jerusalem served as a center for various Christian liturgical celebrations and processions, reflecting the city's role in commemorating the life of Christ and the Blessed Virgin.

## The Feast of the Ascension
In the 7th century, the Feast of the Ascension was observed in Jerusalem with a professional pilgrimage. According to the descriptions provided by Adamnan, the abbot of Iona, this procession took place in commemoration of the passing of the apostles and Christ to the Mount of Olives. The feast was celebrated specifically in the church located on Mount Olivet. 

The Feast of the Ascension is an oecumenical festival of the Christian Church, ranking in solemnity alongside Pentecost, Easter, and Christmas. It is held forty days after the resurrection, falling on a Thursday known as Ascension Day or Holy Thursday. While there are no discoverable traces of the festival before the middle of the 4th century, later references suggest it was established from time immemorial and was likely instituted by the apostles.

## The Festival of the Conception
The Greek Church also celebrated the festival of the Conception of the Blessed Virgin as a distinct event from her Nativity during the 7th century. This is evidenced by the canons of St. Andrew of Jerusalem (also referred to as St. Andrew of Crete), who died approximately A.D. 700. While the Western Church eventually set this feast for December 8, the Greek tradition has always observed it one day later.

## Other Liturgical Traditions in the Holy Places
The archive contains records of various shifts in the dating and celebration of feasts within the "holy places" of Jerusalem and its environs. In earlier periods, such as around A.D. 385, January 6 was observed in Jerusalem, or more specifically in Bethlehem, as the physical birthday of Christ. 

There was a period during which the feasts of the Birth and the Baptism were joined on January 6. Cosmas Indicopleustes, writing around 550, noted that believers in Jerusalem joined these feasts based on the reasoning that the Annunciation occurred on April 6, making the Birth fall on January 6, which coincided with the Baptism. 

Historical correspondence from a bishop of Jerusalem to the bishop of Rome describes the logistical difficulties of this combined celebration. The faithful would meet before dawn in the cave at Bethlehem to celebrate the Birth from the Virgin; however, they were forced to leave before hymns and lections were completed to travel 13 miles on the other side of Jerusalem to Jordan to celebrate the Baptism. This necessitated a request to the pope to consult archives in Rome to determine the real date of the Birth, which the pope identified as December 25 through the works of Josephus.
