# Bavaria

Bavaria is a region in central Europe situated in South Germany. Geographically, it is traversed by the Danube river, which serves as a central artery for the region, as well as for Austria, Hungary, and Württemberg. The territory of Bavaria shares a border to the southwest with the kingdom and crownland of Bohemia.

## Early Medieval and Imperial History
In the early history of the region, the Bavarians are noted for marching eastward across the Danube. During the 14th century, the region was governed by the House of Wittelsbach. Louis IV (c. 1287–1347), known as the Bavarian, served as the Roman emperor and duke of Upper Bavaria. The son of Louis II and Matilda, Louis IV initially inherited Upper Bavaria and the Palatinate jointly with his elder brother, Rudolph. Following a period of variance over their joint possessions, a partition of the lands occurred in 1310, granting Louis the north-western part of the duchy of Upper Bavaria.

During this period, Louis IV also undertook the guardianship of the two young sons of Stephen I, duke of Lower Bavaria, upon Stephen's death in 1310. This led to a conflict that lasted until June 1313, ending with a peace agreement made at Munich. Louis further established his renown on November 9, 1313, by defeating Frederick I (the Fair), duke of Austria, at Gammelsdorf, after Frederick had been called in to aid nobles in Lower Bavaria who were angered by Louis.

## Relations with the House of Habsburg
Bavaria and the House of Habsburg (the rulers of Austria) maintained a long-standing secular rivalry in southern Germany. During the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748), Bavaria acted as a constant ally to France and Prussia.

Throughout the 18th century, Austrian statesmen considered the annexation of Bavaria, which was then ruled by the "decadent" house of Wittelsbach, to increase Habsburg strength in South Germany and remove a perpetual menace to Austrian peace. This tension culminated in 1777 following the death of elector Maximilian III. While the heir was elector palatine Charles Theodore, Emperor Joseph II claimed the inheritance and prepared to use force. This resulted in the War of Bavarian Succession. However, no actual fighting occurred between the armies of Joseph and Frederick; Maria Theresa overruled her son to avoid further struggle with Prussia. Through the treaty of Teschen, Austria accepted the cession of some districts and the Quarter of the Inn (Innviertel) instead of full annexation.

## 19th Century Political Transitions
In the mid-19th century, Bavaria faced significant political challenges. Louis, who succeeded to the throne on March 10, 1864, at age eighteen, struggled against the powerful policies of Bismarck regarding the Schleswig-Holstein question. In the war of 1866, Louis and his minister von der Pfordten allied with Austria. The subsequent defeat forced Bavaria to surrender small portions of its territory and agree to the foundation of the North German Confederation under Prussian leadership. Bavaria, along with Austria, Baden, Hesse, and Württemberg, was excluded from this Confederation.

Following this defeat, Louis implemented army reforms under the Liberal ministry of Hohenlohe (1866–1870) and war minister Prauckh. Domestically, Louis remained true to the Catholic Church but sought greater independence from the Vatican, maintaining friendly relations with Ignaz von Döllinger and liberal Catholics who opposed the definition of papal infallibility. Due to opposition from Old Bavarians regarding the Liberalism of the Hohenlohe ministry, Louis formed a Conservative cabinet under Count Bray-Steinburg in early 1870.

During the Franco-Prussian War, Louis mobilized and sided with Prussia. In 1871, prompted by Bismarck, Louis offered the imperial crown to the king of Prussia.

## Modern Borders
In the aftermath of the First World War, the treaty terms specified that the western and northwestern frontiers of Austria, which face Bavaria and Switzerland, were to remain unchanged.
