# BOHUN

## The Bohun Family in England
The Bohuns were a family of significant importance in English history during the 13th and 14th centuries. The family name originated from a village in the Cotentin, located between the estuary of the Vire and Coutances. While their early history in England following their arrival at or shortly after the Norman Conquest is obscure, the family's rise to prominence began with Humphrey III. During the later years of Henry I, Humphrey III served as a *dapifer* (steward) in the royal household. He married the daughter of Milo of Gloucester and maintained an ambiguous political position during the reign of Stephen, shifting his allegiance from the king to the empress. Humphrey III remained loyal to Henry II, fighting against East Anglian rebels at Farnham in 1173, and lived until 1187.

## Earldoms and Political Influence
Humphrey III was succeeded in the family estates by his grandson, Henry, who was the son of Margaret, a sister of William the Lion, connecting the Bohuns to the royal house of Scotland. This connection likely aided Henry in obtaining the earldom of Hereford from John in 1199. The family's lands were primarily situated on the Welsh Marches, establishing the Bohuns as leading Marcher barons. Henry de Bohun was one of the twenty-five barons elected to enforce the terms of the Great Charter. During the subsequent civil war, he fought for Louis and was captured at the battle of Lincoln in 1217. He died on a pilgrimage on June 1, 1220.

His son and heir, Humphrey V, returned to royal loyalty and inherited the earldom of Essex before 1239 from his maternal uncle, William de Mandeville. However, by 1258, Humphrey V shifted his support to the baronial cause, serving as an opposition nominee on the committee of twenty-four appointed at the Oxford parliament to reform administration. He eventually returned to royal allegiance following the alliance between Montfort and Llewelyn of North Wales.

## Later History and Hereditary Offices
The Bohuns held the office of Lord High Constable of England, a position originally held by the commander of the royal armies and master of the horse. This constableship had been granted as a grand serjeanty with the earldom of Hereford by the empress Maud to Milo of Gloucester and passed to the Bohuns through his heiress. The office eventually descended to the Staffords, dukes of Buckingham, through a Bohun coheiress, before merging with the crown during the reign of Henry VIII following the attainder of Edward Stafford, the third duke of Buckingham.

In the late 13th and early 14th centuries, Humphrey Bohun, earl of Hereford, collaborated with Roger Bigod, the 5th earl of Norfolk. In 1297 and 1301, they prevented the collection of an aid for war and forced King Edward I to confirm the charters. Later, Humphrey Bohun, earl of Hereford, was involved in land disputes in Glamorganshire with the younger Hugh le Despenser, supporting the Mowbrays.

The family's influence extended through marriage into other noble houses, including the Courtenays. Additionally, in 1327, Eleanor de Bohun—daughter of Humphrey, earl of Hereford and Essex and high constable of England—married James, the son of Edmund the Butler. James was subsequently created the Irish earl of Ormonde in 1328. Later, Thomas, the 7th earl of Ormonde (Lord Rochford), held a title derived from a Bohun manor in Essex.

## Ruggero Bonghi
Ruggero Bonghi (1828–1895) was an Italian politician, writer, and scholar born in Naples on March 20, 1828. Following the movement of 1848, he was exiled from Naples to Tuscany, and later fled to Turin after writing a pungent article against the Bourbons. In Turin, he focused on philosophic studies and the translation of Plato. He declined a professorship of Greek at Pavia under the Austrian government in 1858, but accepted the position in 1859 under the Italian government following the liberation of Lombardy.

Bonghi was elected deputy in 1860 and became known for the biting wit of his speeches and acrimonious polemical journalism. He opposed the work of Bertani, Crispi, and Garibaldi at Naples in 1860 and served as secretary to Luigi Carlo Farini. In 1865, he became the chair of Latin literature at Florence and editor of the Milanese publication *Perseveranza*.

As a supporter of the historic Right, Bonghi was tasked by the Lanza cabinet in 1870 with defending the law of guarantees. Appointed minister for public instruction in 1873, he reformed the Italian educational system, founded the Vittorio Emanuele library in Rome, suppressed the privileges of the university of Naples, and blocked the creation of a Catholic university in Rome. After the Right fell from power in 1876, he joined the opposition and successfully secured the rejection of Baccelli's University Reform Bill. 

Bonghi was a critic of King Humbert, which led to his exclusion from court in 1893; he was readmitted shortly before his death on October 22, 1895. In foreign policy, he was a Francophil who opposed the Triple Alliance and participated in organizing the inter-parliamentary peace conference.
