## Achille Luchaire and French Institutions
Denis Jean Achille Luchaire (1846–1908) was a prominent French historian born in Paris on October 24, 1846. His academic career included serving as a professor at Bordeaux starting in 1879 and as a professor of medieval history at the Sorbonne beginning in 1899. He was elected a member of the *Académie des sciences morales et politiques* in 1895, and he received the Jean Reynaud prize shortly before his death on November 14, 1908.

Luchaire's scholarly contributions focused heavily on French monarchical and medieval history. His earlier significant works include:
* *Histoire des institutions monarchiques de la France sous les premiers Capétiens* (published in 1883 and 1891).
* *Manuel des institutions françaises: période des Capétiens directs* (1892).
* *Louis VI. le Gros, annales de sa vie et de son règne* (1890).
* *Étude sur les actes de Louis VII* (1885).

Additionally, Luchaire contributed to E. Lavisse's *Histoire de France*, writing two of the earlier volumes, including a volume titled *Les Premiers Capétiens* and a section in tome iii, 1st part (pp. 1-81).

In his later career, Luchaire shifted his focus toward the history of the papacy, producing an elaborate six-part work on Pope Innocent III. These parts include *Rome et Italie* (1904), *La Croisade des Albigeois* (1905), *La Papauté et l'empire* (1905), *La Question d'Orient* (1906), *Les Royautés vassales du Saint-Siège* (1908), and *Le Concile de Latran et la réforme de l'Église* (1908).

## Honor in Classical Greek Narrative
In the context of Greek history and literature, specifically regarding Achilles, honor is presented as a central motivation. In the first book of the *Iliad*, the prayers of Achilles and Thetis to Zeus seek honor for Achilles and redress for the wrongs he suffered. This is intended to make the Greeks and Agamemnôn acutely aware of the wrong done to the bravest warrior.

The narrative indicates that Zeus inflicted defeats upon the Greeks—including the battle of the eighth book (Κόλος Μάχη)—reluctantly, but as a necessity to ensure Achilles was honored. By the ninth book, a tender of honor is made to Achilles, including the offer of Agamemnôn's daughter in marriage without the usual bride price and the restitution of Brisêis. While Achilles is depicted in the ninth book as declaring he does not care for being honored by the Greeks, other passages, such as the address by Patroclus to the Myrmidons, emphasize fighting bravely specifically to bring honor to Achilles and to highlight the "mad folly" of Agamemnôn in dishonoring him.

## Forced Labor and the Corvee
The concept of the corvee—forced labor—has been utilized in Egypt since time immemorial, primarily for cleaning irrigation canals. Originally, when only one harvest was reaped annually, this was not a significant burden. However, the introduction of sugar-cane and cotton under Mehemet Ali increased the demand for watering at various seasons, often calling the fellah away from his own crops.

The system was characterized by inequality, as wealthy individuals could purchase exemptions for the same price as the humblest fellah, placing the hardship on the poorer classes. Between 1888 and 1891, under the premiership of Riaz Pasha, the corvee was gradually abolished and replaced by a small land rate to fund canal cleaning. Currently, the corvee is only used to a limited extent for guarding the banks of the Nile during floods. Scholarly references for this topic include the *Glossarium inf. et med. Lat.* by Du Cange and the *Manuel des institutions francaises* (1892) by A. Luchaire (pp. 346-349).
