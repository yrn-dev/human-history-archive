# Scots

The historical records within the archive regarding the Scots primarily detail their military interactions with English border towns and the political influence of families with ties to Scotland.

## Impact on Bamburgh
The town of Bamburgh suffered significant disruptions due to conflicts with the Scots. In 1318 and 1327, the burgesses of the town were acquitted of their annual fee farm rent of 26 marks, which had been granted by Henry III. in a charter of 1254-1255, specifically because of the "robberies and fires" inflicted upon them by the Scots. Later, during the Scottish wars that occurred during the reign of Henry V, Bamburgh again experienced severe suffering. The impact on the population was drastic; by 1439, the number of burgesses had decreased from 120 to only 13. Consequently, the burgesses petitioned for a remission of their farm, which was reduced to £10 yearly in 1446.

## The Baliol Family
The Baliol family played an important role in the history of Scotland. The family's presence in England began with Guy (or Guido) de Baliol, a Norman baron who held fiefs in Normandy and received lands in northern England from William II. after arriving with William the Conqueror. 

A later member of the family, Bernard de Baliol (likely the son of an elder Bernard), was among the northern barons who raised the siege of Alnwick in July 1174, during which he took William the Lion, the king of Scotland, prisoner. 

The family's connection to Scotland expanded through John de Baliol, who married Dervorguila, the daughter of Alan, earl of Galloway. Through this marriage, John is said to have possessed one half of the lands in Galloway. He served as one of the regents of Scotland during the minority of Alexander III, though he was deprived of this office and had his lands forfeited for treason in 1255. John de Baliol died in 1269, and his lands eventually passed to his son, also named John de Baliol, who became king of Scotland.
