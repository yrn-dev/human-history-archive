# Worship and Religious Practices

The archive's records detail various forms of worship, ritual, and religious celebration across different cultures and eras, ranging from the mystic rites of the Pelasgi to the oecumenical festivals of the Christian Church.

## Pelasgic and Greek Worship
The Pelasgi, described in legends as having a cruel and piratical character, practiced several distinct forms of ancient worship. They were known for the worship of Hêphæstus and the performance of mystic rites in honor of the Kabeiri. These Kabeiri rites were specifically noted at Imbros and Lemnos, with Philostratus providing a description of the periodical Lemnian solemnities. Additionally, the Pelasgi performed human sacrifices to their Great Goddess.

In Athens, the Hephaesteum (also known as the Theseum since the middle ages) stands as a primary example of Greek religious architecture. This Doric peripteral hexastyle temple, built of Pentelic marble with Parian marble used for the cella's zophoros, is identified by Pausanias as a temple to Athena and Hephaestus. While the temple's sculptures represent the exploits of Theseus and the labors of Heracles rather than Hephaestus, the building's preservation is attributed to its conversion into a church during the middle ages.

## Christian Festivals and Traditions
The Feast of the Ascension is one of the oecumenical festivals of the Christian Church, ranking in solemnity alongside Pentecost, Easter, and Christmas. Celebrated forty days after the resurrection (ten days before Whitsunday), it commemorates Christ's ascension into heaven and always falls on a Thursday, known as Holy Thursday or Ascension Day.

While no traces of the festival are discoverable before the middle of the 4th century, St Augustine noted it had been kept from time immemorial and was likely instituted by the apostles. Historical records indicate various local celebrations:
* In the church of Romanesia outside Antioch, as mentioned by Chrysostom.
* In a suburb of Constantinople, where the people celebrated the feast "of old custom" in the year 390.
* In the Church of the Nativity at Bethlehem, according to the *Peregrinatio* of Etheria (c. A.D. 385).
* In the church on Mount Olivet in Jerusalem during the 7th century, as described by Adamnan, abbot of Iona.

These celebrations were often associated with professional pilgrimages to commemorate the passing of the apostles and Christ to the Mount of Olives. In the West during the middle ages, this tradition evolved into processions featuring banners and candles outside the church, serving as a symbol of Christ's triumphant entry into heaven.

## Beliefs in the Middle Ages and Other Traditions
Religious and spiritual beliefs in the middle ages included the concept of the incubus and succubus. These were sometimes regarded as spiritual beings, though they were believed to provide real proof of their bodily existence. This reflects a broader pattern among primitive peoples who did not clearly distinguish between material and immaterial beings.

In the context of the middle ages, the epic poetry of the era was deeply imbued with religious ideas. However, unlike the Grecian epics—where heroes were closely linked to sacred localities and existing worship—the medieval epic was not directly amalgamated with the religion of the people and did not always find favor with the clergy.

In the Koran, Alexander is described as "the Horned," a detail based on the legend that God caused horns to grow on his head to enable him to overthrow all things. This is believed to be traceable to coins depicting Alexander with the ram's horns of Ammon.
