# Austria, Salzburg, Tirol, Vorarlberg, and Styria

The territories comprising Upper and Lower Austria, Salzburg, Tirol (excluding the south), Vorarlberg, and the majority of Styria (excluding the southern districts) formed the purely German kernel of the Austrian empire. These regions were characterized by a strong sense of loyalty to the ruling house and the concept of the Austrian state, though they experienced internal political divisions between Centralists and Federalists.

## Political and Social Structure
Political tension in these regions often aligned with racial and ideological divides. The Liberal party, which held dominance in Styria and Lower Austria, advocated for the development of central institutions. Conversely, a strong Clerical and Conservative party supported local institutions as a means of protection against the influence of a centralized bureaucracy and parliament. This Conservative faction, including the clergy and bishops, frequently allied with Federalists.

In some areas, local feeling was particularly intense, notably in Tirol. To manage racial rivalries, the government utilized a system of separate constituencies and registers for electors of different races. In mixed districts, Germans were required to vote for German candidates, ensuring that while internal struggles between Clerical and Liberal Germans occurred, Germans could not be defeated by other racial groups.

## Administrative and Legal History
The administration of these lands saw the continuation or revival of provincial diets and estates—specifically in Vorarlberg and Tirol in 1816, and the circle of Salzburg in 1828. These bodies were not representative, as eligibility was limited to nobles, clergy, and a few town delegates. Their functions were largely restricted to local police matters and the registration of imperial decrees regarding taxation and recruiting.

During the 19th century, the state attempted to curb the growth of internal Liberalism through rigid supervision of the press and the re-establishment of clerical control over education. Economic stagnation accompanied this administrative rigidity, with trade hampered by internal *octrois* and high frontier tariffs, and agriculture burdened by the feudal privileges of a noble caste.

## Territorial Evolution and the Habsburgs
The Habsburg family established their presence in these lands starting in 1273 when Rudolph, count of Habsburg, became German king. After the submission of Ottakar in 1276, Rudolph renounced the duchies of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia. On December 27, 1282, Rudolph invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Styria and Austria.

Throughout the 14th and 15th centuries, these lands were frequently divided among heirs:
* In 1379, Leopold III and Albert III divided their lands; Albert kept Austria proper and Carniola, while Leopold received Tirol, Carinthia, and Styria.
* In 1406, Ernest and Frederick divided their holdings, with Ernest becoming duke of Styria and Carinthia, and Frederick becoming count of Tirol.
* Later, Ferdinand divided the German Habsburg inheritance among three sons: Maximilian received Austria proper, Archduke Ferdinand received Tirol, and Archduke Charles received Styria, Carniola, and Carinthia.

## Religious Transitions
The religious landscape of these regions shifted significantly between the 16th and 17th centuries. Under Emperor Maximilian II (1564–1576), a liberal policy was maintained, though Catholicism remained almost exclusively confined to the mountains of Tirol, while nearly all of Styria and the whole of Austria were mainly Lutheran.

This changed with the accession of the fanatical Spanish Catholic Rudolph II (1576–1612), who encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation. Later, Ferdinand II, who had already crushed Protestantism as archduke of Styria, sought to win back Germany to the Catholic faith.

## Regional Profiles

### Upper Austria
Upper Austria (*Oberoesterreich*) is an archduchy and crown-land bounded by Lower Austria, Salzburg, Styria, Bavaria, and Bohemia. It has an area of 4,631 sq. m. The region is divided by the Danube; the northern part is a prolongation of the Bohemian forest, while the southern part belongs to the Eastern Alps, including the Salzkammergut. The population in 1900 was 809,918, the highest density of any Alpine province, consisting almost exclusively of Roman Catholics of German stock. Its capital is Linz, and other principal towns include Steyr, Wels, Ischl, and Gmunden.

### Styria and Marburg
Styria is home to the town of Marburg, situated on the left bank of the river Drave. As of 1900, Marburg had a population of 24,501 and served as the seat of the bishop of Lavant. The town is a center for traffic in grain and wine due to its fertile surroundings and connections via railway to Vienna, Tirol, Carinthia, and Trieste. Its industries include iron and tin wares, leather, and sparkling wine.

## Medicine and Science
Homoeopathy was introduced to Austria around 1817. Although forbidden by law in 1819, it gained traction after the physician to archduke John adopted the practice. By 1901, it was estimated that approximately seventy-five homoeopathic physicians were scattered across Salzburg, Tirol, and other provinces, in addition to those practicing in various cities.
