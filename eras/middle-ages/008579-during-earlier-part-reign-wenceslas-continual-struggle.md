# The Reign of Wenceslas IV and the Struggle for Power in Bohemia

The reign of Wenceslas IV, who succeeded his father Charles at the age of seventeen, is characterized by the decline of the house of Luxemburg's rule over Bohemia. Historians have described Wenceslas as an incapable and weak sovereign, though some of the exaggerated accusations against him are attributed to the fact that he and his queen, Sophia, supported the cause of church reform, which drew the ire of Romanist writers.

## Conflict with the Nobility and the Church
During the earlier portion of Wenceslas's reign, a persistent struggle occurred between the king and the powerful Bohemian nobles. This conflict was severe enough that the nobles imprisoned their sovereign on two separate occasions. In addition to his struggles with the nobility, Wenceslas entered into a dispute with the archbishop. This ecclesiastical conflict resulted in the death of John of Nepomuk.

## Religious Conflict and the Rise of Huss
The later years of Wenceslas's reign were marked by incipient religious conflict. The influence of the Church of Rome in Bohemia had already begun to diminish during the reign of King Charles, driven by pious priests such as Waldhauser and Milic, who attacked the immorality of the clergy. This alienation was further intensified by the church schism, during which rival pontiffs engaged in medieval theological strife, utilizing wild threats and objurgations.

Consequently, nearly the entire Bohemian nation supported the cause of Huss. While Wenceslas exhibited his characteristic irresolution and weakness during these disputes, Queen Sophia openly supported Huss, who served as her confessor for a period.

## The Council of Constance and the Hussite Wars
Huss traveled to the council of Constance carrying a letter of safe conduct provided by Sigismund, the king of the Romans and brother of Wenceslas. Despite this, Huss was declared a heretic and was burnt on July 6, 1415. Because Huss was deeply revered by the majority of the population in Bohemia, his execution led immediately to the outbreak of civil war.

On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia convened in Prague and dispatched the *Protestatio Bohemorum* to the council. In this document, they protested the execution of Huss, describing him as a "good, just and catholic man" known for his fame, conduct, and life, and asserting that he had been convicted of no offense. The nobles further declared that anyone claiming heresy existed in Bohemia was a "vile traitor," a "liar," and a "son of the devil." They vowed to defend the humble and steadfast preachers of the law of the Lord Jesus Christ at the cost of their own blood, regardless of human decrees.

This protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church and signaled the start of the Hussite wars. Although the council summoned the nobles to appear before its tribunal, they refused to do so.
