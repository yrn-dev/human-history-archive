# The Battle of Lipan and the Defeat of the Taborites

The Battle of Lipan, fought on May 30, 1434, was a decisive military engagement in Bohemia that marked the collapse of the radical wing of the Hussite movement and paved the way for a political reconciliation between the Bohemian people, the Church of Rome, and Sigismund.

## Background and Internal Conflict
Following years of conflict, the impossibility of conquering Bohemia by force became evident. In 1431, the Bohemians had defeated German forces at Domazlice (Taus) on August 1. Subsequently, negotiations began at Basel, where the Hussites were represented by a large embassy led by Prokop the Great. Although these negotiations proceeded slowly and the Bohemians returned home in 1433 accompanied by council envoys, internal stability remained elusive.

Dissensions broke out within Bohemia that were political rather than religious in nature. The Hussite movement split into two primary factions: a moderate party consisting of more aristocratic Hussites, who formed the "army of the nobles," and the extreme Taborite party, who organized the "army of the towns." This split was largely driven by the drift of the Taborites toward a primitive communism, a tendency that alarmed the more influential and wealthier Czech noblemen.

## The Battle of Lipan
The two opposing forces met on May 30, 1434, at Lipan, located near Kolin. The engagement resulted in a victory for the moderate party and a crushing defeat for the Taborites. The battle was particularly devastating for the leadership of the radical faction; the two Prokops and the majority of the other Taborite leaders perished on the battlefield.

## Aftermath and the Compacts
The victory of the moderate party removed the primary obstacle to a settlement with the external powers. This outcome facilitated a reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund, whom the Bohemians recognized as their sovereign.

As part of this reconciliation, the Bohemians obtained significant concessions regarding religious matters. These agreements, known as the Compacts, granted the Bohemians the following rights:
* The right of communion in both kinds.
* The right to preach the gospel freely.
* Certain limitations on the ability of the clergy to acquire worldly goods.

These distinctions from general Catholic practice remained in effect until the German Reformation in the sixteenth century.

## Related Historical Context
The conflict in Bohemia had been characterized by long-standing volatility. Earlier, in 1420, Sigismund had attempted to conquer Prague with a large international army, but he was repulsed at the Vitkov hill by the bravery of the Taborites under the leadership of Zizka. Sigismund had also suffered a total defeat at the battle of the Vysehrad on November 1, 1420. 

The town of Kolin, near the site of the 1434 battle, had its own history of conflict during this era. In 1421, it was captured by the men of Prague, and German inhabitants who refused to accept "the four articles" were expelled. Later, in 1427, the town declared against Prague and was besieged and eventually surrendered to Prokop the Great.
