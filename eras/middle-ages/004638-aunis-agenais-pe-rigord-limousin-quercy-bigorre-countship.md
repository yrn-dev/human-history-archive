# Aunis, Agenais, Périgord, Limousin, Quercy, and Bigorre

The territories of Aunis, Agenais, Périgord, Limousin, Quercy, and Bigorre are regions of France that have been subject to various administrative changes and territorial transfers throughout history, most notably during the conflicts between the French and English crowns.

## The Treaty of Brétigny
A significant turning point for these regions occurred on May 8, 1360, with the conclusion of the Treaty of Brétigny. This agreement was reached between John II (known as the Good) of France and Edward III of England in the French town of Brétigny. The negotiations, which began in early April, were complicated by the English desire to maintain as many advantages as possible from the previous Treaty of London.

Under the terms of the Treaty of Brétigny, Edward III obtained several territories, including:
*   Aunis, Agenais, Périgord, Limousin, Quercy, and Bigorre.
*   Guienne, Gascony, Poitou, and Saintonge.
*   The countships of Gaure and Guines.
*   Angoumois, Rouergue, Ponthieu, Calais, Sangatte, Ham, and Montreuil-sur-mer.

In exchange, the king of England relinquished the countships of Anjou and Maine, the duchies of Touraine and Normandy, and the suzerainty of Flanders and Brittany. To guarantee the payment of a three-million gold crown ransom for John II, the French king provided hostages, including two of his sons, several nobles and princes, four residents of Paris, and two citizens from each of France's nineteen principal towns. The treaty was ratified and sworn to by the kings and their eldest sons on October 24, 1360, at Calais, accompanied by renunciatory clauses and special conditions.

## Regional Characteristics and Administration

### Agenais and Périgord
The Agenais and Périgord regions are closely linked to the modern department of Dordogne, which was formed in 1790. This department was created from nearly all of Périgord, a portion of Agenais, and small sections of Angoumois and Limousin. Dordogne is characterized by sterile plateaus in its center and northeast, with highlands in the north exceeding 1,300 feet. The region is known for its fertile river valleys, specifically those of the Dordogne and the Isle. Agriculture in the south and southwest is prominent, with the production of tobacco, wheat, maize, and potatoes. The area is particularly famous for the quality and abundance of the truffles of Périgord.

The Agenais region also relates to the department of Lot-et-Garonne, where the capital, Agen, serves as a bishopric seat. Roman remains are found in this department at Aiguillon and Mas d'Agenais.

### Limousin
Limousin is a region that historically included the "lower Limousin," of which the town of Brive (known to the Romans as *Briva Curretiae*) served as the capital during the middle ages. The region also provided the origin for the house of Loménie, which produced various statesmen, including the minister of Louis XV, Cardinal Étienne Charles de Loménie de Brienne. Additionally, a former province known as La Marche (or *Marche Limousine*) was originally a small district carved partly from Poitou and partly from Limousin.

### Quercy
In the middle ages, the town of Cahors served as the capital of Quercy. Before the Albigensian Crusade, the territory of Cahors was a fief of the counts of Toulouse. During the Hundred Years' War, Quercy and Cahors consistently resisted English occupation until they were relieved in 1428. Cahors was a significant financial center in the 13th century due to a colony of Lombard bankers, leading to the term *cahorsin* becoming a synonym for "usurer" or "banker."
