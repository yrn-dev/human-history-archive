# Þegnas and Gesiðas

In the early Anglo-Saxon state, the king's council or court—referred to by terms such as *witan*, *þeod*, *concilium*, and *plebs*—served as the primary central authority outside of the king himself. This governing body was composed of two distinct classes of individuals: senior officials who were summoned by the king periodically, and young warriors who remained in constant attendance upon the monarch.

## Classification and Terminology
The historian Bede used the Latin terms *comites* and *milites* (or *ministri*) to describe these two groups. In the Anglo-Saxon version of these accounts, these classes are identified as *gesiðas* and *þegnas*, respectively. 

Membership in these classes was not restricted to born subjects of the king, nor was it limited to members of the royal family, although both groups did include royal family members. The appeal of certain leaders was such that popular kings, such as Oswine, were able to attract young nobles from various quarters into their service.

## Functions of the Council
The role of the council was primarily deliberative. It was the custom for the king to seek the moral support and advice of the *þegnas* and *gesiðas* when facing important questions. However, there is no evidence to suggest that the king was required to follow the opinion of the majority.

Some historians have argued that the council possessed the authority to depose or elect kings. However, the available information does not sufficiently support the existence of such a strong feeling for constitutionalism. The events cited as evidence for this theory can be interpreted with equal probability as instances of the transference of personal allegiance or professions of loyalty.

## Regional Variations in Social Organization
The social structure involving these noble classes varied by kingdom. In Mercia and Wessex, two distinct noble classes existed. In contrast, the social organization of the Kentish nation was entirely different. In Kent, there was initially only one noble class, known as the *eorlcund*, which was later called the *gesithcund*, similar to the terminology used in Wessex.

Other distinctions in Kent included the existence of *laetas* (likely freedmen) below the ordinary freeman, a group not found in other kingdoms. Additionally, the *wergeld* (compensation) for a *ceorl*, or ordinary freeman, in Kent was two or three times higher than that of the same class in Mercia and Wessex. These differences in fines and compensations, as well as land tenure, may be linked to the fact that the settlers in Kent were Jutes, a different nationality from those who founded the other kingdoms.

## Related Social Ranks
Beyond the primary noble classes, Anglo-Saxon magnates were surrounded by dependants and retainers of various conditions. Among these were the *cnihtas*, who were not always the lowest in rank. In some instances, the term *cniht* was used to describe the thegn of a noble person, defining a social status rather than a specific service. 

By the reign of Edward the Confessor, Domesday evidence indicates a large class of landholders who had commended themselves to lords. This condition was similar to the military and feudal tenants who appeared after the Norman Conquest. While the English used the term *knight* (derived from *cniht*), the Normans used the term *chevaliers* to define the nature of the military service. Early records, such as the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle for the year 1085, indicate that William the Conqueror used the term *ridere* (rider) when dubbing his son Henry.
