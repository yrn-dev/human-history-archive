# Conspiracy to Dethrone Richard II

## The 1397 Conspiracy at Arundel
In 1397, Arundel Castle served as the location for a conspiracy aimed at the removal of King Richard II. The plot was organized by a group that included the duke of Gloucester, the archbishop of Canterbury, and the earl of Arundel. The objectives of this conspiracy were to dethrone Richard II and to murder the lords who served on his council. However, the plot was discovered by authorities before it could be executed.

## Key Figures and Political Alliances
The individuals involved in the opposition to Richard II were often linked through family and political associations. Thomas, duke of Gloucester (also known as Thomas of Woodstock), was the uncle of Richard II and had been created earl of Buckingham at the king's coronation on July 15, 1377. 

Thomas Arundel, the archbishop of Canterbury, was the third son of Eleanor (daughter of Henry Plantagenet, earl of Lancaster) and Richard Fitzalan, earl of Arundel and Warenne. Archbishop Arundel's family was influential, and he was closely associated with a political party led by the duke of Gloucester, Henry (the earl of Derby and future King Henry IV), and his own brother, Richard, earl of Arundel. 

The relationship between these figures and the king was volatile. In 1386, the duke of Gloucester and Archbishop Arundel were sent to Eltham to persuade Richard II to return to parliament. This mission was successful, leading to Arundel's appointment as lord chancellor in place of Michael de la Pole, duke of Suffolk. Arundel further assisted in mediating peace between the king and the supporters of the commission of regency.

## Rise and Fall of Thomas Arundel
Thomas Arundel's ecclesiastical and political career saw rapid advancement. He became archdeacon of Taunton in 1373 and was consecrated bishop of Ely in April 1374. He served as lord chancellor during two separate tenures; during the second, he moved the courts of justice from London to York, though they were eventually returned to the metropolis. In April 1388, he was made archbishop of York, and in September 1396, he was translated to the archbishopric of Canterbury.

Despite his high standing—having officiated at the marriage of Richard II and Isabella of France in November 1396—Arundel's position collapsed following the king's sudden attack upon the lords appellant in 1397. This attack resulted in the arrest of Arundel, Warwick, and Gloucester. Although Richard II initially offered hopes of safety, the archbishop was impeached by the Commons with the king's consent. He was charged with assisting in the procurement of the commission of regency in a manner that derogated royal authority. Consequently, a sentence of banishment was passed, granting him forty days to leave the realm.

## Related Political Conflicts
The instability of the era is further evidenced by the shifting loyalties of the nobility. Thomas le Despenser (1373-1400) initially supported Richard II against the lords appellant and the duke of Gloucester in 1397, at which time Despenser was created earl of Gloucester. However, Despenser deserted the king in 1399. He was later degraded from his earldom for his participation in the death of Gloucester. Despenser eventually joined a conspiracy against Henry IV, which led to his seizure and execution by a mob at Bristol in January 1400.
