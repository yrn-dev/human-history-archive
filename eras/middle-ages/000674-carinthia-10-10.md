# Carinthia

Carinthia is an Austrian duchy and crown-land. Its capital is Klagenfurt, located 212 miles southwest of Vienna by rail.

## Geography and Capital
Klagenfurt (known in Slovene as *Celovec*) is situated on the river Glan, which connects to the Wörther-see via the 3-mile Lend canal. As of 1900, the city had a population of 24,314. Notable architectural landmarks in the capital include the *Landhaus* (house of assembly) from the end of the 14th century, which houses collections of antiquities, minerals, paintings, sculptures, seals, and a natural history museum. Other significant buildings include the *burg* (castle), in its current form since 1777, the palace of the prince-bishop of Gurk, and several churches: the cathedral of SS Peter and Paul (1582-1593), the parish church of St Ægidius (1709), the church of the order of St Elizabeth (1710), the church of the Benedictines (1613), and the church of the Capuchins (1646). The city's principal square features a prominent public monument from 1590 known as the *Lindwurm* or Dragon. Economically, Klagenfurt is active in commerce, particularly regarding regional mineral products, and hosts machine and iron foundries as well as white lead factories.

The region's borders include the duchy of Carniola to the south. The Karawankas mountain range forms the boundary between Carinthia and Carniola, featuring the Stou or Stuhlberg as its highest peak (7,344 ft.) and the Loibl Pass (4,492 ft.). Additionally, the peak of the Grintouz or Grintovc (8,393 ft.) sits on the threefold boundary of Carinthia, Carniola, and Styria.

## Medieval Political History
In the late 13th century, Rudolph, count of Habsburg and German king, sought to recover imperial lands. Following a conflict with Ottakar, who submitted in November 1276 and renounced the duchies of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia, Rudolph administered these territories. In 1286, Rudolph bestowed Carinthia upon Meinhard IV., count of Tirol, in exchange for valuable services.

By 1335, upon the death of Henry, duke of Carinthia (and count of Tirol), Carinthia passed to Albert II., duke of Austria. Later, in 1379, a division of lands between the brothers Leopold III. and Albert III. resulted in Leopold receiving Carinthia, Styria, and Tirol. Following the death of Leopold III. in 1386 at the battle of Sempach, his sons—William, Leopold, Ernest, and Frederick—ruled their lands in common. In 1406, Ernest and Frederick divided these lands, with Ernest becoming the duke of Carinthia and Styria. He was succeeded in 1424 by his sons, Frederick and Albert.

## Later Administration and Demographics
In the 19th century, Carinthia was involved in various political reorganizations. In 1809, parts of Carinthia were included in the Illyrian Provinces, which were ceded by Austria to Italy at the peace of Schönbrunn on October 14, 1809. These provinces were governed in the interest of Napoleon and occupied by French troops until the occupation ceased to be effective around the end of 1813, at which point the territories reverted to Austria. In 1816, the kingdom of Illyria was constituted, incorporating the crown-lands of Carinthia, Carniola, Istria, Görz and Gradisca, and Trieste; this remained a kingdom of the Austrian crown until 1849.

Regarding demographics and language, the town of Klagenfurt has been a point of contention. In 1880, the government declared Slovenian a customary language in the town, requiring public offices and law courts to conduct business in Slovenian. This occurred despite the town's population consisting of 16,491 German-speaking Austrians and only 568 Slovenian-speakers (including 180 residents of the hospital or gaol), although the surrounding rural villages were chiefly Slovene.

## Cultural Figures
Margaret Maultasch (1318-1369), the daughter and heiress of Henry, duke of Carinthia, is remembered in the region's folk memory. Known as the "Wicked Gretl," she was regarded by the people of Carinthia as an amazon.
