# John de Baliol

John de Baliol (1249–1315) was the King of Scotland who ascended to the throne following a period of succession crisis at the end of the 13th century. He was the son of Dervorguila, daughter of Alan, earl of Galloway, and John de Baliol of Barnard Castle, Durham (who died in 1269). Upon the death of his elder brother in 1278, John became the head of the Baliol family and the lord of extensive lands located in Scotland, France, and England.

## Accession to the Scottish Throne
Baliol rose to prominence in 1290 when the Scottish throne became vacant. This vacancy was caused by the death of Margaret, known as the "maid of Norway," who was the granddaughter of King Alexander III. Margaret had been the titular queen of Scotland, and her right to the throne had been acknowledged; however, she died in the Orkneys around the end of September 1290 while traveling from Norway.

Following this death, Baliol emerged as one of three candidates whose claims to the crown were seriously considered. His claim was based on his maternal grandmother, Margaret, who was the eldest daughter of David, earl of Huntingdon (d. 1219) and a grandson of King David I. Baliol's primary rival for the crown was Robert Bruce, earl of Annandale. The dispute between the two was characterized as a conflict between the nearest of kin (Bruce) and the eldest by descent (Baliol).

## The Intervention of Edward I
King Edward I of England closely monitored the situation in Scotland and was invited to settle the dispute. In May 1291, Edward met with Scottish nobles at Norham. During this meeting, Edward demanded a formal recognition of his position as the overlord of Scotland. Although there was some delay, the Scottish nobles tacitly admitted this position, and the competitors for the crown, including Baliol, acknowledged it and agreed to abide by Edward's decision.

To resolve the claim, a court was appointed consisting of twenty-four Englishmen and eighty Scotsmen. Baliol argued his case based on the principles of feudal law regarding an indivisible inheritance. After traversing the statements made in favor of Robert Bruce, the court advised Edward, who decided the matter in Baliol's favor.

## Reign and Relations with England
Before his coronation, Baliol swore fealty to the English king. He was crowned King of Scotland at Scone on November 30, 1292. Following his coronation, he performed homage to Edward I at Newcastle. In January 1293, Baliol released the English king from all obligations and promises that had been made while the kingdom of Scotland was in Edward's hands.

The initial amicable relations between the two monarchs were eventually disturbed. This tension arose when a Scottish vassal brought a case to Edward I in his capacity as Baliol's overlord. Shortly thereafter, Baliol himself was summoned to the English court.

## The Baliol Family and Kinship
The Baliol family included a cadet branch descended from the younger Bernard de Baliol's son, Ingelram. This branch produced several figures of importance in Scottish administration. Henry, son of Ingelram, became chamberlain about 1223 and inherited Valoines family fiefs in 1234. Henry's son, Alexander de Baliol, served as the lord of Cavers in Teviotdale and as chamberlain of Scotland, first mentioned in that role in 1287.

Alexander de Baliol played a significant role in the diplomatic efforts of the late 13th century, participating in negotiations between Scottish nobles and Edward I that led to the treaty of Brigham in 1290 and the treaty of Salisbury in 1289. It is probable that Alexander was deprived of his office as chamberlain around 1296 and may have been imprisoned alongside his kinsman, King John de Baliol. Alexander later fought for Edward in Scotland and was summoned to English parliaments before his death around 1309.
