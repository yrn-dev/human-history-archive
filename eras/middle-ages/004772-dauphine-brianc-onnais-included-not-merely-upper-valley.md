# Dauphiné

Dauphiné is one of the old provinces of pre-Revolutionary France, located in the south-east portion of the country between Savoy and Provence. Following 1790, the region was reorganized into the departments of the Hautes Alpes, the Drome, and the Isere.

## Political History and the Dauphins
The territories that later became known as Dauphine were part of the realm of the last king of Burgundy, Rudolf III., until his death in 1032, at which point they reverted to the emperor. In the ensuing confusion, the counts of Albon, situated between Vienne and Valence, rose to prominence. The first dynasty of these counts ended in 1162 with Guigue V. His daughter and heiress, Beatrice, passed the house's possessions to her husband, Hugh III., duke of Burgundy. Their son, Andre, established a second dynasty that expanded its holdings, acquiring the Gapencais and the Embrunais in 1232 through marriage.

This second dynasty concluded in 1282 when the heiress Anna brought the territories to her husband, Humbert, lord of La Tour du Pin. The head of this house held the title of Count (and later Dauphin) of the Viennois. The last independent Dauphin, Humbert II. (1333–1349), sold his dominions in 1349 to Charles of Valois. Upon becoming King Charles V of France, he bestowed Dauphine upon his eldest son. Consequently, the title of Dauphin was adopted by all subsequent eldest sons of the French kings.

Further territorial additions occurred in 1424 when the Valentinois and the Diois were annexed to the Dauphine. Louis XI (who served as Dauphin from 1440 to 1461) was the final Dauphin to hold a semi-independent position before the province was annexed to the crown in 1456. While the emperor had named the Dauphin "Imperial Vicar" within Provence and Dauphine in 1378, this suzerainty eventually faded.

## The Briançonnais
The Briançonnais region was historically linked to the fate of the Dauphiné after coming into the possession of the counts of Albon (the later dauphins of the Viennois) around 1040. In Roman times, Briançon was known as *Brigantium* and was part of the kingdom of King Cottius.

The Briançonnais encompassed several valleys on the eastern slope of the Alps:
* The upper valley of the Durance, including the Guil and Gyronde affluents.
* The valley of the Dora Riparia, including Exilles, Bardonnèche, Oulx, and Césanne.
* The valley of the Chisone, including Pragelas, Pérouse, and Fénestrelles.

Under the treaty of Utrecht in 1713, these valleys were transferred to Savoy in exchange for the valley of Barcelonnette, located on the west slope of the Alps. In 1815, Briançon was the site of a three-month siege by the Allies, which the city successfully withstood. This event is marked by an inscription on one of the city's gates: *Le passé répond de l'avenir*.

## Grenoble and Regional Culture
Grenoble serves as a major center within the region and is the seat of a bishopric founded in the 4th century. The city contains several significant historical structures:
* **The Palais de Justice:** Built between the late 15th and 16th centuries on the site of the old Parlement of the Dauphine.
* **The Town Hall:** A modern building constructed where the palace of the dauphins once stood.
* **St Andre:** A 13th-century church that previously served as the chapel for the dauphins of the Viennois. It houses a 17th-century monument to Bayard (1476–1524), though the presence of his bones is uncertain.
* **Notre Dame and St Laurent:** The cathedral of Notre Dame dates partly from the 11th century, while the church of St Laurent, located on the right bank of the Isere, is the city's oldest church (11th century) and features a crypt from Merovingian times.

The city's university and hospital (founded in the 15th century) are housed in modern buildings. Other institutions include the Academie Delphinale, founded in 1772. The town library holds 250,000 printed volumes and 7,000 manuscripts, many of which belonged to the monastery of the Grande Chartreuse until 1793. Additionally, the natural history museum contains geological specimens from Savoy and the neighboring districts of the Dauphine.

## Late History and the French Revolution
Prominent figures in the history of Dauphine during the 16th century include the duke of Lesdiguieres (1543–1626) and the reformer Guillaume Farel (1489–1565). The "States" of Dauphine, which existed from approximately the middle of the 14th century, were suspended by Louis XIII in 1628. However, an unauthorized meeting of these States took place on July 21, 1788, at the castle of Vizille's tennis court (*Salle du Jeu de Paume*) near Grenoble. This gathering is regarded as one of the earliest premonitory signs of the French Revolution of 1789.
