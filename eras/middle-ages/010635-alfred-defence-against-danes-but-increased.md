# Alfred the Great and the Danish Invasions

## The Viking Invasions of England
The Danish invasions of England were part of a broader "mysterious upheaval" of Scandinavian activity, often attributed to over-population and a love of adventure. While early Northmen raids began as far back as the ravaging of Lindisfarne in 793, organized invasions developed as the ninth century progressed. The Danes, described by Alfred as occupying Scania, the islands, and Jutland, began a real settlement of England in 866. This began with a large army appearing in East Anglia, which then moved north to capture York and overthrow the Northumbrian kingdom, installing a puppet king.

By 868, the Danes had encamped in Nottingham, and Northern Mercia soon fell into their hands. In 870, the king of the East Anglians, Edmund, fell before them. During this period, a Danish leader named Alfdene minted coins in London. The invaders continued their advance, occupying Cambridge and reaching as far north as the Tyne during the winter of 874-875.

## Alfred's Struggle and Resistance
King Alfred the Great engaged in a great struggle to withstand these incursions. In 868, Burgred, the king of Mercia, had appealed to Alfred and Æthelred for assistance against the Danes in Nottingham, though the armies of Wessex and Mercia did not engage in serious fighting at that time, and the Danes remained through the winter. Later, in 876, the invaders captured Wareham, but Alfred drove them out the following year, resulting in the wreck of 120 Danish ships at Swanage.

Despite these efforts, there were periods where Alfred was unable to withstand the Danish incursions. Between 878 and 879, Alfred sought retreat at Athelney, a small eminence in Somersetshire that was isolated by marshes and accessible only by artificial causeway or boat. After regaining his throne, Alfred founded a monastery at Athelney in gratitude for the refuge.

## The Peace of Wedmore and the Danelagh
The conflict was terminated by the peace of Wedmore in 878. Under the terms of this treaty, the Danes agreed to be baptized if they were assured of their conquests. This agreement led the Danes to withdraw from Wessex and settle in East Anglia under their king, Guthrum. The peace was further ratified in a document known as the peace of Alfred and Guthrum, likely dating to 880.

This agreement established a formal boundary for Guthrum's East Anglian kingdom. The boundary ran from the Thames estuary to the mouth of the Lea, up the Lea to its source near Leighton Buzzard, north to Bedford, and eastwards up the Ouse to Watling Street near Stony Stratford or Fenny. The districts handed over to the Danes—including East Anglia, Northern Mercia, Northumbria, and the greater part of Essex—became known as the Danelagh (or Dane-law).

## Defensive Works and Legacy
To defend against the Danes, Alfred is believed to have thrown up an earthwork or fortification at Arundel (Harundell). This site, which later consisted of a mill and the fortification, increased in importance before the Conquest and was recorded in the Domesday Book as a port and thriving borough. In his will, Alfred left Arundel to his nephew, Aethelm, as early as 877.

Beyond military defense, Alfred contributed to the legal continuity of England. Around 900, he and his successors issued a series of edicts. These laws strongly resembled Frankish capitularies, suggesting a case of imitation, and established a tradition of kings publishing laws that persisted through the reigns of later rulers, such as Canute the Dane and the Norman duke.
