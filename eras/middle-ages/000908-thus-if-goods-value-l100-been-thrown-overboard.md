# Acts of Throwing Overboard

The act of throwing items or persons overboard occurs across various historical contexts, ranging from maritime legal principles and naval warfare to political protest and judicial execution.

## Maritime Law and General Average
Under the law of General Average (G.A.), the objective is to ensure that a party whose property is sacrificed for the general safety is placed on an equal footing with others, rather than a better one. For example, if goods valued at L100 are thrown overboard to ensure the safety of the venture, the owner does not receive the full L100 in contribution. Because the goods were part of the adventure that was saved, the owner must bear a portion of the loss and contribute alongside other saved interests.

In estimating the amount to be "made good" to the owner, the value of the sacrificed property is not calculated based on its condition at the time of sacrifice. Instead, it is estimated as it would have been upon arrival, had the goods remained on board throughout the voyage. This prevents the owner from being put in a better position than those whose goods were not sacrificed by effectively withdrawing the sacrificed goods from the subsequent risks of the voyage.

## Naval Tactics and Warfare
Throwing items overboard has been used as a strategic maneuver to lighten ships or deceive enemies. During a confrontation between King Harald and the Danish forces under King Svein, Harald’s fleet was heavily laden and soaked with water, making them slower than the Danish ships. To escape, Harald first ordered his men to throw wood overboard topped with clothes and other valuable articles. The Danes, seeing their own goods floating in the calm sea, turned back to save them, causing them to lose ground.

As the Danes resumed their pursuit, Harald ordered further lightening of the ships by casting overboard liquor, wheat, malt, and bacon. He subsequently ordered the prisoners, empty puncheons, casks, and bulwark-screens to be thrown overboard. Although King Svein ordered his men to save the prisoners, the resulting delay allowed the fleets to separate.

In other naval conflicts, the loss of items overboard has provided intelligence. Following the stranding of the *Magdeburg* off Odensholm, secret papers thrown overboard were recovered by the Russians and shared with their Allies, confirming that the British Admiralty possessed the secret cyphering system of the German Fleet.

## Political and Judicial Acts
The act of throwing items or people overboard has also been recorded as a form of political protest or a method of execution. In 1773, a band of men disguised as Indians threw three cargoes of tea into Boston harbour. This action was a response to legislation that favored the London East India Company over American shippers and served as a precursor to the fighting that began in 1775.

In a judicial or punitive context, Erling Skakke used the practice as a means of execution. After capturing Frirek Keina and Bjarne the Bad, Erling had Frirek bound to an anchor and thrown overboard. This specific act led to Erling being detested in the Throndhjem country, as many powerful men in that region were relatives of Frirek.

## Combat and Survival
During fierce naval battles, leaping or being thrown overboard often occurred during the collapse of a ship's defense. In the battle involving the ship *Serpent*, as the crew fell to a superior force, King Olaf and Kolbjorn the marshal both sprang overboard. While the earl's men used boats to kill those who leaped, King Olaf managed to sink beneath the waters by throwing his shield over his head. Kolbjorn, however, fell upon his shield, which prevented him from sinking quickly; he was captured and brought before the earl, who ultimately spared his life upon realizing he was not the king. Shortly thereafter, the remaining surviving men of King Olaf, including his brother Thorkel Nefia, sprang overboard.
