# Buckinghamshire

## Post-Conquest Transitions
At the time of the Norman invasion, Buckinghamshire was likely part of the earldom of Leofwine, son of Godwin. Because the county provided support to Leofwine at the battle of Hastings, it was subjected to sweeping confiscations following the Conquest. 

The redistribution of estates after the Conquest resulted in only two Englishmen retaining estates of any significant importance. The primary landowners during this period were Odo, bishop of Bayeux, and Walter Giffard, the first earl of Buckingham. However, few of the great estates in the county remained with the same proprietors for long; some were annexed by religious establishments, while others reverted to the crown to be redistributed via various grants. The family of Hampden is the only group claiming to have held their namesake estate in an unbroken line since Saxon times.

## Ecclesiastical History
The early churches of Buckinghamshire were likely subject to the West Saxon see of Dorchester. Following the Conquest, the bishop's stool was moved to Lincoln, though no change in jurisdiction occurred. Buckinghamshire remained part of the Lincoln diocese until 1837, when it was transferred to Oxford. A proposal to create a new diocese encompassing both Buckinghamshire and Bedfordshire after the dissolution of the monasteries was abandoned.

The arch-deaconry was likely established toward the end of the 11th century by Bishop Rémy, with rural deaneries following shortly after. Records from the 12th century mention a dean of Thornborough, and the taxation of Nicholas IV listed eight deaneries consisting of 186 parishes. By 1855, these deaneries were reconstructed into eighteen.

## National Involvement and Conflict
Due to its proximity to London, Buckinghamshire was involved in many of the great national events of the subsequent centuries. During the conflict between King John and his barons, William Mauduit defended Hanslape Castle against the king until it was captured and demolished by Falkes de Bréauté in 1216.

During the civil war, Buckinghamshire was among the first counties to join an association for mutual defence supporting the parliament, maintaining important garrisons at Brill and Aylesbury. Royalist troops briefly garrisoned Newport Pagnell, and in 1644, the king established his headquarters at Buckingham.

## Health and Demographics
The county was severely impacted by the Black Death. Winslow was one of several districts that were almost entirely depopulated as a result of the plague. In more recent records, the ancient county's area was 475,682 acres, with a population of 185,284 in 1891 and 195,764 in 1901. The administrative county area is listed as 479,358 acres.

## Administration and Geography
The administrative county consists of 230 civil parishes and is divided into eight hundreds. Three of these—Desborough, Burnham, and Stoke—constitute the "Chiltern Hundreds," while the hundred of Aylesbury is known as the "three hundreds of Aylesbury." 

The county town is Buckingham (population 3,152), and other municipal boroughs include Wycombe (officially Chepping Wycombe, also known as High or Chipping Wycombe), which had a population of 15,542. Other urban districts include:
* Slough (11,453)
* Aylesbury (9,243)
* Chesham (7,245)
* Fenny Stratford (4,799)
* Marlow (4,526)
* Newport Pagnell (4,028)
* Eton (3,301)
* Beaconsfield (1,570)
* Linslade (2,157)

Lesser market towns include Amersham, Olney, Prince's Risborough, Stony Stratford, Wendover, Ivinghoe, and Winslow. At Wolverton, the London & North-Western railway maintains carriage works. Several villages near the Thames, including Bourne End, Burnham, Wooburn, Cookham, Taplow, and Taplow, have become residential centres.

The county is part of the midland circuit, with assizes held at Aylesbury. It is divided into thirteen petty sessional divisions, though the boroughs of Wycombe and Buckingham maintain separate commissions of the peace. Parliamentarily, the county is split into three divisions—Southern (Wycombe), Mid (Aylesbury), and Northern (Buckingham)—each returning one member. A small portion of the parliamentary borough of Windsor also falls within the county.

## Notable Institutions and Figures
Eton College, a famous public school founded by Henry VI, is the county's most notable institution. Other figures associated with the region include Richard Grenville, the 2nd duke of Buckingham and Chandos, who served as the member of parliament for Buckinghamshire from 1818 to 1839 and was known as the "Farmers' Friend." 

In the field of science, geologist Peter Bellinger Brodie served as a curate at Steeple Claydon and described the Portland and Purbeck Beds outliers in the county.
