# Bookselling and Stationers in Medieval Oxford

## The Role of Sworn Stationers
In the medieval period, the supply of books to students at the university of Oxford was primarily managed by "sworn" university booksellers, also known as stationers. These individuals held a significant monopoly over the trade. A notable example of such a figure was John More of Oxford, who served as a sworn stationer. More's business model involved not only supplying pupils with the books they required for their studies but also acting as a pawnbroker.

The necessity for these stationers was driven by the fact that most students could not afford to purchase books. Furthermore, the academic environment of the time was such that instruction was almost entirely oral, meaning students did not find as much advantage in owning books as might be expected. Despite this, large numbers of books were constantly changing hands through the sworn stationers.

## Unsworn Booksellers and the Statute of 1373
Alongside the official sworn stationers, there existed a number of booksellers in Oxford who were not sworn. The presence of these unauthorized sellers led to legal concerns and the passage of a statute in the year 1373. This statute expressly cited the negative impacts of unsworn booksellers, claiming that their activities resulted in books of great value being sold and carried away from Oxford. Additionally, the statute alleged that the owners of such books were being cheated and that the sworn stationers were being deprived of their lawful business.

To combat this, the 1373 enactment stipulated that no bookseller—with the exception of two sworn stationers or their deputies—could sell any book that exceeded half a mark in value, regardless of whether the book was their own property or belonged to another.

## Broader Context of Book Production and Trade
The demand for books in England was driven largely by the spread of Christianity, which created a need for copies of the Gospels, sacred books, and later, devotional volumes such as missals for private and church use. Early efforts to bring texts into the country include those of Benedict Biscop, the founder of the abbey at Wearmouth, who brought a cargo of books from France in 671.

By the period preceding the Reformation, text writers and stationers (_stacyoneres_) had established a presence in London, specifically in the neighborhood of St Paul's. In 1403, these sellers—who provided manuscript copies of prayers, the Creed, the Paternoster, the Ave Maria, and the ABC—formed a gild. Some of these London stationers operated out of stalls built directly against the walls of the cathedral.

## Academic and Religious Regulation in Oxford
The university of Oxford was subject to various ecclesiastical and legal disputes during the later medieval period. In 1397, an attempt was made to vindicate the right of visitation over the university. This remained unsettled until 1411, when Pope John XXIII issued a bull recalling a previous bull by Pope Boniface IX that had exempted the university from the authority of the archbishop.

Additionally, the university was a site of religious conflict regarding the Lollards. In 1408, a council was summoned at Oxford to draw up constitutions against the Lollards. These were published in January 1409 and included a prohibition against translating the Bible into English unless consent was granted by a provincial synod or the bishop of the diocese.
