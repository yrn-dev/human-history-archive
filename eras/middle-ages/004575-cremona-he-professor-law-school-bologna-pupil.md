# Joannes Bassianus

Joannes Bassianus was a prominent Italian jurist of the 12th century. While little is known regarding his origins, Corolus de Tocco states that he was a native of Cremona. Bassianus served as a professor in the law school of Bologna, where he was a pupil of Bulgarus and later became the master of Azo.

## Legal Philosophy and the School of Bulgarus
Bassianus was an adherent of the school led by Bulgarus, one of the "Four Doctors" of the university of Bologna. This school was characterized by a strict adherence to the letter of the law, positioning it in opposition to the school of Martinus Gosia, which sought to accommodate the law through what opponents termed the "equity of the purse" (*aequitas bursalis*). The school of Bulgarus eventually prevailed, and Bassianus, along with Azo and Accursius, exercised a commanding influence over the trajectory of legal studies at Bologna.

## Major Works and Contributions
Bassianus was noted for his talent in creating ingenious forms to explain his ideas with precision. His most celebrated work is the *Arbor Arborum*, or "Law-Tree." In this work, he presented a tree where various types of actions were arranged on the branches like fruit. He divided these actions into two primary groups:
* **Civil actions** (*actiones stricti juris*), which numbered forty-eight and were arranged on one side of the tree.
* **Equitable or praetorian actions**, which numbered one hundred and twenty-one and were arranged on the opposite side.

To further organize these, Bassianus created a scientific division of actions under twelve heads. He employed an ingenious system of notation that allowed students to immediately classify any civil or praetorian action under its corresponding scientific head. He also included a few glosses beside the tree to justify and explain this classification.

Another of his preserved writings is the *Summary on the Authentica*. This work was regarded by Savigny as one of the most precious contributions of the school of the Gloss-writers.

## Lost Works and Legacy
Bassianus also delivered lectures on the *Code* and the *Pandects*. These lectures were collected by his pupil, Nicolaus Furiosus, but they have since perished. 

His influence continued through his pupil Azo (c. 1150–1230), who was born in Bologna and became a professor of civil law in his native town. Azo occupied a significant position among the glossators, and his own *Readings on the Code* became so authoritative in tribunals that it was said, "Chi non ha Azzo, non vada a palazzo." Through this lineage, the teachings of Bassianus and the school of Bulgarus remained central to the development of Roman law.
