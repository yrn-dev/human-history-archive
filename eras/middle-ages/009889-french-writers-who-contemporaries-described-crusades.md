# French Writers of the Crusades

The historical record of the crusades has been preserved through the works of various French writers and the subsequent efforts of scholars who collected and published these contemporary accounts. These sources provide insight into the military campaigns, the motivations of the participants, and the geopolitical interactions of the era.

## Collection and Preservation of Texts
A significant figure in the preservation of these accounts was Jacques Bongars (1554–1612), a French scholar and diplomatist born in Orleans. Bongars collected the works of several French writers who described the crusades as contemporaries and published them in 1611 under the title *Gesta Dei per Francos*. While Bongars adopted this title, some critics have suggested the alternative reading *Gesta Diaboli per Francos*. 

Other collections of crusade-era writings include those compiled by Duchesne. These collections contain original French histories of the second crusade, specifically the *Gesta Ludovici VII*, as well as original letters from King Louis and his minister, Suger.

## Contemporary Chroniclers and Authors
The archive identifies several key writers whose works document the crusades. Among the authors utilized for the first crusade are:
*   **Robertus Monachus**, who recorded the acclamation "Deus vult, Deus vult!" by the Latin-speaking clergy.
*   **Fulcherius Carnotensis**
*   **Guibertus**
*   **Albertus Aquensis**, whose work is noted for being more sensible of the importance of transactions between the crusaders and the caliph of Egypt than other contemporary writers.
*   **Baldricus**
*   **Raimundus de Agiles**

Other notable writers include **Willielmus Tyriensis** (William of Tyre), who also documented transactions with the caliph of Egypt, and **Villehardouin**, whose writings capture the genuine feelings of French knights regarding the influence of pardons on their decision to take the cross. Additional contributors to the historical record include **Petri Tudebodi Sacerdotis Sivracensis**, whose *Historia de Hierosolymitano Itinere* was integrated into the first anonymous writer in Bongars' collection.

## Perspectives on the Crusades
The contemporary writings reveal the social and linguistic divisions among the crusaders. For example, the phrase "Deus vult" was the acclamation of the clergy; however, the illiterate laity, speaking the Limousin or Provincial idiom, corrupted this to "Deus lo volt" or "Diex el volt."

The texts also provide details on the identification of the forces. During the first crusade, the crosses worn on garments—typically in gold, silk, or cloth—were all red. By the third crusade, the French continued to use red, while the English adopted white and the Flemings adopted green.

## Military and Political Accounts
The writings describe the scale and composition of the forces involved. The second crusade featured the presence of sovereigns from France and Germany, with the cavalry of King Louis and the Emperor each consisting of seventy thousand knights and their attendants. 

Contemporary accounts also document specific hardships and conflicts. The anonymous writer in the Collection of Canisius describes the land as "terram horroris et salsuginis" (a land of horror and salt) and "terram siccam sterilem" (a dry, sterile land). Other accounts detail the conduct of regional leaders, such as the ambiguous behavior of Kilidge Arslan, the sultan of Cogni, who feared and hated both Frederic and Saladin.
