# Brigandage

Brigandage refers to the activities of outlaws who conduct warfare as irregular or partisan soldiers. The term "brigand" is believed to derive from the Old French *brigan*, a form of the Italian *brigante*, meaning a partisan or irregular soldier. Similarly, the word "bandit" shares this meaning. In Italy, a *bandito* was specifically a man declared an outlaw by a proclamation, known as a *bando*. In Scotland, a similar proclamation was called a "decree of horning," so named because it was delivered via a blast of a horn at the town cross.

## Nature and Characteristics of the Brigand Life
While often romanticized in fiction, the reality of brigandage is characterized by violence and desperation. Many bands were recruited from men who had committed homicide due to gambling quarrels or jealousy; these individuals often remained in the bands not out of a love for the lifestyle, but from fear of the gallows. Internal dynamics were frequently brutal; for example, a reformed brigand named Passo di Lupo (Wolf's Step) reported around 1820 that bullies within the bands terrorized and robbed weaker members, and that murderous conflicts were constant.

Brigands typically sustain their operations through plunder, the extortion of blackmail, and the capturing of prisoners for ransom. Those prisoners who are unable to pay are often killed. Similar patterns were observed among the "dacoits" of India, though the Pindaris were considered more than brigands and the Thugs were categorized as a religious sect.

## Favorable Conditions for Brigandage
The development of brigandage is primarily driven by bad administration, and to a lesser extent, the availability of convenient hiding places in forests and mountains. Examples of such terrains include:
*   **The Apennines and mountains of Calabria:** Homes to Italian *banditos*.
*   **The Sierras of Spain:** Homes to *bandoleros* (banished men) and *salteadores* (raiders).
*   **The Highlands of Scotland:** Refuge for "gentlemen reavers" who stole cattle from Sassenach landlords.
*   **The forests of England:** Cover for outlaws, including those depicted in the ballads of Robin Hood.
*   **The "maquis" (bush) of Corsica and Australia:** Provided cover for Corsican brigands and Australian bushrangers.

Despite these terrains, a government that employs an intelligent police force supported by law-abiding citizens can suppress brigandage.

## Regional Histories

### Italy and the Mediterranean
Italy is considered the home of the most accomplished European brigands. Until the mid-19th century, the division of Italy into small states allowed brigands to flee across borders to avoid pursuit. Marco Sciarra of the Abruzzi, for instance, evaded the Spanish viceroy of Naples by crossing into the papal states or taking service with Venice before he was eventually trapped and killed. Another figure, Benedetto Mangone, was known for allowing Torquato Tasso to pass unharmed due to his reverence for poetry, though Mangone was eventually beaten to death with hammers in Naples.

In Naples and Sicily, brigandage became chronic due to the survival of feudal power, competing jurisdictions, and the helplessness of the peasantry. The Bourbon dynasty reduced brigandage, but it revived during the French invasion. This era saw the rise of Fra Diavolo, who transitioned from a brigand to a patriot before being executed by the French. Later, General Sir Richard Church was employed by Ferdinand to suppress brigands; Church restored security by maintaining strict discipline among his soldiers and gaining the trust of the peasantry. He brought to justice Don Ciro Anicchiarico, a priest and brigand who claimed to have murdered approximately seventy people.

Brigandage in these regions was often sustained by *manuténgoli* (maintainers), which included corrupt officials, political parties, great men, and terrorized peasants who sold the brigands food and clothes.

### Greece and the Balkans
In the Balkan peninsula under Turkish rule, brigands—called *klephts* by Greeks and *hayduks* or *haydutzi* by Slavs—viewed themselves as representatives of their people against oppressors. The Turkish administration permitted some of the population to carry arms to repress the *klephts*; these individuals, called *armatoli*, often became allies of the *klephts* instead.

Following independence, Greece suffered from poor administration, with politicians using brigands for their own purposes. In 1870, a party of English travelers, including Lord and Lady Muncaster, were captured at Oropos. While the Muncasters were released to seek a ransom of £25,000, the other prisoners were murdered. The perpetrators were subsequently hunted down and executed.

### Spain
Spanish brigandage was endemic south of the Sierra Morena and flourished in the north during periods of weak government or foreign invasion. In Catalonia, it began as a peasant struggle against feudal landlords, featuring the traditional hero Roque Guinart. Following the War of the Succession (1700-1714), Pedro Veciana formed a band called the Mozos to resist brigands. This led to the creation of the *escuadra de Cataluna*, a police corps commissioned by Philip V. Since 1844, the organization of "La Guardia Civil" by the duke of Ahumada has kept brigandage well under control.

## Political and Social Context
Brigandage is sometimes a last resource for people subject to invasion. For example, the Spanish irregular levies (1808-1814) and Calabrians fighting for Ferdinand of Naples were labeled brigands by their enemies. Generally, an invader who creates anarchy often labels their opponents as brigands.
