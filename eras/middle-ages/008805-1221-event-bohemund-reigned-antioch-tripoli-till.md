# The Bohemunds of Antioch and Tripoli

The Bohemunds were a series of princes who ruled the principality of Antioch and, from the time of Bohemund IV., also the county of Tripoli. Their history is inextricably linked to the governance and territorial struggles of these two regions.

## Bohemund I and Bohemund II
Bohemund I (c. A.D. 1058–1111), born Marc, was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*. Early in his career, he served under his father during attacks on the East Roman empire between 1080 and 1085, during which he penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly before being repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. Following the death of Guiscard in 1085, a war broke out between Bohemund and his younger brother Roger over the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This conflict was resolved through the mediation of Urban II, who awarded Bohemund Otranto and other possessions.

Bohemund I is regarded as the leader of the First Crusade. After arriving in Constantinople in April 1097 and doing homage to Emperor Alexius, he led the crusade across Asia Minor to Antioch. 

His son, Bohemund II (1108–1131), born in the year of his father's defeat at Durazzo, arrived in Antioch from Apulia in 1126. He married Alice, the younger daughter of Baldwin II, in 1127. After participating in an attack on Damascus, Bohemund II was defeated and killed in 1131 by a Mahommedan army from Aleppo on his northern frontier.

## Bohemund III and the Succession Dispute
Bohemund III succeeded his mother, Constance, in the principality of Antioch in 1163. In 1164, while serving as regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem, he was defeated and captured at Harenc by Nureddin. He was ransomed by Emperor Manuel and returned to Antioch with a Greek patriarch. 

The later years of Bohemund III's life were dominated by a succession dispute between his younger son, Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV), and his grandson, Raymund Rhupen. Raymund Rhupen was the son of Bohemund III's eldest son, Raymund, who had married the daughter of the Armenian prince Rhupen. Leo of Armenia championed Raymund Rhupen's cause, even capturing Bohemund III at Gastin in 1194 in an attempt to force the cession of Antioch. Bohemund the younger vigorously pursued his own claim and evicted his father from Antioch around 1199, though he was subsequently ousted by Leo. Bohemund III died in 1201 while still in possession of his principality.

## Bohemund IV and Bohemund V
Bohemund IV became the count of Tripoli in 1187 and succeeded his father as prince of Antioch in 1201. His reign was marked by a prolonged dispute with Raymund Rhupen, supported by Leo of Armenia and the Hospitallers. Bohemund IV was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch—to whom he granted their own patriarch in 1207—and the Templars. Although Leo captured Antioch in 1216 and installed Raymund Rhupen, Bohemund IV regained possession before Leo's death in 1220. Following the death of Raymund Rhupen in 1221, Bohemund IV reigned in both Antioch and Tripoli until his death. He was a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, leading to his excommunication in 1230. He joined and later deserted Emperor Frederick II during the crusade of 1228–29. He died in 1233, shortly after concluding peace with the Hospitallers and being released from excommunication by Gregory IX.

He was succeeded in 1233 by his son, Bohemund V, who was the son of Plaisance, daughter of Hugh of Gibelet. Bohemund V maintained the alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia until 1251, when peace was achieved through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund V also connected Antioch with Cyprus through his 1225 marriage to Alice, the widow of Hugh I of Cyprus. He resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving Antioch to be governed by a commune and its bailiff.

## Bohemund VI and Bohemund VII
Bohemund VI, born in 1237, succeeded his father in 1251. He was knighted by St Louis in 1252 and established himself in Antioch, leaving Tripoli to its own administration. In 1257, he secured the recognition of his nephew, Hugh II, as king of Jerusalem. Bohemund VI allied with the Mongols to oppose the Egyptian sultan; however, in 1268, he lost Antioch to Bibars. By the time of his death in 1275, he held only the title of count of Tripoli.

The final ruler of the line was Bohemund VII, son of Bohemund VI and Sibylla. He succeeded to the county of Tripoli in 1275 with his mother serving as regent. His reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Bohemund VII died without issue, and the county of Tripoli became extinct within two years of his death when the city was captured.
