# BASS

The term "bass" (pronounced the same as "base," though influenced in spelling by the Italian *basso*) primarily denotes something deep or low. In the context of music, it refers to the lowest male voice, the lowest-pitched instrument in a specific class, or the lower part of a composition's harmony. Outside of music, the term is applied to a fish that closely resembles the perch. Additionally, "bass" or "bast" (pronounced *b[a]s*) refers to the fibrous bark of the lime tree, which is utilized for making soft plaited baskets, mats, and for tying up plants in gardening. Related botanical terms include basswood (*Tilia Americana*) and white basswood (*T. heterophylla*), both of which are American lime-trees.

## Musical Instruments

### The Double Bass
The double bass evolved from the bass viol, sharing a remote origin with the violin. Historical constructions include those by Maggini and Gasparo da Salo; the latter created a three-stringed instrument for St Mark's in Venice, which was later the favorite concert instrument of Dragonetti. Dragonetti utilized a straight bow, similar to that of the violoncello, held overhand. Other historical variations include the *gross contra-bass geige* (or sub-bass *viol da gamba*) described by Praetorius.

In England and Italy, the double bass sometimes featured three strings tuned in fourths, while in Germany and France, they were tuned to fifths. To obtain additional lower notes required by modern composers, four-stringed instruments were adopted, though this resulted in a loss of the sonorous beauty found in three-stringed versions. Some orchestras use a mix of both. A.C. White improved the four-stringed bass by adding a lower D string and widening the bridge to approximately 5 inches and widening the neck to allow for vibration and equal sonority. While five-stringed basses were used in Germany, they were largely superseded by four-stringed models, with the exception of a larger five-stringed version introduced by Karl Otho of Leipzig between 1880 and 1890. Giovanni Bottesini (1822-1889) is noted as the world's greatest virtuoso of the double bass.

### The Bass Clarinet
The bass clarinet (known as *clarinette basse* in French, *Bass-Klarinette* in German, and *clarinetto basso* or *darone* in Italian) typically speaks an octave lower than the A, Bb, or C clarinet. Because of its length, it features a large gloxinea-shaped bell joint that is bent upward in front of the instrument, and a mouthpiece attached via screws and a strong ligature to a serpent-shaped crook made of silver or brass.

The modern orchestral bass clarinet generally shares the same compass as higher clarinets but an octave lower. For the instrument in C, the sounds are [Notation: E2 B5.]; the Bb instrument is one tone lower, and the A instrument is 1-1/2 tone lower. While notation is the same for all three, the treble clef is sometimes used, in which case the C instrument speaks an octave lower, the Bb a major ninth lower, and the A a minor tenth lower. The tenor clef is also frequently employed.

Specialized versions include:
* **Joseph Uhlmann's bass clarinet:** Also called a "bass basset horn," it featured twenty-three keys and a compass of four complete octaves from Bb, including all chromatic semitones. It resembles a saxophone in its bent bell and backward-angled crook, though it differs in having a non-conical bore.
* **Georg Streitwolf's bass clarinet:** Produced in 1828, this instrument resembled the fagotto and had a beak mouthpiece. It featured nineteen keys and a compass from Ab to F. In 1834, the Prussian infantry adopted this instrument as the bass for the wood-wind.

## Architectural and Linguistic Uses

In architecture, a "base court" (*basse cour*) is the first open space located within a castle's gates, historically used for keeping livestock during sieges and exercising cavalry. The "base of a wall" or "ground table" refers to the mouldings located just above the ground around a building. These often run around buttresses, and the flat band between the upper mouldings and the plinth may be carved with shields or panelled, as seen in the Henry VII Chapel at Westminster.

In linguistics, specifically regarding Sanskrit and Marathi, the "base" refers to the unmodified form of a word to which suffixes are attached. For example, the suffix *tsa*, *ci*, or *ce* (derived from the Sanskrit *tyakas*) can be added to either the unmodified base or the locative in Sanskrit. In Marathi, it is usually added to the general oblique base, though adding it to the unmodified noun gives it a more distinct adjectival force.
