# The Holy Empire and the House of Habsburg

The Holy Empire, also referred to as the Holy Roman Empire, was a political entity immemorially associated with the house of Habsburg. For centuries, this association defined the structure of Central European power, establishing a tradition of Austrian hegemony that influenced German statesmen and the political organization of the region long after the empire's formal dissolution.

## Origins and the Rise of the Habsburgs
The foundation of the territories that would eventually form the Austrian empire began around A.D. 800 with a mark along the south bank of the Danube, east of the river Enns. Established as a defense for the Frankish kingdom against the Slavs, this region was known as *Oesterreich* (east empire or realm), a term first appearing in a charter from 996. While the house of Babenberg initially focused on strengthening this mark, the house of Habsburg later pursued a policy of extending rule over neighboring territories.

The Habsburgs' ascent to imperial power was marked by the election of Rudolf, count of Habsburg, as German king in 1273 following the extinction of the Hohenstaufen house. Rudolf expanded his influence by challenging the Bohemian king, Ottakar, eventually forcing him to renounce the duchies of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia in 1276. On December 27, 1282, Rudolf invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Austria and Styria, effectively transferring the Habsburg headquarters from the Rhine to the Danube and making these lands hereditary possessions of the family. Over time, the distinction of being Emperor of Germany became practically hereditary within the Habsburg line.

## Conflict and Territorial Expansion
The growth of the Habsburg family was often met with resistance. In the thirteenth century, peasant farmers in the valleys around Lake Lucerne sought to manage their own affairs without an overlord. This led to a conflict with the Habsburgs, resulting in the burning of the castle of New Habsburg in 1245. By 1291, the men of Uri, Schwyz, and Unterwalden formed an Everlasting League to avoid Habsburg rule, eventually evolving into the independent Swiss Confederation.

Elsewhere, the Habsburgs expanded through marriage and diplomacy. For example, the duchy of Luxemburg passed to the house of Habsburg through the marriage of Elizabeth, daughter of Emperor Sigismund, to the German king Albert II. Though later seized by the duke of Burgundy, it was regained in 1477 when Maximilian I married Mary, the heiress of Charles the Bold.

## The Transition to the Empire of Austria
The traditional Holy Empire faced a permanent revolution due to the rise of Napoleon and his interference in Germany. By 1801, the treaty of Luneville had practically sealed the fate of the old Empire. Francis II, the last of the Holy Roman emperors, feared that the house of Habsburg would be relegated to a position inferior to the Bonapartes or that Napoleon might be elected as his successor on the throne of Charlemagne.

To forestall this, on August 11, 1804, Francis II proclaimed himself Francis I, hereditary emperor of Austria. This new title, "emperor of Austria," replaced the title of *Imperator Romanorum semper Augustus* when the Holy Empire formally ended in 1806, following the treaty of Pressburg and the creation of Napoleon's Confederation of the Rhine.

## Legacy and Political Influence
Despite its formal end, the "shadow of the Holy Empire" continued to influence German politics. In 1848, during the meeting of the parliament of united Germany at Frankfort, German statesmen struggled to break loose from the tradition of Austrian hegemony. This imperial tradition, along with the army, served as a "sheet-anchor" that allowed the Habsburg monarchy to weather political storms.

However, the title of emperor of Austria was often viewed as a poor substitute for the political unity of the German race previously represented by the Holy Empire. Within the monarchy, Magyars and Slavs resented the title as it implied the superiority of German elements and ignored their national rights. The "spell" of the imperial title was finally broken by the struggle with Prussia in 1866 and the subsequent proclamation of the German empire in 1871, which stripped the Austrian imperial title of everything but territorial significance.
