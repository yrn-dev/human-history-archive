# Styria and Carinthia: Territorial History and Administration

The regions of Styria and Carinthia have historically been integral components of the Austrian territories, often linked through shared governance, dynastic inheritance, and geographical proximity. Located within the broader region known to the ancients as Noricum and Pannonia—an area extending between the Inn, the Danube, and the Save—these lands have frequently been administered as part of the patrimony of a single family.

## Early Medieval Transitions and the House of Habsburg
In the mid-13th century, the duchies experienced significant instability. Following the deaths of Emperor Frederick II and Hermann VI, margrave of Baden, in 1250, the estates of Austria sought stability by choosing Ottakar, son of Wenceslaus I, king of Bohemia, as their duke in 1251. While Styria initially fell under the control of Bela IV, king of Hungary, the Styrians resented Hungarian rule. Ottakar intervened, and by a treaty in March 1261, he was recognized as duke of Styria. He further expanded his power in 1269 by inheriting the duchy of Carinthia upon the death of Duke Ulrich III.

The transition to Habsburg rule began with Rudolph, count of Habsburg, who became German king in 1273. Rudolph challenged the titles of the Bohemian king, and in November 1276, Ottakar submitted to Rudolph, renouncing the duchies of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia. Rudolph initially administered these lands as head of the Empire but sought to make them hereditary possessions for his family. On December 27, 1282, Rudolph invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Austria and Styria. Carinthia remained under Rudolph's personal control until 1286, when he bestowed it upon Meinhard IV, count of Tirol.

## Dynastic Divisions and Joint Rule
The House of Habsburg frequently utilized a system of joint rule rather than primogeniture. For many years, the duchies were governed by two or three family members simultaneously. A significant division occurred in 1379 when Leopold III and Albert III divided their lands; Albert retained Carniola and Austria proper, while Leopold received Tirol, Carinthia, and Styria.

Following the death of Leopold in 1386 at the battle of Sempach, Albert served as guardian for Leopold's four nephews. These nephews eventually ruled their lands in common. However, by 1406, a new division was made between the remaining brothers, Ernest and Frederick. Ernest became the duke of Carinthia and Styria, while Frederick became the count of Tirol. In 1424, Ernest was succeeded by his sons, Frederick and Albert.

## The Regency of Frederick III and Ladislaus Posthumus
The mid-15th century was marked by the infancy of Ladislaus Posthumus, the son of King Albert II and Elizabeth (daughter of Sigismund, king of Hungary and Bohemia). Born on February 22, 1440, Ladislaus succeeded to the kingdoms of Hungary and Bohemia, as well as the duchy of Austria. 

During this period, Frederick—who had succeeded Albert II as German king and was crowned Emperor Frederick III—acted as guardian for the minor Ladislaus and also for Sigismund of Tirol. Frederick's regency was characterized by struggle and disorder, attributed partly to his own character and partly to the ambitions of his brother, Albert, who wished to share his dignities. This era of instability eventually led to discontent among the estates of Austria and the Tirolese, with Sigismund being declared of age in 1446.

## Geography and Ethnic Composition
Styria and Carinthia are characterized by their Alpine terrain. The Steiner or Santhaler Alps feature the Grintovc peak (8393 ft.), which serves as a threefold boundary between Styria, Carinthia, and Carniola. The Karawankas form the boundary between Carinthia and Carniola, with the Stou or Stuhlberg (7344 ft.) as the highest peak.

Historically, the "kernel" of the empire consisted of purely German districts, which included a large part of Carinthia and all of Styria except for the southern districts. In later centuries, linguistic and racial divisions became prominent. While the diet of Styria remained a center for German influence, the government eventually recognized Slovenian as an official language in the south of Carinthia and in the district of Cilli in Styria.
