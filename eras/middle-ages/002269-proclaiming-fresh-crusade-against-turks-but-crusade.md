# The Crusades

The Crusades were a series of military expeditions initiated by Western Christendom, primarily driven by the objective of recovering Jerusalem and the Holy Sepulchre from Moslem control. These campaigns evolved from a wave of unifying emotion in the eleventh century into an established process that eventually lost its vitality.

## The First Crusade and Early Establishments
The First Crusade was summoned in 1095 by Pope Urban II at Clermont. This initial expedition is characterized as a vital event comparable to the discovery of America. The Franks marched to Constantinople, where they were influenced by the policy of the Greek Emperor Alexius. The campaign resulted in the conquest of Nice, Antioch, and Jerusalem, with Godfrey of Bouillon becoming the first King of Jerusalem.

Following these victories, Latin princes established several small principalities in Syria, including Edessa, which served as a buffer between the Turks and the Greeks. However, hostility quickly emerged between the Latin and Greek churches. The Greek patriarch of Jerusalem found his situation under the triumphant Latins to be worse than it had been under the Turks.

## Subsequent Expeditions and the Loss of Jerusalem
The precarious nature of the Latin possessions became evident in 1144 when Edessa fell to the Moslim. This event led to the Second Crusade (1147), which failed to recover Edessa but succeeded in saving Antioch. Jerusalem was later captured by Saladin, the Sultan of Egypt, in 1187, prompting the Third Crusade (1189).

The Fourth Crusade (1202) diverged from its original goals by attacking the Eastern Empire, leading to the capture of Constantinople by the Latins in 1204. Later, in 1244, the Christians lost Jerusalem again to the Sultan of Egypt after attempting an intrigue against him. This loss provoked the Seventh Crusade, led by King Louis IX of France (St. Louis), who was taken prisoner in Egypt and ransomed in 1250. Louis IX later led another expedition to Tunis, where he died of fever.

## Divergent Forms of Crusade
As the movement progressed, the nature of the crusades shifted. The Sixth Crusade (1229) is described as bordering on absurdity. Emperor Frederick II, who had been excommunicated by Pope Gregory IX, secured a part of the kingdom of Jerusalem through a private treaty and commercial convention with the Sultan of Egypt, rather than through military conquest.

The concept of the crusade was also applied internally within Europe to combat heresy. In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade for the destruction of Wycliffites, Hussites, and other heretics in Bohemia. This resulted in five separate crusades; the first four failed, and the fifth crossed the frontier in 1431 under Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg.

## The Ottoman Advance and the End of the Era
The Byzantine Empire maintained a complex relationship with the Turks, often intertwining diplomacy and intermarriage. By 1453, the Ottoman Sultan Muhammad II attacked Constantinople from the European side using powerful artillery. The city fell to the Moslems, the Greek Emperor was killed, and the church of Saint Sophia was turned into a mosque. Although this event sparked an attempt to organize a crusade, the era of the crusades had passed.

In a later attempt to revive the movement, Cardinal Tamas Bakocz returned to Hungary as a papal legate bringing a bull from Leo X proclaiming a fresh crusade against the Turks. However, this effort degenerated into a *jacquerie* that ravaged the kingdom and discredited Bakocz.
