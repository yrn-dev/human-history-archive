# Danish Settlements and Fortifications in Britain

## The Emergence of Fortified Towns
The landscape of urban settlement in Britain underwent a significant transformation at the end of the 9th century. Prior to this period, the primary urban centers were Roman cities (*civitates*) and fortresses (*castetta*). While some of these, such as London, Canterbury, York, and Lincoln, recovered their importance relatively early after the initial invasions, many others fell into obscurity or were deserted; for example, Chester remained deserted for three centuries following its destruction in the early 7th century.

The arrival of Danish settlements at the end of the 9th century, combined with a defensive system initiated by King Alfred, led to the creation of a new series of fortified towns. These developments served as the primary ancestors of the boroughs found in the Middle Ages.

## Danish Activity in the Midlands
The district of Derbyshire provides a specific example of the impact of Danish incursions and settlements during the 9th century. The region, which had been settled by the West Angles (known as the Pecsaetan) in the 6th century and later became the northern division of Mercia, suffered frequent ravages from the Danes. In 874, the Danes wintered at Repton, where they destroyed a famous monastery that served as the burial place for the kings of Mercia.

Under the leadership of Guthrum, Derby was established as one of five Danish burghs. The control of this area shifted over the following decades: Æthelflæd recovered Derby in 917, but the town later fell under the "Danish yoke" again until it was regained by Edmund in 942. During this era of conflict, Edward the Elder also fortified Bakewell in 924.

## The Nature of the Danish Invaders
The Danes are identified as the southernmost branch of the Scandinavian family. According to Alfred (c. 890), they occupied Scania, the islands, and Jutland. By 777, they possessed sufficient strength to defy the Frank empire by providing harbor to its fugitives. 

The period of Danish expansion, often associated with the "Viking period," began with the ravaging of Lindisfarne in 793 and concluded virtually with the establishment of Rollo in Normandy in 911. These "Northmen," as they were indiscriminately called, were the rovers who eventually colonized Normandy and southern England.

## Military Organization and Social Class
The Danish invasions occurred during a period when Anglo-Saxon military structures were evolving. Contrary to some beliefs, the regulation requiring every five or six hides to supply a warrior was not a result of the Danish invasions, but existed at least since the beginning of the 9th century. 

Military service during the 9th century involved the *fird* (*fyrd*), which consisted of mounted warriors who typically fought on foot. While the *ceorls* (the bulk of the population) participated in military expeditions, their roles were primarily supportive, focusing on the construction of bridges and stockades and the transport of provisions. The actual fighting was largely the responsibility of the *gesiðcund* classes, who were essentially military in nature and possessed the necessary weapons and horses.
