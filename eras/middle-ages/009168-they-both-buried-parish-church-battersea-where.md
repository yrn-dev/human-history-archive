# Parish Churches and Burial Customs

The parish church serves as a central ecclesiastical and communal landmark across various regions, functioning as a site for religious services, the administration of local parishes, and the interment of the deceased.

## The Role and Development of Parish Churches
In certain regions, such as Belgium, the number of parishes grew during the tenth and eleventh centuries. New chapels were established when existing parish churches were too distant or when population density allowed for a new parish. In some instances, wealthy landlords ordered the construction of a chapel on their manor to improve their own opportunities for attending church. These chapels were typically granted the right to have a parish priest authorized to bury the dead in the parish cemetery and baptize infants.

In Scotland, parish churches often reflect the architectural and political history of their towns. For example, the parish church in Kirkintilloch, which dates from 1644, is cruciform with crow-stepped gables. In Dornoch, the cathedral—built by Bishop Gilbert de Moravia (who died in 1245)—was restored in 1837 by the 2nd duke of Sutherland and has since functioned as the parish church. In Greenock, the north parish church is a Gothic edifice dating from 1591.

## Burial Laws and Ecclesiastical Regulations in England
In England, the law provides that every person has the right to be buried in the burial ground or churchyard of the parish where they die, with the exception of executed felons, who are buried in places appointed by the home office or within prison precincts. 

The mode of burial is a matter of ecclesiastical cognizance. Prior to 1880, bodies could only be buried in consecrated ground if the service of the Church was performed by the parish incumbent or an authorized person. Certain individuals were refused this service, including unbaptized persons, those against whom a verdict of *felo de se* had been found, and those excommunicated (*majori excommunicatione*) for notorious crimes who could not testify to their repentance.

The Burial Laws Amendment Act 1880 modified these requirements, allowing those entitled to burial in parochial grounds to be buried without the Church of England service. They may be buried without any religious service or with a Christian and orderly service conducted by a person invited by the funeral's person in charge. However, the act does not authorize the use of the service for the burial of a *felo de se*.

## Notable Interments in Parish Churches
Parish churches frequently serve as the final resting place for significant historical, political, and artistic figures:

*   **Battersea:** Bolingbroke, who died on December 12, 1751, and his wife, who predeceased him in 1750, were both buried in the parish church at Battersea. A monument featuring inscriptions and medallions composed by Bolingbroke was erected to their memory.
*   **Greenwich:** The parish church of Greenwich, dedicated to St Alphege, contains the burials of the musician Tallis and Wolfe, who died at Quebec in 1759.
*   **Kirkintilloch:** The poet David Gray is buried in the Kirkintilloch churchyard, where a monument was erected in 1865.
*   **Dornoch:** The cathedral serves as the burying-place for the Sutherland family, containing the remains of sixteen earls, as well as Richard de Moravia, whose effigy was found in the chancel.
*   **Greenock:** The kirkyard of the north parish church is the burial site of Burns's "Highland Mary" (1786).
*   **Axminster:** Tradition holds that the bodies of Danish princes who died in the battle of Brunanburh were buried in Axminster church.

## Administration and Finance
The management of state churches involves various funding sources. These include annual state provisions for the maintenance of fabrics, clerical stipends, and other ecclesiastical needs. Additionally, supreme councils administer endowments for clerical pensions, foreign missions, and the supply of books. Each parish is legally permitted to levy ecclesiastical assessments for defined purposes. The appointment of benefices is handled by a combination of the state (56%), private and municipal patrons (34%), and congregations (10%).
