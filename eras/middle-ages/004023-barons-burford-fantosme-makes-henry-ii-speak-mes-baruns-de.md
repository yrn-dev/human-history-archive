# Barons of Burford

The barons of Burford were a family, specifically the Cornwalls, to whom the title of baron was accorded. This designation was granted independently of peerage dignity and on grounds that are considered somewhat uncertain.

## The Concept of the Baron in England
The term "baron" was introduced into England following the Conquest. Its origin is uncertain, but it was used to denote "the man"—referring to one who had performed homage—of a great lord, particularly the king. In theory, any individual who held land "in chief" (directly) from the king was considered a *barones regis*. These individuals were members of the king's council and were required to perform stipulated services.

While the king's direct tenants were called barons, great nobles—regardless of whether they were earls—also referred to their own tenants as "barons." This practice was especially prominent in earldoms of a palatine character. For example, in the palatinate of Chester, the "barons of Kinderton" (the Venables family) existed until 1679. Similarly, the bishop of the palatinate of Durham had his own barons, such as the Hiltons of Hilton Castle, who used the style "Barons of Hilton" until the line became extinct in 1746.

## Evolution of Baronial Status
Initially, the position of a baron was tied strictly to the tenure of land. In practice, those who held a large amount of land were viewed as barons. However, a significant shift occurred when presence in the council of the realm (which became the House of Lords) ceased to depend on land tenure and instead depended on the king's will via a writ of summons. While some attributed this change to Henry III after the Barons' War, others, such as Stubbs, assign it to Edward I.

During this transition, those summoned were not yet distinguished from commoners by a specific title; the prefix *Dominus* (lord) was common to both barons and simple knights. Writs of summons were even issued to the lowest order of peers as *chevaliers* (knights). The formal style of "baron" was introduced by Richard II in 1387 through the creation of John de Beauchamp as "Lord de Beauchamp and baron of Kidderminster." Later, in 1433, Sir John Cornwall was made baron of Fanhope.

## Variations in Usage
The term "baron" was applied in various contexts beyond the landed nobility:
* **Urban Usage:** In London, the term was used more loosely. Fantosme records Henry II speaking of "mes baruns de Lundres," and a charter by John mentions "our barons of our city of London." Documents from London also refer to "the greater barons of the city," and aldermen were sometimes deemed equivalent to barons, being assessed for the poll-tax as such under Richard II.
* **Irish Usage:** Due to the palatine nature of great lordships in Ireland, the title was not uncommon, as seen with the barons of Slane, the barons of the Naas, and the barons of Galtrim.
* **Class Distinctions:** Because all those holding direct military service from the crown were called barons, a distinction emerged between "greater" and "lesser" barons. By the time of Henry II, the *Dialogus de Scaccario* distinguished between greater and lesser baronies. By 1164, greater barons received special summons to the council, whereas the Magna Carta (1215) stipulated that lesser barons be summoned only through sheriffs.

## Other Notable Baronies
The archive records various other baronial titles and figures, including:

* **Baron Loughborough:** Created in 1780 for Alexander Wedderburn.
