# Arab and Persian Influence from the 8th to the 11th Century

Between the 8th and 11th centuries, Arab and Persian populations expanded their influence across various regions, establishing political supremacy, trading networks, and cultural exchanges that spanned from the coast of East Africa to the Indian subcontinent.

## Settlements in East Africa
From the 8th century through the 11th century, Arabs and Persians established settlements along the coast of East Africa. These activities led to the acquisition of political supremacy in numerous locations and resulted in the formation of the entity known as the Zenj empire. Following this period, the history of the coastal towns remained closely identified with that of Zanzibar until the eventual establishment of British rule.

## Expansion into South Asia and India
Arab influence expanded significantly into the east during this era. In 712, the Arabs conquered Sind. In the region of Baluchistan, the influx of Arabs into Makran led to the rise of Bela (known as Armel or Armabel) as a critical link in a chain of trading towns connecting Persia and Sind. This network included locations such as Kambali and Yusli, characterized by the presence of bazaars, caravanserais, and citadels, as documented by the medieval Arab geographer Ibn Haukal. However, the site of Armabel was likely destroyed in 710 by Mahommed Kasim during his march to Sind.

By the end of the 11th century, the entirety of northern India had come under Moslem control. This expansion followed a period of instability in India, where Hindu history had become a collection of small, transitory states unable to resist the advancing Mahommedan peril.

## Literary and Cultural Transmission
The period saw significant intellectual exchange between Persian, Arabic, and other traditions. A notable example is the transmission of the *Fables of Bidpai* (or Pilpay). These stories originated from the Hindu *Pancha Tantra* and the *Hitopadesa*. In the 6th century A.D., a physician at the court of the Persian king Chosroes I. Anushirvan translated a number of these fables into Pahlavi. Nearly two centuries later, Abdallah-ibn-Mokaffa translated the Persian version into Arabic. This Arabic version, titled the "Book of Kalilah and Dimna"—named after two jackals in the first story—served as the primary channel for transmitting these fables to Europe.

Later, in the close of the 11th century, Simeon Sethus translated the work into Greek. The fables eventually reached a wider European audience through a Hebrew version by Rabbi Joel, which was translated into Latin in the 13th century by John of Capua.

## Political and Artistic Context
The broader Arab political landscape was dominated by the Caliphate, first under the Omayyads of Damascus and subsequently the Abbasids of Bagdad, which became the principal power in the nearer East. While the Caliphate lacked a coherent organization for permanent stability—leading some parts to become independent or be absorbed by the Turks—the Arabs successfully imposed their political authority and civilization across a vast area, including Morocco and Spain in the west and central Asia and India in the east.

In terms of artistic production, Arabic and Persian manuscripts from the 13th to 16th centuries demonstrate a legacy of intricate detail. These works feature exquisitely drawn title pages and ornaments resplendent with gold and color, which are considered comparable to western illuminations.
