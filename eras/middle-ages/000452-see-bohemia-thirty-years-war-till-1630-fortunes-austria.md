# Bohemia and the House of Habsburg

## Early Rule and the Premyslide Dynasty
In the 13th century, Bohemia experienced periods of instability. Following the death of Wenceslaus I's son, Ottakar, the estates of Austria met at Truebensee in 1251 and chose Ottakar, son of Wenceslaus I of Bohemia, as their duke. Ottakar, who became king of Bohemia in 1253, expanded his influence to include the duchy of Styria by 1261 and inherited Carinthia in 1269. He is noted for restoring order, benefiting town inhabitants, and introducing German colonists to eastern districts.

The Premyslide dynasty's rule eventually faced crisis. Wenceslaus II, who became nominal ruler at age twelve after the unpopular guardianship of Otto of Brandenburg, governed Bohemia himself from age nineteen. His reign was a period of great happiness, and he extended his rule to Poland. However, he died suddenly in 1305 while preparing to invade Austria. His successor, Wenceslas III, ruled for only one year before being murdered by unknown assassins in 1306, ending the Premyslide dynasty.

## Habsburg Consolidation and Religious Conflict
The House of Habsburg eventually secured claims to Bohemia. By 1519, the Habsburg dominions in eastern Germany included the county of Tirol and the duchies of Upper and Lower Austria, Styria, Carinthia, and Carniola. Emperor Charles V resigned his Austrian lands and claims on Bohemia and Hungary to his brother, Ferdinand.

Under Emperor Maximilian II (1564-1576), who was king of Bohemia and Hungary, a liberal policy maintained peace, though he could not establish permanent religious liberty. During this time, Lutheranism was prevalent in Austria and most of Styria, while various Christian beliefs struggled for mastery in Moravia, Silesia, and Bohemia. This shifted under Rudolph II (1576-1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic who encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation. Under Rudolph, Bohemia existed in a state of semi-independence.

The situation remained unstable under Matthias (emperor 1612-1619), and by the time of his death, Bohemia was practically independent. His successor, Ferdinand II (1619-1637), was determined to win Germany back to the Catholic faith. Having already crushed Protestantism as archduke of Styria, Ferdinand II sought to establish the rule of the Jesuits in Bohemia after being elected its king in 1618.

## The Thirty Years' War and its Aftermath
Ferdinand II's efforts to impose Jesuit rule led to the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War, which began in the Bohemian capital. Bohemia served as a primary theatre of hostilities for much of the conflict. In 1620, the battle of the White Mountain occurred, after which a large portion of the Protestant population left the town of Königgrätz. In 1631, Saxons occupied large parts of Bohemia in an attempt to re-establish Protestantism. Later in the war, Swedish troops frequently pillaged the region; the town of Königgrätz was occupied by Swedes for eight months in 1639, and the war concluded in 1648 with the Swedish general Konigsmark taking part of Prague. Despite Swedish efforts, imperial representatives remained inflexible regarding toleration for Bohemian Protestants.

## Habsburg Succession and Administrative Changes
In the early 18th century, the possibility of the extinction of the Habsburg male line led the estates of Bohemia to meet in Prague on October 16, 1720. They sanctioned female succession and recognized the Pragmatic Sanction, which proclaimed the indivisibility of the Habsburg realm. This was intended to favor archduchess Maria Theresa.

Upon the death of Emperor Charles VI, Maria Theresa faced opposition from Charles, elector of Bavaria, who invaded Bohemia with French, Saxon, and Bavarian troops and occupied Prague in December 1741. Maria Theresa eventually recovered Bohemia and other Habsburg lands. During her reign and that of her son, Joseph II, the autonomy of Bohemia was further limited. A 1749 decree abolished separate Bohemian law-courts, and an Austro-Bohemian chancellor was later appointed to control the administration of Bohemia and the German domains of the house of Habsburg.

## Later Conflicts and Political Transitions
Bohemia remained a site of strategic military engagement. On June 18, 1757, the Austrians under Daun defeated the Prussians under Frederick the Great at the battle of Kolin, resulting in the evacuation of Bohemia by Prussian forces. In 1866, the decisive battle of the Austro-Prussian war took place near Königgrätz on July 3.

In the 19th century, political instability returned. Following the fall of Metternich, the Austrian government promised a separate constitution to Bohemia on April 8. Later, following international conflict, the boundaries of Bohemia and Moravia were used to form the boundary between the new Republic of Austria and Czecho-Slovakia. Austria was required to recognize the complete independence of Czecho-Slovakia.
