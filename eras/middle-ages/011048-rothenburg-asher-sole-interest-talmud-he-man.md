# Rothenburg

The name Rothenburg appears in the archive's records in two distinct contexts: as a geographical location in Europe and in association with the intellectual lineage of the rabbi Asher ben-Yehiel.

## Geographical Locations
Rothenburg is identified as one of the chief towns located in Upper Lusatia. This region is characterized as generally picturesque and mountainous, with primary industries including coal mining, cloth making, and linen weaving. The area is served by the Neisse, the Black Elster, and the Spree rivers.

Separately, Rothenburg is listed as one of the districts acquired by a town to form a canton. This acquisition took place in 1394, alongside other regions such as Horw, Sempach, Kriens, and Hochdorf.

## Asher ben-Yehiel and Meir of Rothenburg
In the history of rabbinic scholarship, Asher ben-Yehiel (known as *Rosh*) is recorded as a disciple of Meir of Rothenburg. Asher was a rabbi and codifier born in the Rhine district around 1250 and died in Toledo in 1327. 

Asher's intellectual focus was characterized by a profound and narrow austere piety, with his sole interest being the Talmud. He was a determined opponent of the study of philosophy, which placed him in opposition to the Spanish spirit, even though the people of Spain remained devotees of rabbinical lore and secular sciences.

## Contributions to Rabbinic Law
Asher ben-Yehiel was the first German rabbi to demonstrate a strong talent for systematization. Between 1307 and 1314, he compiled his chief work, a *Compendium* of the Talmud. This work was noted for its simplicity and its deference to German authorities; it largely superseded the work of 'Al-phasi and is included in most editions of the Talmud.

The legacy of systematization continued with Asher's son, Jacob, who died in Toledo before 1340. Jacob authored the four *Turim*, a popular and profound codification of rabbinical law. This work served as the standard code until it became the direct basis for the widely accepted Code of Jewish law known as the *Shulhan 'Arukh*, authored by Joseph Qaro.

## Historical Context of the Rabbinate
The life of Asher ben-Yehiel reflects a shift in the status of medieval rabbis. Due to persecutions inflicted on the people of Germany in the 13th century, Asher was forced into exile in Spain, where he became the rabbi of Toledo. This enforced exile left him impoverished. 

Prior to the 14th century, rabbis typically did not receive a salary for their rabbinic functions and instead earned a livelihood through secular professions, most notably medicine. Following this period, this arrangement changed.
