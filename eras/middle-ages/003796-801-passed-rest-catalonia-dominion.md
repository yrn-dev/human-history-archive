# Barcelona and the History of Catalonia

## Early Foundations and Roman Period
The city of Barcelona, known in antiquity as Barcino, is traditionally believed to have been founded in the 3rd century B.C. by Hamilcar Barca, a Carthaginian. Following the Roman conquest, the city was renamed Julia Faventia by Augustus (who reigned from 27 B.C. to A.D. 14) and was granted the status of a Roman colony. It later received the names Augusta and Pia.

Located on Monte Taber, the city grew rapidly to become a primary mart of the western Mediterranean by the 2nd century A.D., competing with Massilia (Marseilles) and Tarraco (Tarragona). In the broader administrative context of the Roman Empire, Barcelona was situated within Tarraconensis, a region that encompassed much of Spain, including the kingdoms of Navarre, Arragon, Catalonia, Biscay, the Asturias, Gallicia, the kingdom of Leon, Murcia, Valentia, and the majority of the two Castiles.

## Medieval Transitions and Independence
The bishopric of Barcelona was established in 343. During the Visigothic period, the city served as a temporary capital in 415 and 531, and hosted church councils in 540 and 599. The city, then referred to as Bardjaluna or Barcinona, was captured by the Moors in 713. In 801, Barcelona and the remainder of Catalonia passed under the dominion of the Franks.

By 874, the counts of Barcelona began ruling as independent monarchs. The city reached the zenith of its wealth and fame following the union of Aragon and Catalonia in 1149, which provided larger resources. During this era, Barcelona's merchant ships traded as far east as Alexandria and as far west as the Baltic and North Seas, rivaling the maritime power of Venice, Ragusa, and Genoa.

## Maritime Law and Commercial Supremacy
Barcelona became a leader in maritime commerce and regulation. In 1258, James I of Aragon authorized the city to issue the *Consulado del Mar*, a code of maritime law that many European states recognized as authoritative. The city was also among the first communities to implement the practice of marine insurance and maintained consuls in major commercial centers across or near the Mediterranean.

## Decline and Political Conflict
The commercial supremacy of Barcelona began to wane following the union of Aragon and Castile in 1479, which benefited other Spanish cities. This decline was accelerated by the 1492 discovery of America, which shifted commercial dominance to the ports of western Spain.

The citizens of Barcelona attributed these misfortunes to the government of the Castilians. This led to the rise of a strong political faction that favored annexation by France. In 1640, Barcelona became the center of a Catalonian rebellion against Philip IV and sought French protection. Although the city returned to its previous allegiance in 1652, it was captured by the duke of Vendome in 1697. The city's status was later addressed at the peace of Ryswick in that same year.

## Notable Figures
Catalonia was the birthplace of Jaime Luciano Balmes (1810–1848), a philosopher and Spanish ecclesiastic born in Vich. Balmes is recognized as a political writer who defended Catholicism in his work *El Protestantismo comparado con el Catolicismo en sus relaciones con la Civilisacion Europea* (1842–1844), arguing that Catholicism represents order and obedience in contrast to the "spirit of revolt" found in Protestantism. He also authored the *Curso de Filosofia Elemental* (1847) and *Filosofia Fondamental* (1846), which provided expositions of the scholastic system of thought.
