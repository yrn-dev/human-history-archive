## Burke and Hamilton
In 1765, Burke accompanied Hamilton back to London. At this time, Burke held a pension of £300 per year on the Irish Establishment. This financial arrangement lasted for approximately one year. Hamilton, recognizing Burke as a powerful and laborious subaltern, attempted to bind him permanently to his service. Burke refused to enter into this form of final bondage. Following Hamilton's continued pursuit of "odious pretensions," the two men quarreled, resulting in Burke relinquishing his pension. Subsequently, Burke obtained a piece of preferment more important than any he could have acquired through Hamilton.

## Marco Antonio de Dominis
Marco Antonio de Dominis, an Italian natural philosopher and theologian born in 1560, arrived in England in December of a year following his resignation from his see in September 1616. De Dominis had previously served as the archbishop of Spalato and primacy of Dalmatia and Croatia, but his efforts to reform the Church led to conflicts with his suffragans. Additionally, the papal court interfered with his rights as metropolitan, a situation worsened by a quarrel between Venice and the papacy.

After an unsuccessful attempt to speak with Pope Paul V in Rome, de Dominis traveled through Rotterdam, Heidelberg, and Switzerland to reach England. Upon his arrival, he was received with great respect by the Anglican clergy and the king. He was received into the Church of England at St Paul's cathedral. His subsequent appointments included serving as the dean of Windsor in 1619 and the master of the Savoy in 1618. He also presented himself to the living of West Ilsley in Berkshire. While contemporary writers described him as pretentious, avaricious, irascible, and fat, his ability was considered undoubted, and he became a leading figure in the theological controversies of that era, publishing attacks on the papacy.
