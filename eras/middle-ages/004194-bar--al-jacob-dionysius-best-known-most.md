# BAR-SALIBI, JACOB (DIONYSIUS)

Jacob Bar-Salibi, also known as Dionysius, was a prominent 12th-century writer within the Syrian Jacobite church. He is recognized as the most voluminous writer of his church during this period.

## Life and Ecclesiastical Career
Bar-Salibi was a native of Malatia, located on the Upper Euphrates, a place of origin he shared with Bar-Hebraeus. His ecclesiastical ascent began in 1154 when Patriarch Athanasius VIII created him bishop of Mar'ash. His responsibilities expanded a year later when the diocese of Mabbog was added to his charge. 

In 1166, Michael I, who succeeded Athanasius, transferred Bar-Salibi to the metropolitan see of Amid in Mesopotamia. He remained in this position until his death in 1171. It is noted that Jacob was his baptismal name, while he assumed the name Dionysius upon his consecration to the bishopric.

## Literary Works and Contributions
Bar-Salibi was an able theologian characterized by a combination of terseness in argument and vigour, qualities particularly evident in the introductory sections of his commentary on St Matthew. While he was a prolific author, he did not claim to be more than a compiler, specifically regarding his commentaries. Due to his tendency to omit detailed indications of the sources for his interpretations and arguments, judging his originality is difficult.

His most significant works include:
*   **Biblical Commentaries:** He produced exhaustive commentaries on the texts of the New and Old Testaments. In these works, he summarized and interwoven the interpretations of previous writers, including John of Dara, Moses Bar-Kepha, Cyril, Chrysostom, and Ephrem.
*   **Polemical and Theological Treatises:** He wrote a treatise against heretics, which included polemics against the Mahommedans and the Israelites.
*   **Other Works:** His bibliography includes homilies, epistles, and liturgical treatises.

## Reception and Modern Scholarship
The works of Bar-Salibi have been the subject of various scholarly efforts over the centuries. In the 17th century, Dudley Loftus made use of his commentaries on the Gospels to some extent. 

Systematic editing of his output began in 1903 with H. Labourt's translation and edition of the *Exposition of the Liturgy* in Paris. Subsequently, J. B. Chabot and J. Sedlavek edited and translated his commentaries on the Gospels (Fasc. I., Paris, 1906). Additionally, J. de Zwaan edited the Syriac text of his treatise against the Israelites in Leiden in 1906. Further accounts of his writings and extracts are provided by Assemani in *Bibl. Orient.*, and W. Wright provided additional details regarding the manuscripts in *Syriac Literature*.
