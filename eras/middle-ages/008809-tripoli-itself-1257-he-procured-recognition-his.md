# Tripoli

Tripoli has functioned historically as a county and a strategic center of governance and exploration, appearing in records ranging from the medieval period to the 19th century.

## The County of Tripoli and the House of Bohemund
The history of the county of Tripoli is closely linked with the principality of Antioch and the lineage of the Bohemunds. Bohemund IV. held both titles, and he was succeeded by his son, Bohemund V., in 1233. Bohemund V. served as both prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. During his rule, he maintained an alliance with the Templars but faced hostility from Armenia. This conflict remained unresolved until 1251, when peace was achieved through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of the future Bohemund VI. to the sister of the Armenian king. Bohemund V. resided primarily in Tripoli, leaving the governance of Antioch to its commune and bailiff until his death in 1251.

Bohemund VI., the son of Bohemund V. and Luciana (daughter of the count of Segni), succeeded his father in 1251. He was knighted by St Louis in 1252. In that same year, Bohemund VI. established himself in Antioch, leaving Tripoli to govern itself. In 1257, he secured the recognition of his nephew, Hugh II. (the son of Henry I. of Cyprus and Plaisance), as the king of Jerusalem. Bohemund VI. formed an alliance with the Mongols to oppose the Egyptian sultan; however, he lost Antioch to Bibars in 1268. By the time of his death in 1275, he held only the title of count of Tripoli.

He was succeeded in 1275 by his son, Bohemund VII., who was the son of Sibylla (sister of Leo III. of Armenia). Bohemund VII.'s reign was short and troubled, characterized by conflicts with the Templars established in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Because Bohemund VII. died without issue and Tripoli was captured within two years of his passing, the county of Tripoli is considered to have become extinct with him.

## Political Transitions and Imperialism
In more recent history, Tripoli was the subject of European imperialist ambitions. During the period of the "Armed Peace" in Europe, Italy experienced an "imperialist fever." After a period of hesitation following the bloodletting at Adowa, Italy resumed imperial expansion in 1911. This resulted in a war against Turkey and the subsequent annexation of Tripoli. Italian imperialists at the time encouraged their citizens to view themselves as heirs to the Roman Empire, urging them to remember Julius Caesar.

## Exploration and Scientific Missions
In the mid-19th century, Tripoli served as a starting point for European exploration of the African interior. In early 1850, a party consisting of James Richardson, Adolf Overweg (a Prussian astronomer), and Barth departed from Tripoli. The mission, initiated at the request of scientists such as Bunsen, aimed to open commercial relations with the states of the central and western Sudan. 

Following the deaths of Overweg in 1852 and Richardson in 1851, Barth continued the expedition alone. He traversed the Sahara and visited various emirates and sultanates, including Gando, Sokoto, Nupe, Kano, and Bornu, as well as Timbuktu and Lake Chad. Barth returned to Europe in September 1855. His findings were published in the five-volume work *Travels and Discoveries in North and Central Africa* (1857-1858), which became a standard authority on the regions later known as Nigeria. Despite the extent of his work, the British government provided Barth no recognition for his services other than a C.B.
