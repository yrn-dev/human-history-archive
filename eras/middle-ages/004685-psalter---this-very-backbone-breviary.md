# The Breviary and the Psalter

The Breviary (from the Latin *breviarium*, meaning epitome or abridgment) is the book containing the offices for the canonical hours, which constitute the daily service of the Roman Catholic Church. It is more comprehensive than the Anglican Book of Common Prayer in that it provides hymns and lessons for every day of the year, though it is less comprehensive because it excludes the Eucharistic office (found in the Missal) and special offices for ordination, burial, marriage, and baptism (found in the Pontifical or Ritual).

## Evolution and Structure
In early Christian worship, the Bible provided all necessary materials for lessons and psalms, following Jewish custom. The first step toward the creation of the Breviary was the separation of the Psalter into a choir-book. Initially, the leader of the choir or the bishop chose psalms as appropriate. By the 4th century, psalms began to be grouped together, a process accelerated by monastic practices of reciting all 150 psalms daily. To manage the time required, monks spread the recitation over a week, dividing the day into hours and allotting a portion of the Psalter to each. In the 6th century, St Benedict drew up such an arrangement, likely based on an older, less skillful Roman division.

Over time, these choir-books were expanded with collects, responses, and antiphons to assist those unable to improvise metrical compositions. By the 12th century, liturgical author Jean Beleth listed several necessary books for the canonical office, including the *Psalterium*, *Collectarium*, *Antiphonarium*, *Passionarius* (for martyrs), *Legendarius* (for saints), *Homiliarius* (Gospel homilies), *Sermologus* (sermons), the New and Old Testaments, and the works of the Fathers.

## The Psalter
The Psalter serves as the groundwork of the Catholic prayer-book and the backbone of the Breviary; versicles, responsories, and antiphons grew out of it. In the Breviary, the psalms follow a disposition from the 8th century:
* **Matins:** Psalms 1–108 (with some omissions) are recited—twelve each day from Monday to Saturday and eighteen on Sunday.
* **Lauds, Prime, and Compline:** These hours include the omissions from the Matins sequence.
* **Vespers:** Psalms 109–147 (excluding 117, 118, and 142) are recited, five each day.
* **Lauds:** Psalms 148–150 are always used here, giving the hour its name.

The text used is the Gallican Psalter, which was Jerome's second revision (A.D. 392) of the old *Itala* version used in Rome. Jerome's first revision (A.D. 383), the Roman version, remains in use at St Peter's in Rome. However, the Gallican version was introduced into Gaul in the 6th century by St Gregory of Tours and subsequently ousted the Roman version elsewhere. It was accepted in Ireland by the 7th century (as proven by the Antiphonary of Bangor) and by the English Church in the 10th century.

## The Roman and Sarum Breviaries
The Roman Breviary is divided into four seasonal parts: winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Each part contains the Psalter, the *Proprium de Tempore* (special office of the season), *Proprium Sanctorum* (special offices of saints), *Commune Sanctorum* (general offices for saints), and Extra Services. Introductory materials include the calendar, general rubrics, and tables for determining the date of Easter.

The Sarum or Salisbury Breviary was also widely used. Its first edition was printed in Venice in 1483 by Raynald de Novimagio, and its latest in Paris in 1557–1556. Unlike modern Breviaries, which typically use four volumes, Sarum editions never exceeded two parts. The Sarum Rite was particularly favored in Scotland as a protest against the jurisdiction of the church of York.

## Historical Revisions and Usage
Until the Council of Trent, bishops and monastic communities had the power to regulate their own Breviaries. Pope Pius V (1566–1572) made the Roman Breviary obligatory, except for those that could prove 200 years of existence. The Roman Breviary underwent various revisions: Francis Quignonez (1536) created a model that later influenced the 1549 reform of the Church of England. Other revisions were made by Sixtus V in 1588 (introducing the revised Vulgate), Clement VIII in 1602 (regarding rubrics), and Urban VIII (1623–1644), who modified the hymns.

In the 17th and 18th centuries, a revision movement in France, influenced by the Port Royal school and scholars like Louis Sébastien le Nain de Tillemont and Jean de Launoy, sought to simplify services and ensure all responses and antiphons used exact Scriptural words. These reformed Breviaries, such as those by Archbishop François de Harlay (1680) and Archbishop Charles Gaspard Guillaume de Vintimille (1736), were eventually suppressed by an Ultramontane movement during the pontificate of Pius IX, with the last diocese (Orleans) surrendering in 1875.

## Ritual and Art
Every member of a religious order and every clerk in orders must read the daily Breviary services aloud. In large churches, these are grouped: Matins and Lauds (approx. 7:30 A.M.), Prime, Terce, Sext, and None (approx. 10 A.M.), and Vespers and Compline (4 P.M.). This process can take between four and eight hours depending on the number of high masses and music.

The Breviary was also a subject of high art. In 15th-century France, the school of Paris produced magnificent illuminated volumes. Notable examples include the Psalter for the boy-king Henry VI and the breviary illuminated for John the Fearless, duke of Burgundy (assassinated 1419), the latter of which is noted for its extreme refinement and detail.
