# Genoa and Florence

Genoa and Florence are prominent Italian cities that emerged as significant centers of trade, finance, and art, particularly during the later Middle Ages and the Renaissance. Both cities played pivotal roles in the development of urban life in Italy following the weakening of the Holy Roman Empire's hold on North and Central Italy after the end of the Hohenstaufen line in the thirteenth century.

## Economic and Political Development
From the twelfth century onward, Europe experienced a great revival of town life driven by an increase in trade. Genoa, along with its rival Venice, became one of the great trading seaports of the era. These cities were characterized by their noble palaces and lordly paintings. The rivalry between Genoa and Venice was often violent; for example, a dispute in 1255 over the church of St. Sabba’s on the boundary of their estates in Acre led to a war that lasted for years. While Venice initially held the upper hand, driving Genoa from Acre and taking porphyry figures as trophies for St. Mark’s, Genoa later allied with Michael Palaeologus to upset the Latin Dynasty and challenge Venetian preponderance on the Bosphorus.

In contrast to the maritime focus of Genoa, Florence developed as an inland trading and financial center. By the fifteenth century, Florence was under the almost monarchical rule of the Medici family, a period described as a second "Periclean age."

## Art and Architecture
Florence became the center for the imitation, restoration, and rediscovery of antique art—the Renaissance—towards the end of the fourteenth century. The city is home to the Duomo, designed by Brunellesco (1377–1446), and Giotto’s tower (Giotto, 1266–1337). Other significant artists associated with Florence include Donatello, Botticelli, Filippo Lippi, Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Raphael. The city also attracted foreign talent, such as the French sculptor Giovanni da Bologna (Jean Boullongne), who settled there in 1553. His celebrated works in Florence include the bronze figure of Mercury in the Bargello gallery, the equestrian statue of Cosimo de' Medici, and the marble group "The Rape of the Sabines" in the Loggia de Lanzi.

Genoa also fostered a distinct artistic tradition, notably the Genoese school of painting. Giovanni Benedetto Castiglione (1616–1670), known as Il Grechetto, was born in Genoa and excelled in painting rural scenes, markets, and fairs. His *Presepio* (Nativity of Jesus) in the church of San Luca is among his most famous works. Additionally, Giovanni da Bologna was employed in Genoa, where he produced various works primarily in bronze.

## Historical Figures and Visitors
Both cities have served as residences or destinations for various historical figures. Joseph, the brother of Napoleon, resided in both Genoa and Florence for a time after visiting England. He eventually died in Florence on July 28, 1844. 

In the 19th century, the scholar Yule visited Genoa, where he wrote the Dedication to Sir Bartle Frere for his translation of the *Travels of Friar Jordanus*. Yule also spent time in Florence, where he formed friendships with Italian men of letters, including the Marchese Gino Capponi and the historian Villari, as well as the writer Charles Lever.

Earlier, in the late 15th century, the English scholar William Grocyn visited Florence, Rome, and Padua between 1488 and 1491 to study Latin and Greek under Politian and Demetrius Chalchondyles.

## Regional Geography and Related Sites
Genoa is the primary reference point for other towns in the Liguria region. For instance, Bordighera, located 91 miles southwest of Genoa by rail, is a popular winter resort for English visitors known for its gardens and the export of flowers and palms. Historically, from 1682 until the Napoleonic period, Bordighera served as the capital of a small republic consisting of villages in the neighboring valleys.
