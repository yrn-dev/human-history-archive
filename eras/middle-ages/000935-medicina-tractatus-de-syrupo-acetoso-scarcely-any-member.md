# Tractatus de Syrupo Acetoso

The *Tractatus de Syrupo Acetoso* is a medical work that was translated into Latin. It is one of several medical texts attributed to Avicenna.

## Context within Avicenna's Works
Avicenna is credited with approximately 100 treatises, which vary significantly in length, ranging from short tracts of a few pages to extensive works spanning several volumes. While he is most renowned in Europe for the *Canon of Medicine*—a work that saw an Arabic edition in Rome (1593), a Hebrew version in Naples (1491), and roughly thirty Latin editions based on Gerard of Cremona's translation—his contributions extended across many other medical texts.

Among the medical works translated into Latin, the *Tractatus de Syrupo Acetoso* is listed alongside the *Canticum de Medicina* and the *Medicamenta Cordialia*.

## Influence and Scope of Avicenna's Treatises
The treatises of Avicenna had a profound impact on the Arabian circle of sciences. His writings touched upon nearly every major field of study, including:
*   Physics and Astronomy
*   Mathematics and Music
*   Philology and Theology

Many of these treatises likely remained similar in content, though they differed in extent, form, or the specific patron who commissioned them. In addition to his medical and scientific works, Avicenna wrote at least one treatise on alchemy, though other works on the subject have been falsely attributed to him. 

His broader intellectual output included a book on animals (translated by Michael Scot) and several treatises that provided synoptic views of Aristotelian doctrine, specifically his works on *Physics*, *De Caelo*, *Logic*, and *Metaphysics*. The latter two were printed in Venice in 1493, 1495, and 1546. Some of his shorter essays on logic and medicine were composed in poetical form.

## Philosophical and Encyclopedic Works
Beyond his specific medical tracts like the *Tractatus de Syrupo Acetoso*, Avicenna produced two encyclopedic treatises on philosophy. The larger of these, *Al-Shif[=a]* (*Sanatio*), exists in manuscript form in the Bodleian library and other locations. A portion of this work concerning the *De Anima* was published in Pavia in 1490 as the *Liber Sextus Naturalium*. Additionally, the philosopher Shahrastani provided a long account of Avicenna's philosophy that appears to be a reproduction and analysis of the *Al-Shif[=a]*. A more concise version of this philosophical work is known as the *An-naj[=a]t* (*Liberatio*).
