# Transylvania: Political Status and History

Transylvania is a region located near the Carpathian Mountains. Historically, it has functioned as a principality and has been integrated into various political entities, including the crown of Hungary and the Austrian monarchy.

## Recognition as an Independent Principality
In the mid-17th century, the region was the site of Ottoman aggression, which led to a conflict between the Turks and the Austrian empire under Emperor Leopold I. This war concluded with a victory for the Austrian forces, led by Montecuculi, over the grand vizier at St Gothard on the Raab on August 1, 1664. 

Despite this military success, the general political climate prevented Leopold I from fully capitalizing on the victory. The resulting Peace of Vasvar, signed on August 10, 1664, allowed the Turks to retain possession of the fortress of Ersekujvar (Neuhaeusel) and Nagyvarad (Grosswardein). Under the terms of this peace agreement, Transylvania was recognized as an independent principality.

## Integration with Hungary and Austria
Following various revolutions, Transylvania and Temeswar were eventually annexed to the crown of Hungary. During the 19th century, the region's governance was closely tied to the Austrian empire. Between 1811 and 1834, the diet in Transylvania was not summoned.

By the mid-1860s, tensions rose as the Magyar nation and Czechs sought the restoration of the independence of the kingdom of Hungary, refusing to recognize the 1861 constitution that had established a common parliament for the empire. In June 1865, Emperor Francis Joseph visited Pest and replaced Count Francis Zichy, the chancellor of Transylvania (along with Count Nadasdy of Hungary), with Count Majlath, a leader of the old conservative magnates.

Following the war of 1866 and the subsequent Treaty of Prague, a reorganization of the government occurred. An agreement was reached in which the emperor would be crowned at Pest and take the ancient oath to the Golden Bull. Under this arrangement, Hungary—which included Croatia and Transylvania—was granted its own parliament.

## Regional Centers and Administration
Kolozsvár (also known as Klausenburg or Cluj) served as the capital of the whole of Transylvania and remains the capital of the county of Kolozs. Situated in a valley on the banks of the Little Szamos, the town is a scientific and literary center. It features a citadel erected in 1715 and a university founded in 1872 with faculties in law, philosophy, medicine, and theology.

The town is noted for its religious and cultural diversity, serving as the seat for a Unitarian bishop and the superintendent of the Calvinists for the Transylvanian circle. Notable architecture includes the Gothic church of St Michael (1396–1432) and a Reformed church built by Matthias Corvinus in 1486, which was later ceded to the Calvinists by Bethlen Gabor in 1622. The town also contains numerous mansions belonging to Transylvanian nobles.

## Geographic and Ancient Context
Ancient accounts place the territory of the Cacoenses, referred to as Caucaland in Gothic form, toward the Carpathian Mountains on the side of present-day Transylvania. This area is associated with the canton of Cacava, located to the south of Hermanstadt, which is identified as the capital of the principality.
