# St Basil and the Basilian Institute

## Life and Background of Basil the Great
Basil the Great (c. 330–379), also known as Basil, was a prominent 4th-century churchman and the bishop of Caesarea. Born in Caesarea, Cappadocia, he came from a distinguished family known for its enthusiastic piety and support of the Church. His siblings included Macrina, celebrated for her saintly life; Gregory of Nyssa; Peter, the bishop of Sebaste; and the Christian jurist Naucratius. 

Basil's education took him to Constantinople and Athens, where he spent four or five years. During this time, he was a fellow-student of Gregory of Nazianzus. Both were deeply influenced by Origen, and together they compiled the *Philocalia*, an anthology of Origen's writings. While in Athens, Basil resolved to study the methods of famous hermit saints in Arabia and Syria to learn how to achieve enthusiastic piety and maintain bodily discipline through ascetic devices and maceration.

Before his ordination as a presbyter in 365, Basil led a convent near Arnesi in Pontus, where his widowed mother, Emilia, his sister Macrina, and other women engaged in charitable works and prayer. He was eventually ordained—likely at the request of ecclesiastical superiors who wanted his talents to combat the Arians, who were numerous in the region and supported by Emperor Valens. In 370, Basil succeeded Eusebius as the bishop of Caesarea.

## The Basilian Monastic Institute
Around 360, Basil established a monastery in the neighborhood of Neocaesarea in Pontus. This institute was a deliberate departure from the monastic trends he observed in Syria and Palestine, where the monastic life had become increasingly eremitical and characterized by extravagant bodily austerities.

Basil declared the cenobitical life (communal living) to be superior to the eremitical life (hermitage). His system was based on several core principles:
* **Integration of Work:** Basil taught that work should be an integral part of monastic life, not merely as a way to pass time, but for its own sake and to benefit others.
* **Balance of Austerity:** He maintained that fasting and austerities must not interfere with work or prayer.
* **Location:** Because of the emphasis on doing good for others, he believed monasteries should be located near towns.

The life established by Basil was strictly cenobitical, featuring common meals, common work, and common prayer conducted seven times a day. Despite these new ideas, it remained an austere, contemplative life focused on heavy field-work, the reading of Scriptures, and prayer.

## The Rules and Influence
The monastic reconstruction led by St Basil serves as the basis for monasticism in the Slavonic and Greek Churches to this day, although the monks do not typically call themselves "Basilians." The "Rules" associated with him—the Longer and the Shorter—are viewed more as catechisms of spiritual life than as corporate regulations. While it appears that no formal vows were taken, the institute strongly emphasized virtues such as self-denial, chastity, personal poverty, and obedience.

The propagation of Basilian monachism was ensured by the influence of St Basil and the fact that his institute was more suitable to European ideas. According to Sozomen, in the neighboring provinces and Cappadocia, there were no hermits, only cenobites. 

Despite the success of the Basilian model, a preference for the eremitical life persisted in the east. The prevalence of the Basilian cenobitic form throughout Greek-speaking lands was eventually achieved through civil and ecclesiastical legislation, specifically the Justinian Code and the council of Chalcedon, although eremitical forms continued to maintain themselves.
