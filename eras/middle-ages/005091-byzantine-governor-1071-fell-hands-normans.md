# Byzantine Governance and Territorial Loss in the 11th Century

The administration of the Byzantine Empire during the 11th century was characterized by the appointment of governors to maintain control over distant territories, though this period was marked by significant territorial erosion due to the rise of the Normans in the west and the Seljuk Turks in the east.

## Byzantine Administration and Governors
Byzantine governors were tasked with the reconstruction and management of strategic regions. In one instance, a governor known as Lupus the protospatharius rebuilt a town in the 11th century that had previously been captured and destroyed by the Saracens in 836. In Barium, the city served as the residence of the Byzantine governor starting in 885. Similarly, in the effort to regain Sicily from the Arabs, the emperor utilized Maniaces, the governor of Lombardy. Maniaces successfully reduced the majority of Sicily and thirteen cities to imperial obedience, aided by five hundred Norman knights who had been enrolled by Arduin, a Greek agent and interpreter.

## The Norman Conquests
The Normans entered the Mediterranean from the west, initially arriving as pilgrims or mercenaries in scattered bands by land through Italy and the Rhineland. Over time, they became independent invaders and rapacious robbers. Their growth in power led to a feeble alliance between the Pope and the Eastern Emperor in 1053 to oppose them. 

The Normans established themselves in South Italy and Calabria, and between 1060 and 1090, they conquered Sicily from the Saracens. Under the leadership of Robert Guiscard—a former brigand and pilgrim adventurer—the Normans began to threaten the Byzantine Empire directly. In 1071, Robert Guiscard captured Barium. Other territories also fell during this era; for example, a town rebuilt by Lupus the protospatharius fell into Norman hands in 1071. Later, in 1081, Robert Guiscard crossed from Brindisi to Epirus with an army that included Sicilian Moslems and laid siege to the Byzantine stronghold of Durazzo.

## The Seljuk Turkish Advance
While the empire faced Norman aggression in the west, it encountered a different threat from the northeast. Turkish tribes, who had converted to Islam, grew strong in the tenth century as Arab power decayed. In the eleventh century, the Seljuk Turks entered Mesopotamia, treating the Caliph as a nominal ruler and a tool. After conquering Armenia, they attacked the remnants of Byzantine power in Asia Minor.

The year 1071 proved catastrophic for the empire's eastern holdings. The Byzantine army was utterly smashed at the battle of Melasgird. Following this defeat, the Turks swept forward until Byzantine rule was entirely erased from Asia. The Turks eventually took the fortress of Nicæa, positioned opposite Constantinople, and prepared to attack the city itself.

## Imperial Response and the Papacy
The convergent dangers of the Norman attacks from the west and the swift Turkish advance in the east created a sense of final disaster for the empire. Emperor Michael VII, who ruled from 1071 to 1078 (nominally with his brothers Andronicus and Constantine), appealed to Pope Gregory VII for assistance. This request for help was later repeated with greater urgency by Michael's successor, Alexius Comnenus, who appealed to Pope Urban II.

The counselors in Rome viewed these appeals as an opportunity to assert the Pope's headship over the Christian world. Pope Urban II sought to use the situation to organize the energy of the Normans and extend the influence of the Latin Church over Egypt, Palestine, and Syria. This culminated in church councils at Piacenza and Clermont in 1095, where Urban II organized a universal war propaganda against the Moslems to reclaim the site of the Holy Sepulchre.
