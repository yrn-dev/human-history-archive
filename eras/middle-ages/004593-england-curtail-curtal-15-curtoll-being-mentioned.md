# The Curtail

The term "curtail" (also appearing as "curtal" or "curtoll") refers to a specific musical instrument used in England, as well as a term appearing in various historical and legal contexts within the archive's records.

## The Musical Instrument
The curtail was an English name for an instrument known in France as the *courtaud* and in other regions as the *dulceian*, *dolcian*, or *doucaine*. It was developed to increase the portability of the tenor pommer in C by shortening its unwieldy proportions. 

Construction of the instrument involved using a block of wood with a diameter more than double that of a pommer. Two bores were cut into this block, which communicated at a flat bottom. At the top of the instrument, the bell and the crook—which held the double reed mouthpiece—were positioned side by side. The curtail featured two keys, one hole at the back, and six holes in the front.

Historical records from 1582 mention the "curtoll" as part of "common bleting musick," alongside the Hobius (Hautboy) and the Drone.

### The Double Curtail
The evolution of the instrument led to the "double curtail," which was a converted bass pommer. This version was an octave below the single curtail, making its construction and pitch identical to the early fagotto in C. A description and drawing of a double curtail are found in the manuscript of *The Academy of Armoury* by Randle Holme, written before 1688. Holme described the double curtail as being "double the bigness of the single" and played eight notes deeper. It consisted of two pipes fixed within one thick bass pipe; the shorter pipe's top featured a crooked brass pipe containing a reed.

### Legal and Financial Contexts
In England, the abolition of "days of grace" (three days added to the nominal due date of bills of exchange) was opposed by some. The grounds for this opposition were that removing these days would curtail the credit of small traders who relied on bills drawn at fixed periods of currency.
