# Bolton

Bolton (also known as Bolton-le-Moors) is a municipal, county, and parliamentary borough located in Lancashire, England. Situated 196 miles northwest of London and 11 miles northwest of Manchester, the town is divided into Great and Little Bolton by the Croal, a small tributary of the river Irwell. The area is characterized by surrounding high moorland and covers 15,279 acres. As of 1901, the population was 168,215, having risen from 146,487 in 1891.

## Industrial Development
Bolton established a reputation for woollen manufactures in its early days. During the reign of Richard I, the town's status as a centre for the woollen cloth trade was evidenced by the appointment of an aulneger to stamp and measure bundles of woollen goods. The industry grew further in 1337 following the settlement of Flemish clothiers. By 1566, an act of parliament appointed deputies to assist aulnegers in Bolton. In 1558, Leland's *Itinerary* noted that the town produced "cottons," though these were actually woollen goods.

The manufacture of actual cotton goods did not begin in Lancashire until 1641, at which time Bolton was the primary seat for the production of dimities, vermilions, and fustians. This industry was later stimulated by the arrival of French refugees following the revocation of the edict of Nantes. Further innovations occurred in the 18th century: Jeremiah Clarke produced velvets around 1756, and cotton quiltings and muslins were made in 1763.

The cotton trade experienced an "astonishing impetus" due to the inventions of Samuel Crompton (1780) and Sir Richard Arkwright (1770), both of whom were born in the parish. The first spinning factory in Bolton was reportedly established in 1780. By 1851, the town housed 66 cotton mills employing 860,000 throstle spindles. The town's damp climate specifically assisted its specialty in fine spinning. Other significant industries include the making of spinning and weaving machinery, iron and steel works, collieries, and the printing, dyeing, and bleaching of calico and cotton; the latter cognate industry of bleaching has been active since the early 18th century.

## Infrastructure and Geography
The town is served by the Lancashire & Yorkshire and London & North-Western railways, with the Midland railway also having running powers. In 1791, a canal was constructed connecting Bolton to Manchester. Additionally, an act of parliament in 1792 led to the enclosure of Bolton Moor.

## Civic and Cultural Institutions
While the town's general appearance is modern, it retains two 16th-century houses in Little Bolton, including one notable example of Tudor architecture. The site of the church of St Peter has hosted a parish church since at least the 12th century, though the current building was constructed in 1870.

Bolton's civic amenities include:
* A town hall featuring a tower and a fine classical facade.
* A market hall and an exchange.
* Museums dedicated to art, industry, and natural history.
* Assembly rooms and various benevolent institutions.
* Several free libraries.

Education is provided via municipal technical schools and Lever's grammar school, which was founded in 1641. Former masters of the grammar school include John Lempriere, author of the classical dictionary, and Robert Ainsworth, the Latin lexicographer.

The town also features various pleasure grounds and a large public park opened in 1866. This park was created as a relief work for unemployed operatives during the cotton famine of the early 1860s. To the northwest, another public park encompasses Rivington Pike, which reaches an elevation of 1,192 feet.

## Governance
Bolton was created as a parliamentary borough in 1832, returning two members.
