# Austria, Styria, and Carinthia

The territories of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia, along with Carniola, comprise a region that was known to the ancients as Noricum and Pannonia. These lands, situated between the Save, the Danube, and the Inn, eventually became the patrimony of a single family and the center of Austrian power.

## The Babenberg Era and Transition
Under the Babenberg dynasty, Austrian policy was characterized by loyalty to the emperors and ongoing warfare with eastern neighbors, specifically the Hungarians. This conflict resulted in a gradual eastward advance for Austria. However, this loyalty wavered under Margrave Leopold II and Duke Frederick II. The latter, known as "the Quarrelsome," was placed under the ban by Emperor Frederick II in 1236 after refusing to appear before the princes. The emperor declared the duchies of Austria and Styria vacant and made Vienna an imperial city. Following the death of Duke Frederick in battle against the Hungarians in June 1246, the Babenberg family became extinct.

Following this extinction, the duchies of Austria and Styria were claimed as vacant imperial fiefs. Government was briefly entrusted to Otto II, Duke of Bavaria, and later to Hermann VI, Margrave of Baden. Due to a lack of order and the deaths of both Hermann and the emperor in 1250, the estates of Austria met at Truebensee in 1251 and chose Ottakar, son of Wenceslaus I, King of Bohemia, as their duke. Ottakar strengthened his position by marrying Margaret, sister of Duke Frederick II. He eventually secured Styria after a war with Bela IV of Hungary, receiving recognition as duke of Styria in March 1261. In 1269, Ottakar inherited the duchy of Carinthia upon the death of Duke Ulrich III. During his rule, Ottakar restored order, benefited town inhabitants, and introduced German colonists into eastern districts.

## The Rise of the Habsburgs
In 1273, Rudolph, Count of Habsburg, became German king. He questioned the Bohemian king's title to the three duchies (Austria, Styria, and Carinthia) and sought to recover imperial lands. After Ottakar was placed under the ban in July 1275, war ensued. In November 1276, Ottakar submitted to Rudolph and renounced the three duchies.

Rudolph initially administered the duchies as head of the Empire but sought to make them hereditary possessions for his family, moving the Habsburg headquarters from the Rhine to the Danube. On December 27, 1282, Rudolph invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Austria and Styria. He retained Carinthia until 1286, when he bestowed it upon Meinhard IV, Count of Tirol. 

Government was primarily undertaken by Albert, who is described as an arbitrary ruler; in 1288, he suppressed a popular rising in Vienna. Because the principle of primogeniture was unknown in the House of Habsburg for many years, the duchies were often ruled in common by two or three family members.

## Territorial Divisions and Governance
The 14th and 15th centuries saw frequent divisions of the lands among the Habsburgs:
* **1379:** Leopold III and Albert III divided the lands; Albert kept Austria proper and Carniola, while Leopold received Styria, Carinthia, and Tirol.
* **1406:** Ernest and Frederick divided their lands, with Ernest becoming duke of Styria and Carinthia, and Frederick becoming count of Tirol.
* **1519:** Following the death of Emperor Maximilian, the dominions included Upper and Lower Austria, Styria, Carinthia, Carniola, and the county of Tirol. These were eventually divided among the sons of Ferdinand: Maximilian received Austria proper, Ferdinand received Tirol, and Charles received Styria, Carinthia, and Carniola.

## Religious and Political Shifts
By the mid-16th century, under Emperor Maximilian II, much of Austria and nearly all of Styria were mainly Lutheran, while Catholicism was largely confined to the mountains of Tirol. This changed with the accession of Rudolph II (1576–1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic who encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the Counter-Reformation. Later, Ferdinand II, as archduke of Styria, crushed Protestantism within that duchy.

In later political developments, a struggle emerged between Centralists and Federalists. The "kernel" of the empire—the purely German districts—included Upper and Lower Austria, Salzburg, Vorarlberg, Tirol (except the south), all of Styria except the southern districts, and a large part of Carinthia. While the Liberal party dominant in Lower Austria and Styria favored central institutions, a Conservative and Clerical party supported local institutions.

## Geography of Carniola
Carniola is a mountainous duchy and crown-land of Austria bounded by Carinthia to the north, Styria to the northeast, Croatia to the south and southeast, and Istria, Trieste, and Gorz and Gradisca to the west. It covers 3856 square miles. The region is dominated by the Alps in the north and the Karst (or Carso) Mountains in the south. Notable peaks include the Triglav (9394 ft), the Mangart (8784 ft), and the Jaluz (8708 ft). The Karawankas form the boundary between Carinthia and Carniola, while the Grintovc (8393 ft) sits on the threefold boundary of Carinthia, Carniola, and Styria. The Karst region is noted for subterranean streams and caves, such as those at Adelsberg and Planina.
