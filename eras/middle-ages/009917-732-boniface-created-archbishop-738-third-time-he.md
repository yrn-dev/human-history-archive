# Saint Boniface

Saint Boniface (680–754), known by his birth name Wynfrith, was a missionary and ecclesiastical leader recognized as the apostle of Germany. Born into a good Saxon family in Devonshire, specifically at Kirton or Crediton, he entered the monastic life as a young man. His early education in grammar and theology took place at Exeter and subsequently at Nutcell, located near Winchester, where he studied under the abbot Winberht.

## Early Missionary Work
Boniface distinguished himself as a preacher and scholar. In 716, he followed other Saxon monks to Frisia as a missionary. This initial effort was interrupted, likely due to the hostility of Radbod, the king of the Frisians, who was engaged in war with Charles Martel. Following this, Boniface traveled to Rome at the end of 717. In 719, Pope Gregory II commissioned him to evangelize Germany and counteract the influence of Irish monks in the region.

After crossing the Alps, Boniface visited Thuringia and Bavaria. Upon learning of King Radbod's death, he returned to Frisia to preach for three years under the guidance of Willibrord, the first bishop of Utrecht. Around 722, he visited Thuringia and Hesse, where he converted and baptized many heathens and won over several chieftains. Following reports of his success, he was summoned to Rome and consecrated as a bishop on November 30, 722, after swearing an oath of obedience to the pope.

## Ecclesiastical Organization and Reforms
Boniface returned to the field with letters of recommendation to Charles Martel, tasked with both the conversion of heathens and the suppression of heresy. His career was significantly aided by the protection of Charles Martel. In heathen Germany, Boniface conducted a systematic crusade that involved overturning idols, founding monasteries and churches, and baptizing converts. He recruited a group of missionary helpers from England, including monks and nuns such as St. Lioba, St. Thecla, St. Walburge, St. Gregory (abbot at Utrecht), St. Burchard (bishop of Wurzburg), and St. Lull, who would later succeed him in the see at Mainz.

In 732, Boniface was created archbishop. In 738, he traveled to Rome for the third time. Upon his return, he organized the church in Bavaria into four bishoprics: Passau, Salzburg, Freising, and Regensburg. His authority expanded further in 741 when Pope Zacharias appointed him legate and charged him with reforming the entire Frankish church. Supported by Pippin and Carloman—the mayors of the palace who succeeded Charles Martel—Boniface organized the east Frankish church into four bishoprics: Eichstadt, Buraburg, Wurzburg, and Erfurt, placing his own monks in charge of them.

## Councils and Monasticism
In 742, Boniface presided over what is generally considered the first German council. During this period, he established the abbey of Fulda to serve as a center for German monastic culture. He placed the abbey under the leadership of the Bavarian Sturm and implemented a rule that was stricter than the Benedictine rule.

## Theological and Disciplinary Conflicts
Boniface encountered significant opposition in both Germany and France. In Germany, he entered a disciplinary and theological controversy with Virgil, the Irish bishop of Salzburg. Virgil held the heretical belief that worlds other than our own existed. Despite Virgil's leanings toward science, he held his own against Boniface and was eventually canonized after his death.

In France, Boniface faced a challenge from a Frankish bishop of Neustria named Adalbert (or Aldebert). Adalbert had caused great disturbance by claiming he received relics directly from angels rather than from Rome. By planting crosses in open fields, Adalbert encouraged people to desert their churches and gained a substantial following throughout Neustria.
