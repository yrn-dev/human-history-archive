# The Use of the Monogram in the Frankish Empire

## Evolution of Legal Signatures
In the early centuries of the era, Roman practice required signatures and subscriptions for legal documents. This tradition is evidenced in Latin deeds on papyrus found in Egypt, 2nd-century Dacian waxen tablets, and papyrus deeds from Italy, including Ravenna, dating between the 5th and 10th centuries. This practice was similarly maintained within the Frankish empire.

Among the Merovingian kings, those who possessed the ability to write subscribed their great charters and diplomas with their own hands. Similarly, chancellors and other great officers of state provided autograph countersignatures. However, Merovingian kings who were unlettered utilized monograms created from the letters of their names. Over time, these illiterate monograms began to supersede literate subscriptions.

## The Monogram under Charlemagne
The monogram was adopted by Charlemagne and his successors as a recognized symbol of their subscription, serving as their *signum manuale*, or sign manual. This royal practice was subsequently imitated by official personages who adopted monograms and other marks, even in cases where they were capable of writing. Other signs, such as the cross, were also employed as signs manual by those who were illiterate.

The use of the monogram by French monarchs persisted from the reign of Charlemagne until the death of Philip the Fair in 1314. There is significant doubt regarding whether these monarchs actually traced the sign manual themselves. While earlier sovereigns may have drawn one or two strokes—which can be considered their autographs—in later periods, the monogram became entirely the work of the scribe. The practice of using notarial marks in modern times is a survival of this tradition.

## Charlemagne and the Western Empire
Charlemagne became the sole king in 771 and conquered Lombardy in 774. In the year 800, Pope Leo III crowned him Emperor of the West. Charlemagne sought to revive the empire, specifically the Latin portion, which led to a rivalry between the Latin Empire and the Greek Empire in Constantinople. This political tension was mirrored by a religious rivalry between Latin-speaking and Greek-speaking Christianity.

The popes, seeking to separate Rome and Italy from the Byzantine court, translated the empire to Charlemagne. While the name of Charlemagne was sometimes stained by the acrimony of his scribes, the conqueror himself acted with the temper of a statesman. During four visits to the Vatican, he joined in the prayers and processions of the Roman liturgy and knelt before the image of the apostle.

## Expansion of the Faith and Empire
Charlemagne, along with the Merovingian kings and the Othos, extended the dominion of the cross through victories and laws. This contributed to the diffusion of the evangelic light from the neighborhood of the Rhine to the nations of the Baltic, the Vistula, and the Elbe.

Charlemagne died in 814. His empire did not survive his son and successor, Louis the Pious, who died in 843. Following Louis's death, the Carlovingian Empire fell apart into its main constituents. The German-speaking peoples between the Rhine and the Slavs began to develop into Germany, while the Frankish and Latinized Keltic population of Gaul became recognizable as France, though it remained broken into various principalities and dukedoms. A regular succession of Holy Roman Emperors did not return to Western Europe until 962, when a Saxon became emperor.
