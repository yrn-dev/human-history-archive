# Bohemia

## Early History and Ethnic Origins
The region of Bohemia derives its name from the Boii, a Celtic tribe that inhabited the land during the earliest historical period. The Boii, whose capital was Boiohemum, were weakened by constant warfare with neighboring tribes and were eventually subdued around 12 B.C. by the Marcomanni, a Teutonic tribe. The Marcomanni were later expelled by other Teutonic tribes, and the land was eventually conquered by Slavic tribes, most notably the Cechs. While archaeological research indicates Slavic inhabitants existed in Bohemia as early as the beginning of the Christian era, the Cechs became the masters of the country in the 5th century.

Early historical records mention Samo as a ruler who defeated the Franks at Wogatisburg in 630 and defeated the Avars, a Turanian tribe that had held overlordship over the region. Following a period of obscurity lasting approximately 130 years, chroniclers such as Cosmas describe the foundation of a Bohemian principality by Krok and his daughter Libussa, who married a peasant named Premysl. This led to a list of Premyslide princes, though their dates and deeds are considered semi-mythic rather than historical.

## Christianization and Early Medieval Expansion
Christianity first attempted to enter Bohemia from Germany, but these efforts saw little success due to the Bohemians' distrust of their western neighbors. The faith was more successfully introduced via Moravia through the Greek monks Cyrillus and Methodius. Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius around 873, leading to a rapid adoption of Christianity. 

A prominent figure of this era was Wenceslas, surnamed the Holy, who was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized by the Church of Rome. Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II., expanded Bohemian frontiers to include Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and a portion of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. However, this empire was short-lived; Boleslav III. lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland. Internal civil wars between Boleslav III. and his brothers Jaromir and Ulrick eventually led the Bohemians to dethrone Boleslav III. in favor of the Polish prince Vladivoj, the son of Bohemian princess Dubravka.

## Territorial Shifts and the Middle Ages
Bohemia's borders and political ties shifted frequently. Upper Lusatia, occupied by the Milzeni tribe, was connected to Bohemia at various times in the 11th and 12th centuries. Emperor Frederick I granted the district to King Ladislas around 1160, and it was later colonized by German immigrants. Although it passed to the margrave of Brandenburg in 1253, Bohemia recovered Baudissin in 1319 and Görlitz in 1329. By the 14th century, the region was known as the district of the six towns: Bautzen, Görlitz, Zittau, Löbau, Lauban, and Kamenz.

In the 13th century, Bohemian influence extended into Austria and Styria. In 1251, the estates of Austria chose Ottakar, son of Wenceslaus I. of Bohemia, as their duke. Ottakar became king of Bohemia in 1253 and eventually became the duke of Styria following a treaty with Bela IV of Hungary in 1261. By 1269, he inherited the duchy of Carinthia. Ottakar sought to improve these lands by benefiting town inhabitants and introducing German colonists to eastern districts.

## The Hussite Wars and Religious Conflict
The 15th century was marked by intense religious strife following the trial of John Huss and the burning of Jerome of Prague. An insurrection of the Hussites began in 1419. In 1420, Pope Martin V proclaimed a crusade to destroy the Hussites and Wycliffites. Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites—who operated on extreme democratic lines—repulsed several crusades. Sigismund, claiming the Bohemian crown, attempted to conquer Prague in June 1420 but failed after the Taborites defeated his forces at Vitkov hill and the battle of the Vysehrad on November 1, 1420.

By 1431, it became obvious that conquering Bohemia by force was impossible. After the Bohemians defeated the Germans at Domazlice on August 1, 1431, negotiations began at Basel. The Hussites were represented by an embassy led by Prokop the Great. Internal political dissensions later split the Hussites into "the army of the nobles" (aristocratic Hussites) and "the army of the towns" (Taborites). The two clashed at Lipan on May 30, 1434, where the Taborites were defeated and their leaders, including the two Prokops, perished. This victory allowed for reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund. The resulting "Compacts" granted Bohemians the right to preach the gospel freely, the right of communion in both kinds, and limited the clergy's ability to acquire worldly goods.

## The Habsburg Era and the Thirty Years' War
Under Emperor Maximilian II (1564-1576), a liberal policy maintained peace, though various Christian beliefs continued to struggle for mastery. This changed with the accession of the fanatical Catholic Rudolph II (1576-1612), who encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation. By the time of Matthias (emperor 1612-1619), Bohemia had reached a state of practical independence.

Ferdinand II, elected king of Bohemia in 1618, sought to establish the rule of the Jesuits, triggering the Thirty Years' War. In 1619, the Bohemian estates deposed Ferdinand and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as king. However, in September 1620, a large imperialist army comprising forces from Bavaria, Spain, Poland, and several Italian states entered Bohemia. On November 8, 1620, the Bohemians were defeated at the battle of "Bila Hora" (White Hill). Following the defeat, King Frederick fled Prague.

## Natural Resources
Bohemia possesses nearly every useful metal and mineral except salt. Lignite and coal are the most significant; lignite is found around Brux, Dux, Falkenau, Ossegg, and Teplitz, while coal is mined in areas including Kladno, Pilsen, and Radnitz. The region is an important center for the iron industry, with ores found at Nucic and Krusnahora. Other extracted materials include silver (Pribram and Joachimsthal), lead, tin (Graupen), antimony (Milleschau), uranium and radium (Joachimsthal), graphite, and porcelain-earth (Carlsbad). The region also produces copper, sulphur, cobalt, nickel, arsenic, asphalt, peat, and the Bohemian garnet.
