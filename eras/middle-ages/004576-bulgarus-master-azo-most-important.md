# Bulgarus and the School of the Gloss-writers

## Bulgarus of Bologna
Bulgarus was a prominent 12th-century Italian jurist born in Bologna. He is recognized as the most celebrated of the "Four Doctors" of the university's law school, a group that also included Jacobus de Boragine, Hugo de Porta Ravennate, and Martinus Gosia. Due to his eloquence, Bulgarus was frequently called the "Golden Mouth" (_os aureum_) and was regarded as the Chrysostom of the Gloss-writers. He died in 1166 A.D. at a very advanced age.

While popular tradition suggests that all Four Doctors were pupils of Irnerius, the historian Savigny considers this tradition inadmissible for the others, though he notes there is no insuperable difficulty in accepting it regarding Bulgarus.

## Legal Philosophy and Influence
At the law school of Bologna, Bulgarus and Martinus Gosia led two opposing schools of thought, similar to the Sabinians and Proculians of Imperial Rome. Martinus Gosia headed a school that adapted the law to what opponents called the equity of "the purse" (_aequitas bursalis_). In contrast, Bulgarus adhered more strictly to the letter of the law.

The school led by Bulgarus eventually prevailed. Its adherents included Accursius, Azo, and Joannes Bassianus, all of whom later exerted a commanding influence over legal studies at Bologna. Beyond the university, Bulgarus served as one of the most trusted advisers to Emperor Frederick I and played a leading role among the Four Doctors at the diet of Roncaglia in 1158.

## Literary Contributions
The most famous work attributed to Bulgarus is the commentary *De Regulis Juris*. Although it was printed at one time among the writings of Placentius, Cujacius reassigned the work to Bulgarus based on internal evidence, noting that the annexed additions were written by Placentinus. Savigny describes this commentary as a model specimen of the excellence of the method introduced by Irnerius and the earliest extant work of its kind from the school of the Gloss-writers.

## Joannes Bassianus
Joannes Bassianus was a 12th-century Italian jurist and professor at the law school of Bologna. According to Corolus de Tocco, he was a native of Cremona. Bassianus was a pupil of Bulgarus and served as the master of Azo.

He was noted for his ability to invent ingenious forms to explain ideas with precision. His most celebrated work is the "Law-Tree," titled *Arbor Arborum*, which arranges various actions as fruit on the branches of a tree. This system divides actions into two main groups:
* **Civil actions** (_actiones stricti juris_): 48 in number.
* **Equitable or praetorian actions**: 121 in number.

Bassianus further organized these actions under twelve scientific heads using a notation system that allowed students to classify actions quickly. He included a few glosses beside the tree to justify this classification. His *Summary on the Authentica* is also preserved and is regarded by Savigny as one of the most precious works of the Gloss-writers. However, his *Lectures on the Pandects* and the *Code*, collected by his pupil Nicolaus Furiosus, have been lost.

## Azo
Azo (c. 1150–1230), also referred to as Azzo, Azzolenus, or Azo Soldanus (after his father's surname), was an Italian jurist born in Bologna. He must be distinguished from two other Bologna professors of the same name: the 13th-century canonist Azo Lambertaccius and the 14th-century canonist Azo de Ramenghis.

A pupil of Joannes Bassianus, Azo became a professor of civil law in his native city and was active in the municipal life of Bologna during its period of municipal independence. He held a highly important position among the glossators. His *Readings on the Code*—collected by his pupil Alessandro de Santo Aegidio and completed by Odofredus and Hugolinus—provided a methodical exposition of Roman law. These writings carried such weight in the tribunals that the saying "Chi non ha Azzo, non vada a palazzo" emerged. Azo's pupils included Jacobus Balduinus and Accursius. He died around 1230.
