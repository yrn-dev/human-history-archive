# BANNERET

The term banneret (derived from the French *banneret* and *banniere*, meaning banner) refers to a rank of nobility within the system of feudalism. A banneret was a noble who possessed the right to lead their vassals to battle under their own private banner. In the feudal hierarchy, bannerets occupied a position between barons and knights bachelors.

## Nature and Rank
In the medieval period, the distinction between nobility and gentry was not recognized as it is today; a nobleman or gentleman was defined as a man entitled to armorial bearings based on certain conditions of descent. In France and Spain, Du Cange categorized the nobility into three classes: the *ricos hombres* or barons, the *caballeros* or *chevaliers*, and the *infanzons* or *écuyers*. The designation of banneret and the right to lead followers under a square flag (*drapeau quarré*) were limited to the first class, the greater nobility.

In England, the term was occasionally used as an equivalent to baron, as evidenced by parliament rolls. In Scotland, lords of parliament were created both barons and bannerets during their investiture. This ceremony included the display of a quadrilateral banner, which a retainer would carry before the "barones majores" into the field. In both England and Scotland, the term was sometimes corrupted to "baronet" or "bannerent." This linguistic corruption may have suggested the title for the later order of Baronets created by King James I, though Selden argues that the original rank of banneret has no actual relation to that later title.

## Military Role and Command
Within the structure of a European army during the 14th and 15th centuries, the army was divided into various divisions. While the sovereign's chief commands were held by the constable and marshals, smaller divisions were placed under the banners of knights banneret or the greater nobility. Even smaller units were led by knights bachelors under their pennons.

The banner of a banneret signified a more or less extensive command. While every knight was entitled to a pennon and every squire to a pencel, the banneret's flag was specifically square. In contrast, the pennon was pointed or forked, and the pencel ended in a single streamer. By the reign of Edward III, bannerets appeared as commanders of military forces they raised themselves, with their status and relations to the crown and followers based primarily on voluntary contract rather than feudal tenure. Bannerets received wages based on the number of esquires and knights bachelors who served under them.

## Creation and Ceremony
The title of knight banneret was granted for distinguished service in the field. According to English custom, a banneret could only be created in the field when the king was present or the royal standard was displayed. The ceremony involved the solemn delivery of a banner charged with the candidate's arms. If the candidate already possessed a streamer or pennon, the end was cut off to make it square, transforming it into a banner. A knight bachelor seeking promotion would apply to the commander in the field; if granted, heralds (or the commander himself) would publicly cut the tails from the knight's pennon.

The earliest contemporary mentions of knights banneret appear in France during the reign of Philip Augustus and in England during the reign of Edward I. Records from 1282 list Sir Philip Daubeney, Sir Ralph Daubeney, and Sir Alan Plokenet as bannerets in the garrison of Caermarthen Castle. Additionally, the roll of Carlaverock notes eighty-five bannerets who accompanied Edward I in his 1300 expedition to Scotland.

## Decline and Later Instances
The use of the title declined over time. By the end of the 16th century, Sir Thomas Smith noted that the order had almost grown out of use in England. During the 17th century, it was alleged before the privy council that there were no longer any bannerets in existence.

There are conflicting accounts regarding the final conferral of the dignity. Some sources suggest the last occasion was in 1547, when the Lord Protector Somerset created Sir Ralph Sadler, Sir Francis Bryan, and Sir Ralph Fane as bannerets after the battle of Pinkie. Other accounts state that Charles I created John Smith a banneret at the battle of Edgehill in 1642 for rescuing the royal standard. However, some historians argue there is insufficient proof for the latter. Claims that George III created naval officers bannerets in the late 18th century are regarded as errors.

## Other Uses of the Term
In the context of the Tokugawa samurai in Japan, the term "bannerets" is used to describe the *hatamoto*. These individuals were direct vassals of the shogun. While they held a higher status than the *go-kenin* (who made up the bulk of the samurai), they were lower in rank than the *fudai daimyo*.
