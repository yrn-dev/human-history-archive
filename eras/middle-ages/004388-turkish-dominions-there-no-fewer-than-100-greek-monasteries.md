# Greek Monachism and the Turkish Dominions

## Overview of Greek Monachism
Greek monachism is characterized by a variety of organizational structures and lifestyles. Monasteries are categorized into three primary types: *cenobia* proper, which observe a full monastic common life including personal poverty; *idiorrhythmic* monasteries, where monks are permitted the use of private means and lead a more free and mitigated life; and *lauras*, which are semi-eremitical in nature. Monks in both the Greek and Slavonic traditions wear a black habit.

The monastic life is primarily dedicated to devotional contemplative exercises. Church services are noted for being of extreme length, while manual labor has almost entirely disappeared and intellectual study is little cultivated. On Mount Athos, there are many hermits.

## Legal and Historical Development
The legal framework of Greek and Russian monasticism is based on the Rules of Basil, the canons of the Councils, and the Constitutions of Theodore the Studite. While St. Basil's Rules provided the foundation, the second half of the 8th century saw a period of general decadence. Around the year 800, Theodore became the abbot of the Studium monastery in Constantinople. He sought to restore the primitive vigor of St. Basil's spirit by creating a more practical code of laws to regulate daily life. These constitutions spread from the Studium to other monasteries throughout the Greek empire.

## Distribution and Influence
Greek monachism had a significant impact beyond Greece, particularly through the importation of Basilian monachism into Russia, where it became the norm for all Slavonic lands. The first Russian monastery was established at Kiev around 1050 by a monk from Mount Athos. In the Russian empire, there are approximately 400 monasteries for men and 100 for women, many of which operate schools, almshouses, and hospitals.

In addition to Russia and Greece, there are a considerable number of monasteries in other Slavonic lands. Within the Turkish dominions, there are no fewer than 100 Greek monasteries. 

Historically, Basilian monachism also spread to Italy. Greek monasteries existed in Rome, south Italy, and Sicily as early as the 6th and 7th centuries. Between the 8th and 10th centuries, invasions by Arabs, Saracens, and others drove fugitives from Greece and Sicily into southern Italy. By the middle of the 9th century, Greek-speaking Basilian monasteries were established in great numbers in Calabria and spread as far north as Rome. Most of these ceased to exist by the 18th century, with the exception of the monastery founded by St. Nilus around 1000 at Grottaferrata.

## The Orthodox Church in Turkish Dominions
The majority of the Christian population in the Balkan lands subject to Turkish rule belongs to the Orthodox Church. While the oecumenical patriarch at Constantinople is the nominal head and holds precedence over other ecclesiastical dignitaries, the Greek, Montenegrin, Servian, and Bulgarian churches are actually autocephalous. 

Under Turkish government rule, the heads of religious communities serve as the only recognized representatives of the Christian population. These leaders supervise Christian schools and hold seats on local administrative councils. While different branches of the Orthodox Church have sought separate organizations within the Turkish dominions, these efforts are driven by political motives rather than differences in dogma.

## Scholarly Interest and Documentation
In modern times, Western scholars have visited Greek monasteries to search for manuscripts, specifically focusing on Mount Athos and St. Catherine's on Mount Sinai. These expeditions have produced descriptions of contemporary monastic life. Early accounts include Robert Curzon's *Monasteries of the Levant* (published in parts in 1834 and 1837), and more recent English accounts include Athelstan Riley's *Athos* (1887).
