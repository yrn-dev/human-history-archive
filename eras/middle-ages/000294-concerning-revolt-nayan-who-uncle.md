# The Revolt of Nayan

The revolt of Nayan was a significant rebellion against the authority of the Great Kaan Cublay (Kublai). Nayan was a Tartar Chief and a prince of the imperial family who held a vast domain on the borders of Manchuria. While described in some accounts as the uncle of Cublay Kaan, other records clarify that he was a cousin in a junior generation; specifically, he was the great-great-grandson of Uchegin (also called Pilgutai), who was the brother of Chinghiz.

## Rise of the Rebellion
Nayan was a lord over many provinces and had expanded his inherited territories, becoming very powerful. His possessions stretched south to Kwang-ning, from which he took the title of prince, and included territories on the rivers Liao, Tiro (or Torro), and Kweilei. In some records, his appanage is associated with the area of Abalahu, a region rich in fish near the Amur River.

Despite being a liegeman to his nephew, the Great Kaan, Nayan became proud due to his youth and power. He commanded a force of 300,000 horsemen and sought to wrest the empire from Cublay. To achieve this, Nayan formed an alliance with another Tartar Prince named Caidu. Caidu was also a kinsman and nephew of the Great Kaan, but he was already in a state of bitter enmity and rebellion against his sovereign. Nayan proposed that they attack Cublay from two sides simultaneously. Caidu agreed and mustered a host of 100,000 horsemen to support the effort.

## Military Campaign and Battle
Cublay Kaan became disquieted by rumors of the conspiracy and dispatched his lieutenant, Bayan, to reconnoitre. Although Bayan narrowly escaped capture, he returned to court to report the threat. In 1287, Nayan marched from his *ordo* with 60,000 men through Eastern Mongolia. In response, Cublay Kaan marched against him from Shangtu.

The ensuing battle took place in South-Eastern Mongolia in the vicinity of the river Liao. During the campaign, the Emperor advanced with a small force and faced a reconnaissance by Nayan’s General, who led 100,000 men. Despite the danger, the Emperor remained resolute. Under the cover of night, reinforcements arrived, with horsemen carrying foot soldiers on their cruppers. 

The tide of the battle turned when a commander named Liting used ten men to discharge a Fire-*Pao*, the report of which caused panic among Nayan’s ill-disciplined troops. The Emperor's forces—consisting of Chinese troops led by Liting, Mongols led by Yusitemur, and the Emperor's own guards and *Kincha* (Kipchak) troops led by Tutuha and the Kaan himself—attacked from all sides. Nayan's forces were completely defeated.

## Aftermath and Execution
Following the defeat, Nayan attempted to flee to the south-east across a mountain range. However, forces had been stationed beforehand at Kuang-ning and Shin-chow (modern Mukden) to watch the pass, and Nayan was taken prisoner. He was subsequently put to death.

The suppression of the rebellions associated with Nayan and Hatan lasted four years. Following Nayan's capture, a further uprising occurred in the summer of 1288. Hatan, a grandson of Chinghiz's brother Kajyun (Hachiun) and a member of Nayan's league, threatened provinces north-east of the wall. Cublay sent his grandson and designated heir, Teimur, who defeated the rebels after a two-day fight on the banks of the River Kweilei.

## Religious Context
Following the conquest, a dispute arose among the various peoples present—including Saracens, Idolaters, and Jews—who mocked Christians because Nayan had borne a cross on his standard. The critics argued that the cross had provided no help to Nayan. 

Cublay Kaan sharply rebuked those mocking the Christians. He stated that if the cross had rendered no help to Nayan, it had "done right well," because Nayan was a "disloyal and traitorous Rebel" who deserved his fate. The Christians responded that their cross could not aid anyone in wrongdoing or disloyalty.
