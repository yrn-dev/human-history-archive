# The Taxation of the Bagimond's Roll Fragment

## Overview of Bagimond's Roll
In 1274, the council of Lyons established a tax consisting of a tenth part of all church revenues, to be collected over the following six years for the relief of the Holy Land. In Scotland, the responsibility for collecting this tax was entrusted by Pope Gregory X to Master Boiamund de Vitia (commonly known as Bagimund), a canon of Asti. 

Bagimund sought to innovate the assessment process by basing the tax on the true value of the benefices at the time of assessment, rather than relying on old conventional valuations. This proposal met with objection from the Scottish clergy. In August 1275, the clergy held a council at Perth and successfully persuaded Bagimund to return to Rome to convince the pope to revert to the older method of taxation. However, the pope insisted that the tax be collected according to the true value, and Bagimund subsequently returned to Scotland to oversee the collection.

## The Durham Fragment and Its Actual Taxation
A fragment of Bagimond's Roll, preserved in a form very similar to its original, is kept at Durham and was printed by James Raine in *Priory of Coldingham*. This specific fragment contains two columns: one listing the real values of the benefices in the archdeaconry of Lothian and another listing the tenth parts.

Although the roll was created by Bagimund, the actual taxation referenced in this specific fragment was not the tenth collected by Boiamund. Instead, it refers to a tenth of all ecclesiastical property in Ireland, Wales, Scotland, and England that was granted to Edward I of England by Pope Nicholas IV in the year 1288. Because of this, the fragment is considered supplementary to the *Taxatio Ecclesiastica Angliae et Walliae*, which was printed by the Record Commissioners in 1802.

## Legacy and Documentation
While no contemporary copy of Bagimond's Roll is known to exist, the original roll served as the basis for ecclesiastical taxation for several centuries. Specifically, at least three documents from the 16th century provide details regarding the taxation of the Church of Scotland based upon the original roll.

## Related Historical Contexts of Tenths and Taxation
The use of "tenths" as a form of taxation appears in various other historical and legal contexts:

*   **Ecclesiastical Tithes:** In the Middle Ages, the church functioned as a "state within the state," possessing its own law courts and claiming jurisdiction over matters such as marriages, wills, and heresy. The church demanded a tax of a tenth, known as the tithe, from its subjects as a right rather than a pious benefaction. Additionally, the church claimed the right to levy a one-tenth tax on the property of laymen, while the clergy themselves claimed exemption from lay taxation, paying their taxes to Rome instead.
*   **Roman Imperial Taxation:** Under the Roman Empire, Augustus introduced a twentieth tax on legacies and inheritances. This was later increased by Caracalla, who exacted a tenth of all inheritances and legacies, a measure that crushed the empire under its weight until the original proportion was restored after his death.
*   **Legal Taxation:** In the High Court, the "taxing" of costs refers to the process of determining the costs of litigation in the central office. This is conducted via prescribed scales (either lower or higher, depending on court orders) under an elaborate set of regulations. Clients may request an order for taxation within a month of receiving a solicitor's bill.
