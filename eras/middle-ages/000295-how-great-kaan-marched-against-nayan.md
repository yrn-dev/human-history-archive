# The Campaign of the Great Kaan Against Nayan

## The Rebellion of Nayan
Nayan was a Tartar Chief and a young man of approximately thirty years who served as the uncle to the Emperor Cublay Kaan. As the Lord over many provinces—specifically Chorcha, Cauly, Barscol, and Sikintinju—Nayan possessed a vast dominion and the ability to field 300,000 horsemen. Despite being a liegeman to the Great Kaan, Nayan became proud due to his youth and power, eventually deciding to cease being a vassal and attempting to wrest the empire from his nephew.

To achieve this, Nayan contacted another Tartar Prince named Caidu. Caidu was a kinsman of Nayan and a nephew of the Great Kaan; although he was also a lawful liegeman, he was already in rebellion and at bitter enmity with his sovereign. Nayan proposed a coordinated attack, suggesting that by attacking Cublay from two sides simultaneously, they could seize his dominion. Caidu agreed to the plan and mustered a host of 100,000 horsemen.

## Preparations of the Great Kaan
Upon learning of the conspiracy, Cublay Kaan viewed the rebellion as a serious and perilous affair. While he typically sent his barons or sons to lead campaigns, he insisted on commanding this expedition personally. He vowed that he would never wear his crown again if he did not bring the two disloyal chiefs to an ill end.

The Kaan's preparations were conducted with extreme speed and secrecy, known only to his Privy Council and completed within ten or twelve days. He assembled a force consisting of 360,000 horsemen—described as merely the falconers and whippers-in from the court—and 100,000 footmen. The Kaan noted that this was a small force, as his other vast armies were engaged in distant expeditions. Before marching, the Kaan consulted his astrologers, who predicted a glorious victory.

## The March and Surprise Attack
The Great Kaan marched his army for 20 days to reach a great plain. The distance was normally thirty marches from the Court, but the Kaan covered it in twenty due to his eagerness for battle. To maintain total secrecy, he ordered strict watches for scouts, ensuring that anyone appearing in the vicinity was instantly captured.

Because of these precautions, Nayan remained entirely unaware of the Kaan's approach. Nayan lay in his tent in a remote wilderness, feeling so secure that he kept no vedettes in the front or rear. At the moment the Great Kaan's army arrived, Nayan was asleep in the arms of a wife for whom he was extravagantly fond.

## The Battle
The conflict is described as one of the most fierce and fearful battles of the era. The total forces engaged included no fewer than 760,000 horsemen, in addition to numerous footmen. The Great Kaan positioned his host upon a hill overlooking the plain where Nayan was stationed.

The battle lasted from morning until noon. The Great Kaan's army performed such feats of arms that Nayan's forces were eventually routed and fled. Nayan and his barons were taken prisoner and forced to surrender all their arms. Nayan was a baptized Christian who bore the cross on his banner, though this provided him no protection against the consequences of his rebellion.

## Aftermath and Execution
Following the victory, a dispute arose among the gathered people, including Saracens, Idolaters, and Jews, who mocked the Christians because the cross on Nayan's standard had not saved him. The Great Kaan sharply rebuked those mocking the Christians, stating that the Cross had done right by not helping Nayan, as Nayan was a disloyal and traitorous rebel who deserved his fate.

The Great Kaan ordered that Nayan be put to death immediately and in secret to prevent any appeals for pity based on their kinship. To avoid spilling the blood of the Imperial Line upon the ground or exposing it to the Sun and Heaven, the Kaan had Nayan wrapped in a carpet and tossed to and fro until he died.

Following the execution, the barons and people of the four provinces (Chorcha, Cauly, Barscol, and Sikintinju) renewed their fealty to the Great Kaan. The other rebel Lord, Caidu, was greatly troubled by Nayan's defeat and death; while he remained ready for war, he feared he might be handled in the same manner as Nayan. The Great Kaan then returned to his capital city of Cambaluc to take his ease and hold festivities.
