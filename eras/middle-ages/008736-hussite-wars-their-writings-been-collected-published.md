# Hussite Wars

The Hussite wars were a series of religious conflicts in Bohemia that began in 1419 following an insurrection of the Hussites. These wars marked the beginning of the breaking-up of Christendom and were precipitated by events including the trial of John Huss and the subsequent burning of his colleague, Jerome of Prague.

## Crusades and Military Conflict
In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade intended to destroy the Hussites, Wycliffites, and all other heretics in Bohemia. This invitation attracted unemployed soldiers of fortune and "drifting blackguardism" from across Europe. Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites conducted their affairs on extreme democratic lines and were fueled by enthusiasm. Despite the crusaders' attempts to beleaguer Prague, they failed to take the city and suffered a series of reverses that led to their retreat.

A second crusade in 1421, as well as two subsequent crusades, also failed. However, internal dissensions eventually fractured the Hussite movement. This instability encouraged a fifth crusade in 1431, led by Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg. During this period, the Germans, influenced by Sigismund, attempted to subdue Bohemia by force but were defeated by the Bohemians at Domazlice (Taus) on August 1, 1431.

## Internal Divisions and the Battle of Lipan
The Hussite movement was split between moderate and extreme factions. The extreme party, known as the Taborites, drifted toward a primitive communism—a tendency rooted in doctrines of equal human brotherhood—which alarmed influential and wealthier Czech noblemen. The more moderate Hussites were known as the Calixtines or Utraquists.

By 1434, this tension escalated into civil war. Two opposing forces emerged: "the army of the nobles," consisting of aristocratic Hussites, and "the army of the towns," composed of Taborites. These forces clashed at Lipan, near Kolin, on May 30, 1434. The Taborites were defeated, and their leaders, including the two Prokops, perished on the battlefield.

## Reconciliation and the Compacts
The victory of the moderate party enabled a reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund. Negotiations began at Basel in 1431, with the Hussites represented by an embassy led by Prokop the Great. Following the defeat of the Taborites, the Bohemians recognized Sigismund as their sovereign.

On July 5, 1436, a treaty was signed at Iglau. The resulting agreements, known as the Compacts, granted the Bohemians several concessions: the right to preach the gospel freely, the right of communion in both kinds, and certain limitations on the clergy's ability to acquire worldly goods. These distinctions from general Catholic practice remained in effect until the German Reformation in the sixteenth century.

## Literature and Historical Records
The Hussite period was characterized by theological strife and a preference for the national language over Latin, as the movement was largely democratic. However, much of the literature from this era was lost due to the systematic destruction of Bohemian writings by the Jesuits after the battle of the White Hill in 1620. The writings of the extreme Taborite party were entirely destroyed.

Preserved works from the moderate Hussites include *Of the Great Torment of the Holy Church* and the *Lives of the Priests of Tabor*, both of which were hostile toward the Taborite community. Archbishop John of Rokycan also wrote a *Postilla*, a work similar to, though inferior to, the *Postilla* of Huss.

Another significant figure of this period was Peter Chelcicky (born late 14th century, died 1460). An independent writer who avoided joining any Hussite party, Chelcicky was a strenuous enemy of the Church of Rome but advocated for the unresisting acceptance of all decrees of worldly authorities and expressed a horror of bloodshed. His masterpiece is the *Sit viry* (the net of faith); he also wrote a *Postilla* and polemical letters to Bishop Nicolas of Pelhrimov and Archbishop John of Rokycan.

While the period is poor in historical works written in the national language, the writings of contemporary chroniclers and eye-witnesses have been collected and published by Frantisek Palacky under the title *Stare ceske letopisy*.
