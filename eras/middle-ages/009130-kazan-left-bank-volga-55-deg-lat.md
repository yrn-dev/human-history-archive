# Bolgari

Bolgari (also referred to as Bolghar or Bolgara) is a ruined town located in the government of Kazan, situated 4 miles from the left bank of the Volga at 55 deg. N. lat. (alternatively cited as latitude 54° 54′). The site is located in the district of Spask, province of Kazan, approximately 83 to 90 miles from Kazan, where the village of Uspenskoye (also called Bolgarskoye) now stands.

## Historical Significance and Governance
Bolgari is generally considered to have been the capital of the Bulgarians, who were established in that part of Europe from the 5th to the 15th century. The Bulgars of the Volga were of Turkish origin and may have assimilated Slavonian and Finnish elements. After some Bulgars removed to the Balkans and others were subjugated by the Khazars on the shores of the Azov Sea, they marched northwards to the Kama and Volga at the beginning of the 9th century to establish the kingdom of Great Bulgaria.

The city served as the capital of this region, and some sources suggest it may have been the capital of the Golden Horde before Sarai. In the second half of the 15th century, Bolgari became part of the principality of Kazan and eventually became part of Russia. In 1438, Ulugh Mohammed—the cousin of Toka Timur and younger son of Juji—transformed the country into the khanate of Kazan, which lasted until 1552.

## Trade and Economy
Arab writers viewed Bolghar as nearly the limit of the habitable world, noting its brief summer nights and extreme cold. The city was a center for trade with the Russians, Khazars, India, and Persia, as well as Biarmia in the Urals. Staple articles of trade included:
* Peltry (furs) and Russia leather (from which the latter derived its name)
* Wax, honey, and hazel-nuts
* Fossil ivory found in the vicinity

The inhabitants engaged in cattle breeding and agriculture. By the end of the 10th century, the Arab geographer Ibn Haukal stated the town had 10,000 inhabitants.

## Religious and Cultural Development
The Bulgars were converted to Islam in 922. Following this conversion, they began constructing forts. Early settlements consisted of wooden houses, but Arabian architects soon introduced stone houses. The city was visited by various curious travelers, including Ibn Batuta, who came to witness the phenomena of the short summer nights.

## Destruction and Archaeological Remains
The city suffered multiple destructions:
* It was invaded by the Mongols under Subutai in 1223 and conquered in 1236.
* The capital was destroyed by the Mongols in 1237 or 1238.
* It was destroyed again by Tamerlane (Timur) in 1391.

The ruins were practically discovered during the reign of Peter the Great and were later described by Humboldt and Pallas. Surviving remains include old towers and walls, as well as numerous *kurgans* (burial-mounds). These tombs contained utensils, implements, weapons, and copper and silver coins. 

Inscriptions found at the site are written in Turkic, Armenian (dated 557, 984, and 986), and Arabic (dated 1222-1341). The Arabic inscriptions include both ordinary Arabic and Kufic (an epigraphic style). Antiquities collected from the site in 1722 are currently preserved in museums in St Petersburg, Moscow, and Kazan.

## Geography
Bolghar was originally located on the bank of the Volga. However, because the Volga and the Kama rivers have since changed their courses, it is possible that the city was originally built at the junction of these two rivers.
