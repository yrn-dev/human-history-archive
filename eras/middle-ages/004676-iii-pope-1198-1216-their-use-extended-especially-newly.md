# Pope Innocent III

Pope Innocent III, who served from 1198 to 1216, was one of the most vigorous and aggressive leaders of the papacy. He ascended to the papal throne at an exceptionally young age, becoming Pope before he was thirty-eight (some sources state thirty-seven). His reign represented a period where the Pope of Rome came closer to being the monarch of a united Christendom than at any other time in history.

## Political Supremacy and Influence
Under the leadership of Innocent III and the five Popes who followed him, the papacy exerted immense influence across a vast geography. The Pope was supreme from Norway to Sicily, from Ireland to Bulgaria, and as far as Jerusalem. This period of supremacy was aided by the fact that Constantinople was in Latin hands and the empire was weakened by internal dissensions.

However, this supremacy was more apparent than real. While the power of faith had been strong during the time of Pope Urban, by the era of Innocent III, the papacy had lost its hold on the hearts of princes. Furthermore, the faith and conscience of the common people were turning against a church that had become merely aggressive and political.

## Relationship with Emperor Frederick II
A defining feature of Innocent III's papacy was his conflict with Emperor Frederick II. Frederick, the son of Emperor Henry VI and grandson of Frederick Barbarossa, inherited the kingdom of Sicily in 1198 at the age of four. Following the death of Frederick's mother, who served as his guardian for six months, Innocent III became the child's regent and guardian.

Frederick grew up in a Sicilian court that was half oriental and staffed by highly educated Arabs. This mixed education provided him with both a Moslem view of Christianity and a Christian view of Islam. The result was a cynical perspective, exceptional for the Age of Faith, in which Frederick believed all religions were impostures. His recorded blasphemies and heresies grew as he matured under the arrogant rule of Innocent III.

The conflict between the two intensified as Frederick sought the imperial crown of Germany. Innocent III, seeking to prevent the coalescence of power in Italy and Germany, intervened with strict conditions for Frederick's election. To secure the Pope's support, Frederick promised to:
* Put down heresy in Germany with a strong hand.
* Relinquish his possessions in South Italy and Sicily.
* Ensure the German clergy were freed from taxation and lay jurisdiction.

Despite these vows, Frederick had no intention of keeping his word. He remained in Sicily, which he preferred over Germany, and failed to fulfill his promises to the Pope. Innocent III also urged Frederick to crusade against the Moslim to recover Jerusalem, but Frederick remained slack in his performance. Innocent III died in 1216, baffled by his ward.

## Religious Policy and Crusades
Innocent III is described as one of the great persecuting Popes—an able, grasping, and aggressive man. He preached a cruel and bloody crusade against the Waldenses and heretics in the south of France, an operation that eventually evolved into a looting expedition that escaped his control. He demanded similar exemplary cruelties be practiced upon heretics in Germany.

## Succession and Legacy
Innocent III was succeeded in 1216 by Honorius III, a pious and highly educated Roman. Honorius III attempted to carry out the decrees of the Lateran Council of 1215 against the Albigenses and continued the crusade proclaimed by Innocent III. While Honorius III managed to make peace with Frederick II—crowning him and his wife Constance in St. Peter's in 1220—Frederick continued to refuse to interrupt his Sicilian reforms to go on the crusade.

The struggle between the papacy and Frederick II eventually reached a turning point. Although Rome ultimately defeated Frederick and destroyed his dynasty, the conflict left the prestige of the Pope and the church so badly wounded that the resulting decay festered. This conflict eventually led to a clear historical statement regarding the pretensions of the Pope to be the absolute ruler of all Christendom versus the claims of secular rulers.
