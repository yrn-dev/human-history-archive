# The Evolution of the Term "Bond" and its Relation to Serfdom

The term "bond" possesses a complex linguistic history, characterized by a phonetic confusion between two distinct roots that led to its association with the "villein" and the "serf." In its legal and social evolution, the word transitioned from describing a householder to denoting a state of servitude and slavery.

## Linguistic Origins and Phonetic Confusion
The association of "bond" with servitude stems from an early confusion between two different words. One "bond," meaning "that which binds," is a phonetic variant of "band" and is derived from the Teutonic root *bindan* (to bind). 

A separate, now obsolete "bond" originally referred to a householder. This version of the word is the Old English *bonda*—a root also found in the word "husband"—and is derived from the verb *bua*, meaning to dwell or to have a house. This root is cognate with the English "boor" and the German *Bauer*, and is related to the Latin *colere*.

The transition in meaning toward the idea of serfdom and slavery occurred because of the "wrong connexion" between these two terms. This linguistic overlap also affected the word "bondage"; while it properly referred to "tenure in villeinage," it became synonymous with "slavery." A remnant of the original meaning of the householder survives in the term "bondager," which generally refers to a servant. In Northumberland and the south of Scotland, a "bondager" specifically denoted a female outworker provided by a married farm-labourer as a condition of his tenancy to address a dearth of field-labour.

## Transition to Villeinage and Serfdom
In the laws of Canute, the term "bond" (in the sense of a householder) was used as an equivalent to the Old English *ceorl*. Following the Norman Conquest, the social position of the churl became less free. Consequently, the "bond" approximated the status of the "villein," and eventually that of the "serf."

A specific type of villein tenure is known as "bordage" (from the Latin *borda*, meaning a cottage). A "bordar" (Medieval Latin *bardarius*) was a villein who received a cottage from his lord in exchange for menial services.

## The Social Condition of Serfs and Villeins
Throughout various historical periods and regions, serfs and villeins occupied the lowest strata of the social hierarchy:

*   **England:** By the 13th century, more than half of the English population were serfs. They were excluded from the privileges of the Magna Carta, and disputes between a serf and his lord were settled in the lord's own court, though the king's courts did attempt to protect the serf's necessary work implements and their life and limb. During the 13th century, while the upper classes, parliament, and courts used the Anglo-Norman dialect of French, English was relegated to "farms and hovels" among the serfs and villeins.
*   **France and Brittany:** Under French feudal law, the villein had no appeal from his lord except to God. In Brittany, following the 5th-century emigration of Celtic inhabitants from Britain to Armorica, a powerful aristocracy emerged that utilized serfs or villeins to cultivate their estates.
*   **Ancient Greece:** In the Dorian states of the Peloponnese, particularly Sparta, a professional political aristocracy of soldiers was maintained by the labor of a population of industrial and agricultural serfs. These serfs were strictly forbidden from social advancement or intermixture and were monitored by masters who feared their recurrent, though ineffectual, revolts. Similar systems of serfage and militant aristocracy were present in Cretan cities.

## Legal and Financial Bonds
Distinct from the social status of a "bondman," the legal "bond" serves as a security for the performance of various obligations. While often used for the payment of money at a fixed date, bonds are also used to guarantee the fidelity of rent collectors, clerks, or public officials. They may secure building contracts, ensure a husband settles a sum on a surviving wife, or restrict a rival business within certain limits of time and space.

In many legal practices, covenants have largely superseded bonds. However, bonds remain preferable in certain instances, such as securing an annuity; if the obligor defaults, judgment can be entered for the penalty as security for future payments without requiring a new action for every payment. Under an act of 1696, the remedy for a breach of a bond with special conditions involves the assessment of damages. Courts frequently must determine if the sum in such a bond is a "true penalty" or "liquidated damages," as seen in the leading case of *Kemble v. Farren* (1829).
