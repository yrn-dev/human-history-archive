# Wisdom

In the historical record, the concept of wisdom is presented through various cultural, philosophical, and educational frameworks, ranging from the structured logic of ancient Greece to the character-based training of the Japanese samurai.

## Wisdom in Greek Philosophy and Aristotle
In the writings of Aristotle, a distinction is made between "human wisdom" (ἀνθρωπίνη σοφία) and "primitive theology" (οἱ ἀρχαῖοι καὶ διατρίβοντες περὶ τὰς θεολογίας). Aristotle's influence on the pursuit of wisdom and knowledge was extensive; he is noted for having collected and improved upon the expositions of ancient rhetoricians, superseding the treatises of those who came before him, such as Tisiâ. 

Aristotle's comprehensive view of the theory of rhetoric remained instructive, treating the narrow, practice-based precepts of teachers like Thrasymachus and Theodôrus as mere hints and materials. Furthermore, the logic of Aristotle, alongside that of the stoics, was utilized by Roman civilians such as Servius Sulpicius as an "infallible rule" for discerning truth from falsehood and reducing particular cases to general principles.

## Wisdom in the Bushido Tradition
In the context of samurai training in Japan, wisdom was central to the framework of Bushido. The tripod supporting this framework consisted of *Chi* (Wisdom), *Jin* (Benevolence), and *Yu* (Courage). Within this system, the term *Chi* denoted intellectuality, but it prioritized wisdom over knowledge, placing the latter in a subordinate position.

Knightly pedagogics for the samurai focused on building character, which took precedence over the "subtler faculties" of intelligence, dialectics, and prudence. While philosophy and literature were part of their intellectual training, they were not pursued for the sake of objective truth. Instead, philosophy served as a practical aid for the formation of character or as a means to address political or military problems.

## Philosophical Expositions and Later Influence
The pursuit of wisdom and the exposition of philosophy continued through various eras and cultures:

*   **Bar-Hebraeus:** This author wrote a work titled *[H.][=e]wath H[=e]khm[)e]th[=a]* (Butter of Wisdom), which serves as an exposition of the entire philosophy of Aristotle.
*   **Roman Jurisprudence:** Cicero sought to deduce the wisdom and justice of the Roman constitution from a celestial origin. He identified Plato, Aristotle, and Zeno as the only teachers capable of instructing a citizen for the duties of social life.
*   **Medieval and Byzantine Periods:** The philosophy of Aristotle was later employed to systematize Christian doctrine. In the 11th century, Michael Psellus helped revive interest in both Plato and Aristotle.
*   **The Semitic World:** A renascence in the Semitic world saw the "seed of Aristotle" and the museum of Alexandria germinate, leading to advances in physical, medical, and mathematical sciences. This philosophy eventually reanimated the Christian world and the medieval philosophy of Italy and France.

## Related Intellectual Frameworks
Other historical figures approached the organization of human knowledge and wisdom through different lenses. M. Auguste Comte proposed a doctrine of three successive stages of the human mind regarding scientific study: the theological, the metaphysical, and the positive. In the 19th century, philosophers like Elme Marie Caro focused on defending Christianity against modern Positivism, emphasizing exposition and criticism. Similarly, Beneke published treatises applying philosophy to ordinary life and education, with his system later summarized in the English translation *Elements of Psychology* (1871).
