# Liber Tartarorum

The *Liber Tartarorum* (also known as *Tatarorum*) is a medieval treatise written by Friar Joannes (John of Pian de Carpine) detailing his mission to the Mongol world between 1245 and 1247. The work is also variously titled in manuscripts as *Historia Mongalorum quos nos Tartaros appellamus*. It is regarded as a primary literary memorial of European overland expansion prior to the travels of Marco Polo and served as the first revelation of the Mongol world to Catholic Christendom.

## Content and Structure
The treatise is organized into nine chapters. The first eight chapters provide an extensive examination of the Tatars, focusing on the following subjects:
* Country and climate
* Manners and customs
* Religion and character
* History and policy
* Military tactics
* Strategies for opposing them

The ninth and final chapter is dedicated to the regions the author passed through during his journey.

## Author and Narrative Style
Friar Joannes was a member of the Franciscan Order. Records from the annals of his order describe him as an old man at the time of his mission, as well as a "fat and heavy man" (*vir gravis et corpulentus*). This physical condition was such that, during his previous preachings in Germany, he was forced to ride a donkey, which departed from standard Franciscan precedent.

Despite these personal challenges, the narrative is noted for its lack of authorial egotism. Even in the final chapter, there is almost no personal narrative or complaint. While the work is praised as perhaps the best treatment of Tatar manners and history by any Christian writer of the middle ages, it has been noted for its credulity in incorporating "Oriental tales" that are sometimes characterized as having "childish absurdity." In terms of geographical and personal detail, it is considered inferior to the later work of William of Rubruck (Rubruquis), another Franciscan brother and envoy to the Mongols.

## Military Observations
The *Liber Tartarorum* provides insights into the military tactics of the Mongols. Carpini describes tactics similar to those noted by Hayton, specifically the practice of retreating while keeping companies together. During such retreats, the Mongols shoot back over their heads, causing significant casualties among those pursuing them.

## Documentation and Lists
A distinctive feature of the *Historia* is the inclusion of four specific name-lists:
1. Nations conquered by the Mongols.
2. Nations that had successfully resisted the Mongols up to the period of 1245–1247.
3. Mongol princes.
4. Witnesses to the truth of the narrative, which includes various merchants trading in Kiev whom Carpini encountered.

## Manuscripts and Editions
Several important manuscripts of the work exist, including:
* **Corpus:** Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, No. 181 (dated before 1300).
* **Petau:** Leiden University, 77 (dated before 1300).
* **London-Lumley:** British Museum, MSS. Reg. 13 A xiv. (late 13th century).
* **Colbert:** Paris, National Library, Fonds Lat. 2477 (approximately 1350).

The work has been edited and published in various forms, including the *Relations des Mongols ou Tartares* by d'Avezac (Paris, 1838). Other scholarly treatments can be found in the works of W.W. Rockhill and C. Raymond Beazley.
