# Barcelona

Barcelona is a flourishing city and the principal seaport of Spain, located in eastern Spain at 41deg 23' N. and 2deg 11' E. on the Mediterranean Sea. It is situated on the sloping edge of a small plain between the Llobregat river to the south and the Besos river to the north. The city's geography is defined by the Montanas Matas to the north-east, the heights of Tibidabo (1745 ft.) to the west, and the fortified hills of Montjuich (650 ft.) to the south-west. As of 1900, the city had a population of 533,000.

## Early History and Roman Period
The city's ancient name, Barcino, is traditionally connected to Hamilcar Barca of Carthage, who is credited as its founder in the 3rd century B.C. Following the Roman conquest, the city was named Julia Faventia by Augustus (who ruled from 27 B.C. to A.D. 14), and it was later known as Augusta and Pia. Established as a Roman colony, the city occupied Monte Taber and grew rapidly into a leading mart of the western Mediterranean. By the 2nd century A.D., it rivaled Massilia (Marseilles) and Tarraco (Tarragona).

## Visigothic and Moorish Influence
The bishopric of Barcelona was founded in 343. During the Visigothic period, the city served as a temporary capital in 415 and 531. In 415, Ataulphus (the Latinized form of the Gothic Ataulf), king of the Goths, crossed the Pyrenees into Spain and died in Barcelona after being assassinated by a groom. Ataulphus had previously led his army across the Alps into Gaul in 412 and married Placidia, the sister of Emperor Honorius, in 414. Ataulphus is noted for his recorded confession that his countrymen were unable to establish a civilized or abiding kingdom, leading him to aim for a blend of the two nations built upon Roman foundations.

In 540 and 599, Barcelona hosted church councils. The city, then called Bardjaluna or Barcinona, was captured by the Moors in 713.

## Medieval Development and Commercial Zenith
In 801, Barcelona and the rest of Catalonia passed under the dominion of the Franks. Starting in 874, the counts of Barcelona ruled as independent monarchs. The city reached the zenith of its wealth and fame following the union of Aragon and Catalonia in 1149. During this era, Barcelona's merchant ships traded as far east as Alexandria and as far west as the Baltic and North Seas, competing with the maritime powers of Ragusa, Venice, and Genoa.

The city was a pioneer in maritime commerce, being among the first communities to adopt marine insurance. In 1258, James I. of Aragon authorized the issuance of the *Consulado del Mar*, a code of maritime law that many European states recognized as authoritative. To support its trade, Barcelona maintained consuls at major commercial centers near the Mediterranean.

## Later Political History
The commercial supremacy of Barcelona declined following the discovery of America in 1492, as trade shifted to the ports of western Spain. This decline was further exacerbated by the 1479 union of Aragon and Castile, which favored other Spanish cities. This led to resentment toward the "Castilian" government and a movement among citizens favoring annexation by France.

In 1640, Barcelona became the center of a Catalonian rebellion against Philip IV and sought French protection. Although it returned to its allegiance in 1652, the city was captured by the duke of Vendome in 1697. The city's status later evolved; it was formerly the capital of Catalonia and became the capital of the province of Barcelona in 1833.
