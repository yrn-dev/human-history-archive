# Eleanor of Provence and the English Court

## Relationship with Henry III and the English Church
Eleanor of Provence was the queen of Henry III of England. Through her favor and influence, her uncle, Boniface of Savoy, was appointed primate as the archbishop of Canterbury in 1243. Boniface was characterized as a man of violent temper who was frequently absent from his see. Despite these traits, he demonstrated sympathy toward the reforming party within the English church. 

Boniface's tenure was marked by conflict and leadership; in 1250, he provoked English bishops by claiming the right of visitation in their dioceses. However, by 1258, he took a leading role at the council of Merton to vindicate the privileges of his order. During the barons' war, Boniface supported the royalist side, though he was not noted for great activity.

## Family Connections and French Relations
Eleanor was the daughter of Raymond Berenger V, count of Provence. She had two sisters: Beatrice, who married Charles of Anjou, and Margaret, who became the queen of France after marrying Saint Louis (Louis IX) at Sens on May 27, 1234. 

The relationship between the sisters was often strained. Margaret of Provence maintained a strong devotion to her sister Eleanor and her brother-in-law Henry III. Conversely, Margaret was hostile toward her other brother-in-law, Charles of Anjou. This familial tension extended into political disputes over the county of Provence, which Margaret claimed obstinately until after the death of Charles of Anjou in 1285.

## Court Officials and the Stewardship
The coronation of Eleanor, queen of Henry III, is documented in the Exchequer Red Book, which describes the service of Simon V. de Montfort, the earl of Leicester, who acted as steward during the ceremony. Simon de Montfort believed his hereditary stewardship—an office that had been the highest state position in France before its suppression—entitled him to a high official position in England. 

Following his victory at Lewes, Simon frequently appeared as the steward of England in official documents under the great seal. After his death at Evesham, his estates and a life-grant of the stewardship were given to his son, Edmund of Lancaster. This was later succeeded by Thomas, earl of Lancaster, who received a grant of the stewardship for himself and his heirs from Edward II.

## Royal Grants and the Castle of Kenilworth
During his reign, Henry III granted the castle of Kenilworth to Simon de Montfort, earl of Leicester. The castle had previously been a crown possession since the time of King John. Following the battle of Evesham, rebel forces rallied at Kenilworth, but the castle was surrendered by its governor, Henry de Hastings, after a six-month siege due to pestilent disease and a lack of food. Subsequently, the king granted the castle to his son, Edmund.

## Military Conflicts
The reign of Henry III involved military friction with France. In 1242, a rising of nobles in the south-west of France was stirred up by Hugh de Lusignan, count of the Marche, and Isabella, the widow of King John of England. This conflict resulted in the king's armies overrunning Count Hugh's territories and defeating Henry III.
