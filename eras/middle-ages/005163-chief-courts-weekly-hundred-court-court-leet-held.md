# Local Courts and Manorial Governance

In various English boroughs and manors, local governance was historically administered through a system of manorial courts, most notably the court leet and the court baron. These institutions served as the primary mechanisms for legal administration, the appointment of local officials, and the regulation of community agreements.

## The Court Leet and Court Baron
The court leet was a common feature of manorial administration across different regions. In some locations, such as Bury, a court-leet and view of frank pledge were held half-yearly at Michaelmas and Easter, while a court-baron was held in May. Similarly, in other manors, the lord of the manor maintained the ancient court-leet and court-baron half-yearly in November and May. In these specific proceedings, the court took cognizance of breaches of agreement among tenants, with a particular focus on the cultivation of lands and the repair of roads.

In Kingsbridge, the steward of the manor continued to hold both the court baron and court leet into the present day, using these courts to appoint constables and a portreeve. In Ashburton, the court leet held by the lord of the manor was the venue where the bailiff and portreeve were elected on an annual basis.

## Bristol's Manorial Organization
Before its transition to a municipal structure, Bristol was organized manorially. Its primary legal administration consisted of a weekly hundred court and a court leet that was held three times a year. These courts were presided over by a reeve, who was appointed by the earl of Gloucester.

The manorial system in Bristol shifted following the marriage of Earl John to the heiress of Earl William of Gloucester, which brought Bristol into the royal demesne. A significant turning point occurred with a charter granted by John in 1190, which allowed burgesses to hold by burgage tenure and ensured they would not be impleaded outside the walls. Eventually, the reeve was displaced by a mayor. Unlike the reeve, the mayor was not appointed by an overlord but was chosen by the merchants of Bristol who belonged to the merchant gild. Robert FitzNichol became the first mayor in 1200.

## Other Judicial and Administrative Bodies
Beyond the court leet, other specialized courts existed to handle specific jurisdictions. In Derbyshire, the miners formed an independent community governed by barmasters and a steward who held two Barmote courts annually. Additionally, the forests of Duffield and Peak maintained their own officers and courts; the pleas of Duffield Forest were held at Tutbury, while the justice seat for Peak was located in an extra-parochial area.

In Lostwithiel, the town's legal landscape evolved from early liberties granted by the lord of Restormel and later by Richard, king of the Romans. While the burgesses were eventually exempted from the jurisdiction of the county and hundred courts, a 1609 charter of incorporation established courts of record and pie powder.

## Local Officials and Appointments
The courts were often the mechanism for appointing the officials who governed the town's daily affairs. In Honiton, the right to nominate a portreeve and hold a view of frank-pledge and court leet was confirmed to Sir William Courtenay in 1698. The portreeve served as the returning officer and the borough's chief magistrate until the town was incorporated by charter in 1846.

In Bury, the court-leet was used to annually choose three constables to govern the area until 1846, when parliament authorized the appointment of twenty-seven commissioners for local government.
