# The House of Habsburg and its German Territories

The house of Habsburg was an acquisitive family that expanded its possessions throughout Germany and neighboring territories over several centuries. Its rise to prominence began in 1273 when Rudolf of Habsburg was elected Emperor of Germany following the extinction of the Hohenstaufen house. This distinction eventually became practically hereditary within the family.

## Early Expansion and the Austrian Nucleus
The growth of the Habsburg dominions was a slow process that followed the earlier efforts of the house of Babenberg. The original nucleus of these lands was a mark founded around A.D. 800 along the south bank of the Danube, east of the river Enns. Established as a defense for the Frankish kingdom against the Slavs, this region became known as *Oesterreich* (Austria), meaning "east empire" or "realm," a term appearing in a charter from 996.

Rudolf of Habsburg expanded the family's reach by challenging the Bohemian king, Ottakar, regarding his title to three duchies. After Ottakar was placed under the ban in July 1275 and subsequently submitted in November 1276, he renounced the duchies of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia. Rudolf initially administered these as head of the Empire but sought to make them hereditary family possessions, shifting the Habsburg headquarters from the Rhine to the Danube. On December 27, 1282, Rudolf invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Austria and Styria. He later bestowed Carinthia upon Meinhard IV, count of Tirol, in 1286.

## Territorial Challenges and Conflicts
Despite the family's growth, certain regions resisted Habsburg rule. In 1291, the men of Unterwalden, Schwyz, and Uri formed an Everlasting League to avoid being ruled by any Habsburg, eventually evolving into an independent confederation.

In later centuries, the Habsburgs sought further "compensation" for losses, such as the loss of Silesia, by looking toward Bavaria. The house of Wittelsbach, which ruled Bavaria, was a secular rival to the Habsburgs in southern Germany. This tension culminated in the War of Bavarian Succession in 1777 after the death of elector Maximilian III. Emperor Joseph II claimed the inheritance, but the conflict was settled without actual fighting when Maria Theresa overruled her son. Through the treaty of Teschen, the Habsburgs received some districts and the Quarter of the Inn (Innviertel).

## The Habsburg Realm and the German State
The relationship between the Habsburgs and a unified German state was complex. While the Emperors were of German origin, they often lacked a grip on north Germans due to their focus on eastern frontiers, Hungarian affairs, and the Turkish danger. For example, Charles V became Spanish and Burgundian in spirit. After Charles V, the empire and Austria were taken by his brother Ferdinand, while Philip II took the Netherlands, Spain, and South Italy.

During the 19th century, the position of the Habsburg monarchy in a united Germany was a subject of intense debate. At the parliament of united Germany in Frankfort in May 1848, a dilemma arose: whether to include only the German provinces of the Austrian monarchy or the empire in its entirety. The Austrian government refused any settlement that would split the monarchy or create double allegiance, while German patriots rejected the inclusion of a vast non-German population.

## Constitutional Shifts and Exclusion from Germany
The 1815 Congress of Vienna had placed Bohemia within the Germanic Confederation without consulting the country's estates. However, the mid-19th century brought significant changes. Following the Italian campaign of 1859, absolutism in the Austrian empire became impossible, leading to attempts to establish a constitutional system. A decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, but a subsequent decree on February 26, 1861, created a central parliament in Vienna with an electoral system that heavily favored Germans.

The constitutional position shifted drastically following the Austro-Prussian war of 1866. A treaty signed at Prague on August 23, 1866, explicitly excluded all lands ruled by the house of Habsburg from Germany. This resulted in a decline of German influence within the Austrian empire and specifically in Bohemia. While Hungary achieved complete independence, the new constitution of 1867—which applied to the Slavic and German parts of the empire—attempted to maintain centralization and the waning German influence, a move energetically opposed by the Bohemians.
