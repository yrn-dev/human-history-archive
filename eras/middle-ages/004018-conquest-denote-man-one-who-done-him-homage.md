# Homage and the Concept of the Baron

The term "homage" and the associated social structures of vassalage appear across various historical contexts, ranging from the feudal systems of medieval Europe to the diplomatic ambitions of ancient and imperial powers. In the context of the English Conquest, these concepts were central to the organization of land and loyalty.

## The Baron and the English Conquest
The word "baron" was introduced into England at the time of the Conquest. Its original purpose was to denote "the man" of a great lord, specifically referring to someone who had performed "homage" to that lord. This was most notably applied to those who held land "in chief," meaning they held it directly from the king. These individuals, known as *barones regis*, were theoretically members of the king's council and were required to perform stipulated services.

While the king had his barons, great nobles—including earls—also referred to their own tenants as "barons." In contrast, lesser magnates typically referred to their subordinates simply as "men" (*homines*). This distinction was particularly evident in palatinates. For example, in the earldom of Chester, the earl's barons were a recognized body, including the Venables family (the "barons of Kinderton"), who existed until 1679. Similarly, in the palatinate of Durham, the bishop maintained his own barons, such as the Hiltons of Hilton Castle, a title used until 1746.

Other families held the title of baron independently of peerage dignity on uncertain grounds, including the Cornwalls ("barons of Burford"), the "barons of Stafford," and the "barons of Greystock." The term was also applied to urban figures; Henry II referred to "mes baruns de Lundres," and charters regarding the election of a mayor mentioned the "barons of our city of London." In London, aldermen were loosely considered equivalent to barons and were assessed for the poll-tax as such under Richard II. In Ireland, the title was also common due to the palatine nature of great lordships, as seen with the barons of the Naas, Slane, and Galtrim.

## The Feudal Relationship and the Fief
The foundation of the feudal relationship was the "fief," which was typically land but could also be a revenue in money or kind, an office, the operation of a mill, or the right to collect a toll. Through the ceremony of homage and investiture—described as the creative contract of feudalism—a man became a "vassal" of his lord. During this process, the vassal knelt before the lord, placed his hands between the lord's hands, and promised service and fealty.

The vassal's right and title to the fief depended on the faithful performance of the duties assumed during homage. As long as these duties were met, the vassal and his heirs held the fief as their property. The specific obligations were generally determined by local custom rather than exact terms. These services were divided into two categories:
*   **General services:** These included loyalty, protecting the lord's family, keeping secrets, seeking the lord's interests, and betraying the plans of enemies.
*   **Specific services:** These were more definitively stated in custom or documents. The most characteristic was military service, which required appearing in the field on summons with a specific force for a set length of time. This often included guarding the lord's castle or holding one's own castle according to the lord's defensive plans.

## Homage in Other Historical and Cultural Contexts
The concept of homage and submission appears in various other global and ancient settings:

**Ancient Macedonia:** In the court of Alexander, homage was expressed through physical submission. Guests at table would drink from a cup, rise, stand by the hearth, kneel before Alexander, and kiss him. The philosopher Kallisthenes famously refused to kneel and do homage to Alexander, an act that Aristotle noted showed a lack of common sense despite Kallisthenes' power of speech.

**The Byzantine Empire:** During the crusades, the Byzantine Emperor Alexius sought to have leaders hold their Asiatic conquests as loyal and humble vassals of the Roman empire. While some resisted this "voluntary servitude," others submitted. Alexius dignified the homage of Godfrey of Bouillon with the rights of adoption and the filial name, calling him the champion of the empire. Other figures, including the two Roberts (kinsmen of three queens and the son of the conqueror of England), also bowed before the Byzantine throne.

**Imperial China/Mongolia:** In 1265, a Korean traveller suggested to Kublai Khan that he obtain the homage of the "Island Empire" of Japan. Kublai subsequently sent embassies via Korea to include Japan as one of his friendly tributaries.

**Ancient Peru:** In Peru, universal homage was paid to the sun-god. Additionally, the Incas accorded theoretical supremacy to Pachacamac (the "world-animator"), a deity they found upon conquering the land.

## Legal and Political Applications
Homage also functioned as a formal political tool. In one instance, a committee summoned to organize a new government discussed whether a new oath of homage should be taken to the king. It was decided in the affirmative, though the king (Frederick III) was simultaneously expected to promulgate a Recess or new constitution to limit royal power.
