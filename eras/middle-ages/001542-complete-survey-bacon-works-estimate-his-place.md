# The Works and Legacy of Bacon

The archive contains records regarding two distinct figures named Bacon: the 13th-century scholar Roger Bacon and the later philosopher and statesman Francis Bacon. Their works and their respective positions in the history of science and philosophy differ significantly in character and impact.

## Roger Bacon: Works and Standing
Roger Bacon was a 13th-century figure who attained the degree of doctor of theology and was known by the title *doctor mirabilis*. His career was marked by periods of renown and restriction; while his fame spread at Oxford, he faced suspicions regarding his orthodoxy and dealings in the black arts. In 1257, Bonaventura interdicted his lectures, leading to a ten-year period of supervision in Paris where he was prohibited from publishing.

Bacon's major literary output was spurred by a 1266 command from Pope Clement IV to write a treatise on the sciences. In approximately eighteen months, Bacon produced three large treatises: the *Opus Majus*, the *Opus Minus*, and the *Opus Tertium*. The *Opus Minus* served as a summary of the *Opus Majus* and included treatises on practical and speculative alchemy and the vices of theology. The *Opus Tertium* acted as a preamble to the first two works; while some consider it a complete treatise, others argue it was a preface to further discussions on metaphysics, moral philosophy, general physics, mathematics, logic, and grammar. Additionally, Bacon wrote the *Compendium Studii Philosophiae* (1271), in which he attacked the insufficiency of existing studies and the vices of monks and clergy.

In terms of his historical place, Roger Bacon is ranked alongside Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventura, and Albertus Magnus. While modern criticism suggests he was a "modern thinker" ahead of his age, this view is considered by some to be an exaggeration. He is described as a keen, systematic thinker who worked within established tracks, though he was less influenced by theology and metaphysics than his contemporaries.

## Francis Bacon: Classification of Works
Francis Bacon, Lord Verulam (1561-1626) and Lord Chancellor to James I, produced a body of work that can be categorized into three classes: Professional, Literary, and Philosophical.

His Professional works include the *Maxims of Law*, the *Reading on the Statute of Uses*, and a treatise on the *Use of the Law*. These writings are noted for their comprehensive intelligence of abstract jurisprudential principles and ethical fullness, contrasting with the technical detail found in the reports of Sir Edward Coke.

His Philosophical works center on the reorganization of the sciences. Bacon sought to establish a new method to allow the human mind to proceed with certainty toward the true end of thought and action. Key components of this effort include:
*   **The Organum and Natural History**: Bacon viewed these as correlative sides of a true philosophy. He argued that the *Organum* alone would not advance the "Instauration of the Sciences" without a complete Natural History—a collection of instances, observations, and canons.
*   **The New Atlantis**: This work is described as a "fantastic book" in which Bacon envisioned a "palace of invention" and a temple of science organized for maximum efficiency.
*   **Other Writings**: His early efforts included the *Advancement* and a small tract titled *Cogitata et Visa* (1607). In 1609, he wrote *De Sapientia Veterum* and the panegyric *In felicem memoriam Elizabethae*, as well as the *Redargutio Philosophiarum*, a treatise concerning the "idols of the theatre."

## Evaluation of Francis Bacon's Influence
Francis Bacon has been titled the "Father of Experimental Philosophy," though some historians argue this overstates his role, suggesting he was the "apostle" rather than the founder of the scientific method. His ideas were likely influenced by the work of Dr. Gilbert on magnetism.

Bacon's methods have faced criticism from figures such as Liebig, who argued that little science actually proceeds according to Bacon's rules. Liebig accused Bacon of using imagination and arbitrary interpretations of phenomena. However, other scholars, such as Fischer and Sigwart, have defended Bacon, suggesting that critics often misinterpret the nature of his works, such as the *Sylva Sylvarum*, or fail to grasp the true nature of his method.
