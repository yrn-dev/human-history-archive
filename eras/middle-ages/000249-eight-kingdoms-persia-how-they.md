# The Eight Kingdoms of Persia

Persia is described as a very great country that was illustrious and powerful in ancient times, though it was later wasted and destroyed by the Tartars. According to the accounts of Marco Polo, the region is divided into eight distinct kingdoms.

## The Eight Kingdoms and Their Geography
The eight kingdoms of Persia are named as follows:
1. **Casvin**: Located at the beginning of Persia.
2. **Curdistan**: Situated further to the south.
3. **Lor**
4. **Suolstan**
5. **Istanit**
6. **Serazy**
7. **Soncara**
8. **Tunocain**: Located at the further extremity of Persia.

Geographically, seven of these kingdoms lie in a southerly direction. Tunocain is the exception, as it lies toward the east and borders the country of the Arbre Sol.

## Trade and Natural Resources
Persia is noted for its supply of fine horses, which are highly valued and exported to India for sale. The price of a single horse varies based on quality, with some reaching a value of 200 livres Tournois. To facilitate this trade, dealers transport the horses to two cities on the shores of the Sea of India: Kisi and Curmosa. In these cities, they meet merchants who carry the animals onward to India.

In addition to horses, Persia is home to the finest asses in the world. These animals are characterized by their large size, speed, and a "capital amble," with a single ass valued at 30 marks of silver.

## Historical Context and Populations
Historical records indicate that Persia has been a site of forced migration and imperial conflict. During the reign of Shalmaneser, the king of Assyria, the government of the Israelites was demolished after the siege of Samaria. Shalmaneser transplanted the Israelites into Media and Persia. Subsequently, other nations from a place called Cuthah—where a river of the same name is located in Persia—were transplanted into Samaria and the country of the Israelites.

The region was also the seat of the house of Sassan, which ruled the monarchy of Iran (Persia). This power frequently engaged in conflicts with the Romans and the Turks, who were separated from Persia by the river Oxus (or Gihon). Under the Sassanid ruler Kobad, the empire faced civil and religious instability, including the influence of Mazdak, who advocated for the equality of mankind and the community of women.
