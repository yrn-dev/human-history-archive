# Feast of the Ascension

The Feast of the Ascension is an oecumenical festival of the Christian Church, holding a level of solemnity comparable to the festivals of Pentecost, Easter, and Christmas. It is observed forty days after Easter, or ten days prior to Whitsunday, to celebrate the ascension of Christ into heaven forty days after his resurrection. The day is referred to as Holy Thursday or Ascension Day and always falls on a Thursday.

## Origins and Early History
The festival is characterized by great antiquity. While no discoverable traces of the feast exist before the middle of the 4th century, subsequent records indicate it was long established. St Augustine noted that the feast had been kept from time immemorial and was likely instituted by the apostles. Other early references include Chrysostom, who mentioned a celebration at the church of Romanesia outside Antioch, and Socrates, who recorded that the people of Constantinople celebrated the feast in a suburb of the city in the year 390 as an "old custom."

## Rituals and Traditions
Historically, the festival was associated with professional pilgrimages to commemorate the passing of Christ and his apostles to the Mount of Olives. In the 7th century, Adamnan, the abbot of Iona, described such a procession taking place at Jerusalem, with the feast celebrated in the church on Mount Olivet. Similarly, the *Peregrinatio* of Etheria (dating to approximately A.D. 385) states that the festival was held in the Church of the Nativity at Bethlehem. In the West during the middle ages, processions featuring banners and candles outside the church served as symbols of Christ's triumphant entry into heaven.

In the Roman Catholic Church, a primary ritual feature is the solemn extinction of the paschal candle following the Gospel at high mass. This candle, which is lit at every mass for the forty days following Easter, symbolizes the presence of Christ with his disciples; its extinction symbolizes his parting from them. This custom originated in 1263, was initially limited to the Franciscans, and was prescribed for the universal church by the Congregation of Rites on May 19, 1697.

Other now-obsolete customs included the blessing of new beans after the Commemoration of the Dead in the canon of the mass. During the middle ages, some churches practiced a ritual where an image of Christ was raised from the altar through a hole in the roof, followed immediately by the throwing down of a burning straw figure representing Satan.

## Regional Terminology and Concepts
The festival is known by different terms in the East and West, reflecting different conceptual emphases:

*   **The East:** The festival is known as *analaepsis* (Greek for "taking up") or *episozomenae*. The latter term, first used in the Cappadocian church, has a disputed meaning but likely signifies the feast "of completed salvation."
*   **The West:** The term *ascensio* is used, which implies that Christ ascended by his own power. This is distinguished from *assumptio*, which refers to the taking up of the Virgin Mary into heaven by the power of God.
