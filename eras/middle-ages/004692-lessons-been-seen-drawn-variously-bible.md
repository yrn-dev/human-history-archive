# The Use and Production of Biblical Texts

## Liturgical Lessons and Church Practice
In the early church, "lessons" were drawn from a variety of sources, including the Bible, the Fathers of the Church, and the Acts of the Saints. During the primitive era, certain texts that were later excluded from the canon were frequently read, such as the *Shepherd of Hermas* and the letters of Clement of Rome. In Africa, churches utilized memorials of martyrdom to supplement the reading of Scripture.

The practice of adding patristic exposition or commentary to biblical passages was influenced by monasticism. This led to the compilation of books of homilies featuring the writings of figures such as Athanasius, Hilary, Isidore, Gregory the Great, and Augustine. These works formed part of the library that eventually culminated in the Breviary. 

Within the liturgical structure, lessons are read at Matins, which is divided into three nocturns. Additionally, "little chapters"—which are very short lessons—are read during other "hours," followed by short responsories known as "versicles." The normal order of ferial offices and consecutive reading is occasionally interrupted by the order for special days.

## Biblical Manuscripts and Illumination
The production of Bibles and psalters underwent significant artistic changes following the Norman Conquest, which improved English draughtsmanship. The Anglo-Saxon style of outline drawing, which tended toward affectation, was replaced by a bold, freehand style that persisted for three centuries. 

By the 12th century, an artistic outburst across western Germany, Flanders, France, and England led to the creation of large folios. These manuscripts featured magnificent initial letters on a grand scale, characterized by brilliant colors, a generous use of gold, and a wealth of interlacing branches and foliage containing animal and human life. Figure drawing in these initials and miniatures was typically bold but stiff, with elongated figures and sweeping lines in the draperies. 

Notable examples of these works include:
*   **The Stavelot Bible:** Dating from the close of the 11th century.
*   **The Bible of Floreffe:** Dating from approximately 1160.
*   **The Worms Bible.**
*   **The Winchester School:** A psalter from approximately 1160 (Cotton, Nero C. iv.) demonstrates the continued excellence of this English school.

## Translation and Criticism
For centuries in western Europe, the New Testament was current only in the form of the Latin Vulgate. In 1516, Erasmus published the *Novum Instrumentum*, which included the Greek text and his own Latin rendering. Erasmus sought to convey the meaning of the Greek rather than following the conventional phraseology of the Vulgate. His editions, including his prefaces and annotations, are considered the first great monument of modern Biblical study.

Erasmus introduced a scientific approach to criticism by discriminating between different types of sources. He distinguished between direct statements found in the Acts and inferences drawn from the statements of ancient non-canonical writers or incidental allusions in the Pauline Epistles. Medieval Bibles typically contained stereotyped information in short prefaces written by St Jerome and others.

In England, the *Paris Psalter* (MS. lat. 8824) represents a shift toward real translation rather than word-for-word glossing. This rendering of the first fifty Psalms is assigned to the late 9th or early 10th century; some scholars attribute it to King Alfred, though this is disputed. Additionally, the *Lindisfarne Gospels* (also known as the *Durham Book* or *Book of St Cuthbert*) feature a Latin text from the close of the 7th century by Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne, with an English gloss added around 950 by Aldred.

## Biblical Geography and Historical References
The Bible refers to Beersheba as the southern limit of Palestine ("Dan to Beersheba"). Its foundation is attributed to Isaac and Abraham in the documents of Genesis. It served as a holy place where Abraham planted a sacred tree and where divine manifestations occurred for Elijah, Jacob, Isaac, and Hagar. The prophet Amos mentions Beersheba alongside Gilgal and Bethel, though he denounces oaths made by its *numen*.

Beyond the texts themselves, the Bible has appeared in historical military contexts. Salvian ascribes a Vandal victory to their superior piety, noting that they prayed, fasted, and carried a Bible at the front of their host to reproach the sacrilege and perfidy of their enemies.
