# The Welsh Marches

The Welsh marches were the boundary districts located between England and Wales. Following the Norman Conquest, these disturbed border regions were granted to powerful nobles, known as lords marcher, who were tasked with the defense of the neighboring English counties. In exchange for this responsibility, these lords were granted special privileges.

## Administration and Governance
The original purpose of the marcher lordships—the defense of the realm—was frequently neglected as the lords became occupied with increasing their own wealth and power and fighting among themselves. This led to a deterioration of conditions in the region. During the reigns of Edward II and Henry III, the marches became centers of rebellion and rampant lawlessness.

The legal status of the region shifted in 1354 during the reign of Edward III, when the marches were declared to be directly subject to the English crown rather than part of the principality. Stability improved after the Wars of the Roses, and in 1542, Henry VIII established a council of Wales and the marches, which resulted in a notable decrease in disorder. By the era of Elizabeth, the Welsh marches had lost their practical significance and retained only historical importance.

## Geography and Extent
The boundaries of the Welsh marches varied over time, making them difficult to define precisely. However, under the Lancastrian kings and Edward I, the marcher lordships covered more than half of the area of Wales. This included nearly the entire principality with the exception of the southern counties of Cardigan and Carmarthen, and the northern counties of Merioneth, Carnarvon, and Anglesea. Additionally, the marches encompassed portions of the English border counties of Shropshire, Hereford, and Monmouth.

## Prominent Marcher Families

### The Mortimers
The earls of March on the Welsh borders descended from Roger de Mortemer, a Norman connected by marriage to the dukes of Normandy. His son, Ralph (who died circa 1104), held vast estates in Herefordshire, Shropshire, and other western parts of England. Ralph's grandson, Hugh de Mortimer, founded the priory of Wigmore in Herefordshire. Although Henry II compelled Hugh to surrender the castles of Wigmore and Cleobury, the Mortimers maintained almost undisputed sway over the Welsh marches and western counties as lords of Wigmore.

In 1328, Roger Mortimer was created earl of March (*comes de marchia Waliae*). The 8th baron of Wigmore and 1st earl of March, also named Roger (c. 1286–1330), expanded the family's holdings through his marriage to Joan de Joinville (or Genevill), acquiring the castle of Ludlow, which became the Mortimers' chief stronghold. Roger Mortimer later became involved in the opposition to Edward II and his favorites, the Despensers, supporting Humphrey de Bohun, earl of Hereford, in 1321. After being imprisoned in the Tower of London and escaping to France, Roger became the mistress of Isabella, wife of Edward II, and eventually assisted her in an invasion of England in September 1326.

### The Bohuns
The Bohun family, originating from a village in the Cotentin between the estuary of the Vire and Coutances, became prominent in English history during the 14th and 13th centuries. Their rise began with Humphrey III, a steward (*dapifer*) in the royal household during the later years of Henry I. Humphrey III remained loyal to Henry II and fought against East Anglian rebels at Farnham in 1173.

Humphrey III was succeeded by his grandson, Henry. Due to his mother Margaret's connection to the royal house of Scotland (as sister of William the Lion), Henry obtained the earldom of Hereford from John in 1199. From this point, the Bohuns became foremost among the Marcher barons, with their lands lying chiefly on the Welsh Marches. Henry de Bohun was one of the twenty-five barons elected to enforce the Great Charter. He later fought for Louis in a civil war and was captured at the battle of Lincoln in 1217; he died in 1220 during a pilgrimage. His son, Humphrey V, inherited the earldom of Essex before 1239 but later joined the baronial cause against the crown in 1258, serving on a committee of twenty-four to reform administration. He returned to royal allegiance only after Llewelyn of North Wales allied with Montfort.
