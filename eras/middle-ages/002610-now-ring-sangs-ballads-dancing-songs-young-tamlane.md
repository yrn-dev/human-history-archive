# Ballads and the Tradition of Song-Dance

The term "ballad" is derived from the Old French word *baller*, meaning to dance. Originally, a ballad was a song performed to the rhythmic movement of a dancing chorus. This etymological root connects the ballad to other forms such as the *ballet* (from the Sicilian Greek *ballizein*) and the *carol* (Old French *carole*). While the modern meaning of "ballad" is confined to singing and "ballet" to dancing, the Old French *ballet* once incorporated the meanings of dancing, singing, and rejoicing.

## Origins and Communal Nature
Some ballads are considered distinctly popular and impersonal in their origin, springing from the institution of dances. This process of improvisation—where rhythmic speech is used as a natural utterance for emotions during solemn occasions or high-wrought feeling—is observed in various cultures. In Brittany, Italy, and Greece, researchers have described how song grows out of the custom of dancing in large bands. In Russia, the *kolos* dances feature ballads identical in many respects to those that have vanished from oral tradition in the British Isles.

Historically, there was a period when dance, song, and poetry began with "communal consent." This is evidenced by the practice where a singer in a dancing circle chants words while the dancers join in with a refrain. Similar customs are found among the tribes of Delagoa Bay and in Australian song-dances, though in the latter, some songs are devised by individuals inspired by the "All Father" or dreams.

## Characteristics and Development
Folk-ballads are characterized by a directness and simplicity, utilizing recurring epithets such as "salt sea," "green grass," and "shadowy hills." They often feature repetitions of speeches and a "barbaric profusion" in the description of silver and gold. These elements are also found in the early epic poetry of Homer and French *epopees*, though the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* are regarded as high artistic epics rather than collections of ballads.

While some argue that ballads are decayed metrical romances, others suggest that 15th-century folk-ballads preserve formal elements derived from communal song and dance. By the 15th century, these communal processes had largely ceased, and traditional formulae became conventional guides for individual singers. These frameworks allowed singers to express universal appeals regarding battles, sudden griefs, and treacheries.

## Regional Examples and Variations
In Scotland, the connection between song and dance is noted in the *Complaynt of Scotland*, which describes shepherds and their wives singing "melodi sangs" before dancing in a ring. The chronicler Fabyan mentions that a song of triumph over Edward II was sung in dances to the carols of minstrels and maidens. A specific example of a ballad that likely possessed an appropriate air for dancing is *Young Tamlane*.

In France, it is noted that every dance has its own words; if a new dance arrives from Paris, words are fitted to it. In the British tradition, some ballads were derived from medieval literary romance (such as Arthurian ballads), while the romances themselves often borrowed from ancient popular *Maerchen*.

## Related Forms: Carols and Ring-Sangs
The "ring-sang" is defined as a ballad or dancing song. Historical descriptions mention "wenches and damosels" in green dresses wandering by spring wells and singing "ring-sangs, dances, ledes, and rounds."

Similarly, the carol (from *carole*) was originally a "ring dance" or "circle." The site of Stonehenge was frequently known as the "Carol" or "Giants' Dance." Originally, carols were lyrics written to dance measures, which served as a starting point for Germanic lyric poetry. These practices eventually entered the Christian church, where the "crib" of the Saviour became the center of a dance. Despite prohibitions from the councils of Toledo (589), Auxerre (590), and Avignon (1209) against secular or theatrical dances in churches, religious dances persisted, such as the castanet dance performed three times a year by choristers in the cathedral of Seville.
