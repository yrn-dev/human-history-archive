# Celtic Missionaries and Baptismal Practices

The activities of Celtic missionaries in the early church were marked by specific theological and liturgical practices, particularly regarding the administration of baptism. These practices occasionally led to significant friction with the ecclesiastical authorities in Rome.

## The Baptismal Formula and the Trinity
A primary point of contention between the church of Rome and Celtic missionaries concerned the baptismal formula. According to records from Pope Zachariah, Celtic missionaries omitted one or more persons of the Trinity when performing baptisms. This deviation from the trinitarian formula—which the church of Rome defined as "I baptize thee in the name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit"—served as one of the reasons why the church of Rome anathematized the Celtic missionaries.

The broader historical context of baptismal formulas shows a lack of early uniformity. While the *Teaching of the Apostles* prescribed baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, it also referred to those baptized into the name of the Lord, which was the normal formula of the New Testament. In the 3rd century, baptism in the name of Christ remained widespread enough that Pope Stephen declared it valid, opposing Cyprian of Carthage. Later, Pope Nicholas (858-867) allowed baptism to be valid *tantum in nomine Christi* (only in the name of Christ), as seen in the Acts. However, other figures, such as Basil in his work *On the Holy Spirit*, condemned baptism into the Lord alone as being insufficient.

## Celtic Missionary Activity in Britain
Celtic missionaries were active in establishing monastic settlements and preaching Christianity across Britain. In Ireland, a distinct style of book ornamentation developed, which was subsequently practiced in Scottish monastic settlements, most notably at the foundation of St Columba on the island of Iona.

In 635, at the request of King Oswald of Northumbria, a monk of Iona named Aidan was sent to preach Christianity in that kingdom. Aidan founded the see and abbey of Lindisfarne on the Holy Isle off the Northumbrian coast. The brethren who accompanied Aidan established the school of Lindisfarne, which produced a series of finely written and ornamented manuscripts in the Celtic style. A prominent example is the *Lindisfarne Gospels* (also known as *St Cuthbert's Gospels* or the *Durham Book*), completed in the early 8th century. While primarily Celtic in style, this manuscript shows foreign influence through the sparing use of gold and the portrayal of the four evangelists based on Byzantine models. This influence is attributed to the visit of the Greek Theodore, archbishop of Canterbury, and Adrian, an abbot from a monastery near Naples, who arrived in Britain in 688.

## Influence and Cultural Impact
The influence of Irish missionaries extended into the literary and cultural spheres of Northumbria. For instance, the names given to the wives of Noah and his three sons in the Northumbrian *Genesis*—Phercoba, Olla, Olliua, and Olliuani—have been traced back to an Irish source.

Regarding the social customs of the regions they entered, there is no evidence that cremation was still practiced by the time Roman and Celtic missionaries arrived, although it had been a common heathen custom in northern counties and the valley of the Thames.
