# Backgammon

Backgammon is a game for two persons played on a special board with draughtsmen and dice. The board is divided into four "tables," and each table is marked with six differently colored "points." A projecting bar separates the inner and outer tables. The game is typically played with fifteen white and fifteen black men, which are "set" or arranged on the board. Equipment includes a pair of dice used by both players and two dice-boxes, one for each player.

The dice are marked one through six. In the game's terminology, one is called "ace," two is "deuce," and three is "trey." Historically, four was called "quatre" (pronounced "cater"), five was "cinque" (pronounced "sank" or "sink"), and six was "six." To determine who starts, each player throws one or two dice; the player with the higher number plays first and may either use those numbers or throw again using both dice.

## History and Etymology
The game is said by Strutt to have been invented around the 10th century. The Romans played a similar game called *Ludus duodecim scriptorum* (the "twelve-line game"), which divided the table (*alvevolus*) into twelve lines where two armies of fifteen men (*catculi*) were moved according to the dice (*tesseroe*). Plato also alluded in the *Republic* to a game involving the throwing of dice and the placement of men. In Japan, a kind of backgammon is called *sugoroku*. In France, the game is known as *trictrac*, a name that imitates the rattle of the dice.

The etymology of "backgammon" is disputed. One theory suggests it is Saxon, from *baec* (back) and *gamen* (game), referring to players being sent back. Other derivations include the Danish *bakke* (tray) and *gammen* (game), or the Welsh *bach* (little) and *cammaun* (battle). Chaucer referred to a game of "tables" played with three dice, and the *ludus Anglicorum* is described in the Harleian MSS (1527).

## The Laws of Backgammon (Hoyle)
The laws of the game, as provided by Hoyle, include the following rules:
1. If a man is touched by the caster, it must be played if possible; if it is impossible, there is no penalty.
2. A man is not considered played until it is quitted and placed upon a point.
3. There is no penalty if a player omits a man from the board.
4. If a player bears any number of men before entering a man that was taken up, those borne men must be entered again.
5. If a player mistakes their throw and plays it, and the adversary has already thrown, the move cannot be altered unless both players agree.
6. A fresh throw is required if one or both dice are "cocked," meaning they do not lie squarely on the table.

## Strategy and Probability
Playing backgammon well requires knowledge of the thirty-six different possible throws of two dice. By analyzing combinations, players can determine where "blots" may be left to minimize the probability of being hit. For example, if a blot can only be taken up by an ace, there are eleven ways an ace may be thrown (two aces, or an ace combined with any number up to six, each in two different ways). This results in odds of 25 to 11 against being hit on an ace.

Strategic guidelines include:
* **Taking up men:** A man should always be taken up if the blot left behind can only be hit with double dice. However, it is unwise to take up a third man if two of the opponent's men are already in the player's tables.
* **Entering men:** When entering a man where it is advantageous for the adversary to hit, the blot should be left on the lowest possible point (e.g., the ace-point rather than the deuce-point).
* **Bearing off:** If an adversary is bearing his men and the player has two men on the adversary's table (such as the ace-point) and several in the outer table, it is advantageous to leave one man on the ace-point. This prevents the adversary from bearing his men to the greatest advantage and creates a chance for the adversary to leave a blot. However, if the player can likely save the gammon by bringing both men out of the table, they should not wait for a blot.

The average throw is eight points.

## Variations and Legal Status
*Russian Backgammon* differs from the standard game in that men are entered into the same table via dice throws, and both players move in the same direction toward the opposite table. Rules vary; some allow players to take up blots at any time during entry, though a player with a man "up" must enter it before moving other men or entering more. If the man that is up cannot be entered, the player loses the benefit of the throw.

In England, during the 18th century, many dice games (such as hazard, basset, and faro) were prohibited as common nuisances. Backgammon was an exception to these prohibitions, as were games played in the royal palace.
