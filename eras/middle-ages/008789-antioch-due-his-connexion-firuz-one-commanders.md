# Antioch

Antioch was a significant city of the ancient and medieval world, serving as a center for diverse populations, early Christian development, and various political dynasties.

## Early Population and Social History
The city was known for its largeness and hosted a substantial population of the Jewish nation, who were intermingled with Syria due to the proximity of the two. Following the reign of Antiochus Epiphanes—who had spoiled the temple and laid Jerusalem waste—subsequent kings provided the Jews of Antioch with a habitation of undisturbed tranquillity. These rulers restored brass donations to the synagogue and granted the Jewish population equal privileges of citizenship with the Greeks. Consequently, the community grew in number, gloriously adorned their temple, and made many Greeks proselytes.

However, this period of stability ended during the time of Vespasian's arrival in Syria. A member of the Jewish nation named Antiochus, the son of the governor of the Jews at Antioch, acted as an informer. He accused his father and others of plotting to burn the city in a single night and identified foreign Jews as partners in the plan. This led to a violent reaction from the people of Antioch, who burned the accused on the theater and attacked the wider Jewish multitude.

## Early Church and Biblical Connections
Antioch played a pivotal role in the early Christian church. It is noted as the location where the name "Christians" was first coined. Internal evidence suggests that Luke practiced his profession in Antioch (or Tarsus) and likely met Paul there. Luke's knowledge of the Gospel in Antioch, as recorded in the Acts of the Apostles, suggests a strong connection to the church in that city. Luke may have started his second great missionary enterprise with Paul from Antioch, potentially serving as his medical attendant.

The city was also a center for the translation of sacred texts. The Peshito is recognized by some scholars as having been produced by Rabbula with the assistance of the contemporary Greek text of the Antiochene Church. Additionally, during the late 2nd century (A.D. 190-203), while Serapion was bishop of Antioch, a translation of the "separated Gospels" (*Evangelion da Mepharreshe*) was created using manuscripts available in the city. A 6th-century lectionary, once thought to be from Jerusalem, has also been identified as originating from the neighborhood of Antioch, likely produced during Justinian's attempts to abolish Judaism.

## Political and Military History
In the Hellenistic period, Antioch featured in triumphal processions, including one that included a corps of *Basilikoi paides* numbering 600 strong. In later eras, the city was a focal point for Norman ambitions during the First Crusade. Bohemund I, the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, served as the real leader of the First Crusade from Constantinople to Antioch. Bohemund I, who had previously commanded Normans in Thessaly and fought against Alexius Comnenus, sought to carve out an eastern principality for himself.

The principality of Antioch continued through later generations of the Bohemund line. Bohemund VI, who succeeded his father in 1251, established himself in Antioch in 1252. He maintained a connection to Cyprus through the marriage of Bohemund V and Alice, the widow of Hugh I. Bohemund VI allied himself with the Mongols to oppose the Egyptian sultan; however, in 1268, he lost Antioch to Bibars.

## Other Notable Occurrences
The city served as a destination for high-ranking figures of the era. For instance, Herod conducted Archelaus as far as Antioch after presenting him with seventy talents, a golden throne set with precious stones, eunuchs, and a concubine named Pannychis.
