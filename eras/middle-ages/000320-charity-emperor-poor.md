# OF THE CHARITY OF THE EMPEROR TO THE POOR

## Provisions for the Indigent in Cambaluc
The Great Kaan implemented a systematic program of charity for the poor residing in his city of Cambaluc. This initiative involved the selection of a vast number of families living in a state of indigence. These families varied in size, with some consisting of six, eight, or ten members, or other numbers as happened. To support these households, the Emperor ensured that each selected family was supplied annually with wheat and other corn in quantities sufficient to last the entire year, a practice he maintained without fail.

In addition to these annual supplies, the Emperor established a daily dole at the Court. This service provided a large, hot loaf of bread to every person who chose to attend, with no one being denied. This daily distribution served approximately 30,000 people every day throughout the year. Because of this perceived goodness and pity for the poor, the beneficiaries viewed the Emperor with such reverence that they worshipped him as if he were God.

## Management of Food Stores and Dearth
Beyond direct alms, the Great Kaan managed the distribution of necessities to his people during times of dearth by creating stores during periods of cheapness. The Archbishop of Soltania, writing in A.D. 1330, described the Emperor as compassionate and pitiful, noting that when dearth affected the land, the Emperor would open his garners to provide wheat and rice at half the price charged by others. 

Records of these measures are found in the annals of the Dynasty. Similar practices of maintaining public granaries were attributed to the sovereigns of the T’ang Dynasty in old Arab Relations. While these systems were established to aid the people, later accounts in the *Lettres Edifiantes* provided an unfavorable view of the public granaries, citing instances of rascality associated with them.

The texts teach that those who spend their wealth—whether in secret or public, by night or by day—will have their reward with their Lord and shall be free from fear and grief. Furthermore, the Qur'an contrasts charity with usury, stating that Allah deprives usury of blessing but causes charitable deeds to prosper. The practice of regular charity, alongside the establishment of prayer and belief in righteousness, is presented as a path to reward from the Lord. In the description of successful believers, being active in deeds of charity is listed as a primary characteristic.

## Other Imperial and Charitable Contributions
In other historical contexts, imperial figures have contributed to charitable medical institutions. In Russia, the emperor Alexander III provided 5,000 roubles toward the founding of a homoeopathic hospital in St. Petersburg, an institution established to commemorate the virtues of emperor Alexander II. This hospital included sixteen free beds, three of which were specifically named in honor of emperor Nicholas, the empress Maria Feodorovna, and emperor Alexander III. Additionally, an imperial edict was issued on January 28, 1899, granting public service rights to the doctors of the Charity Society's hospital and dispensaries.
