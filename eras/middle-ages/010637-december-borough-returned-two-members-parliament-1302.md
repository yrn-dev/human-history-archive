# Parliamentary Representation of Selected Boroughs and Counties

The history of parliamentary representation across various English and Irish regions reflects a complex evolution of franchise rights, the rise and fall of "rotten boroughs," and the systemic restructuring of government through various Reform and Redistribution Acts.

## The Borough of Arundel
Arundel, originally known as Harundell, was first mentioned in 877 in the will of King Alfred, who left it to his nephew Aethelm. By the time of Edward the Confessor, the town consisted of a fortification or earthwork and a mill. Following the Conquest, it was granted to Roger de Montgomery, who constructed a castle on the site of the ancient earthwork. Domesday records describe it as a thriving port and borough.

The borough's parliamentary history began in 1302, when it started returning two members to parliament. This arrangement continued until 1832, when the Reform Act reduced its membership to one. By 1868, Arundel was disfranchised entirely. Local governance was further defined in 1586 when Elizabeth acknowledged the rights of the burgesses and mayor to be a body corporate. In 1688, James II confirmed a charter that incorporated the borough with a mayor, 12 burgesses, and 4 aldermen.

## Representation in Cornwall and Kent
Cornwall was noted for its high number of "rotten boroughs." In 1295, Edward I summoned two members for the county and granted the same privilege to six boroughs: Bodmin, Helston, Liskeard, Lostwithiel, Truro, and Launceston. Between 1547 and 1584, fifteen more towns and villages received the franchise to increase the power of the crown against the House of Commons. Consequently, by 1821, Cornwall sent 44 members to parliament. The Reform Act of 1832 led to the disfranchisement of fourteen Cornish boroughs, including East Looe, West Looe, Fowey, Camelford, Bossiney, and Lostwithiel. Even after this, some boroughs like St Ives, Liskeard, Helston, Bodmin, and Launceston remained separately represented until 1885.

In Kent, the county returned two members in 1290. By 1295, Rochester, Canterbury, and Tunbridge were represented, though Tunbridge ceased making returns after that date. Representation expanded in the 16th century with Queenborough (1572) and Maidstone (1552). The Reform Act of 1832 changed the county to four members in two divisions and disfranchised Queenborough. Subsequent acts in 1868 and 1885 increased county representation to six and then eight members, respectively, while reducing the representation of Rochester, Maidstone, and Canterbury to one member each.

## Representation in Dorset, Buckinghamshire, and Durham
Dorset returned two members in 1290. As chief towns gained representation, the total rose to twenty members (the county and nine boroughs) by 1572. The Reform Act of 1832 reduced the county to three members and disfranchised Corfe Castle. Lyme Regis was disfranchised in 1868, and all remaining boroughs were disfranchised by the Redistribution Act of 1885.

Buckinghamshire was first represented by two members in 1290. By 1603, the county and its boroughs returned a total of fourteen members. This was reduced to eleven by the Reform Act of 1832, and by 1885, the boroughs lost representation entirely, leaving the county with three members for three divisions.

In Durham, a 1614 bill to secure representation for the city, county, and the borough of Barnard Castle was opposed by the bishop. The county was not summoned to return members until 1654. Following the Restoration, the city and county returned two members each. The 1832 Reform Act granted representation to the boroughs of Sunderland, South Shields, and Gateshead. From 1868 to 1885, the boroughs of Hartlepool, Stockton, and Darlington each returned one member.

## Irish Parliamentary Representation
In Ireland, representation varied by county and borough. In County Kerry, before the Union, the county returned eight members to the Irish parliament: two for the county and two each for the boroughs of Ardfert, Dingle, and Tralee. After the Union, this was reduced to three (two for the county and one for Tralee), and later expanded to four divisions, each returning one member.

Louth was represented in the Irish parliament by ten borough members and two county members. Currently, it is divided into north and south divisions, each returning one member.

In Kilkenny, both Irishtown and Kilkenny originally returned two members each to the Irish parliament. Since the Union, only one member has been returned to Westminster for the city of Kilkenny.

## The Borough of Honiton
Honiton was founded before 1217 by William de Vernon. Its parliamentary history was intermittent; it was represented by two members in 1300 and 1311, but did not return members again until 1640. From 1640, it returned two members until it was disfranchised by the act of 1868. The portreeve served as the returning officer and chief magistrate until the borough's incorporation in 1846.
