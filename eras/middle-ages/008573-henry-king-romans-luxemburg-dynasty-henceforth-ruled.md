# Henry, King of the Romans and the House of Luxemburg

## Henry VII and the Rise of the Luxemburgs
The House of Luxemburg descended from Count Conrad, who died in 1086. The fortunes of the dynasty were significantly advanced through the election of Count Henry IV as German king in 1308. Following this election, he was crowned emperor under the title of Henry VII. 

## Transition of Power in Bohemia
During the early 14th century, Bohemia experienced a period of instability. Following the death of Wenceslas II in 1305, his son and successor, Wenceslas III, ruled for only one year before being murdered by unknown assassins in 1306. This event marked the end of the Premyslide dynasty's rule over Bohemia.

In the aftermath, Albert, king of the Romans, declared Bohemia to be a vacant fief of the Empire. Through intimidation, Albert induced the Bohemians to elect his son, Rudolph, as their sovereign; however, Rudolph's reign lasted only one year. Despite claims by Habsburg princes to a hereditary right to the throne, the Bohemians asserted their right to elect their own sovereign and chose Henry, duke of Carinthia, who had married a daughter of King Wenceslas II. Henry eventually became unpopular due to accusations that he unduly favored German settlers in Bohemia, leading to the decision to depose him.

## The Luxemburg Dynasty in Bohemia
Following the deposition of Henry, duke of Carinthia, the Bohemians elected John of Luxemburg, the son of Henry, king of the Romans. This election established the Luxemburg dynasty's rule over Bohemia, which continued until the dynasty's extinction upon the death of Sigismund in 1437.

King John strengthened his connection to Bohemia by marrying Princess Elizabeth, a daughter of Wenceslas II. Despite this, John showed little interest in the country, spending most of his life in other lands. His military campaigns spanned from Lithuania in the north to Italy in the south. His influence was so extensive that it became proverbial "that nothing could be done in the world without the help of God and of the king of Bohemia." John maintained a policy of close alliance with France, the country for which he felt the most sympathy. He died fighting as an ally of France at the battle of Crecy in 1346.

## Imperial Successors and Family Branches
The House of Luxemburg continued to hold significant power through John's descendants. His eldest son, Charles IV, became emperor. Two of Charles IV's sons, Sigismund and Wenceslaus, subsequently succeeded to the imperial throne. Additionally, Jobst, the margrave of Moravia and a nephew of Charles IV, was elected German king in 1410 in opposition to Sigismund. Other notable family members included Baldwin, the archbishop of Treves (1285-1354), who was active in imperial affairs.

The dynasty also had a French branch descended from Waleran (d. 1288), a younger son of Count Henry II. This branch included:
* **Guy (d. 1371):** Created count of Ligny in 1367.
* **Waleran (d. 1417):** Constable of France in 1412, who participated in the French civil wars.
* **Peter, count of Brienne (d. 1433):** Succeeded Waleran and aligned with the English.
* **John (d. 1440):** A supporter of England and governor of Paris under Henry V, who sold Joan of Arc to the English.
* **Louis:** Initially fought for England before entering French service as constable around 1440; he was beheaded in 1475 after being accused of treachery by King Louis XI.

The male line of this elder French branch became extinct in 1482 and merged into the house of Bourbon-Vendôme.

## Later History of the Luxemburg Territory
The county of Luxemburg, originally under the rule of Conrad (d. 1086), remained under the control of his descendants until the death of Emperor Sigismund in 1437. Through the marriage of Sigismund's daughter, Elizabeth, to the German king Albert II, the territory (which had become a duchy in 1354) passed to the house of Habsburg. It was later seized in 1443 by Philip III the Good, duke of Burgundy.

The territory returned to the Habsburgs in 1477 through the marriage of Maximilian I to Mary, daughter of Charles the Bold. It eventually passed to Philip II of Spain in 1555. After various transfers, including a period of French conquest starting in 1795, the Congress of Vienna (1814-1815) established Luxemburg as a grand-duchy assigned to William I, king of the Netherlands. Following the partition of 1831 and the treaty of April 19, 1838, the region's status evolved. By the treaty of London on May 11, 1867, the Prussian garrison was withdrawn, the fortress dismantled, and the great powers guaranteed the neutrality of Luxemburg as a sovereign and independent state.
