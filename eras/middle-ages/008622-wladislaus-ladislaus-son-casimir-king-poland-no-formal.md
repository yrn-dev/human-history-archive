# Vladislav of Poland

Vladislav (also referred to as Wladislaus or Ladislaus), the son of King Casimir IV of Poland, served as the King of Bohemia during the late 15th century. His ascent to the Bohemian throne was the result of shifting European political alliances and the internal struggles of the Bohemian state.

## Election to the Bohemian Throne
The path to Vladislav's kingship began during the reign of George of Podebrad. While George had initially intended to establish a national dynasty, his later struggles against King Matthias of Hungary led him to offer the succession of the Bohemian throne to Vladislav. Although no formal agreement was established, the death of George of Podebrad triggered a conflict over the succession. Many Bohemian nobles supported the claim of Matthias of Hungary, who had already proclaimed himself king of Bohemia.

Following protracted negotiations, Vladislav was elected at Kutna Hora on May 27, 1471. This election represented a victory for the national party in Bohemia and reflected the strong anti-clerical sentiment of the era. While Matthias of Hungary was an unconditional adherent of Rome, the Polish envoys representing Vladislav promised that he would maintain the Compacts. This political maneuver was supported by Vladislav's father, Casimir IV, who utilized a shift in the Curia's attitude—driven by the Pope's desire to remove the Hussite king George Podebrad to form a league against the Turk—to procure his son's election.

## Conflict with Matthias of Hungary
The early years of Vladislav's reign were defined by a struggle with Matthias of Hungary, who continued to maintain his own claim to the Bohemian throne. This resulted in a period of prolonged and desultory warfare. The conflict persisted until 1478, when a treaty was concluded at Olmutz. Under the terms of this treaty, Bohemia was secured for Vladislav. Matthias of Hungary was permitted to retain the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—specifically Lusatia, Silesia, and Moravia—for the remainder of his lifetime, with the agreement that these territories would be restored to Bohemia upon his death.

## Religious and Domestic Policy
Vladislav remained faithful to his promise to maintain the Compacts and did not interfere with Bohemians receiving communion in both kinds. However, his broader policy is characterized as reactionary regarding both religious controversies and matters of state. 

In Prague, the king appointed government officials from the section of the Utraquist party that was closest to Rome. Simultaneously, a severe persecution was carried out against the Bohemian Brethren, who were known as the extreme Hussites. These actions led to serious riots in Prague, where advanced Hussites stormed the city's three town halls. In response to the threatened reaction, nobles of the Hussite faith formed a league for their own protection. Peace was temporarily restored following a meeting of all the estates at Kutna Hora in 1485.
