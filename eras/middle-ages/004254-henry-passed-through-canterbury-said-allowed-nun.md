# Elizabeth Barton and the Convent of St Sepulchre

## Early Life and Religious Mania
Elizabeth Barton, known as "the maid of Kent," was born in 1506 at Aldington, Kent. In her nineteenth year, an illness led to hysteria and religious mania; she was described as a neurotic girl who suffered from epilepsy. At this time, she served as a servant for Thomas Cobb, who was the steward of an estate near Aldington owned by William Warham, the archbishop of Canterbury.

During her recovery, Barton entered into trances that could last for several days. While in these states, she uttered ravings that were viewed by local country folk as being of "marvellous holiness in rebuke of sin and vice," leading them to believe she was inspired. Thomas Cobb reported these occurrences to the parish priest, Richard Masters, who then informed Archbishop Warham. Barton later confessed during her trial that once she recovered and noticed the admiration of the local people, she was cunning enough to feign these trances to continue her prophecies.

## Entry into the Convent
As Barton's fame grew, Archbishop Warham took action in 1526 by instructing the prior of Christ Church, Canterbury, to send two monks to investigate the matter. One of these monks, Edward Bocking, secured her admission as a nun to the convent of St Sepulchre's in Canterbury. The convent of St Sepulchre was a Benedictine foundation established around the year 1100, though no remains of the site exist.

Under the instruction of Bocking, Barton's prophecies became more remarkable. She asserted that she was in direct communication with the Virgin Mary, a claim that attracted many pilgrims.

## Political Influence and Conflict with Henry
Barton began to direct her utterances toward political matters. She caused a widespread and profound sensation by declaring that if Henry persisted in his intention to divorce Catherine, he "should no longer be king of this realm ... and should die a villain's death." Her influence was significant enough that Sir Thomas More and Fisher, the bishop of Rochester, corresponded with her.

In 1532, while returning from France, Henry passed through Canterbury. It is said that he allowed Barton to force herself into his presence, where she attempted to terrify him into abandoning his marriage.

## Confession and Downfall
Following the solemnization of the marriage in May 1533, Barton's utterances became increasingly treasonable. She was subsequently examined before Cranmer, who had succeeded Warham as archbishop in March. During this examination, she confessed.
