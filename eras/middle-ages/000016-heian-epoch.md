# THE HEIAN EPOCH

The Heian epoch is a sharply defined period of Japanese history spanning 398 years. It began in 794 with the transfer of the Imperial palace from Nara to Kyoto and concluded in 1192 with the establishment of an administrative capital at Kamakura. The city of Kyoto was given the name "Heian-jo," which translates to "Castle of Peace," shortly after it became the residence of the Mikado.

## Governance and Leadership
The first ruler of the Heian epoch was Kwammu. He had been specifically selected for the throne by his father, Konin, following the recommendation of Fujiwara Momokawa, who believed the young prince possessed the qualities essential for a ruler of men. Historians remain divided on whether Kwammu's actual career as Emperor fully met the promise he showed as a prince.

## Court Life and Amusements
Court society during the Heian epoch was characterized by extravagance, elaboration, and a passion for literature. Pastimes were similar in kind to those of the preceding Nara epoch but differed in their degree of intensity.

A significant "furore" at Court was the couplet-tournament, known as *uta awase*. In the first half of the epoch, Emperors such as Koko hosted splendid verse-making parties in richly decorated palaces, often featuring beautiful flowers. During this earlier phase, the ladies and gentlemen of the Court remained separated, sitting on opposite sides of the room. In the later part of the epoch, the composition of love letters became a favorite competitive amusement; while these fictitious letters followed implicit canons of elegant phraseology, their actual contents were frankly indecent.

Other courtly interests included football and the keeping of animals as pets, particularly dogs and cats. These pets were often given official titles and human names. The treatment of animals reached a high level of consideration; for example, kittens born at the palace at the end of the tenth century were treated similarly to Imperial infants. Courtiers sent ceremonial presents to the cat-mother after childbirth, and a lady-in-waiting was appointed as a guardian to the kittens. When these pets died, they were given elaborate funerals.

## Arts and Culture
The Heian epoch saw a remarkable development in music and dance, driven by the growth of luxury and extravagant entertainment. Musical instruments increased in both variety and complexity; while the six-stringed harp or wagon was used, more complex lutes or harps with thirteen or twenty-five strings were also employed. In total, as many as twenty kinds of musical instruments and several varieties of dance were present. Most dances were pantomimic and foreign in origin, classified as being Chinese, Korean, or Hindu, alongside adaptations of these foreign styles and older native forms.

In the realm of literature, the epoch is connected to the wide use of *kana*, a phonetic script consisting of a syllabary of forty-seven symbols derived from abbreviated Chinese ideographs. There are two varieties: *hiragana* and *kata-kana*. The latter, meaning "side kana," consists of fragments or sides of whole Chinese ideographs. While records suggest *kata-kana* was devised by Makibi and *hiragana* by Kobo Daishi (Kukai), these attributions are doubted. It is certain that both were known before the close of the ninth century, though they were not largely used until the Heian epoch, and even then, they were used almost entirely by women. Notable literary works from this period include the *Makura Soshi* by Sei Shonagon and *The Genji Monogatari* by Murasaki Shikibu.

## Architecture and Society
The architectural style of the dwellings of nobles during this epoch is referred to as "Shindenzukubi." The period also saw the presence of the Ariwara, an *uji* of princely descent who were eligible for high office. Notable figures from this group included the poets Yukihira (818–893), who founded an academy in 881, and Narihira (825–882).
