# English Literature and the Era of King Alfred

The development of English literature is marked by a transition from early vernacular forms to a structured prose tradition, a process that reached a critical turning point during the reign of King Alfred. While the origins of English poetry extend back to heathen times, the effective beginning of English prose is associated with Alfred's reign.

## Early Vernacular Prose
Prior to the reign of Alfred, vernacular prose existed, though it was limited in scope. The laws of Aethelberht of Kent are noted for their high antiquity, characterized by a crude and elliptical style; these were known to Bede, although it is considered unlikely they were written during the lifetime of King Aethelberht (who died in 616) or Augustine (who died in A.D. 604). Other early kings of Wessex and Kent followed this precedent by publishing laws in the native tongue. Additionally, Bede translated the beginning of the gospel of John (up to vi. 9), and some prose annals of pre-Alfredian date likely served as the foundation for the early part of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.

Despite these early efforts, Latin remained the preferred vehicle for works of literary pretension between the 7th and mid-9th centuries. The eventual creation of a vernacular prose literature was facilitated by a decline in the scholarship held by the English clergy since the days of Bede and Aldhelm. King Alfred sought to combat the ignorance of spiritual guides, as well as the need for instruction among the laity.

## The Literary Contributions of Alfred
King Alfred is regarded as the point where literature practically begins. He employed scholars to translate various works, most of which were religious in spirit. These include the *Dialogues* (rendered by Bishop Werferth) and *St Gregory's Pastoral Care*, both of which were expressly addressed to the priesthood. Alfred also produced a translation of the secular *History of Orosius*. 

Alfred is identified as an original writer in several instances:
* The preface to *Pastoral Care*.
* The amplification and free rendering of the *Soliloquies* of Augustine and the *Consolation* of Boethius.
* The insertion of accounts regarding Northern peoples and lands within the *Orosius*.
* The creation of his own Laws, which were preceded by a collection of laws from Ine of Wessex, a 7th-century predecessor.
* The beginnings of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.

## Post-Alfredian Literature and Language
Following Alfred, Old English prose was produced entirely by clerical authors. This body of work consists largely of homilies, saints' lives, and translations from Latin. While Aelfric is noted for his brilliant achievement in developing the native language's capacity for literary expression, much of the literature of this period is viewed as having primary importance from a philological perspective. Other notable works include the sermons of Archbishop Wulfstan, characterized by eloquence and earnestness, and a translation of the gospels executed around the year 1000.

The term *Englisc* (or *English*) originally referred to the dialect of the Angles of Northumbria, where literary culture first appeared. Eventually, it became the general name for the vernacular as opposed to Latin. Even when the West-Saxon dialect of Alfred became the classical or literary form of speech, it retained the name *English*. The term "Anglo-Saxon" was later used by 16th and 17th-century scholars to distinguish the language of Aelfric and Alfred from their own contemporary English, a gap that later scholarship eventually bridged to show historical continuity.

## Legal Continuity and the Norman Conquest
English legal history exhibits a singular continuity starting from the day of Alfred. His edicts strongly resemble Frankish capitularies, suggesting imitation. This tradition of kings publishing laws continued through the 11th century, with the dooms of Canute the Dane being among the most comprehensive statutes in Europe at the time. This tradition persisted even after the Norman Conquest, as the Norman duke subdued a land where kings were still expected to publish laws.

The Norman Conquest brought a significant influx of French and Latin elements into the English vocabulary. While the primary "words of common life" preserved their identity, the scientific, poetic, and rhetorical terms of Old English died out, replaced by abstract and general terms derived from Greek, Latin, and French.

## Modern Scholarship on Early English Literature
In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, several scholars contributed to the study of this period. Stopford Augustus Brooke published *The History of Early English Literature* (2 vols., 1892), which covers the period down to the accession of Alfred, as well as *English Literature from the Beginnings to the Norman Conquest* (1898). Additionally, Hiram Corson, a professor of Anglo-Saxon and English literature at Cornell University, authored a *Hand-Book of Anglo-Saxon and Early English* in 1871.
