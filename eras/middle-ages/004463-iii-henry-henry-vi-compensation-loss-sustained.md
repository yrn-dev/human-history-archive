# Henry V and Henry VI

## Military Organization and Recruitment
During the century of intermittent warfare between France and England, the methods of recruiting and sustaining armies underwent a significant transition. While the feudal system of tenures had provided a permanent provision of strongholds and men-at-arms, it was primarily adapted for defensive warfare or short-term local campaigns. For the distant and prolonged expeditions required when kingdoms were in conflict, the resources of feudalism were found to be inadequate.

Consequently, there was a shift toward commuting personal services into pecuniary payments. In England, this had previously developed from the reign of Henry II to Edward II as a regular war-tax known as *scutage* or *escuage*. By the time of Henry V and Henry VI, the armies were recruited and sustained to a very great extent based on the principle of contract, moving away from the traditional feudal militia raised by writ to sheriffs, earls, and barons.

## Royal Charters and Basingstoke
Henry V and Henry VI are recorded as having confirmed certain privileges and grants for the town of Basingstoke. In 1256, a charter from Henry III had granted the men of Basingstoke the hundred and manor of that name, along with other privileges; these were later confirmed by Edward III, Henry V, and Henry VI.

Additionally, Henry VI specifically granted a fair to be held at Whitsun near the chapel of the Holy Ghost in Basingstoke.

## Legal Concepts of Compensation
While not tied to the specific reigns of Henry V or VI, the archive contains broader legal principles regarding compensation. In primitive law, compensation served as a substitute for personal retaliation, similar to the Anglo-Saxon *wer-gild*. Under modern legal systems, the objective of compensation is to place an injured person as closely as possible in the position they would have occupied had the injury not occurred, with the amount determined by the actual loss sustained.

In the context of contract law, a party suffering from a breach of contract is entitled to compensation for loss or damage that naturally arose in the usual course of things or which the parties knew was likely to result. This compensation does not extend to remote or indirect loss, and the estimation of damage must account for the means of remedying the inconvenience caused by the non-performance.
