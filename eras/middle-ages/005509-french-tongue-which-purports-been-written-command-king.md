# Britton

## Overview
*Britton* is the title of the earliest summary of the law of England written in the French tongue. The work purports to have been written by the command of King Edward I.

## Authorship and Origin
The authorship and origin of *Britton* have been the subject of significant dispute. One theory attributes the work to John le Breton, the bishop of Hereford. This claim is based on a passage found in some manuscripts of the history of Matthew of Westminster. However, this theory faces difficulties because the bishop of Hereford died in 1275, while *Britton* contains allusions to several statutes passed after his death. Specifically, the work references the well-known statute *Quia emptores terrarum*, which was passed in 1290.

An alternative opinion, held by Selden, suggests that the book took its title from Henry de Bracton, who was the last of the chief justiciaries. In the fine Rolls, Bracton's name is occasionally spelled as "Bretton" or "Bratton." Selden proposed that *Britton* was a royal abridgment of Bracton's extensive work regarding the laws and customs of England, supplemented with certain statutes passed later. This theory is contested by the fact that the arrangement of the two works differs and only a small portion of Bracton's work is actually incorporated into *Britton*.

## Historical Manuscripts
A 14th-century manuscript of the work, which was once owned by Selden and is currently held in the Cambridge university library, titles the work *Summa de legibus Anglie que vocatur Bretone*. 

The work is also mentioned in the will of Andrew Horn, who served as the learned chamberlain of the city of London. In 1329, Horn bequeathed a book called "Bretoun" to the chamber of the Guildhall, along with another volume titled *Mirroir des Justices*.

## Linguistic Context
The use of the French tongue for legal summaries like *Britton* reflects the broader linguistic shift in England following the Norman Conquest. The Conquest established a foreign hierarchy, aristocracy, and court, making the Norman dialect of the French language the only polite medium of intercourse. During this period, the native tongue was despised as the language of a subject race and was used primarily by serfs and boors, largely ceasing to be written.

Because the privileged classes held the exclusive inheritance of law, science, theology, and art for generations, the French language became the primary source for terms expressing ideas above those of everyday life. Consequently, as English later revived as a literary language, it adopted a significant number of French words to replace forgotten native literary terms. This included Norman French words and Central or Parisian French words introduced under the early Plantagenets.
