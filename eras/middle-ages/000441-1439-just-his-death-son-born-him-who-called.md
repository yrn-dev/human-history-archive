# Events and Figures of 1439

The year 1439 was marked by significant transitions of power and notable deaths across Europe and Japan, involving the royal houses of Austria, France, and Scotland, as well as the Ashikaga shogunate.

## Transitions in Austria and the Holy Roman Empire
In 1439, King Albert II died. He had previously been Duke Albert V of Austria and had assumed the crowns of Hungary and Bohemia following the death of his father-in-law, Sigismund. Shortly after Albert II's death, a son was born to him named Ladislaus Posthumus. Ladislaus succeeded his father as the duke of Austria and as the king of both Bohemia and Hungary.

Due to the infancy of Ladislaus, Frederick—who had succeeded Albert II as the German king and was soon crowned Emperor Frederick III—became the regent of Austria. Frederick also acted as the guardian for Sigismund of Tirol, who was a minor. Sigismund of Tirol had succeeded his father, Frederick, in 1439. The reign of Emperor Frederick III was characterized by disorder and struggle, caused in part by the feebleness of his character and the desire of his brother, Albert, to share his dignities.

## Political Developments in France and Scotland
In France, Louis XI (the son of Charles VII and Marie of Anjou) began his political career in 1439. He was sent by his father to put down brigandage in Poitou and to direct the defense of Languedoc against the English. However, rebellious nobles induced Louis to betray this trust, leading him to place himself at the head of the Praguerie. Despite this rebellion, which was driven by his ambition and the nobles' proposal to make him regent, he was pardoned by Charles VII.

In Scotland, 1439 marked the death of Archibald, the 5th Earl of Douglas. Archibald had succeeded to his father's Scottish and English honours and had been made count of Longueville in Normandy after fighting at Bauge in 1421. At the time of his death, his two sons, David and William (the 6th Earl), were little more than boys. Following a mock trial on November 24, 1440, both sons were beheaded in the courtyard of Edinburgh Castle by order of Sir William Crichton, the lord chancellor of Scotland. This event dismantled the dangerous power of the Douglases, resulting in the crown acquiring the lordships of Bothwell and Annandale.

## Conflict and Suicide in Japan
In Japan, 1439 was the year of the death of Mochiuji, the kwanryo (governor-general of the Kwanto) and a member of the Ashikaga family. Mochiuji had sided with the Yamanouchi branch of the Uesugi. Following a campaign in which the Kamakura force was shattered by Norizane, Mochiuji fled to the temple Shomyo-ji in Kanazawa and requested to retire from the world.

The shogun refused to pardon Mochiuji, despite repeated petitions from Norizane, who had prayed that Mochiuji be forgiven and allowed to retire in favor of his son, Yoshihisa. Consequently, Mochiuji, his son, his uncle, and many others died by their own hands. This family feud led Norizane to attempt suicide out of remorse before eventually becoming a priest. Following these events, the title of governor-general of the Kwanto passed to the Uesugi. Three of Mochiuji's remaining sons fled to Koga in Shimosa; two were later killed by a Kamakura army, while the third, Shigeuji, survived.

## Other Notable Deaths
The year 1439 also saw the death of Sir John Grey, K.G., the eldest son of Reginald Grey, 2nd Baron Grey de Ruthyn. Sir John had served as the deputy of Ireland in 1427 and had fought at Agincourt. He predeceased his father, and his son, Edmund Grey, eventually succeeded his grandfather as Lord Grey de Ruthyn in 1440.
