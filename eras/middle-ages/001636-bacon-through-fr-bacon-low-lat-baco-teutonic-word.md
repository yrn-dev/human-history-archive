# BACON

## Etymology and Definition
The term bacon derives from a Teutonic word cognate with "back" (such as the Middle High German *backe* and Old High German *pacho*), meaning buttock or flitch of bacon. It entered the language through the Low Latin *baco* and Old French *bacon*. It refers to the flesh of the back and sides of a pig that has been cured through smoking, pickling, drying, and salting.

## Roger Bacon and John Baconthorpe
Roger Bacon was a figure of significant historical and philosophical interest, with his work preserved in several important manuscripts. These include the *Communia Naturalium* (found in the British Museum, the Bodleian, University College libraries at Oxford, and the Mazarin library at Paris), *De Communibus Mathematicae*, *Baconis Physica*, and the *Compendium Studii Theologiae*. Other fragments include the *Summulae Dialectices* and a fragment titled *Quinta Pars Compendii Theologiae*. A note in the *Verb. Abbrev.* indicates that Roger Bacon was a disciple of brother Albert.

John Baconthorpe (died 1346), also known as "the Resolute Doctor," was a learned Carmelite monk born in Baconthorpe, Norfolk. He is believed to have been the grandnephew of Roger Bacon. After being raised at the Carmelite monastery of Blakeney, he studied in Paris and Oxford, where he was recognized as the "Princeps" of the Averroists, though Renan suggests he was merely attempting to justify Averroism against charges of heterodoxy. In 1329, he became the twelfth provincial of the English Carmelites. He is noted for advocating the subordination of the clergy to the king, a position that appeared to anticipate Wycliffe. In 1333, he traveled to Rome, where he initially supported the pope's authority regarding divorce cases before retracting that opinion. He died in London in 1346. His primary work, *Doctoris resoluti Joannis Bacconis Anglici Carmelitae radiantissimi opus super quattuor sententiarum libris*, was published in 1510 and remained studied at Padua nearly three centuries later.

## Francis Bacon
Francis Bacon, also referred to as Bacon von Verulam, is a central figure in the history of philosophy and natural science. His philosophical work involves the concept of *Idola*, a term borrowed from Plato. In Bacon's usage, *Idola* are contrasted with Platonic Ideas, representing false appearances—the fleeting, transient images of real things.

Bacon's theories on "form" and his classification of causes are noted to have roots in Aristotle, specifically regarding the phrase "better known in the order of nature" (*notiora natura* or *notiora naturae*). His scientific method has been a subject of intense scholarly debate. While J. v. Liebig criticized Baconian methods and the results found in *Sylva Sylvarum*, claiming the method relied on undetermined perceptions of the senses and imagination, other scholars like K. Fischer have argued that Liebig's criticisms actually describe the very Baconian doctrine he sought to attack.

Bacon's influence on the scientific movement was extensive. He was regarded highly by leaders such as Gassendi, Descartes, and S. Sorbiere, the latter of whom credited Bacon with powerfully soliciting the interests of physics and encouraging the world to conduct experiments. In France, Voltaire and the encyclopaedists hailed him as "le pere de la philosophie experimentale," and d'Alembert described him as the most eloquent, universal, and greatest of philosophers. Other critical testimonies come from Huygens, who noted his "bonnes methodes," and Leibnitz, who called him "divini ingenii vir." In England, his work *New Atlantis* provided significant impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society. Dr. Thomas Sprat stated that Bacon possessed the "true imagination of the whole extent" of the society's enterprise.

## Other Notable Individuals
The archive records several other individuals with the name Bacon:
* **Delia Bacon**: A woman described by Nathaniel Hawthorne as "gifted" and the subject of a sketch by her nephew, Theodore Bacon.
