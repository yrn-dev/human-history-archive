# De Morali Philosophia

*De Morali Philosophia* is the seventh part of the *Opus Majus*, a comprehensive encyclopaedic work authored by Roger Bacon (d. 1294). The *Opus Majus* was one of three large treatises—alongside the *Opus Minus* and *Opus Tertium*—that Bacon completed in approximately eighteen months and despatched to Pope Clement IV.

## Content and Relation to Other Works
The *De Morali Philosophia* focuses on moral philosophy. While it is the seventh part of the *Opus Majus*, it was not included in the edition produced by S. Jebb. However, the work is discussed at considerable length within the *Opus Tertium* (cap. xiv.). Additionally, extracts from *De Morali Philosophia* have been provided by E. Charles in his 1861 work, *Roger Bacon, sa vie, ses ouvrages, ses doctrines d'apres des textes inedits*.

The *Opus Majus* as a whole has been described by Whewell as both the Organum and the Encyclopaedia of the 13th century. It reflects Bacon's perception of the necessity of encyclopaedic treatment and the unity of science. Other parts of the *Opus Majus* include a section on the causes of error (sometimes called *De Utililate Scientiarum*), a fourth part titled *Specula Mathematica*, and a fifth part concerning perspective.

## Publication History
A version of *De Morali Philosophia* was printed in Dublin in 1860. This follows a history of various printed works by Bacon, which include the *Speculum Alchimiae* (1541), *De Mirabili Potestate Artis et Naturae* (1542), and the *Opus Majus ad Clementem IV*, which saw editions by S. Jebb in 1733 and J. H. Bridges in 1897.

## Context of Composition
Bacon composed the *Opus Majus* and its accompanying summaries following a command from Pope Clement IV (formerly the papal legate Guy de Foulques) in 1266. The Pope ordered Bacon to write a treatise on the sciences despite injunctions from Bacon's superiors in the Franciscan order. Bacon had previously spent ten years under supervision in Paris, where he was strictly prohibited from writing for publication.

The *Opus Majus* served as a preliminary work, establishing principles that Bacon intended to apply to the sciences in later compositions, such as the *Compendium Studii Philosophiae* (1271).
