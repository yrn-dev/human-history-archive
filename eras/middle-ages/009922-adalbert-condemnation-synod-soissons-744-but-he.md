# Adalbert of Neustria

## Overview and Conflict
Adalbert (also referred to as Aldebert) was a Frankish bishop of Neustria who became a central figure in a theological and disciplinary controversy during the 8th century. His activities caused significant disturbance within the region of Neustria, leading to a conflict with Boniface, who had been appointed as a legate by Pope Zacharias in 741.

## Claims and Practices
Adalbert gained a substantial following throughout Neustria by engaging in practices that diverged from established ecclesiastical norms. He claimed to have received his relics directly from angels, contrasting with the practice of receiving them from Rome, as Boniface had done. Additionally, Adalbert claimed to have performed miracles. 

His influence was further extended through the practice of planting crosses in open fields. This action drew people away from established churches and toward his own movement.

## Context of the Frankish Church Reformation
The opposition to Adalbert occurred during a broader effort to reform the Frankish church. Boniface, supported by Pippin and Carloman (the mayors of the palace who succeeded Charles Martel), was charged with this reformation. Boniface's career had been made possible by the protection of Charles, which allowed him to conduct a systematic crusade in heathen Germany. During this crusade, Boniface founded monasteries and churches, overturned idols, and baptized converts. 

Boniface's organizational work included the establishment of four bishoprics in Bavaria (Passau, Salzburg, Freising, and Regensburg) and four in the east Frankish church (Eichstadt, Buraburg, Wurzburg, and Erfurt), over which he placed his own monks. He also founded the abbey of Fulda as a center for German monastic culture, implementing a rule stricter than that of the Benedictines.

## Related Theological Controversies
The period was marked by various disciplinary and theological disputes. While Boniface found more success in France against Adalbert, he faced a different challenge in Salzburg with Virgil, an Irish bishop. Virgil held the belief that there were worlds other than our own, a view characterized as one of several heresies. Despite these leanings toward science and his conflict with Boniface, Virgil was canonized after his death.
