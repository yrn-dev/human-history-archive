# Responsories

Within the liturgical structure of the Roman Catholic Church, responsories are a constituent element of the offices for the canonical hours. They are part of the daily service contained within the Breviary, a book that serves as an epitome of the offices for these hours.

## Nature and Function
Responsories are similar in form to antiphons, which are short liturgical forms of patristic or biblical origin used to introduce a psalm. However, while antiphons serve as introductions, responsories are positioned at the end of the psalm. Originally, the responsory functioned as the reply of the congregation or the choir to the precentor who had recited the psalm.

Responsories, along with antiphons and versicles, grew out of the Psalter, which serves as the groundwork and backbone of the Breviary. In addition to the standard responsories, the liturgy employs "versicles," which are described as short responsories used specifically after the "little chapters" (very short lessons read at the various hours).

## Liturgical Context
The responsories are one of several elements that compose each hour of the office. These constituent parts include:
* Psalms and canticles
* Antiphons
* Responsories
* Hymns
* Lessons
* Little chapters
* Versicles
* Collects

The use of these elements is organized within the *Proprium de Tempore*, which contains the office for the seasons of the Christian year, spanning from Advent to Trinity. This section of the service provides the proper responsories, antiphons, and hymns for every week-day and Sunday, alongside a course of daily Scripture-reading.

## Development and Sources
The evolution of the Breviary began with the separation of the Psalter into a choir-book. Initially, the leader of the choir or the bishop chose psalms as they saw fit. By the 4th century, psalms began to be grouped together, a process accelerated by monastic practices of reciting the 150 psalms daily. To manage the time required for this, monks divided the day into hours and allotted portions of the Psalter to each.

As these psalter choir-books developed, additions were made to assist those who were not skilled in metrical compositions or improvisation. These additions included responses, collects (short prayers), and antiphons. By the 12th century, liturgical author Jean Beleth listed several books necessary for the conduct of the canonical office, including the *Antiphonarium* and the *Psalterium*, which contributed to the structured use of these liturgical forms.
