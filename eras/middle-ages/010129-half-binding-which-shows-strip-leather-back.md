# Half-Binding

Half-binding is a medieval method of bookbinding characterized by a strip of leather that covers the back of the book and projects a short distance over the boards, leaving the remainder of the boards uncovered. This style of binding evolved as a means of protecting the structural components of the book's spine.

## Structural Development
The emergence of the half-binding was a result of the development of sewing techniques for quires. Once the method of sewing quires together was well understood, it became apparent that the projecting bands at the back of the book required protection. To address this, strips of leather were fastened across the back after all the quires had been joined.

Additionally, because vellum leaves had a tendency to curl strongly, binders began placing strong wooden boards on each side of the book to counteract this effect. The loose ends of the sewing bands were fastened to these boards, which functioned as hinges. The protective leather strip at the back was then drawn over the boards far enough to cover these hinges, resulting in the "half-binding" configuration. To keep these boards closed, clasps were usually employed in the front.

## Evolution into Whole Binding
The leather strip used in half-bindings eventually developed to cover the entirety of the boards. This progression led to what is known as "whole" binding. This transition provided binders with fine, flat pieces of leather that served as an ideal field for artistic decoration.

## Related Binding Concepts and Materials
The history of bookbinding includes various other methods and materials that complemented or preceded the half-binding:

*   **Flexible Work:** An early sewing plan where gatherings or quires are fastened at the back around a strong strip of vellum or leather held at right angles to the line of the backs. This method is still utilized today for valuable books.
*   **Materials:** While early bindings utilized leather and vellum, later trade bindings frequently used sheepskin and rough calf. In the 17th century, English prayer-books and small New Testaments, Bibles, were sometimes covered in gold or silver thread and gay embroideries of coloured silks.
*   **Ornamentation:** Early leather ornamentation involved impressions from lines or small metal points, leading to the use of decorative stamps. This "blind" stamping—performed without gold leaf—was perfected during the 13th and 12th centuries in locations such as London, Cambridge, Oxford, and Durham. An example of this is the 12th-century binding of the Winchester Domesday Book. Later, the art of gold tooling on leather appeared, likely brought to Venice from the East, and was practiced in England by Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, until approximately 1558.

## Terminology
In the context of bookbinding, a "band" refers to a cord, tie, or rope used to fasten one thing to another, specifically the straps used to fasten sheets to the back of the book. The term is a variant of "bond" and derives from the Teutonic *bindan*.
