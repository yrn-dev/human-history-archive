# The Literary Works of Barbour

The literary output of Barbour is a subject of considerable controversy among scholars. Depending on the attribution of various long poems, Barbour is regarded either as the father of Scots poetry or as one of the most voluminous writers in Middle English, and certainly the most prolific of all Scots poets.

## The Brus and Accepted Works
The primary accepted work of Barbour is *The Brus*. This text has been subject to various modern editions, including those by J. Pinkerton (1790), who claimed his version was the first genuine edition because it was printed from the Advocates' Library text; Jamieson (1820); Cosmo Innes (1856); and W. W. Skeat (1870-1889), whose work was later reprinted by the Scottish Text Society between 1893 and 1895. Questions regarding the recension of the text have been explored by J. T. T. Brown in *The Wallace and The Bruce restudied* (1900).

## The Troy Fragments
There is a debate regarding the "Troy fragments," which consist of two preserved texts in Lydgate's *Troy-book* totaling 3,713 lines (595 and 3,118 lines respectively). These fragments deal entirely with Trojan matters and are believed by some to be based on Guido da Colonna's *Historia Destructionis Troiae*. 

The ascription of these fragments to Barbour was first made by Henry Bradshaw, the librarian of Cambridge University. However, the current consensus of critical opinion is against this attribution. Furthermore, there is a question of whether these fragments are the same as a "Treteis" mentioned by Wyntoun in *Chronicle* III. iii., which was described as a "genealogy" of "Brutus lynagis." Some doubt this identification because the scale of the Trojan story in the fragments would be an undue allowance for a Scottish genealogy.

## Legends of the Saints
Another work attributed to Barbour is a long Scots poem of over 33,000 lines titled *Legends of the Saints*, which was discovered in the university library of Cambridge by Henry Bradshaw. The poem draws from the *Legenda Aurea* and other legendaries. 

The ascription to Barbour was initially considered plausible due to the poem's style, dialect, and verse-length, as well as the fact that it excludes English saints while including St Ninian and St Machar (the patron saint of Aberdeen). C. Horstmann upholds Barbour's authorship and printed the text in two volumes (1881-1882). Conversely, W. M. Metcalfe disputed the claim in his 1896 edition. 

While later criticism has been divided—often basing negative judgments on vocabulary, assonance, and rhymes—it is considered certain that the author hailed from the north-east of Scotland. This is evidenced by a passage in the fortieth legend (St Ninian) mentioning a man named John Balormy, born in Elgin, Moray, whom the author claims to have known well. Despite this, the available data cannot definitively answer whether this author is Barbour.

## Other Attributed Works
Several other works have been linked to Barbour, often contingent upon the acceptance of the *Legends*:

*   **Gospel Story:** If Barbour wrote the *Legends*, it is concluded he also wrote a Gospel story featuring the later life of the Virgin. This is referenced in the *Legends* prologue as a book "of the birth of Jhesu criste" and a record of the "genology of our lady sanct Mary."
*   **The Buik of Alexander:** In recent years, some have attempted to name Barbour as the author of the *Buik of Alexander* (a translation of the *Roman d'Alexandre* and associated pieces, including the *Voeux du Paon*), known from a unique edition printed circa 1580 by Alexander Arbuthnot. This argument is based on parallel passages in *The Bruce* and *Alexander*, though critics argue this ignores vocabulary and epithets common to all writers of that period.
