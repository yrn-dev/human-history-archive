# Denmark

## Natural History and Wildlife
The wild birds and animals found in Denmark are consistent with those of central Europe. While larger quadrupeds are now extinct in the region, prehistoric evidence from "kitchen-middens" (_kjökkenmödding_) and other sites indicates that the wolf, the bear, the beaver, the wild boar, and the urochs existed after the arrival of humans. The red deer was once highly abundant; for example, a single hunt in Jutland in 1593 resulted in the killing of 1,600 head of deer, though the species is now found only in preserves. 

Among domestic animals, most usual varieties are found abundantly, though the goat is uncommon. The country's sea fisheries are of importance. While oysters were abundant in ancient times—as evidenced by shell moulds on the coast—they have disappeared from many localities, though they still exist in some places. The Gudenaa is noted as the only salmon river in Denmark.

## Medieval History and Monarchy
During the 10th and 11th centuries, Denmark was a source of significant military power and political influence in Scandinavia. In A.D. 954, King Harald Gormson provided a powerful army of Danes to support the sons of Eirik in an expedition to Norway. This army, combined with the troops that had followed Eirik's sons on previous expeditions, sailed from Vendil to Agder and proceeded northwards along the coast.

In the 11th century, the region was under the rule of King Canute the Great. Canute reigned as king of Denmark for twenty-seven years. For twenty-four of those years, he ruled over both England and Denmark simultaneously, and he also held rule over Norway for seven years. Following the death of King Canute the Great in England on November 13 (at the age of forty), his son Svein went to Denmark to share the government with his brother, Hardaknut. King Svein died in Denmark during the winter of A.D. 1036.
