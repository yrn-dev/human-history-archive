# Biological and Regional Developments of Size and Form

The development of size and form varies significantly across different biological families and geographical regions, ranging from the aquatic fauna of Asia to the avian and reptilian species of the New World.

## Aquatic Fauna of Southern Asia
The fresh waters of southern Asia are primarily inhabited by walking and climbing fishes, siluroids, simple or spined eels, and carp. The *Siluridae* find their chief development in tropical regions, though a single *Silurus* species is found across Africa, southern Asia, and Europe. 

The *Cyprinidae*, or carp, are largely represented in southern Asia, where they reach sizes not found in Europe. For example, a *Barbus* captured in the Tigris weighed 300 lb. The chief development of the carp family, in terms of both size and the number of forms, occurs in mountain regions with temperate climates, whereas smaller species are located in low-lying rivers and hotter regions. Other notable fish in the region include the hilsa (of the *Clupeidae* family) and sturgeons, which are found in Asiatic Russia and an allied form in southern China. Additionally, walking or climbing fishes in Africa and south-eastern Asia are organized to breathe outside of water, allowing them to survive periodical drought and rain.

## Neotropical Avian and Reptilian Families
The Neotropical region is characterized by several remarkable families of animals. Among the birds, the *Trochilidae* (humming-birds) are particularly significant, with more than 400 species and nearly 150 genera. Other prominent families include:
*   **Tyrants (*Tyrannidae*):** Over 300 species and more than seventy genera.
*   **Tanagers (*Tanagndae*):** About 300 species and upwards of forty genera.
*   **Piculules (*Dendrocolaptidae*):** Over 200 species and as many genera.
*   **Ant-thrushes (*Formicariidae*):** Nearly 200 species and more than thirty genera.

Other defined groups in this region include the screamers (*Palamedeidae*), trumpeters (*Psophiidae*), todies (*Todidae*), motmots (*Monotidae*), jacamars (*Galbulidae*), toucans (*Rhamphastidae*), and tapaculos (*Pteroptochidae*), as well as the sun-bittern (*Eurypyga*) and seriema (*Cariama*).

In terms of reptiles, the *Iguanidae* (iguanas) comprise about 300 species and 50 genera. While three genera (*Brachylophus* in the Fiji Islands, and *Hoplurus* and *Chalarodon* in Madagascar) are exceptions, the family is primarily characteristic of the Neotropical region, extending from Patagonia north into British Columbia and California. Iguanas vary greatly in size, ranging from several feet in length to a few inches.

## Mammalian Structural Variations
In the family *Viverridae* (including mongooses, genets, and civet-cats), members exhibit a fuller dentition than the *Felidae*, though they do not approach the bodily size of the largest cats. This family is limited to the warmer parts of the Old World. Other mammalian families show diversity in external appearance and size, though structural differences may remain slight. Key differences often appear in the length of the tail, the form of the pupil, the development of the first upper premolar, the form of the cranium (specifically the nasal and adjoining bones), and the coloration or condition of the fur.

## Regional Geography and Administration
Beyond biological forms, the term "development" and "size" apply to geographical and administrative divisions. In northern France, the agricultural district of Brie is a plateau with an area of 2400 sq. m. Its altitude varies between 300 and 500 ft. in the west and 500 to 650 ft. in the east.

Historically, in various kingdoms, administrative districts were managed by royal reeves. In early times, the usual size of these districts was 600, 300, or 1200 hides, though larger divisions existed containing 7000, 5000, 3000, or 2000 hides.
