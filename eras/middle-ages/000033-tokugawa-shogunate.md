# THE TOKUGAWA SHOGUNATE

The Tokugawa Shogunate was the governing authority of Japan established by Tokugawa Ieyasu. Its history is divided into three primary periods: the First Period (1603–1680), spanning from Ieyasu to the fourth shogun, Ietsuna; the Middle Period (1680–1786), from the fifth shogun, Tsunayoshi, to the tenth, Ieharu; and the Late Period, characterized by the eleventh shogun, Ienari (1786–1838).

## Governance and Law
The administration of the shogunate was guided by a body of laws regarded as the Tokugawa Constitution. These laws were re-enacted by each successive shogun upon assuming office. During the investiture ceremony, all daimyo were summoned to Yedo to attend the Tokugawa palace and listen to the reading of these laws. While modifications and additions were made over time, the essential provisions remained unaltered. Until the third shogun, Iemitsu, a high Buddhist priest read the laws; thereafter, this duty was transferred to a representative of the Hayashi family. Infractions of these laws were punished mercilessly, and the shogun occasionally used the laws' loose phraseology for arbitrary interpretation to target enemies.

In addition to the general laws, the "Rules of the Imperial Court, and the Court Nobles" (Kinchu narabi ni Kugeshu Sho-hatto) was promulgated. This seventeen-article enactment bore the signatures of the shogun and the kwampaku and received Imperial sanction.

## The Shoguns and Political Evolution
The shogunate was founded by Tokugawa Ieyasu (1542–1616), who established the foundation of the house's power following the battle of Sekigahara and the distribution of fiefs. He was succeeded by Hidetada (1605–1622), who issued an anti-Christian edict in 1616 and ordered the deportation of Spaniards. The third shogun, Iemitsu (1622–1651), required feudal lords and nobles to reside at Yedo.

Under the fourth shogun, Ietsuna (1651–1680), a significant shift in power occurred. Administrative authority passed from the shogun to a minister, specifically Sakai Tadakiyo. Tadakiyo isolated Ietsuna from the outer world by forbidding access to the shogun's household ladies without ministerial permit. This era was marked by corruption and cupidity, with Tadakiyo and other officials accepting costly gifts and bribes from daimyo.

Later shoguns included Tsunayoshi, whose administration was heavily influenced by his favorite, Yanagisawa Yoshiyasu. Yoshiyasu rose from a low-class squire to a daimyo of Kai province, a position usually reserved for the Tokugawa family. Other notable shoguns included Ienobu (1709–1712), who attempted currency reforms with his adviser Hakuseki, and the seventh shogun, Ietsugu, who succeeded his father in 1713 but died in 1716 at the age of seven.

## Succession and Family Structure
To prevent rivalry and ensure the continuity of the shogunate, the Tokugawa established a system of branch families. Shogun Yoshimune assigned residences within the Tayasu and Hitotsubashi gates to Munetake and Munetada, granting them the titles of those gates and a revenue of 100,000 koku. These, along with a third family, formed the "Three Families" (Sanke), who held the privilege of providing an heir if the principal house failed. Later, Shogun Ieshige established "Three Branch Families" (Sankyo) by assigning his son, Yoshishige, to the Shimizu gate.

## Social and Religious Dynamics
The Tokugawa period saw a shift in the national intellectual landscape. Buddhism, which had been the national religion since the ninth century, was supplanted among the educated by the moral philosophy of Confucius, as interpreted by figures such as Wang Yang-ming and Chutsz. This shift eventually triggered a reaction in the form of a revival of "Pure Shinto." While Ieyasu himself turned national thought toward this new channel, the resulting revival of Shinto eventually demonstrated the anomalous position of the shogun relative to the sovereign, contributing to the shogunate's eventual downfall.

Socially, a rule early in the dynasty forbade Tokugawa ladies from marrying Court nobles. However, the shoguns continued to take consorts from noble houses in the Imperial capital. This practice was adopted by feudatories, leading to frequent marriages between military magnates and Kyoto noble ladies. These unions, driven by the ambition of military men and the financial needs of nobles, resulted in the formation of rival "Imperial" and "Bakufu" parties in Kyoto.

## Economy and Foreign Relations
Currency management was a recurring concern. In 1710, Ienobu and Arai Hakuseki introduced new coins called *kenji-kin* (meaning "great original") after failing to restore the Keicho era system due to a lack of precious metals. These coins were lighter but of higher quality. Simultaneously, daimyo were strictly forbidden from issuing paper currency.

Foreign relations were characterized by periods of restriction and conflict. The shogunate dealt with the presence of Christianity and foreign intercourse, including interactions with figures such as Will Adams. In later years, the shogunate faced challenges from Russians in the Northern islands, where the Matsumae ruled.
