# Tirol

Tirol is a region historically ranked among the provinces of the house of Austria. It has been a subject of significant territorial contention among European princes and empires, characterized by a strong local identity and strategic importance due to its geography.

## Medieval Succession and the Rule of Margaret Maultasch
In the 14th century, the county of Tirol was the inheritance of Margaret Maultasch (1318–1369), the daughter and heiress of Henry, count of Tirol and duke of Carinthia. Margaret received her nickname, Maultasch (meaning "pocket-mouth"), due to the shape of her mouth. Following the death of her father in 1335, while Carinthia passed to Albert II, duke of Austria, Tirol was inherited by Margaret and her husband, John Henry, the son of King John of Bohemia, whom she had married in 1330.

The marriage was unhappy, and the people of Tirol expressed dislike for the government of Charles (the future emperor Charles IV), who ruled the county on behalf of his brother. Consequently, John Henry was driven from Tirol. Emperor Louis IV, seeking to expand the possessions of the Wittelsbach family, espoused Margaret's cause. Louis declared the marriage to John Henry void on the grounds that it had not been consummated and, in 1342, married Margaret to his son, also named Louis, the margrave of Brandenburg.

This action caused a conflict with the Church, leading Pope Clement VI to place both the emperor and his son under a ban, which lasted until 1359. The acquisition of Tirol also created enemies for Louis IV, specifically John of Bohemia and the House of Luxemburg. This led to a war in Germany that forced the emperor to submit to humiliating terms. Charles, margrave of Moravia, subsequently attacked Tirol, but the conflict was interrupted by the sudden death of Emperor Louis IV on October 11, 1347, during a bear-hunt near Munich.

Following the death of her husband in 1361 and her only son, Meinhard, in 1363, Margaret handed Tirol over to Rudolph IV, duke of Austria. She retired to Vienna, where she died on October 3, 1369. In Carinthia, she was remembered as an "amazon" and referred to as the "Wicked Gretl."

## Integration into the House of Habsburg
The county later came under the rule of the House of Habsburg. In 1490, Maximilian I (later emperor) made an arrangement with his kinsman, Sigismund of Tirol, bringing the county under his rule. By 1493, Maximilian had united all Austrian lands. He further expanded Tirol's territory by intervening in a succession war in Bavaria.

Under Maximilian's administration, organic unity began to form among the Austrian lands. In 1518, representatives of various diets (*Landtage*) met at Innsbruck for this purpose.

## Political and Social Dynamics
Tirol has been a center of strong local feeling. In the struggle between Centralists and Federalists regarding the constitution of the Austrian empire, Tirol was part of the "kernel" of the empire—a purely German district that included Upper and Lower Austria, Salzburg, and Vorarlberg. 

While the Liberal party dominated in Styria and Lower Austria, Tirol was characterized by a strong Conservative and Clerical party. This group supported local institutions as a means of protection against the bureaucracy and the liberal influence of a centralized parliament. The clergy and bishops aligned themselves with the Federalists to support these local institutions.

## Territorial Disputes and the Irredentists
The region, particularly South Tirol, became a point of contention with Italy. A party known as the "Irredentists" demanded that Italian-speaking districts under Austrian rule, including South Tirol, Trieste, and Istria, be joined to Italy. This movement led to riots in Italy and Trieste, and necessitated the movement of Austrian troops into Tirol to signal that the government would not surrender territory.

While the desire for separation from Austria was strongest in Trieste and Istria, the feeling was less intense in Tirol. Except for the city of Trent, the population in Tirol primarily desired separate local institutions to avoid being governed from Innsbruck.

Due to the strategic nature of the region, the southern Tirol—which contains the chief passes into Italy—was strongly fortified. Additionally, the Austrian government constructed the Wochein, Karawanken, and Tauern railways to facilitate the movement of troops toward the Italian border.
