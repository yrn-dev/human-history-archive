# Scottish-English Relations and the Baliol Dynasty

## The Accession of John de Baliol
The Scottish throne became vacant in 1290 following the death of Margaret, known as the "maid of Norway," who was a granddaughter of King Alexander III. This vacancy led to a dispute between three candidates for the crown. The primary rivalry existed between Robert Bruce, earl of Annandale, and John de Baliol. Baliol based his claim on the principles of feudal law for an indivisible inheritance, claiming through his maternal grandmother, Margaret, the eldest daughter of David, earl of Huntingdon. Robert Bruce, conversely, was the nearest of kin.

King Edward I of England was invited to settle the dispute. In May 1291, Edward met with Scottish nobles at Norham, where he demanded a formal recognition of his position as the overlord of Scotland. This position was tacitly admitted by the nobles and acknowledged by the competitors for the crown. A court consisting of twenty-four Englishmen and eighty Scotsmen was appointed to hear the case. Following the court's advice, Edward decided in favor of Baliol.

## Reign and Relations with England
John de Baliol was crowned king of Scotland at Scone on November 30, 1292. He performed homage to Edward I at Newcastle and had previously sworn fealty to the English king. In January 1293, Baliol released the English king from all obligations and promises made while the kingdom of Scotland had been in his hands.

However, these amicable relations were soon disturbed. Baliol was summoned to the English court after a Scottish vassal brought a case against him to Edward, who acted as Baliol's overlord.

## The Baliol Family and Scottish Administration
The Baliol family held extensive lands across Scotland, England, and France. A cadet branch of the family descended from Ingelram (or Engelram), a son of the younger Bernard de Baliol. Ingelram's son, Henry, became chamberlain of Scotland around 1223. Henry had married Lora (or Lauretta), the daughter of Philip de Valoines, and in 1234 he inherited a portion of the Valoines family's rich English fiefs.

Alexander de Baliol, likely the son of Henry and lord of Cavers in Teviotdale, also served as chamberlain of Scotland, first mentioned in that role in 1287. Alexander was involved in negotiations between Edward I and the Scottish nobles, contributing to the treaty of Brigham in 1290 and the treaty of Salisbury in 1289. Around 1296, Alexander was probably deprived of his office as chamberlain and may have been imprisoned alongside his kinsman, King John de Baliol. Alexander subsequently fought for Edward in Scotland and was summoned to several English parliaments. He died around 1309. His grandson, Thomas, is the last member of the Baliol family mentioned in Scottish records, having sold the estate of Cavers to William, earl of Douglas, in 1368.

## Linguistic Evolution and Estrangement
Over the course of a century, the English language gained steady ground in England. By approximately 1350, French had become so uncommon that John Cornwal, a master of grammar, changed the teaching of grammar and construction from French to English. By 1362-1363, English replaced French in the pleadings of law courts by statute. The midland dialect became the dominant form of English, serving as the language of London's political and commercial centers, the Wycliffite versions of the Holy Scriptures, and the poetry of Chaucer.

While the northern English dialect eventually disappeared from literature within England, it continued to develop in Scotland. As Scotland became socially and politically estranged from England, this northern speech became the national language of the country, reaching a high literary culture and distinct development during the 15th and 16th centuries.
