# Roger Bacon

Roger Bacon (c. 1210/1214 – c. 1293/1294) was an English philosopher, man of science, and Franciscan of Oxford. Born near Ilchester in Somerset, Bacon came from a family of good circumstances, though during the reign of Henry III, several family members were exiled and their property was despoiled.

## Education and Early Life
Bacon completed his studies at Oxford, though archive sources clarify that he did not study at Brasenose or Merton, as neither college had been founded at that time. He is believed to have taken orders in 1233. Shortly thereafter, or perhaps later, he traveled to France to study at the University of Paris, which served as the intellectual center of Europe. During this period, the Dominicans and Franciscans were leading theological discussions, with figures such as Thomas Aquinas and Albertus Magnus representing the Dominicans, and Alexander of Hales serving as the oracle for the Franciscans.

Bacon's abilities were recognized by his contemporaries, and he maintained friendships with eminent figures including Adam de Marisco and Robert Grosseteste, the Bishop of Lincoln. Grosseteste is regarded by his contemporaries, including Bacon, as the first physicist and mathematician of his age and a pioneer of a scientific and literary movement. It is noted that Grosseteste anticipated several striking ideas that Bacon would later give wider currency.

## Philosophical and Scientific Views
Bacon is characterized as a thinker who was two centuries ahead of his time, described as honest, shrewd, irritable, and hasty. His writings are described as a "long tirade against ignorance," in which he boldly challenged the beliefs of the Middle Ages—a period when people were passionately convinced of the finality of their beliefs and often resented reflections upon them.

Bacon's primary insistence was on the necessity of collecting knowledge and the use of experiment, a focus that reflected the spirit of Aristotle. Despite this, Bacon "fell foul" of Aristotle. This conflict arose because Bacon believed that men of his time were merely poring over "abominable" and "bad Latin translations" of Aristotle rather than facing facts boldly. In an intemperate fashion, Bacon wrote that if he had his way, he would burn all of Aristotle's books, as studying them led to error, a loss of time, and increased ignorance.

Bacon urged mankind to look at the world and cease being ruled by authorities and dogmas. To achieve this, he denounced four chief sources of ignorance:
*   Respect for authority
*   Custom
*   The sense of the ignorant crowd
*   The vain, proud unteachableness of human dispositions

Because of the risk of prison or worse, Bacon often disguised his views to appear in alignment with orthodoxy.

## Scholarly Reception and Legacy
Bacon's work has been the subject of various academic interpretations. E. Charles's 1861 work, *Roger Bacon, sa vie, ses ouvrages, ses doctrines d'apres des textes inedits*, is considered perhaps the best work on him, though it is noted for a modern interpretation and a somewhat enthusiastic estimate. This view is countered by other scholars, including Schneider (1873) and K. Prantl, who provided a severe criticism of Bacon's logical doctrines.

Other specialized studies have focused on different aspects of his work:
*   **Physics and Cosmology:** S. Vogl (1906) and K. Werner (1879).
*   **Hebraism:** S. A. Hirsch (1899) and a 1905 *Book of Essays*.
*   **Philosophy:** Karl Pohl (1893) examined the relationship between philosophy and theology in Bacon's work, while E. Fluegel (1902) looked at his position in the history of philosophy.
*   **Practical Philosophy:** Held (1881).

Additionally, Duehring's *Kritische Gesch. d. Phil.* provides a severe judgment of Bacon's method while prominently highlighting his merits.

## Popular Legend
Beyond his academic contributions, Bacon is the subject of popular legends. These are reflected in works such as the *Famous Historie of Fryer Bacon* (1615), R. Greene's *Friar Bacon and Friar Bungay* (1587 or 1588), and *A Piece of Friar Bacon's Brazen Heade's Prophesie* (1604).
