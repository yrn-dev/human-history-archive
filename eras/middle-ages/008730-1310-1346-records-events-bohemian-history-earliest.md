# History of Bohemia

## Early Origins and Slavic Settlement
The name of Bohemia is derived from the Boii, a Celtic tribe that inhabited part of the land during the earliest historical period. According to traditions accepted by modern Bohemian historians, the Boii established their capital at Boiohemum but were eventually weakened by warfare with neighboring tribes and subdued by the Marcomanni, a Teutonic tribe, around 12 B.C. The Marcomanni were later expelled by other Teutonic tribes, leading to the eventual conquest of Bohemia by Slavic tribes, the most important of whom were the Cechs.

While classical and Byzantine references are considered misleading, archaeological research indicates Slavic inhabitants existed in Bohemia as early as the beginning of the Christian era. The Cechs are believed to have become the masters of the country in the 5th century. Their first recorded ruler, Samo, defeated the Franks at the battle of Wogatisburg (630) and overcame the Avars, a Turanian tribe that had briefly held overlordship over the region.

Following a period of obscurity lasting approximately 130 years, the oldest chroniclers, including Cosmas, record the foundation of a Bohemian principality by Krok and his daughter Libussa. Libussa is said to have married a peasant named Premysl. This semi-mythic figure began a continuous list of Premyslide princes, though the dates of their reigns and their deeds are not considered historical.

## Christianization and Early Rulers
The introduction of Christianity to Bohemia initially faced failure when attempted from Germany due to an innate distrust of the Germans. Success was achieved when the faith arrived from Moravia, taught by Cyrillus and Methodius, Greek monks from Thessalonica. Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius around 873, leading to a rapid adoption of the faith.

Among the early rulers, Wenceslas (surnamed the Holy) is the most famous; he was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized by the Church of Rome. Boleslav, as successor, was generally successful in a resulting war with Germany. Under Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its frontiers to include Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and the part of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. This empire was short-lived; Boleslav III lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland. After a period of civil war between Boleslav III and his brothers, Jaromir and Ulrick, the Bohemians dethroned Boleslav III in favor of the Polish prince Vladivoj, son of the Bohemian princess Dubravka.

## The Luxemburg Dynasty and King John
In a later period, Albert, king of the Romans, declared Bohemia a vacant fief of the Empire and induced the Bohemians to elect his son Rudolph, who reigned for only one year. Despite claims of hereditary right by Habsburg princes, the Bohemians maintained their right to elect a sovereign and chose Henry, duke of Carinthia. Henry became unpopular due to accusations that he unduly favored German settlers and was eventually deposed.

The Bohemians then elected John of Luxemburg, son of Henry, king of the Romans. The Luxemburg dynasty ruled Bohemia until its extinction upon the death of Sigismund in 1437. King John became more closely connected to the country through his marriage to Princess Elizabeth, daughter of Wenceslas II, though he spent most of his life in other lands, campaigning from Italy to Lithuania. His policy was characterized by a close alliance with France. King John died fighting as an ally of France at the battle of Crecy in 1346.

## Bohemian Literature and Historiography
Bohemia has a rich tradition of historical writing. Cosmas of Prague (1045-1125), the dean of the cathedral, is the earliest Bohemian historian; his *Chronicae Bohemorum libri iii* records history and traditions up to nearly the time of his death. Another significant work is the rhymed chronicle often called the chronicle of Dalimil. Written by an author who likely lived during the reign of King John (1310-1346), it records events from the earliest period to the reign of King Henry of Carinthia and is pervaded by a strong feeling of racial antipathy toward the Germans.

Early genuine documents of the Bohemian language include legends of St. Dorothy and St. Catherine, as well as hymns and epic fragments like *Tandarias a Floribella* and the *Alexandreis*. However, the MSS. of Kralodvur and Zelena Hora are considered 19th-century forgeries.

## The 19th-Century National Revival
Following a period where the Austrian government discouraged the use of the Bohemian language, a romantic movement in the early 19th century sparked a revival. Joseph Dobrovsky (1753-1829) was the first to draw attention back to the language through a grammar and a history of Bohemian literature.

The revival was further advanced by four key figures:
* **Joseph Jungmann (1773-1847):** Published translations of English and German writers and created a monumental German and Bohemian dictionary.
* **John Kolar (1793-1852):** The greatest poet of the revival, known for *Slavy dcera* (daughter of Slavia).
* **Paul Joseph Safarik (1795-1861):** Author of *Starozitnosti Slovanske* (Slavic antiquities).
* **Francis Palacky (1798-1876):** Regarded as the greatest Bohemian historian, he wrote a history of Bohemia from the earliest period to 1526.

Other notable historians include Wenceslas Tomek, who wrote the history of Prague; Jaroslav Goll, who studied the Bohemian Brethren; and Professor Joseph Kalousek, who studied the ancient constitution (*Statni pravo*) of Bohemia.
