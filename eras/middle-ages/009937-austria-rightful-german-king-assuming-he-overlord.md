# The Habsburgs and the German Kingship

The relationship between the Austrian territories and the German kingship is characterized by the rise of the Babenberg and Habsburg dynasties, the struggle for territorial control in Central Europe, and the eventual transition of Austria into an independent republic.

## Early Foundations and the Babenbergs
The foundation of Austria is attributed to Otto the Great, who was elected German king in 936. Following a victory over the Magyars at the Lechfeld in August 955, Otto refounded the East Mark to defend his kingdom. In 976, Emperor Otto II entrusted the government of this mark—which became known as Austria—to Leopold of the Babenberg family. 

The Babenbergs ruled Austria until 1246, expanding the mark's importance among the German states. They pushed the eastern frontier down the Danube valley, moving their capital from Poechlarn to Melk, then Tulln, and finally to Vienna around 1140. In 1156, King Frederick I raised Austria to the rank of a duchy, granting it exceptional privileges. These included the right for the duchy to descend to females if male heirs failed and the right for a duke to nominate his successor. Under the Babenbergs, Austria expanded further; Leopold I secured Styria and upper Austria by 1192, and Leopold II became lord of Carniola in 1229.

## Premysl Ottakar II and the Struggle for Power
Following the extinction of the Babenberg family, Premysl Ottakar II, the king of Bohemia, obtained possession of the archduchies of Austria. His power grew significantly as the nobility of Styria recognized him as their ruler, and he later acquired Carinthia, Istria, and parts of northern Italy. His dominions extended from the Giant Mountains in Bohemia to the Adriatic, covering nearly all areas west of the Leitha that would later comprise the Habsburg empire.

Due to his immense wealth and military strength, Ottakar was known as "the man of gold" and "the man of iron." While he encouraged German immigration to counter the power of the Bohemian nobility, his Slavic nationality is cited as a possible reason why he may have refused the German crown when it was offered to him prior to 1273.

## The Rise of the Habsburgs
In 1273, Rudolph, Count of Habsburg, was elected king of the Romans (German king). Rudolph viewed Ottakar’s power as a menace to the German throne. After questioning Ottakar's title to three duchies and seeking to recover imperial lands previously held by Emperor Frederick II, Rudolph had Ottakar placed under a ban in July 1275. Following a war, Ottakar submitted in November 1276 and renounced the duchies of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia.

Rudolph initially administered these duchies as the head of the Empire, but he sought to make them hereditary possessions for his family, moving the Habsburg headquarters from the Rhine to the Danube. On December 27, 1282, Rudolph invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Austria and Styria. Albert took over the government of these lands, though he was noted as an arbitrary ruler who suppressed a popular rising in Vienna in 1288. Albert himself was later elected German king in 1298.

## Imperial Authority and European Influence
The Habsburgs continued to hold significant influence over the German states and the Holy Roman Empire. By 1700, Leopold had been crowned Roman emperor at Frankfort. Acting as the sovereign of Germany, Leopold protested the French Revolution's abolition of feudal rights, which affected the possessions of German princes in Alsace and Lorraine. 

The Habsburgs also extended their reach beyond Germany. They claimed to be the natural heirs of the Habsburgs of Spain, a claim accepted by England, France, Holland, and the Belgian provinces. In Belgium, the Habsburgs assumed the title of natural prince, though their rule was subject to restrictions to maintain the European balance of power and the predominance of the Catholic church.

## The Decline of the Empire
In later centuries, Austria and Prussia existed in a complex relationship, both being partly inside and partly outside a German confederation that included various minor states. This confederation also included Luxembourg and the King of Denmark (via possessions in Holstein). 

Following the conflicts of the early 20th century, the status of Austria was fundamentally altered. Under a preliminary treaty of peace, Austria was recognized as an independent republic under the name "Republic of Austria." The treaty required Austria to recognize the complete independence of Hungary, Czecho-Slovakia, and Jugoslavia. Austria was forced to renounce all extra-European rights, demobilize its naval and aerial forces, and surrender its entire navy to the Allies. The boundaries of the new republic were fixed, with Bohemia and Moravia forming the boundary with Czecho-Slovakia, and Marburg and Radkersburg ceded to Jugoslavia.
