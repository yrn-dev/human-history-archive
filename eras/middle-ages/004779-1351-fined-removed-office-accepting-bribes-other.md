# Judicial and Official Bribery in History

The history of judicial and official bribery is characterized by a gradual process of purification across various civilizations and legal systems. While the prevalence and penalties for such corruption have varied by era and region, the effort to maintain the purity of the bench and administrative offices has been a recurring theme in governance.

## Bribery in English Law and History

In England, judicial corruption has been described as a crime of remarkable rarity. For much of its history, there has been little legislation specifically targeting judicial bribery, with the exception of a statute from 1384, which was later repealed by the Statute Law Revision Act of 1881. 

The earliest recorded instance of judicial bribery in England occurred in 1351, when Sir William Thorpe was removed from office and fined for accepting bribes. Other notable cases of high-ranking officials facing similar charges include the Chancellor of England, Michael de la Pole, in 1387; Lord Chancellor Bacon in 1621; Lionel Cranfield, the Earl of Middlesex, in 1624; and Sir Thomas Parker, the 1st Earl of Macclesfield, in 1725.

In the case of Lord Chancellor Bacon, the official confessed that his actions amounted to corruption and were worthy of condemnation. Bacon identified three ways bribes could be given and admitted to receiving bribes from suitors *pendente lite* in four specific instances. However, he denied the deliberate sale of justice, asserting that he never had a reward in mind when pronouncing a sentence and that his decisions remained just, sometimes even ruling against the party providing the bribe.

## Administrative and Ministerial Corruption in Britain

Corruption extended beyond the judiciary into ministerial and parliamentary offices. During the reign of Queen Anne and the period following the Revolution, corruption was prevalent in high official quarters. This era saw the expulsion of a speaker of the House of Commons for bribery and pecuniary dishonesty involving the great Marlborough. During the administration of Walpole, it is noted that members of parliament were paid in cash for votes, reflecting the sentiment that "every man has his price."

The appointment to office has been identified as one of the most difficult forms of administrative corruption to eradicate. Over time, however, the level of official bribery has descended, becoming extremely rare among lower-level revenue officers.

## Legal Penalties and Statutes in England and Ireland

Under English law, the taking of a bribe by a ministerial or judicial officer, as well as the bribery of a juryman or privy councillor, is punishable as a misdemeanour. Common law also regards the buying and selling of public offices as a form of bribery.

Specific statutes have established strict penalties for various sectors:
* **Customs Service:** The Customs Consolidation Act 1876 mandates instant dismissal and a £500 penalty for officers taking bribes. Those offering bribes to induce an officer to neglect duty or evade customs forfeit £200.
* **Inland Revenue:** The Inland Revenue Regulations Act 1890 imposes a £500 fine for the bribery of collectors, commissioners, or other employed officers.
* **Public Bodies:** The Public Bodies' Corrupt Practices Act 1889 addresses corruption in local government, including town or borough councils, boards, and select vestries. In England and Ireland, the offering or receiving of gifts or loans as inducements for a public body official is a misdemeanour; in Scotland, it is a crime. Conviction can lead to a fine of up to £500, imprisonment for up to two years (with or without hard labour), and a ban from holding public office for seven years.
* **Extradition:** Under the Extradition Act 1906, bribery is classified as an extraditable offence.

## Bribery in Ancient Greece

In Athens, bribery and the pursuit of wealth often intersected with political life. Demades was known to openly pride himself on his wealth and his contempt for the law; he once publicly paid a fine of one thousand drachmas for each of the one hundred foreigners he exhibited in a chorus, despite a law forbidding foreigners from appearing in such a capacity. In contrast, Phokion was noted for his virtue and for remaining in poverty despite being elected general multiple times.

The Athenian legal system also dealt with bribery in the context of official accountability. Following the fall of the Four Hundred oligarchs, magistrates were required to undergo audits and trials of accountability. While some were condemned to exile or fines, many were acquitted, reportedly through the use of bribes paid to the *logistæ* (auditing officers). 

Additionally, Athenian generals were subject to penalties for corruption. Eurymedon was fined for accepting bribes to depart Sicily at a time when the Athenians might have subdued the region. In other instances, such as that of Periklês, sources differ on the penalties; while Plutarch and Diodorus state he was both fined and removed from his office of *stratêgus*, Thucydides mentions only the fine.
