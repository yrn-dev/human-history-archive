# Romanesque Style of Auvergne

The Romanesque style of Auvergne is an architectural tradition found in central France. A notable example of this style is preserved in the town of Brioude, located in the department of Haute-Loire.

## The Church of St Julian
In Brioude, the church of St Julian serves as a significant representative of the Romanesque style of Auvergne. Constructed during the 12th and 13th centuries, the building is considered the only structure of consequence in the town. 

The church is particularly noted for its choir, which includes an apse and radiating chapels. Additionally, the exterior of the building features mosaic ornamentation, which is cited as a fine example of the regional style.

## Geographic and Urban Context
The town of Brioude, where this architectural style is exemplified, is situated on the left bank of the Allier, 1,467 feet above sea level. It is located 47 miles northwest of Le Puy and is accessible via the Paris-Lyon railway. The town is characterized by narrow and irregular streets, though it is surrounded by wide, tree-lined boulevards. Brioude has largely avoided modernization, preserving many of its old fountains and houses.

## Regional Administration and Economy
Brioude serves as the capital of an arrondissement and is the seat for tribunals of commerce and first instance, as well as a sub-prefect. The town is situated in a highly fertile plain, which supports a considerable grain trade and market-gardening in the outskirts. Local industries in the area include:
* Antimony mining and founding
* Lace-making
* Saw-milling
* Brewing

## Historical Context of Auvergne
The region of Auvergne has a complex political history involving the royal house of France. The dauphinate of Auvergne dates from 1155, following the deposition of William VII, count of Auvergne, by his uncle William VIII "the Old." The name "Dauphin" originated as a patronymic from Guigue IV. Dauphin. 

The title "Dauphin of Auvergne" was first used in a will in 1281 by Robert II, count of Clermont. In 1428, the dauphinate entered the royal house of France through the marriage of Louis de Bourbon, count of Montpensier, to Jeanne, the heiress of dauphin Beraud III. The dauphinate was eventually annexed to the crown in 1693.
