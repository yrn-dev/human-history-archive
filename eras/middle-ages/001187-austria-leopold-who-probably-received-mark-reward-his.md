# Austria

## Origins and the House of Babenberg
The foundation of Austria is attributed to Otto the Great, who was elected German king in 936. Following a victory over the Magyars on the Lechfeld in August 955, Otto freed Bavaria from their presence and refounded the East Mark to defend his kingdom. In 976, Emperor Otto II entrusted the government of this mark—which would become known as Austria—to Leopold, a member of the Babenberg family and a count in the Donnegau. Leopold likely received this appointment as a reward for his fidelity to Otto II during the Bavarian rising of 976.

The Babenbergs were a Franconian family whose earliest known ancestor was Poppo, a 9th-century count in Grapfeld. The family had previously suffered a loss of influence in Franconia after Adalbert, one of the sons of Duke Henry, was beheaded in 906 following a feud with the Conradines. However, under Leopold and his descendants, the East Mark grew into a significant state. Leopold extended the territory at the expense of the Hungarians and was succeeded in 994 by his son Henry I, followed by Adalbert in 1018, and Ernest in 1055.

## Territorial Expansion and Elevation to Duchy
The Babenbergs focused on pushing the eastern frontier down the Danube valley through colonization. This progress was marked by the relocation of the capital from Poechlarn to Melk, then to Tulln, and finally to Vienna around 1140. By 1156, the district between the Enns and the Inn was added to the mark.

In 1156, King Frederick I raised Austria to the rank of a duchy to restore peace to Germany, granting it exceptional privileges. These included the right for the duchy to descend to females if there were no male heirs, and the right for a childless duke to nominate a successor. The investiture was bestowed upon Duke Henry and his second wife, Theodora. The duke's primary obligations to the Empire were to send a contingent to the imperial army for campaigns on the borders and to attend any diet held in Bavaria.

Further expansion occurred under Duke Leopold I, who made a treaty with Ottakar IV, duke of Styria, in 1186, bringing Styria and upper Austria to the Babenbergs in 1192. In 1229, Duke Leopold II purchased lands from the bishop of Freising and took the title of lord of Carniola.

## Conflict and the End of the Babenberg Line
The Babenbergs generally maintained loyalty to the emperors, often cemented by marriage. However, this was interrupted by Margrave Leopold II, who deserted Emperor Henry IV during the investiture struggle. Henry IV stripped Leopold of Austria and gave it to Vratislav II, duke of the Bohemians, though Leopold regained possession in 1083.

A later conflict arose between Emperor Frederick II and Duke Frederick II (known as "the Quarrelsome"). The emperor claimed certain rights in Austria and summoned the duke to Italian diets. After Frederick II refused to appear three times, the emperor placed him under the ban in 1236, declared the duchies of Austria and Styria vacant, and invaded the country with the help of the king of Bohemia and the duke of Bavaria. The emperor made Vienna an imperial city and declared the duchies immediately dependent on the Empire. Although Duke Frederick regained his power in 1239, he continued a career of violence and oppression until he was killed in battle by the Hungarians in June 1246, marking the extinction of the Babenberg family.

## Later Dynastic and Imperial History
In the 14th century, the lands were divided between the brothers Leopold III and Albert III in 1379; Albert kept Carniola and Austria proper, while Leopold received Tirol, Carinthia, and Styria. Following the death of Albert II in 1439, his son Ladislaus Posthumus succeeded to the duchy of Austria and the kingdoms of Bohemia and Hungary. During Ladislaus's infancy, Emperor Frederick III served as regent of Austria.

By the 18th century, the House of Austria faced significant European conflicts. Leopold, crowned Roman emperor in 1700, dealt with the French Revolution. Despite his sister Marie Antoinette being the wife of Louis XVI, the Franco-Austrian alliance was practically non-existent. Leopold protested the French Constituent Assembly's abolition of feudal rights in Alsace and Lorraine. This tension, combined with the refusal to disperse *emigres* on the frontier, led to a decree by the French Assembly on January 25, 1792, threatening war if the emperor did not provide a satisfactory reply by March 1.

Following the death of Leopold in 1705 during the War of Spanish Succession, his sons Joseph I and Charles VI inherited the state. The resulting Treaty of Utrecht granted the House of Austria the Spanish Netherlands, Naples, and Sardinia. In the east, Austria's position was strengthened by the Treaty of Karlowitz, which limited Turkish efforts to the resistance of Austrian development toward Constantinople. Under Maria Theresa, Austria acquired Galicia and Lodomeria in 1772 through the first partition of Poland, and Bukovina from Turkey in 1777. Additionally, the House of Austria expanded its Italian influence through the establishment of Archduke Leopold in the grand-duchy of Tuscany.
