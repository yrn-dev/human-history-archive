# Bohemia

## Medieval Dynasties and the House of Luxemburg
The political landscape of Bohemia was marked by struggles over the right to elect its sovereign. Following a period where Albert, king of the Romans, induced the Bohemians to elect his son Rudolph through intimidation, the Bohemians asserted their right to choose their own ruler. They initially selected Henry, duke of Carinthia, who had married a daughter of King Wenceslas II. However, Henry became unpopular due to accusations that he unduly favored German settlers and was subsequently deposed.

The Bohemians then chose John of Luxemburg, the son of Henry, king of the Romans. The Luxemburg dynasty ruled Bohemia until its extinction upon the death of Sigismund in 1437. King John, though married to Princess Elizabeth (a daughter of Wenceslas II), showed little interest in the country, spending most of his life on campaigns from Italy to Lithuania. His foreign policy was centered on a close alliance with France, the nation for which he felt the most sympathy. John died fighting as an ally of France at the battle of Crecy in 1346.

## The Reign of Charles IV
John was succeeded by his son Charles, who had been elected sovereign by the German electors at Rense in 1346. Charles is remembered as one of Bohemia's greatest rulers. He established his favorite residence in Prague, where he founded the *nove mesto* (new town), tripling the city's size and population. He further increased the city's importance by founding the university of Prague.

Charles worked to re-establish order in a country that had been disturbed by incessant feuds during the absent reign of his father. While he attempted to codify the contradictory laws of Bohemia, this effort failed due to resistance from the powerful nobility. In foreign policy, Charles diverged from John; relations with France cooled, and by the end of his reign, he favored an alliance with England. Charles died in 1378 at age sixty-two. During his tenure, the first symptoms of a church reform movement appeared; despite accusations of subservience to the Church of Rome, Charles provided protection to several priests supporting these reforms.

## The Hussite Wars and George of Podebrad
The trial of John Huss and the subsequent burning of his colleague, Jerome of Prague, led to an insurrection of the Hussites in 1419. This sparked a series of religious wars. In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade to destroy the Hussites and Wycliffites in Bohemia. Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites operated on extreme democratic lines and successfully repelled four consecutive crusades, including an unsuccessful attempt to take Prague. Internal dissensions eventually allowed a fifth crusade to cross the frontier in 1431 under Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg.

George of Podebrad, the only Hussite king of Bohemia, is highly revered by his countrymen. His early reign was prosperous and based on a firm alliance with Matthias Corvinus, king of Hungary. However, George later entered a protracted struggle with the papal see. In 1452, George declared at a meeting of the estates in Prague that he would remain true to the communion in both kinds. In response, Pope Paul II excommunicated George and released Bohemians from their oaths of allegiance. This encouraged a confederacy of Romanist nobles at Zelena Hora in 1465. Matthias of Hungary eventually deserted George and was proclaimed king of Bohemia by Catholic nobles at Olmutz on May 3, 1469. George died in 1471.

## Habsburg Rule and Religious Conflict
Under the Habsburgs, Bohemia experienced fluctuating levels of autonomy and religious tension. Emperor Maximilian II (1564-1576) maintained peace through a liberal policy, though various Christian beliefs continued to struggle for mastery in Bohemia, Silesia, and Moravia. This changed with the accession of Rudolph II (1576-1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic who encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation. During Rudolph's reign, Bohemia existed in a state of semi-independence.

The situation shifted further under Ferdinand II, who was elected king of Bohemia in 1618. Resolved to establish the rule of the Jesuits and win back Germany to the Catholic faith, his actions led to the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War. Later, in 1620, a Spanish force prepared to invade the Palatinate during a period of revolution in Bohemia.

## Modern Political Developments
In later history, the fall of Metternich triggered widespread unrest across central Europe. On April 8, a separate constitution was promised to Bohemia as part of the general crisis facing the Austrian government. In a different parliamentary period, a truce between nationalities allowed Germans and Czechs to coexist briefly. While the Czechs avoided obstruction to maintain alliances with Conservatives and Poles, the Germans passed measures to promote material prosperity, specifically a system of canals designed to benefit the manufactures and coal-fields of Bohemia.
