# Bokhara

Bokhara is a city in central Asia that has for ages been regarded as a center of religious life and Mussulman erudition. Historically known to the ancients as Sogdiana, the city's foundation is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Due to its location, it was never under Roman dominion, though it was part of the empire of the Seleucidae following the conquests of Alexander the Great.

## Historical Development
The region experienced various shifts in power over the centuries. The nomad Yue-chi moved into the valley of the Oxus from the east, and by approximately A.D. 45, the dominions of their king, Kadphises I, extended from Bokhara to the Indus. The Sassanian dynasty of Persia later reconquered the district. Islam became the dominant faith shortly after the Arab conquest of Persia between 640 and 642. During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the region—then called Sughd or Ma wara 'l Nahr (Transoxiana)—was one of the most flourishing districts in central Asia. While Samarkand generally served as the seat of government, Bokhara maintained a high reputation as a seat of learning and religion.

In the late 11th century, Malik Shah of the Seljuk dynasty subdued the country watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus. Bokhara was subdued by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm in 1216, but this was overturned by Jenghiz Khan in 1220. Although the region was wasted by Jenghiz Khan, it recovered some prosperity under his son, Ogdai Khan. The city remained under the possession of his posterity until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand.

## Education and Intellectual Life
Bokhara has long been a principal seat of Islamitic learning. It is the birthplace of Avicenna (Ibnsinā), known as the Prince of Physicians (980-1037), who traveled in Khorasan. The city continued to be a chief seat of learning even after the 13th-century Mongol invasions laid waste to other Moslem cities like Samarkand.

The city's educational infrastructure includes numerous primary schools in the capital, other cities, and villages. Higher courses of study are provided by *madrasas*, or theological seminaries, which are comparatively plentiful. While many rich *madrasa* libraries were lost to conflagrations, confiscated by emirs, or scattered, private libraries still contain literary treasures. Consequently, bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan visit Bokhara to purchase rare books, making the city the principal book-market of central Asia.

## Governance and Society
The emir of Bokhara is an autocratic ruler whose power is limited only by the traditional custom of the Mussulmans, known as *sheriat*. Although he maintains an army of approximately 11,000 men, the emir is a vassal of the Russian empire and subject to its control. 

Socially, women occupy a low position, though slavery was abolished at the instance of Russia. The population is estimated by some to be between 75,000 and 100,000, though Russian travellers suggest it does not exceed 50,000 or 60,000. This population includes a large and ancient colony of Jews. About one-fourth of the population is said to be literate. While *mullahs* (priests) hold great influence, the people are described as very superstitious, with beliefs in spirits, omens, the evil eye, and witchcraft.

## Commerce and Urban Geography
Bokhara is the most important trading town in central Asia. Its bazaars specialize in the sale and manufacture of carpets, metal wares (particularly copper), silk stuffs, and Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins.

The city's influence extends to surrounding regions, such as Khojent. Formerly, all commerce between the khanates of Khokand and Bokhara passed through Khojent, though much of this was diverted after the Russian occupation in 1866. The amir of Bokhara held possession of Khojent after assisting Khudayar Khan in regaining his throne in 1864.

Near the railway station, approximately 8 miles from the main city, is "New Bokhara" (also called *Kagan*). This Russian town, situated on territory ceded by the emir, is growing rapidly and has a population of 2,000.
