# Basil I

Basil I, known as the "Macedonian," was the Roman emperor in the East who died in 886. Despite the name associated with the dynasty he founded, he was of Armenian descent, though his family had settled in Macedonia.

## Early Life and Rise to Power
Basil's early years were marked by hardship; in 813, his family was carried into captivity in Bulgaria by the prince Krum. After escaping, Basil entered the service of Theophilitzes—a relative of Caesar Bardas and uncle to Michael III—serving as a groom. During this time, he visited Patrae, where he gained the favor of a wealthy lady named Danielis, who provided him with a fortune.

Basil eventually attracted the attention of Emperor Michael III after winning a wrestling match. He became a close companion to the emperor and was appointed chamberlain (*parakoemenos*). Basil is described as an unprincipled adventurer who advanced unscrupulously; to please Michael III, he divorced his own wife to marry Eudocia Ingerina, a mistress of the emperor. It was commonly believed that Leo VI, Basil's successor, was actually the son of Michael III.

Basil's ascent to the throne involved violence. With the consent of the emperor, Basil murdered the powerful Caesar Bardas in April 866, as Bardas had been the virtual ruler of the empire while Michael III devoted himself to amusement. A few weeks after this act, Basil was raised to imperial dignity. In September 867, he had his former benefactor, Michael III, assassinated, allowing him to reign alone.

## Imperial Administration and Legacy
Upon taking the throne, Basil transitioned from a participant in the debauches of Michael III to a ruler with exceptional capacities for administration and a serious nature. He is credited with inaugurating a new age in the history of the empire and founding the Macedonian dynasty (which the archive notes would more accurately be called the Armenian dynasty).

Under his rule, the empire experienced a period of territorial expansion and became the strongest power in Europe. Basil is noted for undertaking a great legislative work, which served as a revival of Justinianean law, a project that was later completed by his successor. Despite these achievements, Basil was characterized as hard and cruel, lacking refinement or interest in culture, and he frequently dealt with the ill-will of a powerful and wealthy section of his subjects.

## The Paulician Conflict
During the ninth century, the empire faced a desperate and furious rebellion from the Paulicians of Armenia and adjacent provinces. This sect, provoked by long persecution, sought revenge for the wrongs of their fathers. The conflict was intensified by the Imperial mandate to either convert or destroy heretics, including the massacre of a bishop and governor.

A significant escalation occurred during the persecution led by Theodora, which sparked a revolt led by Carbeas, a valiant Paulician who had previously commanded the guards of the general of the East. Carbeas's father had been impaled by Catholic inquisitors. Carbeas and five thousand brethren renounced their allegiance to Rome and sought the support of a Saracen emir, who introduced him to the caliph.

Carbeas founded or fortified the city of Tephrice in the mountains between Trebizond and Siwas, creating a stronghold for Paulician fugitives who utilized both the Bible and the sword. For over thirty years, Asia suffered from domestic and foreign war as the Paulicians joined forces with the followers of Mahomet. The instability was so severe that Michael, the son of Theodora, was forced to lead an army against them in person, though he was defeated under the walls of Samosata and forced to flee.
