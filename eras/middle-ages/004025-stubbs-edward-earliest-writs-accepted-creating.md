# The Evolution of Baronial Summons and the Role of Edward I

## Transition of Baronial Status
Historically, the position of a baron was fundamentally linked to the tenure of land. In theoretical terms, barons were defined as those who held their lands from the king; in practical application, this status was reserved for those who held a substantial amount of land. A significant transformation occurred when the criteria for presence in the council of the realm—which evolved into the House of Lords—shifted. Instead of being determined by land tenure, attendance became dependent upon the king's will through the issuance of a writ of summons.

While the historian Camden previously asserted that this change was implemented by Henry III following the "Barons' War," current historical attribution, as noted by Stubbs, assigns this shift to the reign of Edward I. Consequently, the earliest writs recognized as creating hereditary baronies are those issued during the reign of Edward I.

## Titles and Styles of the Peerage
Despite the shift toward writs of summons, those who received such summonses were not initially distinguished from commoners by specific titles or styles. During this period, the only available prefix was *Dominus* (lord), a term regularly employed by simple knights. Writs of summons continued to be issued to the lowest order of peers using only the designation of knights (*chevaliers*).

The specific style of "baron" was not introduced until 1387 under Richard II. In that year, Richard II used a patent to create John de Beauchamp as Lord de Beauchamp and baron of Kidderminster, designating him "unum parium et baronum regni nostri." This was a rare occurrence at the time, as the next such creation did not happen until 1433, when Sir John Cornwall was made baron of Fanhope.

## Development of Peerage Nomenclature
Under Henry VI, the creation of barons became more common, marking a transition period in peerage styles. However, the title "Baron" did not replace existing terms such as "Sire," "Chevalier," and "Dominus." While patents of creation utilized the formula "Lord A. (and) Baron of B.," the grantees typically styled themselves only as "Lord." 

This historical distinction persists in modern correspondence, where a baron is addressed as "the Lord A." rather than "Baron A." or "Baron B.," the latter of which is described as a recent and unhistorical practice. It is noted that while all peers below the rank of Duke are spoken of as "lords," they are addressed in correspondence by their specific proper styles.

## Legal Disputes Regarding Summons and Rank
The importance of the writ of summons is illustrated by later legal disputes regarding peerage and the right to trial by peers. For example, Charles, the 4th earl of Banbury (1662-1740), had not been summoned to parliament. Following a duel in 1692 in which he killed Captain Philip Lawson, the House of Lords declared that because he had not received a writ of summons, he was not a peer and was not entitled to a trial by his peers. Although the court of king's bench released him from imprisonment on the basis that he was the earl of Banbury rather than a commoner, the House of Lords maintained its position. This lack of a writ continued to affect his descendants; in 1813, the House of Lords decided against a claim for a writ of summons made by General William Knollys.
