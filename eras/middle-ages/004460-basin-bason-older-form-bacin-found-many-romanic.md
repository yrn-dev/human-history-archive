# BASIN

A basin, also referred to as a bason, is fundamentally a round vessel used for holding liquids. The term originates from the Late Latin *baccinus* or *bacchinus*, which is likely derived from *bacca*, meaning a bowl. This older form, *bacin*, is present in many Romanic languages.

## Technical and Engineering Applications
Beyond its use as a simple vessel, the term "basin" is applied to several technical structures:
*   **Maritime and Canal Use:** In the context of docks, a basin may be a structure constructed with flood-gates within a tidal-river. It can also refer to a widening of a canal designed for the unloading of barges. For example, certain harbours may feature a floating basin, such as one measuring 22 acres in extent.
*   **Water Management:** In Roman adjustments of water systems, water was led from cisterns into large rectangular basins. One such example featured a basin at the center of a 50 ft. square quadrangle, equipped with a drainage hole in the northeast corner and a flight of five steps leading down into it. These basins included orifices to prevent overflowing. Such structures served multiple purposes; for instance, water that was sweet to drink was also used for tempering bronze by plunging red-hot metal into the basin, which functioned as a tank to give the bronze a peculiar excellence.

## Geological and Geographical Definitions
In the study of the earth and its landscapes, the term is used in three distinct ways:
*   **Physical Geography:** A basin refers to the drainage area of a river and its tributaries. An example is the basin of the river Annan.
*   **Geology (Structural):** A geological basin is equivalent to a broad, shallow syncline. Strictly defined, a structural basin consists of rock beds exhibiting a centroclinal dip; it is distinguished from an elongated narrow syncline or trough. Notable examples include the Paris basin and the London and Hampshire basins, which consist of Jurassic, Cretaceous, and Tertiary rocks. Additionally, "coal basins" include the Forest of Dean, the N. Staffordshire coalfield, and the S. Wales coalfield.
*   **Geological Depressions:** "Rock-basins" are steep-sided, relatively small depressions scooped out of solid rock in mountainous regions, primarily by glaciers. Other basins may be formed by crustal movements or by the solution and removal of soluble constituents in underlying strata, such as rock salt, which may then be occupied by lakes.

Furthermore, geologic basins serve as sites where fine insoluble particles, carried in suspension by water, are deposited according to their degree of fineness and specific gravity. This process results in the formation of clays, ranging from soft "alluvial" clays to hard shales and slates. These deposits have formed across all geologic epochs, from the Cambrian to the Recent.

## Cultural and Sacred References
The basin appears in various historical and sacred contexts:
*   **Sacred Texts:** In the accounts of the work performed for Sulaiman (Solomon) by the jinn, they created several large-scale items by the command of his Lord. These included "basons as large as Reservoirs" (also described as "basins like wells" or "bowls large as watering-troughs"), as well as images, synagogues, statues, and fixed cooking-pots.
*   **European Legend:** During the Middle Ages, weather-conjuring stories featured magical objects. In the romance of the *Chevalier au Lyon*, a magical fountain is described as having basins of fine gold (*bacins d'or fin*) hanging from long chains. According to the tale, if one were to take water from the basin and spread it upon a stone (*perron*), a great tempest would be unleashed.
