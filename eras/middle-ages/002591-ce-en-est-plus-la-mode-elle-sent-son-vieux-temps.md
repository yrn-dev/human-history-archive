# Ce n'en est plus la mode, elle sent son vieux temps

The phrase "Ce n'en est plus la mode, elle sent son vieux temps" (It is no longer in fashion, it smells of its old time) is a critical observation regarding the perceived obsolescence of certain artistic forms. This specific sentiment is attributed to Philaminte, a lady pedant appearing in Molière's play *Les Femmes Savantes*.

## The Ballade and its Evolution
The phrase is used by Philaminte to describe the "ballade," which she characterizes as a "chose fade" (insipid thing). To understand the context of this dismissal, it is necessary to examine the origins and technical nature of the form.

The term "ballad" is derived from the Old French word *baller*, meaning to dance. In its earliest form, it referred to a song that accompanied the rhythmic movements of a dancing chorus. Over time, however, the term *ballade* evolved into a technical designation for a specific style of old-fashioned French poetry. This poetic form was noted for its recurring and involved rhyme schemes.

## Historical Reception of Old French Poetry
The decline in the popularity of the ballade is reflected in the writings of other figures in French literary history. In 1550, Joachim du Bellay expressed a similar desire to leave "all these old French poems like *ballades*" to the Jeux Floraux de Toulouse, indicating that the form was already viewed as dated by the mid-16th century.

## Contrast with Oral Traditions
While the ballade became a fixed poetic form that eventually fell out of fashion, other forms of narrative tradition evolved differently. In Scandinavia, for example, the "Saga" (meaning "what one says" or "what one tells") developed as a form of oral tradition. Unlike the formal poetry of the ballade, the Saga is spoken rather than sung. It is distinguished from history by its lack of critique and from the novel by its sincerity and faith in its own narrative.

When such spontaneous products of human imagination—which repeat rather than invent—refer to a saint rather than a hero, they are termed "legends." These legends often blend the "puéril" (childish) with the "grand," sometimes including anecdotes that may seem unworthy of the subject's memory, such as stories of St. Columban having gloves returned to him by a crow or preventing beer from flowing out of a pierced barrel.

## The Transition from Orality to Text
The shift in how poetry and stories are consumed often marks the end of certain popular tendencies. In epic poetry, there is a constant tendency among the people to amalgamate various compositions into a single work. This occurs as long as poetry is transmitted via tradition, public recitations, or song. 

This process of communal amalgamation ceases once poetry is fixed in books and accessed through reading. This transition marks the era of "poetic property," where individual poets seek personal glory and existence, and poetry ceases to be a "common treasure" that the people can dispose of and enjoy in their own manner.
