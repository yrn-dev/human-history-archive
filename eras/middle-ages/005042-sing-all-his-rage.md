# Singing and Rage in Historical Context

The concepts of singing and rage appear across various historical, linguistic, and cultural records, ranging from the ritualistic and artistic to the political and emotional.

## Singing in Cultural and Ritual Practice

Singing has served diverse functions in human history, from religious observance to social gathering and mourning. In the context of early carols, singing was sometimes used as a pretext for asking for alms, as seen in an Anglo-Norman carol titled *Seigneurs ore entendey a nus*, which is described as little more than a drinking song. Carols also played a significant role in mystery plays concerning the Nativity; for example, in the *Marguerites de la Marguerite des princesses, tres-illustre reine de Navarre* (1547), shepherds and shepherdesses perform a chorus at the manger that combines singing, dancing, and "rage" (*Dansons, chantons, faisons rage*). Other English carols mixed sacred and profane elements, such as a composition detailing the life of Christ that begins with the line, "To-morrow shall be my dancing day."

In ancient Greece, singing was associated with funeral rites. During the festival of Adonis, women would carry figures dressed as corpses and imitate funeral ceremonies by singing dirges and tearing their hair. Additionally, in the city of Chalkis, a specially-written poem was sung following libations offered to Titus.

## Natural and Philosophical Perspectives on Singing

Ancient accounts often attributed symbolic meaning to the singing of animals. There is a common belief, cited by poets, orators, and philosophers, that swans only sing melodiously in their final moments. Cicero used this image to compare a discourse made by Crassus shortly before his death to the singing of a dying swan. Socrates further suggested that good men should emulate swans, who are said to die with joy and singing because they possess a secret instinct or divination regarding the advantages of death.

## Linguistic Variations of "Sing"

The English language exhibits historical regional variations in the conjugation of the verb "to sing." In Middle English, the Southern, Midland, and Northern dialects differed in their present tense forms:
*   **Southern:** "Ich singe," "We singeth," "Thou singest," "[Gh]e singeth," "He singeth," "Hy singeth."
*   **Midland:** "Ich, I, singe," "We singen," "Thou singest," "[Gh]e singen," "He singeth," "Hy, thei, singen."
*   **Northern:** "Ic. I, sing(e)," "We sing(e)," "Thu singes," "[Gh]e sing(e)," "He singes," "Thay sing(e)."

## Expressions of Rage in History and Literature

Rage is documented as a driving force in military conflict, political instability, and personal vendettas.

### Military and Political Rage
In the *Heimskringla*, King Harald Sigurdson is described as being "in a rage" during a battle against the English. He ran in front of his array and hewed down opponents with both hands, overcoming both armor and helmets. Similarly, in the accounts of Plutarch, Manius marched upon Chalkis in a rage following the retreat of King Antiochus, though he was eventually pacified by the entreaties of Flaminius.

In Athens, the mutilation of the Hermae caused the public to react with feelings of fear and rage, as they suspected an organized plot was underway. In the realm of ancient Persia, King Cambyses exercised "sacrilegious rage" upon the city of Heliopolis, where he demolished palaces, burned temples, and destroyed precious monuments.

### Literary and Sacred Descriptions of Rage
Rage also appears in literary and sacred texts to describe intense emotion or enmity. The poet sings of the "fiero ardire" (fierce daring) and the rage of the outlawed Pietro Mancino. 

In the Qur'an, rage is described in the context of those who claim to believe when meeting others but act with hostility in private. The text states that when these individuals are alone, they "bite off the very tips of their fingers" (or "bite their finger-tips") in their rage. The text commands the response: "Perish in your rage" (or "Die in your rage"), noting that Allah is aware of the secrets of the heart and what is hidden in the breasts.

## Other Uses of the Term "Sing"

The term "Sing" also appears in a non-musical context regarding the administration of the Kaan. According to Rashiduddin, the Kaan erected a place for the sittings of the Great Council called the *Sing*, which dignitaries were expected to attend daily. This term is noted as being evidentially related to the *Shieng* or *Sheng* mentioned by Marco Polo, though some scholars argue it represents the word *Siang*, meaning "a minister" or "the office of a minister."
