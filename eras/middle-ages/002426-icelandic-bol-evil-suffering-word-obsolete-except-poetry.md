# Icelandic Bol and Related Terms

The term *bol* appears in the archive's linguistic records in several distinct contexts, relating to different meanings across Teutonic and Scandinavian languages, ranging from concepts of suffering and evil to physical dwellings and structures.

## Linguistic Variations of Bol
In the study of Teutonic languages, the word *bol* (corresponding to the Old English *balu*) is used to denote evil or suffering. In modern usage, this specific meaning has become obsolete, surviving primarily within poetry. It is frequently encountered in its adjectival form, "baleful," and was used in early alliterative poetry specifically as an antithesis to "bliss."

Distinct from the concept of suffering, the Icelandic word *ból* refers to an abode or a farm. This is related to the Danish *bol* (a farm) and the Old English *bold* (a dwelling). These terms are apparently connected to the Old English verb *byldan* ("to build"), which some scholars trace back to the Sanskrit *bhu*, meaning "to be." In this context, building is interpreted as the construction of a place in which to dwell or be.

Additionally, the term *bol* or *bole* is associated with the Scandinavian origin of the word "bulwark." In this instance, *bol* refers to a tree-trunk. Combined with *werk* (work), it describes a barricade made of earth and beams. In 15th and 16th century fortifications, such works were designed to mount artillery, while in naval terminology, it refers to the woodwork running above the deck level of a ship.

## Icelandic Literature and Epic Poetry
Iceland developed a unique heroic literature during the Middle Ages. While the Teutonic school of epic poetry focused on legends of Germany in the 4th, 5th, and 6th centuries—featuring figures such as Theodoric, Attila, and Ermanaric—Icelandic literature eventually produced the Sagas. These Sagas served as prose reductions of epics concerning the warlike history of the North, replacing a group of archaic Icelandic epics.

One of the principal surviving examples of primitive Icelandic epic poetry is the poem *Atlamal*. Other fragments of Icelandic composition are preserved in the *Codex Regius*, a manuscript discovered in the 17th century titled *The Elder Edda*. Compared to lost Teutonic specimens, Icelandic epics were generally more episodic and shorter in character. Like the Teutonic works, they were written in alliterative verse, though they are noted to lack the organic unity and vitality found in the Sagas.

## Historical Relations with Norway
Historical records from the *Heimskringla* chronicle detail the interactions between Iceland and King Olaf. In A.D. 999, a priest named Thangbrand returned from Iceland to King Olaf, reporting the failure of his mission. Thangbrand noted that the Icelanders had created lampoons about him and some had attempted to kill him, leading to the belief that the country would not become Christian. This resistance was attributed to Thangbrand's conduct, which involved manslaughter and violence.

By A.D. 1024, the relationship had shifted. King Olaf sent timber and a bell to Iceland for the construction of a church on the Thing-field, where the General Thing is held. This occurred after the Icelandic people had altered their laws to introduce Christianity. During this period, several prominent Icelanders, including Thorleif Bollason and Thorkel Eyjolfson, entered King Olaf's service.

In the same year, King Olaf dispatched Thorarin Nefiulfson to the Althing in Iceland. Thorarin delivered a message to the chiefs and the general population, offering that the King would be their sovereign if they became his subjects, proposing a friendship based on mutual assistance in all that is good. The Icelandic people responded favorably, stating they would be the king's friends if he remained a friend to the people of their country.
