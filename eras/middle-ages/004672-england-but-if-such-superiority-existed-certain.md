# England

## Early Political Leadership and the Bretwalda
The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, specifically under the date 827, and a charter of Æthelstan, king of the English, utilize the term "Bretwalda" (appearing in variant forms such as *brytenwalda* and *bretenanwealda*). The term most likely means "lord of Britain" or "lord of the Britons," as the first syllable appears cognate with Britannia and Briton. 

In the *Chronicle*, Ecgbert, king of the English, is identified as the eighth king to hold this title. Retrospectively, the title was applied to seven kings who ruled various English kingdoms, names which were copied from Bede's *Historia Ecclesiastica*. Notably, Oswiu of Northumbria, the last of these seven, lived 150 years before Ecgbert. While it has been assumed that these seven kings exercised a certain superiority over a large part of England, such superiority, if it existed, was certainly unaccompanied by any unity of organization and was extremely vague. 

Alternative theories regarding the term include:
* A reference to *imperium* or war-leadership over the English south of the Humber. This is supported by the *Chronicle's* note that Ecgbert received the title in the year he conquered all south of the Humber and the kingdom of the Mercians.
* A theory by Palgrave suggesting the Bretwaldas claimed the imperial dignity of Rome as successors to Carausius and Maximus.
* A theory by Kemble, who translates the term as "widely ruling," deriving it from the British word *breotan* (to distribute).

## Legal System and the Inns of Court
In England and Ireland, the term "barrister" refers to the highest class of lawyers, who possess exclusive audience in all superior courts. The term is derived from the "bar" found in law courts. To practice as a barrister in England, one must be a member of one of the four ancient societies known as the Inns of Court: Gray's Inn, Lincoln's Inn, the Middle Temple, and the Inner Temple.

The rise of these societies as schools can be traced to the 13th century. This development is attributed to a clause in the Magna Carta that fixed the Common Pleas at Westminster rather than having them follow the king's court, which concentrated law professors in London. Lawyers formed associations and acquired houses to educate students in common law, conferring the degrees of sergeant (corresponding to doctor) and barrister (corresponding to bachelor or apprentice).

## Naval Policy and Strategic Superiority
In the early 20th century, English naval policy involved the development of programmes to secure advantages in shipbuilding. A proposed programme, agreed upon by the Chancellor of the Exchequer and other leadership since 1909, focused on the number of capital ships. This policy was designed to be flexible—capable of acceleration, retardation, diminution, or expansion—and was not necessarily embodied in an Act of Parliament.

The programme was measured against the existing shipbuilding situations of Austria and Germany to ensure "ample margins of superiority" in both establishment and new construction. While the programme for minor construction was expressed in terms of money and remained entirely flexible, the capital ship programme was intended to cover the period of the existing German Navy Law and was subject to revision after four years. Under certain limits, it remained open for Germany and England to agree upon proportionate reductions.

## Religious Institutions
The English congregation of Benedictines consists of a cathedral priory in Hereford, a nunnery in Worcester (Stanbrook Abbey), and three large abbeys: Ampleforth, Downside, and Woolhampton. Additionally, England contains several nunneries subject to bishops and three or four abbeys belonging to foreign congregations.

The polity of these institutions preserves the primitive Benedictine principle of autonomy and self-containment for each monastery. Each congregation is self-governing and independent, lacking a central authority or superior-general. The president of each congregation possesses limited powers.

## Local Governance and Autonomy
In England, municipal corporations exercise local autonomy. This is defined as a freedom of self-government within a sphere marked out by a superior authority. In the case of these corporations, their administrative powers are marked out for them by acts of parliament.

## Geology
The south-west of England contains several notable "bone beds," a term used by geologists for strata containing bones. These include:
* The Ludlow Bone Bed: Found at the base of the Downton Sandstone in the Upper Ludlow series. It consists primarily of scales, teeth, and spines of ganoid fish and can be traced from Ludlow into Gloucestershire for 45 meters.
* The "Lias" or "Bristol" Bone Bed: Found in the Rhaetic Black Paper Shales above the Keuper marls, containing remains of saurians and fish.
* Additional bone beds have been observed at the base of the Carboniferous limestone series in certain parts of the south-west.
