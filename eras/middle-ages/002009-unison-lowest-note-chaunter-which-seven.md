# Highland Bag-pipe and Related Instruments

The Highland bag-pipe is a musical instrument characterized by a specific construction involving a bag, drones, and a chaunter. A preserved example of a Highland bag-pipe from the 15th century, dated MCCCCIX and held by Messrs J. & R. Glen of Edinburgh, illustrates early design features. This instrument featured two drones inserted into a single stock in the form of a wide-spread fork. These drones were tuned to A, which was in unison with the lowest note of the chaunter. The chaunter of this 15th-century model possessed a thumb-hole at the back and seven finger-holes in front.

## Construction and Mechanics of the Highland Bag-pipe
The Highland bag-pipe's chaunter is a conical wooden tube that terminates in a bell, measuring between 14 and 16 inches including the reed. It features seven holes in the front and one thumb-hole at the back for the left hand. The right thumb is used to support the instrument, while the under part of the finger joints stops the holes. Near the bell, there is a double hole that is never covered; its purpose is to regulate the pitch.

Because the performer does not manipulate the double reed with their lips, the chaunter produces only nine notes. These notes do not form a known diatonic scale, as the F and C are too sharp and the notes are not strictly in tune with one another. Consequently, the instrument cannot be transposed into any key other than the one in which the music was first marked. However, the instrument is capable of playing tunes that adapt to the keys of A major, D major, B minor, G major, E minor, and A minor. Some observers, such as Mr. Ellis, have noted that the natural scale of the chaunter most closely corresponds to the Arab scale of Zalzal.

In typical tuning, three drones are used and tuned to A: the largest is tuned two octaves below the A of the chaunter, while the two smaller drones are tuned one octave below.

## Comparative Bag-pipe Varieties
Bag-pipes are categorized into different classes based on their inflation method and structure. Class I includes the Highland bag-pipe, the old Irish bag-pipe, the cornemuse, the bignou or biniou, the Calabrian bag-pipe, the chorus, the Roman *tibia utricularis*, and the Greek *ascaulus*. Class II consists of instruments inflated by bellows, such as the musette, the union pipes of Ireland, the surdelina of Naples, and the Lowland or Northumbrian/border bag-pipes.

### German Sackpfeife and Dudelsack
The German *Sackpfeife* or *Dudelsack* existed in five sizes described by Michael Praetorius. The *Grosser Bock* (double-bass bag-pipe) featured a single long cylindrical drone and a chaunter, both ending in curved ram's horns. Its drone was tuned to G, an octave below the chaunter. The *Bock* was similar but pitched a fourth higher in C. The *Schaeferpfeife* utilized two drones (B flat and F), though Praetorius noted its upper chaunter notes had faulty intonation due to the lack of a thumb-hole. The *Huemmelchen* had drones tuned to F and C. The smallest variety, the *Dudey* or treble sackpfeife, featured three drones (E flat, B flat, and E flat).

Additionally, Praetorius described a Magdeburg *sackpfeife* which was larger than the *Schaeferpfeife* and pitched a third lower. This unique version had two chaunters in one stock, allowing the performer to play simple two-part melodies.

### French and Italian Varieties
Before the musette, the *cornemuse* and *chalemie* were used in Italy, France, and the Netherlands. In France during the 16th and 17th centuries, two types of cornemuses existed. The version used by shepherds as a solo instrument featured a leather bag and two drones (a *gros bourdon* 2-1/2 ft long and a *petit bourdon* 1 ft long), both tuned to C. Its chaunter had eight holes (seven in front, one thumb-hole) providing a compass from C to C'. If the chaunter was removed and played separately, the compass could be increased to E by overblowing.

The second type of cornemuse was played in concert with *Hautbois de Poitou* and featured a single drone with a double reed rather than a beating-reed. Its chaunter had eight holes, with the lowest covered by a key in a perforated box.

## Evolution of Bellows-Blown Pipes
The musette, evolved during the 15th century, replaced the blow-pipe with a small bellows worked by the arm. This removed the facial distortion caused by inflation. The musette utilized a compact barrel droner containing four or five drones with double "hautbois" reeds. These were adjustable via slides called *layettes* to produce a harmonic bass. The musette's chaunter had eleven holes (four with keys) and a compass of twelve notes from F to C.

Later improvements by Jean Hotteterre and his son Martin Hotteterre included the addition of a second chaunter. Martin Hotteterre's *petit chalumeau* had six keys, while the *grand chalumeau* had seven keys in addition to eight finger-holes and a vent-hole.

Similarly, the 18th-century union pipes (modern Irish bag-pipe) were blown by bellows. They featured one chaunter with seven finger-holes, one thumb-hole, and eight keys, enabling a chromatic scale across two octaves. These pipes used drones tuned to A in different octaves and three regulators played by the elbow to produce harmony.
