# Stralsund and Meissen

The cities of Stralsund and Meissen have appeared throughout various periods of German and Central European history as strategic points of military occupation, ecclesiastical administration, and political struggle.

## Military Occupations and Territorial Expansion
During the early 11th century, the Polish king Boleslaus I, known as "The Great," sought to expand the Polish principality on the Vistula into a great power. Following the death of Emperor Otto III, Boleslaus launched an invasion of Germany. During this campaign, he penetrated as far as the Elbe, occupying both Stralsund and Meissen. Through these conquests, he extended his dominions to the Saale and the Elster. Boleslaus also occupied Bohemia, though he was eventually driven out of that territory in 1004 by Emperor Henry IV. The conflict between Boleslaus and the German forces concluded in 1018 with the peace of Bautzen, a settlement that favored Boleslaus and allowed him to retain Lusatia.

## The See of Meissen
Meissen served as a significant ecclesiastical center, notably under the leadership of Benno, who served as bishop of Meissen from 1066 until his death. The son of Werner, count of Woldenburg, Benno was educated at Gosslar and was nominated to the see by Emperor Henry IV. 

Benno's tenure was marked by the political tensions between the papacy and the empire. He participated in conflicts against the emperor, which led to his deposition by the synod of Mainz in 1085. However, following the death of Pope Gregory VII, Benno submitted and was restored to his position on the recommendation of Pope Clement III. As bishop, Benno implemented material developments and ecclesiastical reforms based on the Hildebrandine model. He was revered as a saint within his diocese and was formally canonized by Pope Adrian VI in 1523. This canonization was met with opposition from Luther, who wrote a violent brochure against the act.

## Regional Administration and Conflict
The region surrounding Meissen was frequently a site of political instability and disputes over authority. In the late 11th century, Gebhard, count of Supplinburg, was a leading opponent of Emperor Henry IV in Saxony. His son, Lothair (later Roman emperor Lothair II or III), continued this opposition, assisting Egbert II, the margrave of Meissen, during a rising in 1088.

Later, in 1123, the death of Henry II, the margrave of Meissen and Lusatia, triggered a dispute regarding the right of appointment to the vacant margraviates. This struggle resulted in a victory for Duke Lothair. During his reign, Lothair worked to extend his authority and independence, eventually seconding the efforts of his vassal, Conrad I, the margrave of Meissen and Lusatia, to expand German authority in the districts east of the Elbe.

## Geography and Related Localities
Stralsund is located in proximity to other German towns; for example, the town of Barth, situated on the Barther Bodden lake, is 15 miles northwest of Stralsund by rail. 

In relation to Meissen, the town of Bischofswerda in the kingdom of Saxony served as the residence of Bishop Benno during the 11th century. A route known as the "Bishop's Road" continues to run between Bischofswerda and Meissen.
