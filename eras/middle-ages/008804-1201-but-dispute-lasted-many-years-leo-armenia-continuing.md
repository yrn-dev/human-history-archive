# The Succession Dispute of Antioch (1201)

The succession dispute over the principality of Antioch at the turn of the 13th century was a protracted conflict primarily involving the descendants of Bohemund III and the Armenian monarchy. The struggle centered on whether the principality should be inherited by Bohemund III's younger son, Bohemund IV, or his grandson, Raymund Rhupen.

## Origins of the Conflict
Bohemund III succeeded his mother, Constance, in the principality of Antioch in 1163. His later years were dominated by the question of his successor. Bohemund III had two sons by his second wife, Orguilleuse: Raymund and Bohemund (the future Bohemund IV). Raymund married Alice, the daughter of Rhupen, an Armenian prince and brother to Leo of Armenia. Raymund died in 1197, leaving a son named Raymund Rhupen.

Leo of Armenia became the champion for his great-nephew, Raymund Rhupen. Leo's interest in Antioch predated the marriage of his niece; in 1194, Leo had captured Bohemund III at Gastin in an attempt to force him to cede the principality. As the dispute progressed, Bohemund IV pursued his own claim with vigor, even evicting his father from Antioch around 1199. However, Leo—who had become king of Armenia by the grace of Emperor Henry VI—ousted Bohemund IV, and Bohemund III remained in possession of the principality until his death in 1201.

## The Reign of Bohemund IV and the Armenian Challenge
Upon the death of Bohemund III in 1201, Bohemund IV succeeded to the principality of Antioch, excluding Raymund Rhupen. This sparked a dispute that lasted for many years and required the attention of Pope Innocent III.

The conflict was characterized by shifting alliances and external appeals:
* **Support for Bohemund IV:** He was supported by the Greek inhabitants of Antioch and the Templars, who held estates in Tripoli. In 1207, Bohemund IV granted the Greek inhabitants their own patriarch.
* **Support for Raymund Rhupen:** Leo of Armenia continued to champion his great-nephew's cause. Between 1210 and 1211, Leo appealed to both Emperor Otto IV and Pope Innocent III. He was supported by the Hospitallers.

In 1216, Leo succeeded in capturing Antioch and establishing Raymund Rhupen as its prince. This victory was short-lived, as the city was lost again in less than four years. By the time of Leo's death in 1220, Antioch was once again in the possession of Bohemund IV.

## Later Years of Bohemund IV
Following the death of Raymund Rhupen in 1221, Bohemund IV reigned over both Antioch and Tripoli until his own death. His later reign was marked by hostility toward the Hospitallers, which led to his excommunication in 1230.

During the crusade of 1228–29, Bohemund IV first joined and subsequently deserted Emperor Frederick II. As a result, he was excluded from the operation of the treaty of 1229. Bohemund IV died in 1233, shortly after concluding peace with the Hospitallers and being released from his excommunication by Gregory IX.

## Historical Context of the Region
The struggle for Antioch occurred within a broader landscape of instability in Asia Minor and the Levant. The region had seen the rise of the Seljuk Turks, who captured Emperor Romanus Diogenes in 1071 and established the empire of Rum. While the Seljuks once controlled portions of Syria and Armenia, they were eventually defeated by the Mongols in 1243.

Simultaneously, the support provided by Latin princes to the Armenians in Cilicia aided the development of the state of Lesser Armenia. This warlike state eventually fell in 1375 when the Mameluke sultan of Egypt defeated and captured its last king, Leo VI (also referred to as Leo V in some records).
