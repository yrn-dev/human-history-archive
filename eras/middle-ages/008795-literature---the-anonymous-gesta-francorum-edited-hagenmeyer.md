# Gesta Francorum

The *Gesta Francorum* is a title associated with several historical and literary works within the archive, most notably an anonymous account of the first crusade and a separate historical text concerning the early Franks.

## The Crusade Account
One anonymous work titled *Gesta Francorum* (edited by H. Hagenmeyer) was written by a follower of Bohemund. It is listed as a primary source for the study of the crusades, alongside other authors such as Robertus Monachus, Baldricus, Raimundus de Agiles, Albertus Aquensis, Fulcherius Carnotensis, Guibertus, and Willielmus Tyriensis.

## Early Frankish History
A separate anonymous author of the *Gesta Francorum* provides accounts of the early Frankish people and their leadership. This writer mentions Sunno and Marcomir, identifying Marcomir as the father of Pharamond. The text suggests that the choice of Pharamond, or at least the establishment of a king, was recommended to the Franks by Marcomir while he was an exile in Tuscany. This work is noted as the first mention of Pharamond, an attribution assigned to approximately the year 720.

The *Gesta Francorum* also records the defeat of the Alemanni and provides details regarding the actions of Clovis. In one instance, the author adds the epithet "optimam" (best) when describing the Gallic lands. Additionally, the text describes a specific action by the king involving the throwing of a *Bipennem*, also known as a *Francisca*.

## Other Related Works
The archive distinguishes the aforementioned texts from other similarly named works:
*   **Gesta Regum Francorum**: An anonymous work in which the writer suggests that certain instruments of Christian worship belonged to the temple of Solomon, though it is noted this may imply they were found during the sack of Rome.
*   **Gesta Regis Dagobert**: A text cited in the collection of the Historians of France.
*   **Gesta episcoporum Cambracensium**: A chronicle of the bishops of Cambrai from the 11th century. While once attributed to Balderic, the archbishop of Noyon, it is now believed to have been written by an anonymous canon of Cambrai.
