# Fall of the Hojo and Rise of the Ashikaga

The transition of power from the Hojo regents to the Ashikaga family marked a significant shift in the military administration of Japan, characterized by the collapse of the Kamakura Bakufu and the subsequent struggle for supremacy among the Minamoto descendants.

## The Hojo Regency and Social Conditions
The Hojo family held the office of *shikken* (regent), a position of power increased by Tokimasa, who served as the guardian of Yoritomo and eventually became *shikken* after killing Yoriiye. Under the Hojo, the regency was established and characterized by "excellent rule," though the regents sought to enforce strict frugality. In the middle of the thirteenth century, the Hojo enacted laws interdicting the brewing of sake throughout the empire and vetoing the serving of cakes at meals. While these ordinances could not be strictly enforced, they influenced the samurai class to limit themselves to two meals a day consisting of the coarsest fare.

## The Downfall of the Hojo
The decline of Hojo prestige accelerated following the escape of the Emperor from Oki. Loyal samurai joined the Imperial cause, notably Akamatsu Norimura, whose successes against the Rokuhara army in Settsu pushed his forces toward Kyoto. In response to this crisis, Hojo Takatoki—the last of the Hojo regents (1311–1333)—ordered Ashikaga Takauji to lead a powerful army westward.

Ashikaga Takauji belonged to a junior branch of the Minamoto family, descended from Yoshiiye. Despite his commission from the Hojo, Takauji harbored personal ambitions and resentment toward Takatoki. This bitterness stemmed from being required to attack Masashige's fortress at Kasagi while in mourning for his father, and from frequent summonses to conference with Takatoki despite his own illness.

Simultaneously, Nitta Yoshisada, also of the Minamoto blood but of a closer kinship to the main line, raised the Imperial standard in Kotsuke province in June 1333. The people of the eastern region, weary of heavy requisitions and taxes from a three-year struggle, joined Yoshisada in large numbers. Yoshisada had long resented the subordinate position of Yoritomo's descendants and had planned his desertion of the Hojo while besieging Masashige in Chihaya fortress, eventually obtaining a mandate from Prince Morinaga.

## The Rise of the Ashikaga
Following the fall of Rokuhara, Ashikaga Takauji emerged as a primary figure, though he faced a rival in Nitta Yoshisada. Because Yoshisada had remained unswervingly loyal to Go-Daigo and had not borne arms against the Imperial cause as Takauji had, Takauji took extreme measures to eliminate him. He seized Nitta family domains in the Kwanto to distribute among his own followers and petitioned the Throne to destroy Yoshisada, labeling him a "national thief."

The transition was not immediate. In July 1335, Hojo Tokiyuki, a son of Takatoki who had escaped to Shinano province, led a resurgence. By August, he captured Kamakura, forcing the fugitive Ashikaga Tadayoshi and the ten-year-old shogun, Prince Narinaga, to flee. In Kyoto, Ashikaga Takauji utilized this instability by fanning the discontent of unrecompensed *bushi*. He applied for a commission to quell the Hojo insurrection and requested to be nominated shogun. When this request was rejected, he left the capital without paying final respects to the Throne, a move intended to attract partisans.

## Characteristics of the Ashikaga Era
The Ashikaga administration was marked by a distinct lack of the internal stability seen during the Hojo era. While the Hojo period was conspicuously free from internecine strife, the Ashikaga annals were disfigured by it, which deferred the total supremacy of the sept. An early example of this discord occurred in 1348, when General Ko Moronao was forced to abandon a victory over the Southern Court and return to Kyoto to avoid a plot led by Uesugi Shigeyoshi and Ashikaga Tadayoshi.

## Later Hojo Developments in Odawara
Separate from the regents of Kamakura, a later Hojo line emerged in Odawara. Soun (or Nagauji), active between 1432 and 1519, seized Izu province and reduced taxes. His son, Ujitsuna, expanded this power, crushing the Satomi family in 1538. This was followed by the leadership of Hojo Ujiyasu, who pushed into Suruga and faced a league consisting of the Imagawa, the Ashikaga of Koga, and two branches of the Uesugi. Despite the league attacking with eighty thousand men, Ujiyasu led a small fraction of that number to a victory that established Hojo supremacy in the Kwanto.
