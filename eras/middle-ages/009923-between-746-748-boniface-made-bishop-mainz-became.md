# Saint Boniface

Saint Boniface (680–754), born Wynfrith to a good Saxon family in Devonshire at either Kirton or Crediton, was a scholar and preacher who became known as the apostle of Germany. After studying grammar and theology at Exeter and under abbot Winberht at Nutcell near Winchester, he first attempted a missionary journey to Frisia in 716. This initial effort was cut short, likely due to the hostility of Radbod, the king of the Frisians, who was then at war with Charles Martel.

## Early Missions and Papal Commission
Boniface traveled to Rome at the end of 717. In 719, Pope Gregory II commissioned him to evangelize Germany and counteract the influence of Irish monks in the region. After crossing the Alps and visiting Thuringia and Bavaria, Boniface returned to Frisia following the death of Radbod. For three years, he preached successfully under the direction of Willibrord, the first bishop of Utrecht.

By 722, Boniface had converted and baptized many heathens and won over several chieftains in Thuringia and Hesse. Following these successes, he was summoned to Rome and consecrated as a bishop on November 30, 722, after swearing an oath of obedience to the pope. He returned to the field with letters of recommendation to Charles Martel, tasked with both converting heathens and suppressing heresy.

## Ecclesiastical Organization and Reform
Boniface's career was greatly enabled by the protection of Charles Martel. He conducted a systematic crusade in heathen Germany, which included overturning idols, baptizing converts, and founding monasteries and churches. He recruited a band of missionary helpers from England, including monks and nuns such as St. Lioba, St. Thecla, St. Walburge, St. Gregory (abbot at Utrecht), St. Burchard (bishop of Wurzburg), and St. Lull.

Boniface was created archbishop in 732. After a third visit to Rome in 738, he organized the church in Bavaria into four bishoprics: Passau, Salzburg, Freising, and Regensburg. In 741, Pope Zacharias appointed him legate and charged him with the reformation of the entire Frankish church. With the support of Pippin and Carloman—who had succeeded Charles Martel as mayors of the palace—Boniface organized the east Frankish church into the bishoprics of Eichstadt, Buraburg, Wurzburg, and Erfurt, placing his own monks in charge.

In 742, Boniface presided over what is generally considered the first German council. During this period, he also founded the abbey of Fulda as a center for German monastic culture. He placed the abbey under the Bavarian Sturm and implemented a rule stricter than the Benedictine rule.

## Conflict and Later Years
Boniface engaged in a theological and disciplinary dispute with Virgil, the Irish bishop of Salzburg, who held the heresy that worlds other than our own existed. While Virgil held his own against Boniface and was later canonized, Boniface found more success in France. There, he opposed a Frankish bishop of Neustria named Adalbert (or Aldebert), who had drawn people away from churches by claiming to receive relics directly from angels rather than from Rome.

Between 746 and 748, Boniface was made bishop of Mainz. In this role, he became metropolitan over Utrecht, the Rhine bishoprics, and the sees he had established in Germany, thereby establishing the pre-eminence of the see of Mainz. In 747, a synod of Frankish bishops sent a formal statement of submission to papal authority to Rome, an act of significance given the previous Merovingian tendencies toward national churches.

## Death and Legacy
Boniface did not appear to participate in the anointing of Pippin as king of the Franks in 752. In 754, he resigned his archbishopric in favor of his successor, Lull, to pursue his original plan of a mission to Frisia. However, on June 5, 754, Boniface and his companions were massacred by heathens near Dockum. His remains were later moved to Fulda.

Often described as the "proconsul of the papacy," Boniface is remembered for his organizing genius and missionary zeal. He brought the missionary movement—previously largely independent and carried out by schismatic Irish monks—under the direction of Rome. His efforts to bind scattered centers to the papacy were driven by a zeal for unity of faith rather than political motives.
