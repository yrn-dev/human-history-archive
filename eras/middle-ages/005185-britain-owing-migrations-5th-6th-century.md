# Britain and Migrations in the 5th and 6th Century A.D.

The history of Britain during the 5th and 6th centuries A.D. was defined by significant demographic shifts, linguistic changes, and territorial conquests resulting from foreign migrations. These movements altered the cultural and political landscape of the island and extended the influence of the region into continental Europe.

## Migrations to Brittany
The region of Brittany (known in French as *Bretagne*), located in western France, derived its name from Britain. This naming is the result of migrations that took place from Britain to the continent during the 5th and 6th centuries A.D.

## Linguistic Transitions
The English language is not native to Britain; it was introduced by foreign immigrants many centuries after the dawn of history. Prior to the Roman Conquest, the native inhabitants spoke languages belonging to the Celtic branch of the Indo-European (or Indo-Germanic) family. These were divided primarily into two groups:
*   **Brythonic dialects:** Allied to Cornish and Welsh, these were spoken across the majority of Britain, extending as far north as the firths of Clyde and Forth.
*   **Goidelic dialects:** Akin to Scottish Gaelic and Irish, these prevailed in the west, including the Isle of Man and Ireland.

During the Roman occupation from A.D. 43 to 409, Latin became the probable ordinary speech of the towns. This influence was significant enough that Gildas, writing nearly a century and a half after the renunciation of Honorius in 410, used Latin to address British princes. While Britain might have developed a neo-Latinic language similar to those in Spain or Gaul, this trajectory was changed by Teutonic conquests during the 5th and 6th centuries.

## Teutonic Conquests and Territorial Shifts
The 5th and 6th centuries were marked by Teutonic invasions and a subsequent long period of warfare between the invaders and the native population. By the end of the 5th century, the invaders had likely secured the eastern part of Britain, reaching at least as far as the Humber.

The advance of the invaders faced a significant check at the siege of "Mons Badonicus." While some records date this to the year 517, it may have occurred fifteen or twenty years earlier. According to Gildas, this event ushered in a period of peace lasting at least forty-four years.

However, by the latter part of the 6th century, the invaders expanded their territories further:
*   **The South:** The West Saxons are credited with conquering Wiltshire, the upper Thames valley, and the region extending to the Severn. Later, in the mid-7th century, they conquered Somerset, having likely acquired Dorset previously, followed by parts of Devon.
*   **The North and Center:** The northern frontier pushed forward, potentially into Scotland. The central districts between the Thames and the Trent, as well as the basin of the Trent, were likely conquered during this time.

Further expansions occurred around 615 with the destruction of Chester, the overthrow of the British kingdom of Elmet in south-west Yorkshire, and the occupation of the Lothians and Shropshire. Herefordshire was likely occupied somewhat later.

## Religious Influence in the 5th and 6th Centuries
Parallel to these political upheavals, religious missions were active in the west. In the 5th century, St. Patrick sent missionaries from Ireland, including St. Piran, to Cornwall. St. Piran eventually became the patron saint of tin-miners. This connection to Ireland and Wales was the primary means by which Cornwall was christianized. Evidence of this period remains in the form of oratories—small, rude, parallelogram-shaped buildings typically placed near springs—and crosses that appear throughout the country from the 6th century onward.
