# Responsa ad consulta Bulgarorum

The *Responsa ad consulta Bulgarorum* is a document associated with Pope Nicholas, who served from 858 to 867. The text is noted for its rulings on the validity of baptismal formulas.

## Baptismal Validity and the Formula of Christ
In the *Responsa ad consulta Bulgarorum*, Pope Nicholas allowed baptism to be considered valid *tantum in nomine Christi* (only in the name of Christ). This position aligns with practices found in the Acts. 

This ruling exists within a broader historical context of varying baptismal formulas. While the *Teaching of the Apostles* prescribes baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, it also refers to those baptized into the name of the Lord, which was the normal formula of the New Testament. Similarly, in the 3rd century, baptism in the name of Christ was widespread enough that Pope Stephen declared it valid, opposing Cyprian of Carthage. Other figures and bodies also asserted the validity of baptism into the name of Christ alone, including Hilary, the African monk Ursinus, and the synod of Nemours in A.D. 1284.

## Contrasting Theological Views
The acceptance of the formula *tantum in nomine Christi* was not universal among church authorities. Basil, in his work *On the Holy Spirit*, condemned baptism into the Lord alone, characterizing it as insufficient. Furthermore, Pope Zachariah noted that Celtic missionaries had omitted one or more persons of the Trinity during baptism, a practice that led the church of Rome to anathematize them.

## Comparison of Baptismal Practices
The standard formula used by Rome is "I baptize thee in the name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit." In contrast, the Eastern practice utilizes the phrase "so-and-so, the servant of God, is baptized," adding "Amen" after each person and concluding with "Now and ever and to aeons of aeons, amen."

Regarding the physical act of baptism, Tertullian explained trine immersion as being derived from the triple invocation, stating that believers are dipped thrice for the several names and persons. Jerome supported this, suggesting that the triple plunging demonstrates the one sacrament of the Trinity. However, other figures—including Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Gregory of Nyssa, Leo of Rome, and Theophylactus—viewed trine immersion as symbolic of the three days Christ was entombed. This interpretation is specifically enjoined in the Armenian baptismal rubric and an epistle from Macarius of Jerusalem (c. 330), where Armenian writers associate the practice with baptism into the death of Christ. Armenian fathers often specified that baptism "into the death of Christ" was the only essential element.
