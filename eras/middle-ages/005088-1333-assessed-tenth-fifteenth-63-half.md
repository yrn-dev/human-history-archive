# Historical Assessments and Taxation

The history of global administration is marked by various systems of assessment, ranging from ecclesiastical taxes and property levies to the funding of massive religious monuments and the regulation of municipal fishing villages.

## Ecclesiastical Taxation and Benefices
In the 13th century, the council of Lyons imposed a tax consisting of a tenth part of all church revenues for a period of six years to provide relief for the Holy Land. In Scotland, the collection of this tax was entrusted to Master Boiamund (Bagimund) de Vitia. Boiamund sought to assess the tax based on the true value of benefices at the time of assessment rather than using old conventional valuations. Despite objections from the Scottish clergy, who held a council at Perth in August 1275 to request the older method, the pope insisted on the use of true values. A fragment of "Bagimond's Roll," preserved at Durham, lists the real values and tenth parts of benefices in the archdeaconry of Lothian. This roll later served as a supplement to the *Taxatio Ecclesiastica Angliae et Walliae* and formed the basis for ecclesiastical taxation for several centuries, including the taxation of the Church of Scotland in the 16th century.

In other contexts, benefices with an annual value of £50 or more pay tenths of yearly profits and first fruits (one year's profits) to Queen Anne's Bounty to support poorer clergy. Additionally, tithe rent charges attached to a benefice receive relief from one-half of the agricultural rates assessed upon them.

## Municipal and Property Assessments in Europe
In England, the village of Brighton (historically known as Bristelmeston, Brichelmestone, or Brighthelmston) provides a record of evolving economic assessment. In 1086, the Domesday Book noted its three manors belonged to Earl Godwin and were held by William de Warenne; the village's status as a large fishing town was evidenced by a rent of 4,000 herrings. By 1333, Brighton was assessed for a fifteenth and a tenth at £5:4:6¾, which was half the assessment of Shoreham. Although it had no merchants in 1340—only tenants of lands—its prosperity grew through the 15th and 16th centuries, reaching an assessment of £6:12:8 in 1534.

In Venice, historical documents regarding the family of Marco Polo show that in 1379, Donna Fantina was assessed at 1,300 *lire* as a resident of S. Giovanni Grisostomo. This was part of the *Estimo*, or forced loan, for the Genoese war. Her son, Pietro Bragadino, was assessed at 1,500 *lire*.

In ancient Athens, direct assessments on property were mentioned in speeches by Lysias. A citizen named Euripides proposed a direct property tax of one-fortieth (2.5%) intended to produce 500 talents. While initially popular, the measure was eventually rejected as unsatisfactory.

## Japanese Land and Imperial Projects
In Japan, official revenue was primarily derived from cultivated land (*koden*). By the middle of the 15th century, rice production covered approximately two and a half million acres (one million *cho*), owned by shrines, temples, military magnates, Court nobles, and the Court. Assessments eventually shifted from *cho* and *tan* to *kwan* (one thousand cash or *mori*), which referred to the military service leviable upon the land. In this system, one *kwan-mon* represented 1,000 *tsubo* (where one *tsubo* is a square of 6 feet side).

Imperial patronage also funded massive religious works, such as the "Nara Daibutsu," a bronze Buddha. Proclaimed by Emperor Shomu on November 7, 743, the project welcomed contributors of any means, even those bringing a handful of clay or a twig for the furnace. The resulting image stood fifty-three and a half feet high with a sixteen-foot face. The construction required 986,030,000 lbs. of copper and 870 lbs. of refined gold for gilding.

## Modern Income Tax in Bavaria
By 1900, Bavaria implemented an income tax system based on a scale of earned and unearned income. Earned income rates began at 0.1% for incomes up to £37 10s. and rose to 3% on every £50 for incomes beyond £2,500. Fixed incomes were returned at the actual amount at the time of assessment, while fluctuating incomes (such as those from artistic or scientific work) were assessed based on the average receipts of the previous two years. Assessments for earned income occurred every four years. The system allowed for abatements for serious accidents, protracted illness, the maintenance of poor relations, or the education of children.
