# Thomas Arundel

## Early Life and Ecclesiastical Rise
Thomas Arundel (1353–1414) was the third son of Eleanor, daughter of Henry Plantagenet, earl of Lancaster, and Richard Fitzalan, earl of Arundel and Warenne. Born into an old and influential family, Arundel experienced a rapid ascent within the church. He was appointed archdeacon of Taunton in 1373 and was consecrated as the bishop of Ely in April 1374.

## Political Career and Chancellorship
During the early years of King Richard II's reign, Arundel aligned himself with a political party that included Henry, earl of Derby (the future King Henry IV), Thomas, duke of Gloucester, and his own brother, Richard, earl of Arundel. In 1386, Arundel and Gloucester were sent to Eltham to persuade King Richard to return to parliament. Following the success of this mission, Arundel was appointed lord chancellor, replacing Michael de la Pole, duke of Suffolk. In this capacity, he helped facilitate peace between the king and those supporting the commission of regency.

Arundel's tenure as chancellor was interrupted by his appointment as archbishop of York in April 1388. He resigned the chancellorship in 1389 when Richard II declared himself of age, though he returned to the office in 1391. During his second term as chancellor, he moved the courts of justice from London to York, although they were subsequently returned to the metropolis.

## Archbishopric of Canterbury
In September 1396, Arundel was translated from York to Canterbury, leading to his second resignation as chancellor. As archbishop, he took a vigorous approach to asserting his rights, which included conducting a thorough visitation of his province to decide appeals from the judgments of his suffragans and warning the citizens of London against withholding tithes. In November 1396, he officiated at the marriage of King Richard II and Isabella, the daughter of King Charles VI of France.

## Fall and Banishment
Arundel's downfall occurred in 1397 as a result of King Richard II's sudden attack on the lords appellant. Arundel was arrested alongside Warwick and Gloucester. Despite the king initially offering hopes of safety, Arundel was impeached by the Commons with the king's consent. The charges against him centered on his assistance in procuring the commission of regency, which was viewed as a derogation of royal authority. He was sentenced to banishment and given forty days to leave the realm.

In 1397, Arundel, the duke of Gloucester, and the earl of Arundel were also involved in a conspiracy organized at Arundel Castle to murder the lords of the king's council and dethrone Richard II, though the plot was discovered before it could be executed. Additionally, in 1397, John of Gaunt presided over the trial of the archbishop and others, establishing a precedent for the steward's claim to be the supreme judge in parliament.
