# Zengi and the Fall of Edessa

The atabeg Zengi of Mosul was a rising power in the 12th century whose military campaigns significantly altered the geopolitical landscape of northern Syria and the Latin East. His efforts culminated in the capture of Edessa, an event that marked the beginning of the decline of the Latin kingdom of Jerusalem.

## The Rise of Zengi and Early Conflicts
During the reign of King Fulk of Jerusalem (who ascended the throne in 1131), the progress of Zengi of Mosul became a primary strategic problem. Fulk's reign was generally prosperous, but he is noted for having neglected to head a combined resistance against Zengi's growing power or to sufficiently envisage the needs of the northern principalities. 

The military pressure from Zengi was felt acutely; in 1137, King Fulk was beaten near Barin, where he was surrounded and forced to capitulate after escaping into a fort. To counter Zengi's progress, Fulk strengthened an alliance in 1140 with the vizier of Damascus, who similarly feared Zengi. This alliance allowed Fulk to capture the fort of Banias, located to the north of Lake Tiberias.

## The Fall of Edessa
Edessa served as the farthest and most important outpost of the Franks in northern Syria. It had been founded as the first principality of the Franks or Latins, subsisting for fifty-four years beyond the Euphrates. By 1144, the city fell to the Moslims under Zengi. 

The loss of Edessa was a serious blow to the kingdom of Jerusalem. While the kingdom had reached its zenith under Baldwin II, it began to decline following this capture. The fall of the city occurred shortly after the accession of Baldwin III (who became king in 1143), and it is recorded that the attacks of Zengi on Edessa, combined with Greek attacks in Cilicia, fatally weakened the position of the Franks in northern Syria.

## Aftermath and the Second Crusade
The loss of Edessa triggered the Second Crusade in 1147. Baldwin III, then approximately eighteen years of age, participated in this crusade alongside Louis VII and Conrad III. Although the crusade failed to recover Edessa, it is noted that the effort saved Antioch from a similar fate. In 1148, Baldwin III received the crusading leaders in Jerusalem and planned an attack on Damascus with them, though this operation ended in signal failure.

Following the failure of the crusade in 1149, Baldwin III sought to strengthen the north of Palestine against Nureddin, the successor to Zengi. To achieve this, Baldwin III renewed a politic alliance with Damascus that had been interrupted since 1147. He also ceded Tellbashir—the sole remaining remnant of the county of Edessa—to Manuel of Constantinople.

## Historical Context of the Principality
The principality of Edessa had been established by Baldwin (originally Baldwin de Burg), who was summoned to the city by the king of Jerusalem to serve as count. Baldwin de Burg had conducted continual forays against Mahommedan princes, facing attacks from Mosul and Mardin. He was later succeeded by Joscelin, who maintained the county on both sides of the Euphrates, replenishing the territory with Syrian and Latin subjects and strengthening its castles and magazines.
