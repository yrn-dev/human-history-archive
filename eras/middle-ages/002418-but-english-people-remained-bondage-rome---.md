# The Bondage of Rome and the English People

The concept of the English people remaining in the "bondage of Rome" is a theme explored within the context of early English drama and historical linguistic analysis. This notion appears specifically in the manuscript of the play *Kynge Johan*, where the narrative employs allegorical and biblical parallels to describe the political and religious state of the nation.

## Allegorical Representations in *Kynge Johan*
In the play *Kynge Johan*, the English people are described as remaining in the "bondage of Rome" until the arrival of "duke Josue," who is identified as the late King Henry. This figure is credited with bringing the people "out in to the lande of mylke and honye." The play utilizes biblical imagery to characterize its figures; for instance, King John is compared to a "faythfull Moses" who "Withstode proude Pharao for his poore Israel." 

The work blends real historical figures with allegorical characters. The nation is represented by the character *Ynglonde vidua*. Additionally, a character named Sedwyson, representing sedition, provides a jocular element to the play, serving a role similar to the Vice in a traditional morality play. The manuscript of *Kynge Johan* was discovered between 1831 and 1838 among corporation papers in Ipswich, a location where it was likely performed, as the text references charitable foundations established by King John in that town and its surrounding neighborhood.

The play's structure is noted at the end of the manuscript as consisting of two plays, though there is no obvious division other than the end of the first act. The first part was corrected by Bale, and the second half is written in his handwriting, although his name does not appear in the text. Bale identifies the work in his own list of works as *De Joanne Anglorum Rege*, noting it was written *in idiomate materno*. Within the play's narrative, John is also accused of "heretycall langage" and is called a Lollard, eventually meeting his end by being poisoned by a monk of Swinestead.

## Linguistic Evolution of Bondage
The term "bondage" has undergone a transition in meaning over time. Derived from the Teutonic root *bindan* (to bind), the word is a phonetic variant of "band," meaning "that which binds." Historically, "bondage" properly referred to "tenure in villeinage," but it has since become synonymous with "slavery."

This shift is attributed to an early confusion between two different Old English terms. One was "bond" (from "bind"), and the other was "bonda" (meaning a householder), derived from the root of the verb *bua* (to dwell or have a house). The latter is cognate with the German *Bauer* and the English "boor." In the laws of Canute, "bond" was used interchangeably with the Old English *ceorl*. Following the Norman Conquest, as the position of the churl became less free, the "bond" approximated the "villein" and later the "serf."

A remnant of the original meaning of the term survives in the word "bondager." This term generally refers to a servant, but in Northumberland and the south of Scotland, it specifically denotes a female outworker. Under this system, a married farm-labourer living in a cottage attached to a farm was required by his tenancy to provide this female labourer for field-work, sometimes including boarding and lodging, a practice that arose due to a dearth of field-labour.

## Historical Context of Rome and Britain
While the play *Kynge Johan* uses "bondage of Rome" as a thematic device, historical records provide a different perspective on the relationship between Rome and the British Isles. In the early centuries, the provincials of Britain were subject to Roman influence. Some historians and philosophers suggested that these provincials were totally exterminated and replaced by German colonies, specifically the Angles and Saxons. It is recorded that 300,000 Saxons obeyed the summons of Hengist, and the Angles emigrated in such numbers that their native country was left solitary.

Following the establishment of Saxon dominion, the "patient herd" of the unresisting country—the peasants—were preserved by their new masters to provide food and labor. An example of the transition from bondage is found in the kingdom of Sussex. Wilfrid, the apostle of Sussex, was gifted the peninsula of Selsey, including its inhabitants (eighty-seven families). Wilfrid released these people from both temporal and spiritual bondage, and 250 slaves of both sexes were subsequently baptized.
