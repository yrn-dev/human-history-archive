# European Stage as the Executor of the Ban of the Empire

The role of the European stage in the execution of the imperial ban is exemplified by the political maneuvers of Leopold II, who utilized his position to maintain order and stability within the Habsburg empire during a period of revolutionary unrest.

## Leopold II and the Imperial Ban
Leopold II entered the European stage as the executor of the ban of the Empire directed against the insurgent Liegeois. This action occurred during a time when the progress of the Revolution in France was causing enlightened princes to shift toward reaction. In response to these pressures, Leopold reverted to traditional Habsburg methods, restoring the supremacy of the Church as the primary bond of the empire and resolving the *Einheitsstaat* back into its original elements, including the old abuses and the machinery of estates and diets.

By acting as the executor of the ban, Leopold was able to position himself as the champion of order against the Revolution. This role allowed him to manage his foreign policy with skill, leading to the treaty of Reichenbach on August 15, 1790, which resolved conflicts with Prussia. This success paved the way for the armistice of Giurgevo with Turkey on September 10, and eventually the definitive peace at Sistova on August 4, 1791, which established the *status quo*. Following these diplomatic achievements, Leopold was able to reduce the Low Countries to order before the end of 1790.

## Nature and History of the Imperial Ban
In the context of the medieval Empire and Germany, the "ban" (known in German as *Acht*) functioned as a form of punishment that practically resulted in outlawry. This sentence, often referred to as the *Reichsacht* or imperial ban, was frequently employed by the German king or emperor. It could be applied to individuals, districts, or towns, and was sometimes categorized as either *Acht* (partial outlawry) or *Oberacht* (complete outlawry).

The execution of the imperial ban was typically entrusted to a noble or prince, who might be rewarded with a portion of the outlaw's lands. For the individual placed under the ban, the consequences were severe, often leading to absolute destitution for their relatives and death for the offender. 

Over time, the administration of the ban shifted from the personal act of the emperor to the imperial court of justice (*Reichskammergericht*) and the imperial aulic council (*Reichshofrat*). However, by 1711, these courts lost the power to issue the ban, retaining only the right to suggest its use. The last recorded use of the imperial ban occurred in 1706 against Maximilian Emanuel, the elector of Bavaria.

## Linguistic and Legal Origins of the Ban
The term "ban" originates from a Teutonic root meaning "to proclaim" or "to announce," with the Late Latin form being *bannum*. In the laws of kindred tribes and the Franks, the term had three primary applications:
1. A general proclamation.
2. The fine resulting from the disobedience of such a proclamation.
3. The district over which such proclamations were issued.

The *bannum dominicum* was used by feudal lords, including kings, to administer justice. If an offender failed to make amends for wrongdoing as ordered, the lord would withdraw all protection, resulting in outlawry.

## Variations of the Ban in Other Regions
Outside of the imperial context, the term "ban" evolved differently in other European regions:

**France:** Following the break-up of the Carolingian empire, the ban referred to a summons for military service. This led to a distinction between the *ban* (vassals called by the king) and the *arriere-ban* (sub-vassals called by vassals). These levies were last summoned in 1758, though local levies continued to use the names until the Revolution. Additionally, earlier French law used the "ban of wine" (*bannum vini*) to grant lords exclusive selling rights, and the "ban of March and April" to forbid cattle pasturing. In modern French law, *rupture de ban* refers to a released criminal escaping police surveillance or an obligatory place of residence.

**Other Usages:** In Scandinavia, the ban took on the sense of a curse. This merged with the practices of spiritual lords to create an ecclesiastical sense, where the papal ban became synonymous with excommunication. In modern English, the phrase "under the ban" describes conduct condemned by public opinion, while the term survives in the "banns of marriage."
