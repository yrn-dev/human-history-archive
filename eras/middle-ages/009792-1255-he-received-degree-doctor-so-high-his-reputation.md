# Academic Degrees and Professional Reputations in History

The attainment of a doctoral degree has historically served as a marker of high academic achievement, often leading to significant appointments in theology, law, and philosophy. Across various eras and regions, the reputation gained through such studies frequently propelled individuals into positions of leadership or institutional influence.

## Medieval Theologians and Schoolmen

In the 13th century, the university of Paris was a central hub for theological study. Saint Bonaventura (John of Fidanza), born in 1221 in Tuscany, entered the Franciscan order in 1243. After studying in Paris—possibly under Alexander of Hales and certainly under John of Rochelle—Bonaventura succeeded the latter in his chair in 1253. Having already received permission to read upon the *Sentences* due to his fame, he received the degree of doctor in 1255. His reputation was so high that he was elected general of his order the following year. Later, he was rewarded with the titles of bishop of Albano and cardinal by Gregory X., whose election Bonaventura helped procure. He died in 1274 at the great council of Lyons.

Similarly, John Duns Scotus (born 1265 or 1275) became a prominent schoolman. After studying at Merton College, Oxford, and later moving to Paris, he received his doctor's degree from the university in 1307. In the same year, he was appointed regent of the theological school. Scotus earned the title *Doctor Subtilis* through his dialectical ingenuity in defending the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception. This defense had such an impact that the university of Paris formally condemned the opposing Thomist doctrine in 1387 and later required all doctoral candidates to swear an oath to defend the Immaculate Conception.

## Legal Scholars and Jurists

The pursuit of doctoral degrees in civil and canon law also led to distinguished careers. Petrus Baldus de Ubaldis, an Italian jurist born in 1237, studied civil law in Perugia under Bartolus and was admitted to the degree of doctor of civil law at the age of seventeen. Following this promotion, he taught law at Bologna, Perugia, Pisa, Florence, Padua, and Pavia. Baldus acquired a great reputation among his contemporaries, partly through his consultations and his involvement in public affairs. He served as a master to Pierre Roger de Beaufort (Pope Gregory XI) and was summoned to Rome in 1380 by Urban VI to provide consultations against the anti-pope Clement VII.

In England, Sir Julius Caesar (1557-1636) pursued legal studies at both the university of Paris and Oxford. He was made a doctor of the civil law at Paris in 1581, and two years later, he received the same degree at Oxford, as well as a doctorate in canon law. These qualifications supported a career of high office under James I and Elizabeth, including roles as chancellor and under treasurer of the exchequer and master of the rolls.

## Later Academic and Professional Achievements

In the 18th century, Laura Maria Caterina Bassi of Bologna distinguished herself through extraordinary attainments, for which she received a doctor's degree. This allowed her to be appointed as a professor in the philosophical college, where she delivered public lectures on experimental philosophy until her death in 1778.

In the 19th century, German theologian Gottfried Christian Friedrich Lücke (1791-1855) received his doctor's degree in philosophy from Halle in 1814. His subsequent publications and lectures brought him into considerable repute, leading to his appointment as professor extraordinarius at the university of Bonn in 1818, and professor ordinarius that autumn. He later succeeded K. F. Stäudlin at Göttingen in 1827.

In a different context of academic recognition, the scholar Dollinger was conferred honorary doctorates of laws by the universities of Edinburgh, Marburg, and Oxford, and a doctorate of philosophy by Vienna. Despite his immense reputation as a divine and scholar, he declined to be consecrated as a bishop of the Old Catholic Church to avoid initiating a schism.

## Perspectives on Medical Practitioners

While academic degrees defined status in European theology and law, other cultures viewed medical expertise differently. In China, medical literature was voluminous and medicine was highly esteemed, yet the practice was open to anyone who chose to take it up. This lack of formal restriction led to a skeptical public view of practitioners, as reflected in proverbs stating that "No medicine is better than a middling doctor" and that "Many sons of clever doctors die of disease."
