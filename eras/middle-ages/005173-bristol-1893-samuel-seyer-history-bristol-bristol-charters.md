# Bristol

Bristol is a city whose greatness is attributed entirely to trade rather than its status as a military post, a shire town, or the site of a great religious house. While located near Roman and British camps, there is no evidence of settlements from those eras. The town began as a Saxon settlement around the year 1000 at the junction of the rivers Avon and Frome. Under Danish rule, specifically during the reign of Canute, Bristol became a market for English slaves and initiated a wool trade with Ireland. By the time of the Domesday survey, it was a royal borough governed by a king-appointed reeve, with the king's geld assessed at 110 marks.

## Early Governance and Charters
The town's early importance is evidenced by the existence of a mint at the time of the Conquest. Following a rebellion by Geoffrey de Coutances, Bishop of Exeter, William II granted the town and castle to Robert FitzHamon. Bristol later became the dowry of Earl Robert of Gloucester through his marriage to Mabel, FitzHamon's daughter, in 1119. Under Earl Robert, the castle was strengthened and used as a base for raids and the sale of prisoners into slavery in Ireland.

The transition from manorial to municipal organization was marked by several key charters. In 1172, Henry II granted a charter confirming existing liberties and exempting burgesses from certain tolls throughout the kingdom. In the same year, a charter granted the city of Dublin to the men of Bristol as a colony. Later, the marriage of Earl John to the heiress of Earl William of Gloucester brought Bristol into the royal demesne, allowing the town to shake off the feudal yoke. A pivotal charter granted by John in 1190 established that burgesses could not be impleaded outside the walls, prohibited non-burgesses from selling corn, wool, cloth, or leather in Bristol, and ensured burgesses held by burgage tenure.

By 1200, a commune based on the French model was established, and Robert FitzNichol became the first mayor. Unlike the previous reeve, the mayor was chosen by the merchants of the merchant gild. The mayor was assisted by officials known as provosts until 1267, stewards from 1267 to 1311, and bailiffs thereafter. A 1256 charter from Henry III further increased independence by granting burgesses the right to choose coroners.

## Trade and Industrial Development
Bristol's economy was heavily driven by its connection to Ireland and the Baltic. The wool trade was a primary driver, and the city also developed a wine trade with southern France. Other flourishing industries included tanning and soap-making. In 1337, Thomas Blanket introduced looms, sparking the development of cloth manufacture. By 1353, Bristol was designated as one of the Staple towns.

The city's commercial influence expanded through the Society of Merchant Venturers, whose records began in 1467. By 1500, this society directed all foreign trade and held a lease on port dues. The era of William Canyng (1399–1474), a five-time mayor, saw a massive cloth trade with the Baltic. Merchants also exported cloth to the Levant, Spain, and France. In the 16th century, trade extended to the Canaries, Spain, and Spanish colonies in America. Bristol also participated in attempts to colonize Newfoundland and engaged in the African slave trade, which flourished during the 17th century.

## Urban Expansion and Administration
The city grew by absorbing neighboring areas. A long dispute over the manor of Redcliffe—which had its own quay, market, and courts—was settled by a 1373 charter that extended town boundaries to include Redcliffe and the waters of the Severn and Avon. This charter also made Bristol a county in itself, independent of county courts, and established an elected sheriff and a council of forty. The town was divided into five wards, each with an alderman; only aldermen were eligible to be mayor.

Internal strife occurred in 1312 due to the exclusiveness of the merchant gild, leading to an insurrection that lasted until 1313, when royal forces took the town. Later, a 1499 charter from Henry VII altered the corporation's basis by mandating that the mayor and council elect the aldermen. In 1664, Charles II granted a formal charter of incorporation.

## Decline and Modern Revival
By the early 19th century, Bristol's prosperity diminished due to the rise of Liverpool, the abolition of the slave trade (which impacted the West Indian sugar trade), and high rates charged by the Bristol Dock Company (incorporated 1803). The city was also noted for its narrow, dirty streets and a history of violent riots in 1708, 1753, 1767, and 1831.

Recovery began with the efforts of the Bristol chamber of commerce (est. 1823) and the Municipal Reform Act of 1835. The new corporation bought the docks in 1848 to reduce fees. Further growth followed the construction of docks at Portishead and Avonmouth between 1877 and 1880, which the corporation purchased in 1884. In 1899, the chief magistrate was designated as a lord mayor.
