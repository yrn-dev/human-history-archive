# AVICENNA

Avicenna, known as Abū 'Alī al-Husain ibn 'Abdallāh ibn Sīnā (980-1037), was an Arabian philosopher and physician. He was born in Afshena, located in the district of Bokhara. His mother was a native of Afshena, while his father, a Persian from Balkh, served as a tax-collector in the nearby town of Harmaitin under the Samanid amir of Bokhara, Nūh II ibn Mansur. Following the birth of Avicenna's younger brother, the family moved to Bokhara, a major city of the Moslem world renowned for a culture that predated its conquest by the Saracens.

## Early Education and Intellectual Development
Avicenna demonstrated significant precocity as a child. By the age of ten, he had memorized the Koran and a substantial amount of Arabic poetry. His early education in arithmetic was obtained from a greengrocer, and he began studying higher branches of knowledge under a wandering scholar who earned a living through teaching and curing the sick. Through this teacher, Avicenna was introduced to the first propositions of Euclid and Porphyry's *Isagoge*.

Upon realizing that his teacher was a charlatan, Avicenna utilized commentaries to independently master geometry, logic, and the *Almagest*. By the age of sixteen, he had not only mastered medical theory but had also discovered new treatment methods through his unpaid attendance on the sick.

## Philosophical Studies
Avicenna spent a year and a half focusing on higher philosophy, a pursuit that presented him with significant obstacles. During periods of frustration, he would perform ablutions and pray in the mosque until he found the solutions to his difficulties. He studied late into the night, occasionally drinking wine to stimulate his senses, and reported that problems would sometimes be solved in his dreams.

His struggle with Aristotle's *Metaphysics* was particularly prolonged; it is said he read the work forty times until the words were memorized, yet the meaning remained obscure. He eventually achieved illumination after purchasing a small commentary by Fārābī from a bookstall for three dirhems.

## Medical Contributions and the *Canon*
Avicenna is referred to as the "Prince of the Physicians." His most significant medical work, the *Canon*, was used as a textbook in the universities of Montpellier and Louvain until approximately 1650. Between the 12th and 17th centuries, Avicenna became the primary guide for medical study in European universities, eclipsing other figures such as Avenzoar, Ali ibn al-Abbas, and Rhazes.

The *Canon* is based on the doctrines of Hippocrates and Galen, modified by the system of Aristotle. While similar in content to the works of his predecessors, the *Canon* is distinguished by a greater sense of method, likely a result of Avicenna's logical studies. The work consists of five books:
*   **Books One and Two:** Focus on hygiene, pathology, and physiology.
*   **Books Three and Four:** Address the methods of treating disease.
*   **Book Five:** Describes the preparation and composition of remedies, containing some of Avicenna's personal observations.

Critics of the *Canon*, including Avenzoar, have viewed the work harshly, with some describing it as "waste paper." Modern criticism often cites the book's over-subtlety in discriminating diseases and its excessive classification of bodily faculties. Additionally, while he was ample in enumerating symptoms, he is considered inferior to Ali in the fields of surgery and practical medicine. Avicenna introduced the four causes of the Peripatetic system into medical theory, though he claimed no special knowledge of botany or natural history.

## Professional Relations and Legacy
Avicenna maintained a correspondence with the Arabian scholar Biruni (973-1048), and the responses sent by Avicenna are preserved in the British Museum. 

Further biographical and scholarly details regarding his life and works are documented in various historical sources, including Ibn Khallikan's *Biographical Dictionary* and the *Geschichte der arabischen Litteratur* by C. Brockelmann.
