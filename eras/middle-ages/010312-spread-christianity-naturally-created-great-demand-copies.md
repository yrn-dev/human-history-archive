# The Demand for Sacred Texts and the Spread of Christianity

## Impact on Book Production
The expansion of Christianity led to a significant increase in the demand for written copies of sacred texts. Initially, this demand focused on the Gospels and other sacred books. Over time, the need extended to devotional volumes for both private and church use, including missals. 

An example of this movement of texts occurred in 671, when Benedict Biscop, the founder of the abbey at Wearmouth in England, transported a full cargo of books from France. Some of these volumes had been purchased, though the sources of the books are not specified.

## The Trade of Books in London and Oxford
In the period preceding the Reformation, the sale of books was managed by stationers (also referred to as text writers or *stacyoneres*). In London, these traders operated in the neighborhood of St Paul's, where some maintained stalls or stations built directly against the walls of the cathedral. In 1403, these stationers formed a gild. The books they sold included manuscript copies of prayers, the Creed, the Ave Maria, the Paternoster, and the ABC.

In Oxford, the supply of books was managed by "sworn" university stationers who held a substantial monopoly. One such sworn stationer was John More, who provided books to pupils and also functioned as a pawnbroker. This arrangement existed because most students could not afford to buy books, and the nature of instruction at the time was almost entirely oral. Despite this, large numbers of books frequently changed hands.

The monopoly of the sworn stationers was contested by non-sworn booksellers. This led to a statute passed in 1373, which noted that the presence of these unauthorized sellers resulted in the cheating of owners and the loss of lawful business for sworn stationers, as books of great value were being sold and carried away from Oxford. The statute enacted that no bookseller, other than two sworn stationers or their deputies, could sell any book exceeding half a mark in value.

## Social and Moral Influence of Christianity
Christianity emerged in the Roman world as a doctrine of salvation and immortality. It spread primarily among the unhappy, the lowly, and the downtrodden, including slaves. By providing hope and restoring self-respect, the faith encouraged its followers to stand for righteousness in the face of torment and persecution.

The spread of Christianity also introduced a new power into the human conscience. The spirit of Jesus acted as a great antagonist to the moral standards of the later Roman state, specifically opposing slavery and the "cruel shows" of organized murder used as sport. As the religion spread, these two practices dwindled and eventually disappeared.
