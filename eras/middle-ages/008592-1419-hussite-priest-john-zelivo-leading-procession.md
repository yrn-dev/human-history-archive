# The Hussite Wars

The Hussite Wars were a series of religious conflicts in Bohemia that began in 1419. These wars marked the beginning of the breaking-up of Latin Christendom and were the immediate result of the execution of the Czech reformer John Huss.

## Origins and the Trial of John Huss
The roots of the conflict lay in the weakening hold of the Church of Rome over Bohemia during the reign of King Charles, as pious priests such as Waldhauser and Milic attacked the immorality of the clergy. This alienation was furthered by the church schism, during which rival pontiffs engaged in medieval theological strife. In 1398, John Huss, a learned Czech, began delivering lectures at the university of Prague based on the teachings of the Englishman Wycliffe. These teachings spread rapidly, generating great popular enthusiasm beyond the educated classes.

Huss was eventually tried before the council of Constance. Although he had proceeded to the council with a letter of safe conduct provided by Sigismund, king of the Romans and brother of King Wenceslas, he was seized and declared a heretic. Huss was burnt alive on July 6, 1415. A colleague of Huss, Jerome of Prague, was burnt the following year.

## The Bohemian Response
The execution of Huss, who was greatly revered by the majority of the population, led to an immediate outbreak of civil war. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. In this document, they protested the execution of Huss, describing him as a "good, just and catholic man" who had been convicted of no offence. The nobles declared that those who claimed heresy existed in Bohemia were "liars, vile traitors and calumniators" and "sons of the devil." They pledged to defend the law of Jesus Christ and its humble preachers at the cost of their own blood, regardless of human decrees. This protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church.

## The Crusades and Military Conflict
In 1419, an insurrection of the Hussites broke out in Bohemia. In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade for the destruction of the Hussites, Wycliffites, and all other heretics in the region. This invitation attracted unemployed soldiers of fortune and "drifting blackguardism" from across Europe. 

Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites—who conducted their affairs on extreme democratic lines—resisted the invaders. The crusaders besieged Prague but failed to capture the city and eventually retreated from Bohemia after a series of reverses. A second crusade in 1421, as well as two subsequent crusades, also failed. It was not until 1431 that a fifth crusade, led by Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg, crossed the frontier, encouraged by internal dissensions among the Hussites.

## Internal Divisions and Resolution
The Hussite movement was characterized by theological strife and was largely democratic, utilizing the national language rather than Latin. The movement split into different factions:
*   **The Taborites:** The extreme party whose writings were later systematically destroyed by the Jesuits after the battle of the White Hill in 1620.
*   **The Calixtines (or Utraquists):** The more moderate party. Some of their works survived, including *Of the Great Torment of the Holy Church* and the *Lives of the Priests of Tabor*.

The split was largely driven by the extreme section's drift toward a primitive communism, which alarmed influential and wealthy Czech noblemen. This internal conflict led to a civil war among the Hussites in 1434, resulting in the defeat of the extreme section. In 1436, an agreement was reached between the moderate Hussites and the Council of Basle. This agreement allowed the Bohemian church to maintain certain distinctions from general Catholic practice, which remained in effect until the German Reformation of the sixteenth century.

## Related Figures and Literature
During this period, Peter Chelcicky (died 1460) emerged as an independent religious writer. While he was a strenuous enemy of the Church of Rome, he joined no Hussite party. His masterpiece, *Sit viry* (the net of faith), reflected his horror of bloodshed and his belief in accepting the decrees of worldly authorities, even if they were unjust. Other preserved works from the era include the *Postilla* by Archbishop John of Rokycan. Contemporary historical accounts of the wars were later collected and published by Frantisek Palacky in *Stare ceske letopisy*.
