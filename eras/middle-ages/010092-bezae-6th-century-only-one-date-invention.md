# Codex Bezae

The Codex Bezae (Cambridge Univ. Nu. 2, 41) is an uncial Graeco-Latin manuscript that dates no later than the 6th century, though it is likely considerably earlier. It is recognized as the oldest and best witness in a Greek manuscript to the "Western" text.

## Physical Characteristics and Layout
The Codex Bezae represents a significant point in the evolution of the book's physical form. During the period when papyrus was the predominant material, books typically took the form of the *volumen* (a roll wound around sticks). As parchment superseded papyrus, the *codex*—a form originally derived from three-leaved tablets sawn from a block of wood—became fashionable.

The transition to the codex influenced the arrangement of text. Early codices often mirrored the narrow columns of the *volumen*; for example, the 4th-century Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus featured four and three columns per page, respectively, while the 5th-century Codex Alexandrinus used two. By the 6th century, as seen in the Codex Bezae, the layout shifted to a single column per page. From this date until the invention of printing, the arrangement of books remained relatively conservative, with scribes using either single or double columns as convenient.

In the specific case of the Codex Bezae, the text is written in one column per page, with the Greek text positioned on the left-hand page and the Latin text on the right.

## History and Provenance
The manuscript was given to the University of Cambridge in 1581, although its early history remains a subject of debate. Beza claimed the manuscript originated in Lyons and had been preserved in the monastery of St Irenaeus. While Beza's *bona fides* are not questioned—and it is accepted that he obtained the manuscript following the sack of Lyons by des Adrets in 1562—the accuracy of his belief regarding its long-term residence in that monastery is doubted.

Evidence suggests the manuscript was in Italy shortly before arriving in Lyons. It is identified as the manuscript called [eta], which was collated by friends in Italy for the 1550 edition of Robert Stephanus' Greek Testament. Furthermore, it was likely used in 1546 by Gul. a Prato, the bishop of Clermont in Auvergne, at the council of Trent. This is supported by the fact that in the last edition of the *Annotationes*, Beza refers to the manuscript as *Claromontanus* rather than *Lugdunensis*.

Regarding its earliest origins, H. Quentin suggested the manuscript was used in Lyons during the 12th century, though this evidence is not considered convincing. J. Rendel Harris initially suspected Gallicism in the Latin text, but his later research indicated that corrections and annotations made between the 7th and 12th centuries point to a region that was predominantly Greek at first and later became Latin. This evidence suggests South Italy rather than Lyons.
