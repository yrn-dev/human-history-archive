# The Outrage of Anagni and the Papacy of Boniface VIII

## The Reign of Boniface VIII
Boniface VIII (born Benedetto Gaetano) served as pope from 1294 to 1303. Born into a noble family in Anagni, he was educated in civil and canon law in Italy and potentially in Paris. Before his ascension to the papacy, he served as a notary at the papal court and was created a cardinal deacon in 1281 and a cardinal priest in 1291. During his early career, he was involved in diplomatic missions and was once besieged in the Tower of London by the earl of Gloucester in 1267 while aiding King Henry III, from which he was rescued by the future Edward I.

Boniface VIII was elected pope at Naples on December 24, 1294, following the abdication of Celestine V. Unlike his predecessor, who lived in monastic humility, Boniface asserted the lordship of the papacy over all the world's kingdoms. His reign was marked by great pomp, including a coronation in Rome in January 1295 and the holding of a jubilee in 1300. The jubilee attracted a vast multitude of pilgrims, resulting in such a significant influx of money into the papal treasury that assistants used rakes to collect offerings at the tomb of St. Peter.

## Conflicts with European Powers
Despite his goals to pacify the West and recover the Holy Land, Boniface VIII's nine-year reign was characterized by controversies with major European powers, driven by his arrogance, lofty claims, and avarice. He faced opposition from the Colonna family due to his attempts to build estates for his own family. He also clashed with Albert of Austria regarding the German kingship and declared that the crown of Hungary should belong to the house of Anjou. In England, he attempted to try Edward I for interfering with a papal fief, but the English parliament declared in 1301 that Scotland had never been a fief of Rome.

The most significant conflict of his papacy was with Philip IV of France. Boniface was hostile to the French and sought to maintain the great traditions of Rome, leading to a confrontation that peaked in 1302 and 1303.

## The Outrage at Anagni
In September 1303, as Boniface VIII was preparing to pronounce a sentence of excommunication against the French king, he was surprised and arrested at his ancestral palace in Anagni. The operation was carried out by Guillaume de Nogaret, an agent of the French king, and Sciarra Colonna. Nogaret forced his way into the palace and entered the bedroom of the Pope, who was lying in bed holding a cross. The Pope was subjected to threats, insults, and, according to some accounts, was beaten.

The French king acted with the approval of his people, having gained the consent of a council of the Three Estates of France (the commons, church, and lords) before proceeding. There was no general manifestation of disapproval regarding this treatment of the pontiff in England, Germany, or Italy.

## Aftermath and Death
The townspeople of Anagni, who resented the outrage, rose up against Nogaret and liberated the Pope a day or so later. Boniface returned to Rome, but he was soon seized again and imprisoned by the Orsini family. He died a prisoner a few weeks later, shocked and disillusioned.

## Succession and the Avignon Papacy
Boniface VIII was succeeded by Benedict XI (Niccolo Boccasini), who was elected unanimously on October 22, 1303. Benedict XI, a former Dominican general and cardinal, worked to conciliate the enemies of his predecessor, including the Colonnas, France, and King Frederick II of Sicily. However, on June 7, 1304, Benedict XI excommunicated William of Nogaret and the Italians involved in the capture of Boniface at Anagni.

Benedict XI died at Perugia on July 7, 1304; reports suggested he may have been poisoned, with suspicion falling on Nogaret. His successor, Clement V, subsequently transferred the papal residence to Avignon, marking a shift of the apostolic throne from the Tiber to the Rhône.
