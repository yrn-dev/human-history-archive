# History of Britain After the Roman Withdrawal

The period following the withdrawal of Roman troops from Britain is characterized by significant instability, foreign incursions, and a transition in linguistic and political dominance. While the historical record of this era is described as extremely obscure, several accounts detail the collapse of Roman order and the subsequent rise of Anglo-Saxon influence.

## Post-Roman Incursions and the Saxon Arrival
Following the Roman departure, the inhabitants of the provinces faced devastating raids by the Scots and the Picts. According to the writer Gildas, the Britons sought protection from these incursions by calling upon the Saxons. While these Saxon allies initially achieved a decisive victory, they eventually turned their arms against the Britons, claiming they had not been sufficiently paid for their services.

An alternative account found in the *Historia Brittonum* suggests a different origin for the Saxon presence. This version states that the first Saxon leaders, Horsa and Hengest, arrived as exiles seeking the protection of the British king, Vortigern. After entering his service, they successfully expelled the northern invaders. However, they later overcame the Britons through treachery by persuading the king to allow them to bring over large numbers of their own countrymen. Tradition attributes the origin of the kingdom of Kent to these adventurers.

Various English, Gaulish, and Welsh authorities provide dates for the first invasion that generally fall within a twenty-five-year window, specifically between 428 and the joint reign of Valentinian III and Martian (450–455).

## Regional Shifts and the Selgovae
In the northern regions, specifically the area inhabited by the British tribe known to the Romans as the Selgovae, the withdrawal of Roman forces led to a different set of conquests. The Selgovae were conquered by Scots arriving from Ireland, though the two groups eventually fused. While the Saxons later attempted to conquer Dumfriesshire, the conquest does not appear to have been thorough; inhabitants of Nithsdale and other areas maintained their Celtic institutions until the time of David I.

## Linguistic Transitions
The linguistic landscape of Britain underwent a profound shift during and after the Roman occupation. At the time of the Roman Conquest, the native languages were of the Celtic branch of the Indo-European family. Brythonic dialects, related to Cornish and Welsh, were spoken across most of Britain up to the firths of Clyde and Forth. Beyond these estuaries and in the western isles, Goidelic dialects (akin to Scottish Gaelic and Irish) prevailed.

During the Roman occupation (A.D. 43–409), Latin became the ordinary speech of the towns. This influence was strong enough that Gildas, writing nearly a century and a half after the renunciation of Honorius in 410, addressed British princes in Latin. Had the course of events remained unchanged, Britain might have developed a neo-Latinic language similar to those in Spain or Gaul. However, this trajectory was altered by the Teutonic conquests of the 5th and 6th centuries, which introduced the English language—a tongue not native to Britain—via foreign immigrants.

## Religious and Cultural Notes
Records indicate that the Christian faith penetrated parts of Britain that were inaccessible to Roman arms, according to Tertullian. Later traditions mention a dispute in the Erse language between a foreign missionary and Ossian, the son of Fingal, occurring about a century after Tertullian's time. In the region of Ruthwell, an ancient cross preserved in the parish church utilizes Runic characters to recount the story of the Crucifixion.
