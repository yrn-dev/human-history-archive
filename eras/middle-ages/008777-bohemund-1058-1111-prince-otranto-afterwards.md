# Bohemund I.

Bohemund I. (c. A.D. 1058–1111), originally named Marc, was a Norman leader and prince of Otranto and later of Antioch. He was the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, *dux Apuliae et Calabriae*, born from a marriage contracted before 1059. Bohemund is regarded as the leader of the First Crusade.

## Early Career and Conflict with Alexius Comnenus
Bohemund served under his father during a major attack on the East Roman empire between 1080 and 1085. During Robert Guiscard's absence from 1082 to 1084, Bohemund commanded the Normans and penetrated into Thessaly as far as Larissa, though he was eventually repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early hostility toward Alexius significantly influenced Bohemund's future career and the subsequent history of the First Crusade.

Following the death of Robert Guiscard in 1085, a conflict arose between Bohemund and his younger half-brother, Roger. While Bohemund had been destined by his father for the throne of Constantinople, Roger—who was born "in the purple" to a Lombard princess named Sicelgaeta—succeeded to the duchy of Apulia and Calabria. This war was eventually resolved through the mediation of Urban II., who awarded Bohemund other possessions and Otranto.

## The First Crusade and the Principality of Antioch
In 1096, while attacking Amalfi (which had revolted against Duke Roger) alongside his uncle, the count of Sicily, Bohemund encountered bands of crusaders traveling through Italy to Constantinople. Driven by the zeal of the movement and a desire to realize his father's policy of *Drang nach Osten* (the drive to the east), Bohemund hoped to establish an eastern principality for himself.

He assembled a Norman army, described as perhaps the finest division in the crusading host, and crossed the Adriatic. He followed the route he had previously attempted in 1082–1084 to reach Constantinople. Upon his arrival in April 1097, Bohemund maintained a "correct" attitude toward Alexius Comnenus and performed homage to the emperor. Although he may have negotiated for a principality at Antioch, he received little encouragement from Alexius.

From the time of departure from Constantinople until the arrival at Antioch, Bohemund served as the real leader of the First Crusade. His leadership was instrumental in the crusade's success in crossing Asia Minor.

## Legacy and Historical Sources
Bohemund I. married Constance, the daughter of Philip I. of France. Their son, Bohemund II. (1108–1131), was born in the year of his father's defeat at Durazzo.

The life and career of Bohemund I. are documented in several primary and secondary sources. The *Alexiad* by Anna Comnena serves as a primary authority for his entire life, and the anonymous *Gesta Francorum* was written by one of his own followers. Other historical discussions of his career are found in the works of B. von Kugler, L. von Heinemann, and R. Rohricht.
