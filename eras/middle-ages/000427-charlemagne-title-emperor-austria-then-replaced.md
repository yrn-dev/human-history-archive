# Charlemagne

Charlemagne was a central figure in the early medieval period, serving as a king and emperor whose influence extended across various European territories and whose legacy served as a historical model for later rulers.

## Rise to Power and Conquests
Charlemagne became the sole king in 771, following the death of Pepin in 768. His reign was marked by significant territorial expansion and military conquest. In 774, he conquered Lombardy, and by 776, his reach extended into Dalmatia. He also conquered the district of Carniola, the majority of which he bestowed upon the duke of Friuli.

## Imperial Title and Governance
In the year 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne Emperor of the West. Under his administration, the empire established a system of frontier guards. Charlemagne and his successors appointed counts to protect these frontier districts; these officials were known as margraves (from the German *Markgraf*, meaning "count of the March"). While these margraves possessed the authority of dukes within their own jurisdictions, they were initially subordinate to dukes within the empire's feudal army.

Charlemagne's court also served as a place of refuge for foreigners, such as Egbert, an English refugee who later established himself as the King of Wessex in 802.

## Death and the Carlovingian Empire
Charlemagne died in 814 and was succeeded by Louis the Pious. However, the political unity of the realm did not endure; upon the death of Louis the Pious in 843, the Carlovingian Empire went to pieces. Following this collapse, there was no regular succession of Holy Roman Emperors until 962, although the imperial title continued to appear intermittently.

## Historical Legacy and Influence
The figure of Charlemagne remained a potent symbol of imperial authority centuries after his death. In 1804, Napoleon Bonaparte made himself Emperor of France in direct imitation of Charlemagne. This imitation extended to the coronation ceremony in Paris, where Napoleon took the crown from the Pope and placed it upon his own head, following the directions of Charlemagne.

Additionally, the "throne of Charlemagne" remained a point of political contention into the early 19th century. Francis II, the last of the Holy Roman emperors, feared that Napoleon might be elected as his successor to that throne. This concern, along with the desire to avoid being relegated to a position inferior to the Bonapartes, motivated Francis II to proclaim himself emperor of Austria as Francis I in 1804. This new title eventually replaced the designation "Imperator Romanorum semper Augustus" when the Holy Roman Empire came to an end in 1806.
