# The Muster of Chinghis Kaan Against Prester John

## Rise of Chinghis Kaan
In the year 1187, the Tartars chose a man named Chinghis Kaan as their King. Described as a man of great valour, ability, and eloquence, he quickly gained the allegiance of all the Tartars in the world. As they gathered to him in astonishing numbers, Chinghis Kaan equipped his forces with a vast array of arrows, spears, and other traditional arms. He began a campaign of conquest across eight provinces. His method of governance in these regions involved establishing some of his own men alongside a proportion of the local population, while leading the rest to conquer further provinces. Because he protected those he conquered and treated them well, many joined him "heart and soul," creating a multitude so great that they seemed to cover the earth.

## Conflict with Prester John
In the year 1200, Chinghis Kaan sent an embassy to Prester John to request his daughter as a wife. Prester John responded with great anger, rejecting the proposal as impudent. He informed the envoys that he would rather set his daughter on fire than give her to Chinghis, whom he characterized as a rebel, a traitor, and his own "liegeman and serf," claiming that Chinghis deserved death at his hand.

Upon receiving this brutal message, Chinghis Kaan was seized by a rage that nearly burst his heart. He publicly vowed that he would not remain prince if he failed to take revenge for the insult, declaring that Prester John would soon learn whether he was a serf or not.

## Mobilization and March
Following this insult, Chinghis Kaan mustered all his forces and levied a host described as one never before seen or heard of. He sent word to Prester John to be on his defense. Despite the scale of the approaching multitude, Prester John initially treated the threat as a trifle and a jest, asserting that the forces of Chinghis were "no soldiers." However, Prester John eventually marshalled his own people and forces, assembling a host of many different nations that was considered a "world's wonder."

Chinghis Kaan and his host arrived at a vast and beautiful plain called Tanduc, which belonged to Prester John. The multitude of Chinghis's people was so great that it was impossible to number them. When he received news that Prester John was approaching, Chinghis rejoiced, as the plain provided an ample and fine battle-ground. Prester John eventually reached the plain of Tanduc and pitched his camp 20 miles away from that of Chinghis Kaan. Both armies remained at rest for two days to ensure they were heartier and fresher for the coming battle.

## Divination and Victory
While camped at Tanduc, Chinghis Kaan summoned Saracen and Christian astrologers to determine the outcome of the conflict. While the Saracens could not provide a true answer, the Christians performed a divination using a cane split lengthwise. One half was named "Chinghis Kaan" and the other "Prester John." After reading a Psalm from the Psalter and performing incantations, the piece representing Chinghis Kaan moved without being touched and climbed on top of the piece representing Prester John. This was interpreted as a sign of victory. Delighted by this result, Chinghis Kaan thereafter treated Christians with great respect.

Following the two days of rest, the two hosts engaged in a desperate combat described as the greatest battle ever seen. Although casualties on both sides were very great, Chinghis Kaan obtained the victory, and Prester John was slain. Consequently, Prester John's kingdom passed into the hands of Chinghis Kaan until the entire territory was conquered.

## Later Years and Death
Chinghis Kaan continued to engage in conquest for six years following the battle with Prester John, capturing many strongholds, cities, and provinces. His life ended during an expedition against a castle called Caaju, where he was shot in the knee with an arrow and died of the wound.
