# Bokhara

## Historical Origins and Early Rule
Bokhara, known to the ancients as Sogdiana, is a city located in central Asia. The foundation of its capital is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Due to its eastern location, it was never brought under the dominion of Rome, though it was deeply affected by the various bloody revolutions of Asia. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana became part of the Seleucidae empire and shared a similar fate to Bactria.

Later, the nomad Yue-chi moved from the east into the valley of the Oxus, eventually establishing themselves as a settled territorial power in Sogdiana and Bactria. Around A.D. 45, King Kadphises I ascended the throne, ruling dominions that extended from Bokhara to the Indus. This district was subsequently reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand.

## Islamic Era and Regional Significance
Islam was introduced shortly after the Arab conquest of Persia, which took place between 640 and 642, and it quickly became the dominant faith. During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the region—then called Sughd—was one of the most flourishing and celebrated districts of central Asia. It was also known during the early middle ages as Ma wara 'l Nahr (or Ma-vera-un-nahr), a term translated as Transoxiana.

Within this region, two great cities emerged: Samarkand and Bokhara. While Samarkand generally served as the seat of government, Bokhara earned a high reputation as a center of learning and religion.

## Mongol and Timurid Conquests
The city experienced several shifts in power during the later medieval period. At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah, the third of the Persian Seljuk dynasty, crossed the Oxus and subdued the lands watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus rivers. In 1216, Mahommed Shah Khwarizm subdued Bokhara, but Jenghiz Khan wrested control from him in 1220. Although the savage conqueror wasted the country, prosperity partially returned under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent and humane.

The descendants of Ogdai Khan maintained possession until 1369, when Timur (also known as Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand and bore down everything before him, including Bokhara. Some sources indicate that until the conquest by Chinghiz, cities such as Bokhara, Balkh, and Samarkand were considered to belong to Persia. The first Mongolian governor of Bokhara was Buka Bosha.

## Culture, Learning, and Trade
Bokhara has long served as a center for religious life and learning. During the middle ages, Persian mysticism spread to the city. When Mongol invasions in the 13th century devastated other Moslem cities and Samarkand, Bokhara remained independent and continued to be a primary seat of Islamitic learning. While many rich *madrasa* libraries were lost to confiscation by emirs or destroyed by fire, treasures of literature remain in private libraries. Consequently, bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan visit the city to purchase rare books, making Bokhara the principal book-market of central Asia.

Economically, Bokhara is the most important trading town in central Asia. Its bazaars specialize in the production and sale of carpets, metal wares (particularly copper), silk stuffs, and Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins.

## Demographics and Modern Development
Estimates of the population vary; Russian travellers suggest the population does not exceed 50,000 or 60,000, while other estimates place it between 75,000 and 100,000. The population includes a large and ancient colony of Jews. 

Near the city, a Russian town called New Bokhara, or Kagan, has been rapidly growing on territory ceded by the emir. Located 8 miles from Bokhara near the railway station, Kagan has a population of 2,000.
