# Rupture de Ban

In the context of French law, the phrase *rupture de ban* refers to a specific legal violation concerning the movement and residence of criminals.

## Definition and Legal Application
Previous to the year 1885, *rupture de ban* was used to describe the act of a released criminal departing without notice while living under the special surveillance of the police. The French government maintains the authority to appoint an obligatory place of residence for any criminal; any escape from this designated location is classified as a *rupture de ban*.

## Etymological Context of the "Ban"
The term derives from the word "ban," which originates from a root common to many Teutonic languages meaning "to proclaim" or "to announce" (Late Latin: *bannum*). In the laws of the Franks and kindred tribes, the "ban" served three primary functions:
* A general proclamation.
* The fine resulting from the disobedience of such a proclamation.
* The district over which these proclamations were issued.

Because bans frequently commanded or forbade specific actions under threat of punishment, the concept of wrong-doing became associated with the term. This *bannum dominicum* was utilized by all feudal lords, including the king, to administer justice. Typically, it began as an order for the offender to make amends; failure to comply resulted in outlawry, which was the withdrawal of all protection from the offender.

## Other Historical Uses of the Ban in France
The concept of the ban extended into various other areas of French law and custom:

### Prohibitions and Exclusive Rights
In earlier French law, the "ban" was used to denote prohibitions. For example, the *bannum vini* (ban of wine) granted a lord the exclusive right to sell wine for a specific number of days. Similarly, the ban of March and April prohibited the pasturing of cattle in certain fields during those months.

### Military Service
Following the break-up of the Carolingian empire, the term "ban" was used in France to refer to the summons for the host. Eventually, the men liable for military service themselves became known as "the ban." A distinction later emerged between the *ban* and the *arriere-ban* (or *retro-bannum*):
* **The Ban:** Referred to vassals summoned by the king.
* **The Arriere-ban:** Referred to sub-vassals summoned by the vassals.

Liability for this service was sometimes commuted for monetary payments or subject to exemptions. While the *ban* and *arriere-ban* were noted for a lack of discipline during the 17th and 18th centuries, they were last summoned in 1758, though local levies between that date and the Revolution occasionally used these names.
