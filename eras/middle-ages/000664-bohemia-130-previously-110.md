# Bohemia

## Etymology and Early History
The name of the country is derived from the Boii, a Celtic tribe that inhabited part of the land during the earliest historical period. According to traditions accepted by modern Bohemian historians, the Boii—whose capital was Boiohemum—were weakened by constant warfare with neighboring tribes and were eventually subdued around 12 B.C. by the Marcomanni, a Teutonic tribe. The Marcomanni were later expelled by other Teutonic tribes, and the region was eventually conquered by Slavic tribes, with the Cechs being the most significant among them.

While archaeological research indicates Slavic inhabitants existed in Bohemia as early as the beginning of the Christian era, the Cechs are believed to have become the masters of the country in the 5th century. The first historical ruler mentioned is Samo, who defeated the Franks at the battle of Wogatisburg (630), likely near the current town of Eger, and defeated the Avars, a Turanian tribe that had briefly held overlordship over Bohemia. Following Samo's death, the historical record is obscure for approximately 130 years. Later chronicles, including those of Cosmas, describe the foundation of a Bohemian principality by Krok (or Crocus) and his daughter Libussa, who married a peasant named Premysl. This led to the construction of a continuous, though semi-mythic, list of Premyslide princes.

## The Reign of Charles
Charles, the son of a previous king, was elected sovereign by German electors at Rense in 1346. He is remembered as one of Bohemia's greatest rulers. Charles focused much of his attention on Prague, his favorite residence, where he founded the *nove mesto* (new town), which tripled the city's population and extent. He further increased the city's importance by establishing the famous university of Prague.

During his reign, Charles worked to re-establish order following the incessant feuds that occurred during the reign of John. He attempted to codify the contradictory and obscure laws of Bohemia, though this effort failed due to resistance from the powerful nobility. Charles's reign also saw the first symptoms of a church reform movement. Despite accusations of being overly subservient to the Church of Rome, Charles provided protection to several priests who supported church reform. In foreign policy, he shifted away from his father's approach, cooling relations with France and favoring an alliance with England before his death in 1378 at age sixty-two.

## The Hussite Period and Political Conflict
In the 15th century, royal authority in Bohemia ceased when Sigismund was deposed at a meeting of the diet at Caslav on June 1, 1421. A provisional government of twenty men from various parties was established, and a Polish prince, Sigismund Korybutovic, was chosen as sovereign. Although Korybutovic was recognized as regent by most inhabitants, he was recalled by Polish rulers after a few months due to papal influence.

This period was marked by civil war between the advanced Taborite party and the moderate Hussites, known as Utraquists or Calixtines. The threat of German invasion and the brief return of Prince Korybutovic temporarily reunited the Bohemians, leading to a decisive victory over the Germans at Aussig in 1426. However, Korybutovic later left Bohemia after incurring the dislike of extreme Hussites, ending hopes for an independent Slav dynasty. In 1427, German princes launched a crusade against the Hussites, supported by 1,000 English archers under the papal legate Henry Beaufort, bishop of Winchester. The crusaders fled into Bavaria following panics at Tachau and Mies (Stribro). Subsequently, the Bohemians took the offensive, invading the German states and Hungary.

## Administration and Education
In more recent administrative history, Bohemia is divided into ninety-four districts and two autonomous municipalities: the capital, Prague (population 204,478), and Reichenberg (population 34,204). Other significant towns include Pilsen (68,292), Budweis (39,360), Aussig (37,255), Schonau (24,110), Eger (23,665), Warnsdorf (21,150), Brux (21,525), Gablonz (21,086), Asch (18,675), Kladno (18,600), Pardubitz (17,029), Saaz (16,168), Komotau (15,925), Kolin (15,025), Kuttenberg (14,799), Trautenau (14,777), Carlsbad (14,640), Pribram (13,576), Jungbunzlau (13,479), Leitmeritz (13,075), Chrudim (13,017), Dux (11,921), Bodenbach (10,782), Tabor (10,692), Bohmisch-Leipa (10,674), Rumburg (10,382), and Weipert (10,037).

Bohemia possesses the lowest proportion of illiterates among the Austrian provinces. Education is led by two universities in Prague—one Czech and one German.

## Political Representation and Racial Constituencies
Bohemia sends 130 deputies to the Reichsrat at Vienna. The local diet consists of 242 members, including the two university rectors, the archbishop, and three bishops. 

The allotment of constituencies was based on tax-paying capacity and divided by race. In mixed districts, separate registers and constituencies were established so that electors could only vote for candidates of their own race. This system ensured that Germans voted for Germans and Czechs for Czechs, preventing Czechs from achieving victory over Germans.

In the late 19th century, political tensions rose regarding the constitution. In one instance, the Czechs, supported by Clericals and Feudals, gained a large majority in the diet. However, they declared the diet an informal assembly rather than the legal representative of the Bohemian kingdom and refused to elect delegates for the Reichsrat.
