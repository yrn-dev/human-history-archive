# Hussites

The Hussites were a religious and political movement in Bohemia that emerged in the early 15th century. Their movement was characterized by an insurrection against the established church, leading to a series of religious wars that marked the beginning of the breaking-up of Latin Christendom.

## Origins and the Trial of John Huss
The movement's foundations lay in the teachings of the Englishman Wycliffe, which spread throughout Europe. In 1398, John Huss, a learned Czech, began delivering a series of lectures on Wycliffe's teachings at the university of Prague. These ideas spread rapidly, gaining significant popular enthusiasm beyond the educated classes.

Between 1414 and 1418, the Council of Constance was convened to settle the Great Schism. John Huss was invited to the council under a promise of safe conduct from the emperor. However, upon arrival, he was seized and put on trial for heresy. Huss was burnt alive in 1415. The following year, his colleague, Jerome of Prague, was also burnt. These events are cited as demonstrations of the "evil spirit in priestcraft" and the conflict between "priest and anti-priest."

## The Hussite Insurrections and Crusades
The execution of Huss did not tranquilize the Bohemian people; instead, it triggered an insurrection of the Hussites in Bohemia in 1419. In 1420, Pope Martin V issued a bull proclaiming a crusade for the destruction of the Hussites, Wycliffites, and all other heretics in Bohemia. This invitation attracted various groups, including unemployed soldiers of fortune and "drifting blackguardism" from across Europe.

Under the leadership of Ziska, the Hussites conducted their affairs on extreme democratic lines and were fueled by intense enthusiasm. Despite the crusaders' efforts to besiege Prague, they failed to take the city and suffered a series of reverses. A second crusade in 1421, as well as two subsequent crusades, also failed. In 1431, Catholic Crusaders were dissolved by the Hussites at Domazlice. However, internal dissensions eventually weakened the Hussites, encouraging a fifth crusade in 1431 led by Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg.

## Diplomatic Relations and the Church
The Hussites remained a central concern for church councils. The fathers of the Council of Basle, who met in 1431, treated with the Hussites. There was significant tension between the council and the papacy regarding these negotiations; the fathers of Basel feared that if the council failed to engage the Hussites, the laity would accuse the clergy of shirking reform and the Hussites would perceive the Church as afraid to face them. By 1436, the Hussites eventually came to terms with the church.

## Political Influence and Mercenary Activity
The influence of the Hussites extended beyond Bohemia into the political affairs of neighboring regions. In Poland, King Casimir relied almost entirely on Hussite mercenaries to conduct the thirteen years' war against the Teutonic Order. These mercenaries frequently turned against the king when their wages were not paid.

Later, the papacy viewed the removal of the Hussite king of Bohemia, George Podebrad, as a necessary first step toward forming a league against the Turk. The Curia intervened in European politics to facilitate this, coordinating with Casimir to procure the election of Casimir's son, Wladislaus, as the king of Bohemia.

## Later Legacy
The label of "Hussite heresy" continued to be used by church authorities in later centuries. In the early 16th century, when Martin Luther published his *Theses*, the professor of theology John Mayr of Eck denounced them in a tract titled *Obelisks*, claiming to scent the Hussite heresy within Luther's writings.
