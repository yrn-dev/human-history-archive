# History of Districts under Mahommedan Influence

The historical records of various districts in India and surrounding regions frequently indicate a lack of detailed information regarding their early history prior to the period of Mahommedan influence or invasion. The impact of these invasions varied by region, leading to shifts in political authority, the establishment of new dynasties, and the conversion of local populations.

## Northern and Frontier Districts

In one district, little is known of the history before the Mahommedan invasion of A.D. 1033. During this year, Masaud was defeated and killed by the nobles of Bahraich. However, Mahommedan authority was not fully established over the country until the middle of the 13th century. Later, around 1450, Rajput adventurers known as Raikwars took control of the western portion of the district, a position they maintained into the modern era.

In the North-West Frontier Province, the district of Kohistan—meaning "country of the hills"—is also very little known. It is a region of over 1,000 sq. m. characterized by two main valleys separated by a mountain range exceeding 16,000 ft. The Kohistanis, who are Mahommedans but not of Pathan race, are believed to be remnants of old races driven from plains and valleys into the mountains by Mahommedan invasions. The majority of this population, totaling about 16,000, converted to Islam within the last 200 years.

In Bijnor, early history remains uncertain even after the transition to Mahommedan rule. The district was ravaged by Timur in 1399 and remained largely unheard of until the era of Akbar, when it became part of the Delhi empire. In the early 18th century, Rohilla Pathans established independence in the region of Rohilkhand. By 1748, the Rohilla chief Ali Mahommed began annexing Bijnor. Control later shifted through a feud involving the Mahrattas and the nawab of Oudh, before the district was ceded to the East India Company in 1801.

## Southern and Central Regions

The district of Bellary contains the ruins of the ancient Hindu kingdom of Vijayanagar. Following the overthrow of that state by the Mahommedans in 1564, the area was divided into military holdings managed by chiefs called poligars. The region underwent several changes in sovereignty: it was annexed to the Bijapur dominions in 1635, seized by Sivaji (founder of the Mahratta power) in 1680, and later included in the dominions of Nizam-ul-mulk. It was subsequently conquered by Hyder Ali of Mysore before falling to the nizam of Hyderabad in 1792 and finally being ceded to the British in 1800.

Similarly, the ancient Dravidian kingdom of Kerala (or Chera) was held by the Cholas until they were overthrown by the Mahommedans in 1310. Kerala was later absorbed into the Vijayanagar empire, which was destroyed by the Mahommedans in 1565. After a period of precarious independence under the naiks of Madura, the region was conquered by the Adil Shah dynasty of Bijapur in 1640 and seized by the king of Mysore in 1652.

## Resistance and Regional Variations

Assam remained the province of Bengal that most stubbornly resisted the Mahommedan polity and the Mogul empire. Despite frequent overruns by Mussulman armies and the annexation of its western districts to the Mahommedan vice-royalty of Bengal, Assam maintained an uncertain independence until the end of the 18th century.

In other regions, such as Badakshan, the population is divided by sect; the proper region is peopled by Arabs, Turks, and Tajiks who follow the Sunnite sect of Mahommedan law, while the mountainous districts are inhabited by Tajiks professing the Shi'ite creed. In the district of Backergunje, the Faraizis, described as a Puritan sect of Mahommedans, are noted as being exceedingly numerous.

## Terminology and Titles

The term "Khan" serves as a title of respect in Mahommedan countries, derived from the Turki word *khaqan*, meaning sovereign or emperor. Originally used by Mongol and Turki-nomad hordes—most notably assumed by Jenghis—the title is still applied to semi-independent rulers in Baluchistan and Russian Turkestan. In Persia and Afghanistan, it has evolved into an affix for Mahommedan gentlemen, and in India, it is frequently part of Mahommedan names, particularly those claiming Pathan descent.
