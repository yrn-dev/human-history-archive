# Protestant Sympathies in the Bohemian Army of Ferdinand

## Military Mobilization and Religious Tension
In the mid-16th century, Ferdinand attempted to raise a Bohemian army to support his brother. This task was complicated by the evolving religious landscape of Bohemia following the Reformation in Germany. While some advanced Utraquists held views similar to German Lutherans, the Bohemian Brethren—whose influence had grown significantly due to the support of several powerful nobles—strongly sympathized with the Protestants of Germany.

Because no armed force could be raised in Bohemia without the consent of the estates of the realm, Ferdinand utilized a "tortuous policy" to secure troops. He persuaded the estates to vote for a general levy under the "disingenuous pretext" that the Turks were menacing Bohemia.

## The Assembly at Kaaden
Ferdinand designated the town of Kaaden, located on the Saxon frontier, as the meeting point for the mobilized forces. Upon his arrival, however, he discovered that many nobles and cities had failed to send men, particularly those associated with the community of the Bohemian Brethren. Among the soldiers who did arrive, many were Protestants who sympathized with their co-religionists in Germany. Consequently, the Bohemian army refused to cross the Saxon frontier. By the end of 1546, Ferdinand was forced to disband these forces.

## Subsequent Levies and Political Unrest
Early in the following year, Ferdinand again called upon his Bohemian subjects to provide an army for his brother. This request was met with limited success; only a small number of Romanists and more "retrograde Utraquists" obeyed the order. 

The majority of Bohemians viewed this period as an opportunity to recover the ancient liberties of Bohemia. They felt Ferdinand had encroached upon these liberties by curtailing old privileges of the land and claiming a hereditary right to the crown. In March 1547, without waiting for a royal summons, the estates met at Prague in a proceeding described as unconstitutional. This assembly was characterized as revolutionary, driven largely by the influence of the knights and nobles of the Bohemian Brotherhood and representatives from the town of Prague. This revolutionary sentiment intensified following news of the elector of Saxony's victory at Rochlitz.

## Imperial Victory and Aftermath
The situation for the Bohemians shifted following the imperialist victory at Muhlberg, which temporarily crushed German Protestantism. The Bohemians found themselves in a difficult position, having offended their sovereign without providing aid to the German Protestants. The army of Pflug was hastily dispersed, and the estates at Prague attempted to propitiate Ferdinand by sending envoys to the king and his brother, Charles, while they were besieging Wittenberg.

Ferdinand accepted the envoys but maintained his objective of making Bohemia a hereditary Habsburg kingdom and curtailing its ancient constitution. He demanded that the Bohemians renounce all alliances with German Protestants.

## Restoration of Control
Ferdinand arrived in Prague on July 20 with a large force of Walloon and Spanish mercenaries, occupying the city with almost no resistance. While he treated knights and nobles with forbearance—confiscating only the estates of those most compromised—he dealt severely with the towns, specifically Prague. He revised their ancient privileges, which resulted in a broad confiscation of municipal lands. 

To ensure control, Ferdinand forced the towns to accept state officials known as town-captains (in Prague) and town-judges, who were granted almost unlimited control over municipal affairs. Additionally, the Bohemian Brethren faced severe persecution, and their bishop, Augusta, was imprisoned for many years.
