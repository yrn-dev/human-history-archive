# Bologna

Bologna is a city and archiepiscopal see located in Emilia, Italy. It serves as the capital of the province of Bologna and the headquarters of the VI. army corps. Geographically, it is situated at the base of the Apennines on the edge of the plain of Emilia, 180 feet above sea level. As of 1901, the town had a population of 102,122, while the commune totaled 153,501.

## Urban Development and Architecture
The city's layout reveals a distinction between the original Roman city and the outer city. The Roman city is more or less rectangular, oriented to the points of the compass with streets arranged at right angles. The outer city received its fortifications in 1206, and its gates are served by streets that radiate from the outskirts of the Roman center rather than from the center itself. While no Roman buildings remain above ground, several of the oldest churches are located outside the Roman limits. These include S. Giovanni in Monte, SS. Vitale ed Agricola, and S. Stefano. The latter is a complex of at least seven buildings of various dates; its earliest structure, the former cathedral of SS. Pietro e Paolo, was built around the middle of the 4th century using some debris from Roman buildings. S. Sepolcro, a circular church featuring brick ornamentation and an imitation of *opus reticulatum*, is likely from the 6th or 7th centuries.

The present cathedral, S. Pietro, was erected in 910 and is now primarily in the baroque style. The city's largest church is S. Petronio, dedicated to the patron saint of Bologna. Begun in 1390 in the Gothic style, only the nave and aisles up to the transepts were completed. This fragment measures 157 feet wide and 384 feet long, though the original plan for the cruciform basilica called for a length of over 700 feet, a transept breadth of 460 feet, and a 500-foot high dome. Additionally, the church of S. Domenico contains the body of the saint who died in the city in 1221; while its interior was remodeled in the 18th century, the exterior remains unfinished.

## Political History
Bologna's early political history is characterized by shifting allegiances. It was subject to the Lombards for only a short period, remaining generally under the rule of the exarchate of Ravenna until Pippin gave the exarchate to the papacy in 756. In 902, the city was sacked by Hungarians. While the exact date it acquired its freedom and its motto *Libertas* is uncertain, the first "constitution" of the commune of Bologna dates to approximately 1123, at which time it was a free and independent city.

Between the 12th and 14th centuries, Bologna was frequently engaged in war. It strongly supported the Guelph cause against the neighboring cities of Emilia and Romagna, as well as against Frederick II. In 1249, the Bolognese took Enzio, the son of the emperor, prisoner. Enzio remained a captive in Bologna until his death on March 14, 1272.

Internal struggles between Ghibellines and Guelphs eventually weakened the commune. In 1337, Taddeo de' Pepoli seized mastery of the town, and in 1350, his son sold the city to Giovanni Visconti of Milan. The papacy received the city ten years later, though Bologna soon revolted to recover its liberty. In 1401, Giovanni Bentivoglio became lord of the city but was killed during a rebellion in 1402. The city returned to the Visconti before being secured again in 1438 by the Bentivoglio family. This rule lasted until 1506, when Pope Julius II drove them out and returned Bologna to the papacy. Except for the revolutions of 1831 and 1821, and the Napoleonic period (1796–1815), the city remained under papal sway until 1860, when it became part of the kingdom of Italy.

## Academic and Intellectual Life
Bologna is home to a university that has produced notable scholars. Azo (c. 1150–1230), a professor of civil law born in Bologna, was a prominent figure among the glossators. His *Readings on the Code* provided a methodical exposition of Roman law that carried such weight in tribunals that it was said, "Chi non ha Azzo, non vada a palazzo." Azo was a pupil of Joannes Bassianus and a teacher to Jacobus Balduinus and Accursius. He also participated in municipal life after Bologna gained its independence. Other professors at the university included canonists Azo Lambertaccius (author of *Questiones in jus canonicum*) and Azo de Ramenghis (author of *Repetitiones super libro Decretorum*).

The city also produced Leopoldo Marco Antonio Caldani (1725–1813), an anatomist and physician who taught practical medicine in Bologna before moving to Padua. His brother, Petronio Maria Caldani (1735–1808), served as a professor of mathematics in Bologna and was described by J. le R. D'Alembert as Italy's first algebraist and geometer. In the arts, the painter Domenichino sought refuge in Bologna after facing professional jealousy and "cabals" in Rome before being recalled by Pope Gregory XV.
