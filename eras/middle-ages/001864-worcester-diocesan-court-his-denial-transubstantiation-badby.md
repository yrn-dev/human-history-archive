# John Badby

## Overview and Early Life
John Badby (died 1410) was an early Lollard martyr from the west Midlands of England. Records regarding his profession are not definitive, but he is identified as having been either a blacksmith or a tailor.

## Trial and Theological Conflict
Badby was brought before the Worcester diocesan court, where he was condemned for his denial of the doctrine of transubstantiation. During these proceedings, Badby presented a blunt challenge to the established belief, arguing that Christ did not have his physical body in his hand to distribute when he sat at supper with his disciples. He further asserted that if every host consecrated at the altar were truly the body of the Lord, then there would be 20,000 Gods within England.

## Condemnation and Execution
Following the proceedings in Worcester, Badby was brought before another court located in St Paul's, London. This court was presided over by Archbishop Arundel, who condemned Badby to be burned. The execution took place at Smithfield, which served as a tournament ground situated just outside the walls of the city.

## Final Encounter and Death
Accounts indicate that the prince of Wales, who later became Henry V, witnessed the execution. The prince offered Badby both a pension and his life on the condition that he recant his beliefs. However, according to the writings of Walsingham, Badby declined the prince's advice. He chose to be burned rather than show reverence to the life-giving sacrament. Consequently, he was burnt to ashes and died in his sin.
