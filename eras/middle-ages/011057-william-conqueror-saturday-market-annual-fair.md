# William the Conqueror

## Overview of Conquest and Land Grants
Following the Conquest, William the Conqueror redistributed lands and titles across England. In the case of Arundel (formerly Harundell), the Conqueror granted the town to Roger de Montgomery. At this site, Roger constructed a castle upon an existing ancient earthwork. Prior to this, during the time of Edward the Confessor, Arundel had consisted of a fortification or earthwork—likely established by King Alfred as a defense against the Danes—and a mill. By the time of the Domesday survey, Arundel was recorded as a port and a thriving borough.

Other figures associated with the period of the Conquest include Hugh de Montfort, who arrived in England with William the Conqueror and held Ashford at the time of the Domesday survey.

## Domesday Survey and Regional Impacts
The Domesday survey provides a record of land ownership and value during this era. In the north of England, towns such as Bridlington suffered from the ravages of the Normans. Bridlington, which had been a borough before the Conquest and was part of the possessions of Earl Morcar during the reign of Edward the Confessor, saw its value decrease from £32 to 8s. by the time of the Domesday survey. Following this decline, the town was held by the king due to the forfeiture of Earl Morcar.

In other regions, the Domesday survey notes that Ashburton was held in chief by Osbern, the bishop of Exeter, and rendered geld for six hides. In the Rape of Bramber, which includes Horsham, the land belonged to William de Braose at the time of the survey.

## Development of Markets and Fairs
While the Conqueror established the initial land grants, the subsequent development of commercial rights such as markets and fairs occurred under later monarchs and lords:

*   **Arundel:** Markets were held within the borough on Saturdays and Thursdays from very early times. In 1285, Richard Fitzalan, earl of Arundel, obtained a grant for two annual fairs on December 17 and May 14.
*   **Ashford:** Henry III granted a Saturday market and an annual fair to the lord of the manor in 1243. Later grants for annual fairs were made by Edward III in 1349 and Edward IV in 1466.
*   **Bridgwater:** A charter granted by John in 1201 provided for a fair on June 24 and a Saturday market.
*   **Bridlington:** In 1200, King John granted the prior of Bridlington an annual fair on the morrow, feast, and vigil of the Assumption of the Virgin Mary, as well as a weekly market on Saturday.
*   **Knutsford:** In 1292, the king granted William de Tabley a three-day fair at the Feast of St Peter and St Paul and a Saturday market at Nether Knutsford.
*   **Ashburton:** During the reign of Edward II, Bishop Stapledon obtained two annual fairs (November 11 and August 10) and a Saturday market.
*   **Durham:** The corporation's rights to a Saturday weekly market and three yearly fairs (Whit Monday and the feasts of St Cuthbert in March and September) are claimed under Bishop Pudsey's charter of 1179.
*   **Bury St Edmunds:** James I granted a market and an annual fair in Easter week in 1606; the corporation was later granted the reversion of two markets on Saturday and Wednesday.
*   **Bolton:** A charter for a yearly fair and weekly market was obtained from the crown by William de Ferrers in 1251. While the market day was Monday until the beginning of the 19th century, it was gradually superseded by a customary Saturday market.
