# Brienne

## Origins and the House of Brienne
Under the Carolingian dynasty, Brienne-le-Château served as the capital town of a French countship. In the 10th century, the town was captured by two adventurers, Gobert and Engelbert. The noble house of Brienne descended from Engelbert.

Members of this house achieved significant prominence in the 13th and 14th centuries. John of Brienne (1148-1237) became the king of Jerusalem through his marriage to Mary of Montsserrat, the kingdom's heiress. During his tenure, he led a crusade in Egypt, though it did not achieve lasting success. In 1229, while Baldwin II was a minor, John was elected emperor of the East, during which time he fought and conquered the Greek emperor John III (also known as Vatatzes).

Later, Walter V, who held the titles of duke of Athens and count of Brienne and Lecce (Apulia), campaigned against the Greeks. Although he initially drove them from Thessaly, he was defeated and killed near Lake Copais in 1311. His son, Walter VI, attempted to reconquer Athens in 1331 without success. Walter VI later served under Philip of Valois against the English and defended Florence against the Pisans. In Florence, he obtained dictatorial powers for himself, but his tyrannical behavior led to his expulsion. He was eventually appointed constable of France by John the Good and was killed in 1356 at the battle of Poitiers.

## Transition to the Houses of Enghien and Luxemburg
The countship passed from the original house of Brienne to the house of Enghien through the marriage of Walter VI's sister and heiress, Isabelle, to Walter of Enghien. Subsequently, John of Luxemburg-St Pol (who died around 1397) became the count of Brienne through his marriage to Margaret of Enghien.

The house of Luxemburg maintained control of the countship for several generations. During this period, the French branch of the Luxemburg family—descended from Waleran (d. 1288), a younger son of Count Henry II—produced several notable figures. Peter, count of Brienne (d. 1433), and his brother Louis (d. 1443), the chancellor of France and cardinal archbishop of Rouen, both supported the English during their struggle against France. Another brother, John (d. 1440), was a strong supporter of England and served as the governor of Paris under Henry V; John is noted for selling Joan of Arc to the English.

Peter's successor and son, Louis, initially fought for England but entered French service around 1440, where he obtained the office of constable. However, King Louis XI accused him of treachery. Louis took refuge with Charles the Bold, duke of Burgundy, but the duke surrendered him to the king, and he was beheaded in 1475. The male line of his elder descendants became extinct in 1482, merging into the house of Bourbon-Vendôme via the female line.

## The House of Loménie and Later History
The house of Luxemburg retained the countship until Margaret Charlotte of Luxemburg sold it to an individual named Marpon. Marpon then ceded the countship in 1640 to Henri Auguste de Loménie. Henri's wife, Louise de Béon, was a descendant of the house of Luxemburg-Brienne. 

The Limousin house of Loménie produced several well-known statesmen, including the celebrated minister of Louis XV, cardinal Étienne Charles de Loménie de Brienne (1727-1794). The final lords of Brienne were members of the Loménie family.
