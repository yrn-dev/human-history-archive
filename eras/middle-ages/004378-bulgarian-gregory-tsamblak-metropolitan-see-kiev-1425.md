# Gregory Tsamblak and the Metropolitan See of Kiev (1425)

## Appointment and Political Context
In 1425, Gregory Tsamblak, a Bulgarian, was elevated to the metropolitan see of Kiev. This appointment was carried out by Vitovt, the grand-duke of Lithuania. The elevation of Tsamblak had immediate political ramifications, specifically resulting in the weakening of the hold that Muscovy maintained over the south-western Russian states.

## The Reign of Basil I Dmitrevich
The appointment of Gregory Tsamblak occurred during the reign of Basil I Dmitrevich (1371–1425). Basil, the son of Dmitri (Demetrius) Donskoi, succeeded his father in 1389 and was married to Sophia, the daughter of the Lithuanian grand-duke Vitovt. 

Under Basil's rule, the grand-duchy of Muscovy became practically hereditary and asserted supremacy over surrounding principalities. Basil expanded Muscovy's territory by annexing the principality of Suzdal, as well as Murom, Kozelsk, and Peremyshl. He further acquired territory from the republic of Great Novgorod through a treaty and reduced the grand-duchy of Rostov to a state of vassalage.

Despite this expansion, Basil remained subject to the Golden Horde, receiving his *yarluik* (investiture) from the grand khan Tokhtamuish and paying tribute. The region faced significant instability, including an invasion by Timur in 1395, which ruined the Volgan regions but did not reach Moscow. Timur's raid effectively wiped out the Golden Horde, leading to twelve years of anarchy during which no tribute was paid to the khan; instead, Moscow collected vast sums for military purposes. In 1408, Muscovite territory was ravaged by Mirza Edigei, though Moscow itself was not taken. By 1412, Basil found it necessary to make a visit of submission to the Horde. Additionally, the population during Basil's reign was decimated by a terrible visitation of the "Black Death."

## The City of Kiev
Kiev serves as the headquarters for a metropolitan of the Orthodox Greek Church. Historically and economically, the city is a principal center for the general trade of the region and the Russian sugar industry, featuring the important Stryetenskaya fair. 

Archaeological findings in the city include more than twenty caves on a hill slope at Kirilov Street, one of which was excavated in 1876 and found to belong to neolithic troglodytes. In the same neighborhood, graves from both pagan and Christian periods were discovered, with the Christian graves containing over 2,000 skeletons and various small articles. Other discoveries include silver *dirhems* stamped at Merv, Balkh, and Samarkand, as well as colonial Roman coins from the 3rd and 4th centuries.

The population of Kiev grew steadily in the 19th and early 20th centuries, recorded at 70,341 in 1862, 127,251 in 1874, and 319,000 by 1902. This later figure included 12,000 Jews and 20,000 Poles. The city also serves as the headquarters of the IX. Army Corps.
