# Poetry and History of the Scottish Border

The Scottish Border region has produced a distinctive body of literature and a complex history of noble lineage, characterized by territorial conflict, raiding, and the preservation of folklore through ballads.

## Border Ballads and Literature
Border ballads hold a unique position within English literature. A significant number of these works were preserved from oblivion by Sir Walter Scott, who gathered materials from the district for his *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, published in 1802 and 1803. The stirring, pathetic, and picturesque incidents of the region, along with its folklore and traditions, served as a strong appeal for several writers, including John Mackay Wilson (1804–1835), who published *Tales of the Borders* in 1835, as well as John Wilson ("Christopher North") and James Hogg, known as "the Ettrick Shepherd."

The academic and historical study of the region's poetry and history is further documented in works such as Professor John Veitch's *History and Poetry of the Scottish Border* (1877) and George Ridpath's *Border History of England and Scotland* (1776).

## The House of Douglas
The name Douglas, derived from the Gaelic *dubh glas* meaning "dark water," represents a prominent Scottish noble family. The family's home in Lanarkshire, Douglasdale, remains in the possession of the earls of Home. The family emerged distinctly with William de Douglas (or Dufglas), whose name appears on charters between 1175 and 1213.

The family eventually split into two chief branches: the Black Douglas and the Red Douglas. These historic dignities, including the earldom of Angus and the marquessate of Douglas, are now merged into the Hamilton peerage. Other representatives of the name include the dukes of Buccleuch and Queensberry, the earls of Morton, the earls of Wemyss, and the earls of Home.

## Notable Figures of the Border History
The history of the Borders is marked by the exploits of various Douglas lords and their rivals:

*   **Sir William of Douglas ("le hardi"):** The first to formally assume the title of lord of Douglas, he joined Wallace's rising in 1297 and died as a prisoner in the Tower of London in 1298.
*   **Sir James of Douglas ("the Good" or "the Black Douglas"):** Educated in Paris, he joined Robert Bruce in 1306. He is noted for the "Douglas Larder" on March 19, 1307, and for capturing Roxburgh Castle in 1314 using a stratagem involving disguises as black oxen. He commanded the left wing at the battle of Bannockburn and later defeated an army at "The Chapter of Myton" in 1319.
*   **William Douglas, 1st Earl of Douglas (c. 1327–1384):** He spent his final years repelling and making border raids. In 1353, he killed his kinsman, the knight of Liddesdale, in Ettrick Forest.
*   **James, 2nd Earl of Douglas and Mar (c. 1358–1388):** He conducted a destructive counter-raid on Carlisle and fought in the battle of Otterburn in August 1388. Although the Scots were victorious and captured Hotspur Percy and his brother, the 2nd Earl fell during the fight. This struggle is celebrated in the ballads "The Battle of Otterburn" and "Chevy Chase."

## Middle Scots Poetry and Scholarship
The study of Scottish vernacular literature includes the works of Gavin Douglas. His collected works were issued in four volumes in 1874. Scholarship on Douglas has explored his indebtedness to Chaucer and the nature of his alleged Humanism.

Other significant contributions to the preservation of Scottish poetry include the work of George Bannatyne (1545–?1608). An Edinburgh merchant, Bannatyne spent time in his native Forfarshire during a plague in 1568 copying poems by Scots poets of the 15th and early 16th centuries. The resulting "Bannatyne Manuscript," consisting of eight hundred folio pages, is a primary repository for the texts of poets such as Lyndsay, Dunbar, Henryson, and Alexander Scott.
