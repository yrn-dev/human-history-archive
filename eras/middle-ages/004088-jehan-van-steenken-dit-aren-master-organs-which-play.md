# Jehan van Steenken, dit Aren

Jehan van Steenken, also known as *dit* Aren, was a Flemish organ-builder active during the middle of the 15th century. He is recognized in historical records as a "Master of organs which play of themselves" (in the original Flemish, *Meester van orgelen spelende bij hen selven*). 

## Professional Standing and Appointments
The professional status of Steenken is documented through letters patent preserved in the archives of Belgium. By virtue of these patents, he was appointed as a master of self-playing organs. In recognition of the services he rendered to the duke of Burgundy, Steenken was awarded an annual pension of fifty Rhenish florins. This financial grant was contingent upon a specific condition: Steenken was required to submit any other instruments he might create in the future to his liege, Philip the Good.

## The Self-Playing Organ
The instruments created by Steenken were not portable devices, such as the street organs found in England, but were instead more imposing instruments. Evidence of the scale and nature of these organs is found in documents detailing the payments made to "Maistre Jehan" for the transport of these organs from Bruges to Brussels.

The work of Steenken is linked to the broader origin of the barrel-organ, which is established as having emerged in the Netherlands as early as the middle of the 15th century. This period was characterized by a significant impulse toward the creation of automatic contrivances, fueled by the patronage and appreciation of the dukes of Burgundy. Other examples of Flemish genius from this era included clocks, carillons, and speaking animals.

## Later Developments in Barrel-Organs
While no contemporary illustrations of Steenken's specific work exist, later documentation provides insight into the evolution of the technology. In 1615, Solomon de Caus published detailed diagrams and descriptions of a large stationary barrel-organ operated by hydraulic power. De Caus, who cited Vitruvius and Hero as inspirations, titled his machine "Machine par laquelle l'on fera sonner un jeu d'orgues par le moyen de l'eau." 

This later instrument was constructed on a wall one foot thick. Its mechanism featured a drum pierced with holes at intersecting points, divided into bars and further subdivided into eight beats for quavers. The pins in this mechanism were movable, allowing a performer to rearrange them to change the tune once they grew tired of a particular piece.
