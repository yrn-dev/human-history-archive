# Constantinople

Constantinople served as the capital of the East Roman Empire and was a central point of contention and diplomacy for various European and Mediterranean powers, including the Normans, the Latins, and the Ottoman Turks.

## Norman Relations and Conflicts
The relationship between the Normans and Constantinople was marked by periods of intense hostility and strategic alliance. Bohemund I, the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, served under his father during a major attack on the East Roman Empire between 1080 and 1085. During this period, Bohemund commanded the Normans and penetrated as far as Larissa in Thessaly, though he was eventually repulsed by Alexius Comnenus. This early conflict influenced Bohemund's later role as a leader of the First Crusade.

When Bohemund arrived at Constantinople in April 1097 with a Norman army, he adopted a correct attitude toward Alexius and performed homage to the emperor. While Bohemund may have negotiated for a principality at Antioch, he received little encouragement from Alexius.

Other Norman leaders also interacted with the city. Roger, King of Sicily, is recorded as having invaded Greece and Africa. Later, Roger de Flor was recalled from Natolia to assist the emperor of Constantinople in a war following the death of Asan, king of Bulgaria. Roger de Flor succeeded in turning the tide of the conflict in favor of the emperor and established peace, though he was later murdered by order of Kyr Michael, the emperor's son.

## Dynastic Ties and Diplomacy
Constantinople maintained complex familial links with the Sicilian monarchy. The daughter of Roger the Great, King of Sicily, married Emperor Manuel of Constantinople; their son was Emperor Kirjalax. Later, King William of Sicily—son of Roger the Great—experienced a long period of great hostility with the emperor of Constantinople.

In later centuries, the city remained a hub for international diplomacy. For example, from May 1858 to August 1865, Sir Henry Bulwer served as the ambassador extraordinary to the Ottoman Porte at Constantinople, succeeding Viscount Stratford de Redcliffe.

## Latin Rule and the Ottoman Conquest
The city underwent a significant transition in 1204 following the Latin conquest of Constantinople. This initiated a period of Latin rule that lasted until 1458. During this era, the Greeks eventually recaptured the city from the Latins in 1261.

The final transition of power occurred in 1453, when the Ottoman Turks, led by Muhammad II, took Constantinople.

## Economic Conditions
Historical records indicate periods of severe economic distress in the city, specifically regarding the degradation of its currency. Under Michael Palæologus, poverty forced the striking of a new coin composed of fifteen parts copper alloy and nine carats of gold. While the standard briefly rose to ten carats after his death, it was later reduced to a moiety during times of public distress, which severely impacted commerce and credit.
