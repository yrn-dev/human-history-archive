# Albert, King of the Romans and the Bohemian Fief

## Declaration of the Vacant Fief
Albert, king of the Romans, declared that Bohemia was a vacant fief of the Empire. Following this declaration, he used intimidation as a primary means to induce the Bohemians to elect his son, Rudolph, as their sovereign. However, Rudolph's reign was brief, lasting only one year before his death.

## Succession and the Right of Election
Following the death of Rudolph, a conflict arose regarding the nature of the Bohemian throne. While the Habsburg princes of the period claimed a hereditary right to the throne, the Bohemians remained determined to maintain their right to elect their own sovereign. Consequently, they chose Henry, duke of Carinthia, who had married a daughter of King Wenceslas II.

Henry's popularity declined quickly due to accusations that he unduly favored German settlers within Bohemia. This led to his deposition. The Bohemians then elected John of Luxemburg, the son of Henry, king of the Romans.

## The Luxemburg Dynasty
The election of John of Luxemburg established a dynasty that ruled over Bohemia until its extinction upon the death of Sigismund in 1437. King John's connection to Bohemia was strengthened by his marriage to Princess Elizabeth, a daughter of Wenceslas II, though he showed little personal interest in the country. He spent most of his life in other lands, conducting campaigns that stretched from Lithuania in the north to Italy in the south.

John's political strategy was based on a close alliance with France, a country for which he felt the most sympathy. He died in 1346 while fighting as an ally of France at the battle of Crecy. During this era, the influence of the king of Bohemia became so significant that it was considered proverbial that nothing in the world could be accomplished without the help of God and the king of Bohemia.

## Later Habsburg and Elective Struggles
The question of whether the Bohemian crown was hereditary or elective remained unresolved for centuries, only being decided in 1627. This uncertainty was evident after the death of Sigismund in 1437. He was succeeded by his son-in-law, Albert of Austria, who was chosen as king by the estates. Albert's reign over Bohemia lasted less than two years.

After Albert's death, the nobility split into two parties: a national party led by George of Podebrad and a Roman or Austrian division led by Ulrich of Rosenberg. These parties eventually reached an agreement called the "Letter of Peace" (_list mirny_). Through this agreement, the national party abandoned the candidacy of Prince Casimir of Poland, which allowed for the eventual succession of Albert's heir.

On February 22, 1440, Queen Elizabeth gave birth to a son named Ladislas. The Bohemians formally acknowledged Ladislas as their king, though this occurred only after Albert, duke of Bavaria, had declined the crown. Because Ladislas was a minor, he remained in Austria under the guardianship of his uncle, Frederick, duke of Styria (later emperor Frederick III).
