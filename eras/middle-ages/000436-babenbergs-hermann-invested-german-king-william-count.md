# Babenbergs

The Babenbergs were a Franconian family that ruled the duchy of Austria prior to the rise of the House of Habsburg. The family's earliest known ancestor was Poppo, who served as a count in Grapfeld during the early 9th century.

## Early History and the Babenberg Feud
The family's early prominence included Poppo's sons: Henry, a margrave and duke in Franconia who died fighting Normans in 886, and Poppo, who was margrave in Thuringia from 880 until his deposition by the German king Arnulf in 892. While the family had previously been favored by emperor Charles the Fat, King Arnulf shifted imperial favor toward the rival Conradine family.

The leaders of the Babenbergs were the three sons of Duke Henry, who took their name from their castle, Babenberg, located on the upper Main. This led to a conflict known as the "Babenberg feud" during the reign of the German king Louis the Child, as both the Babenbergs and Conradines sought to extend their authority in the middle Main region. During this struggle, two of the Babenberg brothers were killed. The survivor, Adalbert, was summoned to the imperial court by Hatto I, the archbishop of Mainz and a Conradine partisan. Adalbert refused to appear and defended his castle at Theres against royal forces, but he surrendered in 906 and was beheaded despite a promise of safe-conduct. This event ended the family's influence in Franconia.

## Establishment of the Austrian Mark
The family returned to prominence in 976 when Leopold, a count in the Donnegau and member of the Babenberg family, was described as margrave of the East Mark. This district, located on the eastern frontier of Bavaria and measuring no more than 60 miles in breadth, eventually became the duchy of Austria. Leopold likely received this appointment as a reward for his fidelity to emperor Otto II during a Bavarian rising in 976.

Leopold expanded the mark's territory at the expense of the Hungarians. He was succeeded in 994 by his son Henry I, followed in 1018 by Henry's brother Adalbert, and in 1055 by nephew Ernest. Ernest received various tokens of favor for his marked loyalty to emperors Henry III and Henry IV.

## Expansion and Elevation to Duchy
The Babenbergs focused their policy on loyalty to the emperors—often cemented through marriages—and warfare against the Hungarians and other eastern neighbors. They pushed the eastern frontier down the Danube valley through colonization, moving their capital from Poechlarn to Melk, then Tulln, and finally to Vienna around 1140.

In 1156, King Frederick I raised Austria to the rank of a duchy to restore peace to Germany. He granted the duchy exceptional privileges: the investiture was bestowed upon Duke Henry and his second wife, Theodora; the duchy could descend to females if there were no male heirs; and a duke without children could nominate his own successor. The duke's imperial duties were limited to attending diets in Bavaria and providing a contingent for imperial army campaigns in bordering countries.

The Babenbergs continued to expand their holdings. In 1186, Duke Leopold I entered a treaty with Ottakar IV, duke of Styria, which brought upper Austria and Styria to the Babenbergs in 1192. In 1229, Duke Leopold II acquired lands from the bishop of Freising and took the title of lord of Carniola.

## Conflict and Extinction
Despite a tradition of loyalty, conflicts arose under margrave Leopold II and Duke Frederick II. During the investiture struggle, Leopold II deserted emperor Henry IV and was deprived of Austria in favor of Vratislav II, duke of the Bohemians, though Leopold regained possession in 1083.

Later, Duke Frederick II, known as "the Quarrelsome," clashed with emperor Frederick II. The emperor claimed certain rights in Austria and summoned the duke to Italian diets. When Frederick II refused to appear three times, the emperor placed him under the ban, declared the duchies of Austria and Styria vacant, and invaded in 1236 with the help of the duke of Bavaria and the king of Bohemia. The emperor declared the duchies immediately dependent on the Empire and made Vienna an imperial city. However, after the emperor departed, Duke Frederick II returned and regained his power by 1239.

The Babenberg line became extinct in June 1246, when Duke Frederick II was killed in battle by the Hungarians.

## Aftermath and Succession
Following the extinction of the Babenbergs, emperor Frederick II claimed Austria and Styria as vacant imperial fiefs and entrusted their government to Otto II, duke of Bavaria. However, Pope Innocent IV bestowed the duchies upon Hermann VI, margrave of Baden, whose wife, Gertrude, was a niece of the last Babenberg. Hermann was invested by the German king, William, count of Holland.

Hermann was unable to establish order, and following his death and the death of the emperor in 1250, the estates of Austria met at Truebensee in 1251. They chose Ottakar, son of Wenceslaus I, king of Bohemia, as their duke. Ottakar strengthened his claim by marrying Margaret, a sister of Duke Frederick II.
