# Realists

The term "Realist" has been applied across various disciplines, including philosophy, art, literature, and politics. Because the meaning of the term varies significantly depending on the context—ranging from medieval metaphysical theories to modern artistic movements—it is necessary to distinguish between these different applications.

## Medieval Philosophical Realism
In medieval intellectual history, a primary controversy existed between the "Realists" and the "Nominalists." In this context, the meaning of "Realist" is nearly opposite to how the term is used in modern criticism. While a modern realist focuses on materialist details, the medieval Realist was closer to what is now termed an Idealist, maintaining a profound contempt for incidental details.

Medieval Realists believed that common nouns—or names—possessed an essential reality. For instance, they argued that there existed an "ideal European" or a typical "European" who was more real than any specific individual. From this perspective, every individual European was viewed as a flawed specimen or a departure from this deeper, profound reality. This philosophy was essentially the philosophy of the Catholic Church.

The tendency of Realism in this philosophical sense was toward uncompromising attitudes, harsh judgments, dogma, and harsh divisions. This stood in contrast to Nominalism, which viewed the name "European" as merely a name applied to individual instances and tended toward skepticism, experiment, inquiry, and qualified statements.

## Realism in Art and Literature
In the arts, realism is often associated with the lifelike depiction of subjects, though the definition is contested. In painting, some mistakenly believe the aim of realism is the mere exact copying of nature. However, the work of Manet demonstrates that realism can involve a pronounced *parti pris*, where the artist uses elisions and assertions to select what is significant and beautiful rather than providing a complete natural image. This differs from the Impressionist, who constructs an image based on their own interest in the thing seen.

The influence of Spanish realists is evident in the work of the French painter Leon Joseph Florentin Bonnat, whose vivid portrait-painting is his most characteristic work. In literature, the term is used to describe works that provide a lifelike picture of their times, such as the *Memoirs* of Lieutenant-General Ludlow.

In the works of Castello Branco, realism appears in his novels of manners, where he is described as being partly realist and partly idealist in his perfection of the description of 19th-century Portuguese social and domestic life. Additionally, the Danish author Hermann Joachim Bang published critical essays on the realistic movement when he was twenty years old.

## Other Applications of the Term
The concept of realism is also used to describe the nature of historical recording and archaeological evidence. For example, the historian Froissart is noted for depicting the aspect of the country in Luxemburg realistically when describing the passage of French troops in 1388. In archaeology, the absence of "realistic carvings or engravings or paintings" in certain Neolithic lake dwellings suggests that those people may have used conventional ornaments, such as interlacing designs or spots, rather than realistic depictions.

In a political context, the term "Realists" appears as a designation for a small group within Czech political affiliations, consisting of two members.
