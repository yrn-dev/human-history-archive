# The Old-Slavonian in Comparative Linguistics

## Origins and Classification
The Slavonian dialect emerged from the spreading and breaking up of primitive Aryan speech as it moved toward the east. Aryan-speaking tribes increased and spread north of the Black Sea and the Carpathians, utilizing this distinctive dialect. From this source came various tongues, including Bulgarian, Polish, Serbian, and Russian. 

The Slavonian languages are considered an original branch of the Indo-European family (a term sometimes used for the entire family, while "Aryan" is sometimes restricted to Indo-Iranian speech). Chalcocondyles, a competent judge, affirmed the identity of the language used by the Bulgarians, Servians, Bosnians, Poles, and Bohemians.

## The Role of Old-Slavonian in Comparative Grammar
The study of Old-Slavonian gained a formal position in the field of comparative linguistics through the work of Bopp. Appointed to the chair of comparative grammar and Sanskrit at Berlin in 1821, Bopp developed a comprehensive *Comparative Grammar* (*Vergleichende Grammatik*), published in six parts between 1833 and 1852. 

This work compared several languages, including Sanskrit, Zend, Greek, Latin, Gothic, German, Lithuanian, and Old-Slavonian. While the work was preceded by monographs in the *Transactions of the Berlin Academy* from 1824 to 1831, Old-Slavonian specifically began to take its stand among the languages compared from the second part of the *Comparative Grammar* onwards. Bopp's objective in this research was threefold: to investigate the origin of grammatical forms, to trace phonetic laws, and to describe the original grammatical structure of the languages as deduced from their intercomparison.

## Archaic Slavonic and the Slavonic Apostles
The "Old Bulgarian," also referred to as archaic Slavonic, was a synthetic, inflexional language that contained few foreign elements in its vocabulary. It was written in the 9th century by the Slavonic apostles, St. Cyril (who died in 869) and St. Methodius (who died in 885). Because the original manuscripts were not preserved, scholars rely on copies from the 10th century. Although these copies show the influence of contemporary vernacular speech, the original language can be reconstructed with tolerable certainty because the alterations made by copyists were neither regular nor constant.

The vocabulary of archaic Slavonic was influenced by the missionary labors of the two saints in Pannonia and Moravia:
* **Greek:** Provided the majority of Christian terminology.
* **Latin and German:** Occasional words were derived from these languages.

## Evolution and Recensions
Over time, archaic Slavonic became a liturgical language across various Slavonic countries, undergoing structural and phonetic modifications. This resulted in different recensions, including Russian-Slavonic, Croatian-Slavonic, and Servian-Slavonic. 

Russian-Slavonic is currently the general liturgical language for Orthodox Slavs in the Balkan Peninsula. This is due to the introduction of a large number of ecclesiastical books from Russia during the 18th and 17th centuries. Until recently, this recension was believed to be the genuine language of the Slavonic apostles.

In Bulgaria, the spoken language of the 9th century changed significantly over the following three hundred years. While writers between the 12th and 15th centuries attempted to follow the archaic model, the vernacular diverged widely from the speech of St. Methodius and St. Cyril. The language of manuscripts from this specific period is known as "Middle Bulgarian," serving as a midpoint between modern speech and old ecclesiastical Slavonic.
