# Bohemia and Emperor Henry V

## Conflict with Poland and the German Wars
During the reign of Boleslaus III, king of Poland (1086–1139), the region was marked by instability caused by the intrigues of Boleslaus's natural half-brother, Zbigniew. These internal disputes involved Boleslaus in frequent contests with both Bohemia and the emperor Henry V. 

The first of the German wars began in 1109. During this conflict, Emperor Henry V invaded Silesia, receiving material assistance from the Bohemians. The campaign was characterized primarily by a series of sieges; Henry attempted to take the cities of Breslau, Glogau, and Lubusz, but he failed to capture any of them. The Polish forces avoided open-field encounters, instead employing successful harrying tactics against the Germans around Breslau. This resulted in a disastrous retreat for Henry V, leaving the plain covered with corpses. This location subsequently became known as "the field of dogs." The primary political outcome of this defeat was that Poland achieved complete independence for the following quarter of a century.

## Geography and Strategic Access
Bohemia is described as an irregular rhomb, forming a separate massif bordered by mountain ranges: the Riesengebirge (Giant Mountains) and other Sudetes to the northeast, the Erzgebirge (Ore Mountains) to the northwest, and the Bohmerwald (Bohemian Forest) to the southwest. The Bohmerwald features peaks such as Plockenstein (4513 ft.) and Osser (4053 ft.), though the highest peak in the range, the Arber (4872), is located in Bavaria.

The pass of Neumark has historically served as the principal approach to Bohemia from Germany. This pass, located above the town of Taus (also known as Domazlice or Tuhost), was the site of several significant battles. In the first half of the 7th century, Samo used the pass to repel Avars. Later, in August 1040, Wratislas II defeated the German emperor Henry III in a two-day battle at this location. The pass also saw victories for the Hussites in 1431 and for George of Podebrad against German armies.

## Territorial Shifts and Lusatia
The districts of Upper and Lower Lusatia have had fluctuating connections to Bohemia. Upper Lusatia, originally occupied by the Milzeni tribe and known as Baudissin (Bautzen), was connected to Bohemia, Poland, and Meissen at various times during the 11th and 12th centuries. In 1156, Emperor Frederick I. Barbarossa ceded Upper Lusatia to the Bohemian prince Vladislav II. Later, around 1160, Emperor Frederick I. granted the territory to King Ladislas of Bohemia, under whom it was largely colonized by German immigrants.

Upper Lusatia passed to the margrave of Brandenburg in 1253, but was restored to Bohemia in 1319 (Baudissin) and 1329 (Görlitz). Lower Lusatia was purchased by the margrave of Brandenburg in 1303 and eventually fell to the king of Bohemia and emperor Charles IV in 1368. Both districts later recognized the authority of Matthias Corvinus from 1469 to 1490 before passing to the German king Ferdinand I. In 1635, the treaty of Prague definitely transferred the two Lusatias from Bohemia to Saxony, though the emperor, as king of Bohemia, maintained supremacy to guard Roman Catholic rights and privileges.

## Bohemian Influence and Imperial Relations
Bohemia's relationship with the Empire evolved through various titles and alliances. In 1088, Vratislav obtained the title of king from Emperor Henry IV after assisting him in the contest about investitures. While this title was personal to Vratislav, later the German king Conrad III granted the Bohemian prince Sobeslav (1125–1140) the title of hereditary cupbearer of the Empire, providing Bohemia with influence over the election of emperors.

In the 13th century, Bohemian power expanded under Ottakar, son of Wenceslaus I. In 1251, the estates of Austria chose Ottakar as their duke. By 1253, he became king of Bohemia and eventually recognized as duke of Styria in 1261. After inheriting the duchy of Carinthia in 1269, Ottakar's increased power led him to aspire to the German throne.

## Later Conflicts and the Hussite Wars
In the 15th century, royal authority ceased in Bohemia when Sigismund was deposed at a meeting of the diet at Caslav on June 1, 1421. A provisional government of twenty men was established, and Sigismund Korybutovic, a Polish prince, was recognized as regent. Following his departure, civil war erupted between the Taborite party and the moderate Hussites (Utraquists or Calixtines).

The Bohemians faced several German invasions during this era. In 1426, they achieved a decisive victory over the Germans at Aussig. In 1427, a new crusade was launched by several German princes, which included 1000 English archers serving as the bodyguard to the papal legate, Henry Beaufort, bishop of Winchester. These crusaders suffered sudden panic at Tachau and Mies (Stribro), fleeing into Bavaria. Following these successes, the Bohemians launched their own invasions of the German states and Hungary.
