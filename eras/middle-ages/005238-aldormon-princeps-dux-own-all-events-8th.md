# Dux, Princeps, and Aldormon

The terms *dux*, *princeps*, and *aldormon* appear across various historical, administrative, and musical contexts within the archive, ranging from the governance of early English kingdoms and the Roman Empire to the republics of Italy and the terminology of musical composition.

## Administrative Roles in Early England
In the early kingdoms of England, administrative divisions were established for the purpose of collecting dues in services and payments. While smaller districts were managed by royal reeves (*cyninges gerefa*, *praefectus*, or *praepositus*) based in fortresses (*cyninges burg*) or royal villages (*cyninges tun*), larger divisions existed. These larger divisions, containing between 2,000 and 7,000 hides, included the shires of Wessex, such as Berkshire, Wiltshire, and Hampshire.

From the 8th century onwards, each of these shires had its own leader, referred to as an *aldormon*, *princeps*, or *dux*. These officials were frequently members of the royal family and are sometimes described as *subreguli*, suggesting they may have originally held a kingly title. The use of the title *dux* in an English context is further evidenced in a 1060 charter of Edward the Confessor, which includes the phrases "*Ego Haroldus dux*" and "*Ego Tostinus dux*."

## Roman and Continental Usage
In the Roman context, the term *princeps* was associated with the senate; the censor, according to the constitution of the free republic, could name the *Princeps Senatus*. Later, the term *dux* was used in military and regional capacities. For example, Theodosius Junior was referred to as *Dux Mæsiæ*.

In later European history, the titles *consul* and *senator* were sometimes used among Germans and French, though they often signified nothing more than a lord (*Signeur*) or count. In the 10th century, Greek emperors conferred the titles of *upatoV* or consuls upon the dukes of Venice, Amalphi, and Naples. By the years 952 and 956, individuals such as Georgius and Gratianus were styled as *consul et dux*. In 1015, Romanus, the brother of Gregory VIII, described himself as *consul et dux et omnium Romanorum senator*.

## Italian Republics and the British Peerage
The title of the chief magistrate in the now-extinct republics of Genoa and Venice was the Doge. This title is a modified form of the Italian *duca*, derived from the Latin *dux*, meaning a leader or duke.

In the British peerage, dukes hold a rank that follows the lord Chancellor, the two archbishops of York and Canterbury, and princes and princesses of the blood royal. While a herald may proclaim their full style as "most high, potent and noble prince," they do not possess special privileges beyond those shared by lower-ranking peers. They are not recognized as equals in blood to mediatized or crowned dukes of the continent. A non-royal duke in the British peerage is styled "the Most Noble" and addressed as "Your Grace." Typically, the eldest son of a British duke ranks as a marquess and takes his father's second title by courtesy, while other children use the titles "Lady" or "Lord" before their Christian names.

## Other Applications of the Terms
The term *dux* and *princeps* appear in non-administrative contexts:

*   **Music:** In the study of fugues (specifically *fuga ligata*), the *dux* refers to the leading part. A reproducing voice, known as the *comes*, follows a rule or *canon* to deduce its part from the *dux*.
*   **Bibliography:** The term *Editio princeps* is used to denote a first edition of a work, such as the Aldine edition of 1524.
*   **Geography:** Dux (Czech *Duchcov*) is a town in Austria, Bohemia, located northwest of Prague. Known for its lignite deposits, it manufactures earthenware, porcelain, and glass. The town contains a castle belonging to Count Waldstein, which houses a museum of arms and armour and a gallery featuring portraits of Wallenstein by Van Dyck. The town is also noted as the location where Casanova wrote his *Memoires*.
