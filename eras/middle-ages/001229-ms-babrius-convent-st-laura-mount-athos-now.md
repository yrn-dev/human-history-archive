# MS. of Babrius in the Convent of St Laura

The manuscript (MS.) of Babrius is a significant literary find discovered in the 19th century within the convent of St Laura on Mount Athos. It is currently housed in the British Museum.

## Discovery and Content
The manuscript was discovered in 1842 by M. Minas, a Greek who is also credited with the discovery of the *Philosophoumena* of Hippolytus. The MS. contains 123 fables, which is a portion of the 160 fables believed to have comprised the original collection. These fables are arranged in alphabetical order, although the manuscript breaks off at the letter O.

## Literary Style and Form
The fables in the manuscript are written in choliambic verse, also known as imperfect or limping iambic verse. This specific metre is characterized by a spondee as the last foot and was originally used for satire. The work is noted for its elegant and correct versification, terse and pointed expression, and a high quality of style and story construction.

## About the Author
Babrius was the author of this collection of Greek fables, though very little is known about his life. He is thought to have been a Roman living in the East, likely in Syria, where his fables appear to have first gained popularity. His gentile name may have been Valerius.

There has been significant scholarly speculation regarding the date of his work due to an address in the text to "a son of King Alexander." This has led to assigned dates ranging from the 3rd century B.C. to the 3rd century A.D. One theory suggests the "Alexander" mentioned is Alexander Severus (A.D. 222-235), who was known to keep various literary figures at his court. Another theory identifies the "son of Alexander" as a figure named Branchus mentioned in the fables, suggesting Babrius served as his tutor, though it is probable that Branchus is a fictitious name. Based on his language, style, and the fact that he is not mentioned by ancient writers until the beginning of the 3rd century A.D., it is believed he belonged to that period.

## Scholarly Reconstruction
Before the discovery of the Mount Athos manuscript, Babrius was largely a name rather than a known author. Richard Bentley, in his *Dissertation on the Fables of Aesop*, began the process of recovering the work. Bentley examined prose Aesopian fables that had been passed down in collections since the time of Maximus Planudes and discovered traces of versification. He was able to extract several verses which he attributed to Babrius. These researches were later followed up by Tyrwhitt in *De Babrio* (1776), leading scholars to attempt to reconstruct the original metrical versions of the prose fables until the 1842 discovery of the MS. in the convent of St Laura.
