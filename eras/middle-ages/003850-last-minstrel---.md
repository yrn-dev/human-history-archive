# Last Minstrel

The term "minstrel" refers to a broad tradition of professional reciters, singers, and bards who preserved history, legend, and poetry through oral tradition across various cultures and eras. These figures served as the primary vehicles for storytelling before the widespread adoption of writing, and they continued to flourish long after writing was introduced to Europe.

## Ancient and Oriental Traditions
In ancient Greece, wandering minstrels known as *rhapsodes* (derived from the Greek *rhabdos* for staff, or *rhaptein* meaning to piece together) were responsible for spreading the epic cycles and the poems of Homer. These recitations, which could be performed with or without musical accompaniment, included epic, didactic, and gnomic verse. The rhapsodes were frequent guests at popular festivals, where their performances—particularly the use of iambics and the alternation of poems—introduced the germs of theatrical dialogue and contributed to the transformation of lyrical tragedy into tragic drama.

In Persia, the tradition of the rhapsode continued in various forms. The *Kurroglou-Khans* (from *khaunden*, to sing) were tasked with memorizing and narrating the *mejjlisses* (meetings) of Kurroglou, accompanied by a three-stringed guitar called a sitar or chungur. Similarly, there were *Shah-nama-Khans* for Ferdausi and *Koran Khans* for the prophet Mohammed. These singers were noted for their astonishing memories, capable of reciting for several hours without stammering. In other regions, such as among the Calmuck national bards, a single song could last an entire day.

## Medieval European Minstrelsy
In the Middle Ages, minstrels and *jongleurs* acted as cultural conduits, transporting legends across borders. In the Low Countries, wandering minstrels introduced the sagas of Arthur and Charlemagne into Middle Dutch forms to satisfy the curiosity of noble women. Notable figures included Diederic van Assenede, who completed a version of *Floris et Blanchefleur* around 1250, and Willem the Minstrel, who produced an important Dutch version of *Reynard the Fox* around 1250 and translated a now-lost romance titled *Madoc*.

In France and Brittany, Breton minstrels performed *lais*. Marie de France later gave these stories a new literary form in Norman octosyllabic verse. While Marie's works were destined to be read, Joseph Bédier suggests the original Breton minstrel lays were prose recitals interspersed with short lyrics, similar to the cante-fable of *Aucassin et Nicolette*. These lays contained traditions from Oriental and Scandinavian sources, as well as Celtic legends.

## Germanic and English Traditions
The conversion to Christianity led to a decline in the minstrelsy that celebrated heathen glories, yet interest in Germanic heroes persisted. As late as the 14th century, the "song of Wade" was known to Chaucer. Old English heroic poetry, such as *Beowulf*, *Finnesburh*, and *Waldere*, reflects these traditions. The poem *Widsith* is particularly significant as it is presented from the perspective of a "far-travelled" minstrel who recounts the names of famous kings from German tradition, such as Alboin and Ermanaric.

Professional reciters of this era were often jealous of their traditions and reluctant to commit their compositions to writing. Consequently, much of this secular poetry survived only because it was occasionally preserved by the clergy in manuscripts.

## Scottish Bards and the Transition to Literature
In Scotland, the tradition involved both court-bards kept by kings and strolling *jongleurs* who sang in towns and at farm-houses. While some theories suggest courtly minstrels authored ballads that were later corrupted by wandering crowders, evidence suggests that many romantic ballads share a universal tone and primitive formulae common to Greece, France, Italy, Denmark, and Portugal, indicating an immemorial European inheritance rather than a single author.

The transition from the oral tradition of the minstrel to the written word is exemplified by the figure of Robert Burns. He is described as a link between two eras—the last of the old and the first of the new. While the "minstrel's lay" acted as a requiem for a distinct Scottish nationality (which "went out with two bards," Robert Burns and Walter Scott), Burns acted as both the inheritor of past music and traditions and a herald of the future.

## Notable Accounts of Minstrel Performance
Historical accounts highlight the prestige and specific attire associated with the craft. The Greek account of Arion describes him as the "best of all minstrels upon earth." During a plot by his crew to seize his wealth, Arion requested to stand on the deck in "full minstrel's garb" to perform the Orthian measure on his lyre before leaping into the sea. He was subsequently rescued by a dolphin and returned to Corinth, where he proved his survival by appearing before his betrayers in the same minstrel's guise.
