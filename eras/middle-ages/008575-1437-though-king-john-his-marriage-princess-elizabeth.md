# The Year 1437 and Related Historical Figures

## The Bohemian Throne and the Luxemburg Dynasty
The year 1437 marked a significant transition in the governance of Bohemia. The Luxemburg dynasty, which had ruled the region, came to an end with the death of Sigismund in 1437. Following his death, the estates of Bohemia chose his son-in-law, Albert of Austria, to succeed him as king. Albert's reign over Bohemia was brief, lasting less than two years.

The succession of the Bohemian throne had historically been a point of contention. Earlier, Albert, king of the Romans, had declared Bohemia a vacant fief of the Empire and induced the Bohemians to elect his son Rudolph, though Rudolph's reign lasted only one year. Subsequently, the Bohemians, determined to maintain their right to elect their sovereign, chose Henry, duke of Carinthia, who had married a daughter of King Wenceslas II. However, Henry became unpopular due to accusations that he unduly favored German settlers and was eventually deposed.

The Bohemians then elected John of Luxemburg, the son of Henry, king of the Romans. While John became more closely connected to Bohemia through his marriage to Princess Elizabeth, a daughter of Wenceslas II, he showed little interest in the country. He spent most of his life in other lands, conducting campaigns from Lithuania in the north to Italy in the south. John's political strategy was based on a close alliance with France, the country for which he felt the most sympathy; he died fighting as a French ally at the battle of Crecy in 1346. During his era, it became proverbial that nothing in the world could be accomplished without the help of God and the king of Bohemia.

## The County and Duchy of Luxemburg
Parallel to the royal line in Bohemia, the district of Luxemburg had its own administrative history. Originally included in the Roman province of *Belgica prima*, it later formed part of the empire of Charlemagne and the Frankish kingdom of Austrasia. Around 1060, it came under the rule of Conrad, who took the title of count of Luxemburg. His descendants continued to rule the county—first through the male line and later the female line—until the death of Emperor Sigismund in 1437.

Following Sigismund's death, Luxemburg passed to the house of Habsburg through the marriage of Sigismund's daughter, Elizabeth, to the German king Albert II. Luxemburg had been elevated to a duchy in 1354. The territory later saw various shifts in power: it was seized in 1443 by Philip III the Good, duke of Burgundy, and regained by the Habsburgs in 1477 via the marriage of Maximilian I to Mary, daughter of Charles the Bold. By 1555, it passed to Philip II of Spain. After a portion was ceded to France in 1659, the remainder was given to Emperor Charles VI in 1713. It was conquered by France in 1795 and later became a grand-duchy via the Congress of Vienna (1814-1815), eventually being partitioned in 1831 and 1838.

## Scottish Nobility and the House of Douglas
In Scotland, the period surrounding the early 15th century saw significant shifts in the power of the Douglas family. James Douglas, known as the "Gross" (1371-1444), was the younger son of the 3rd earl and was created earl of Avondale in 1437. He eventually inherited the Douglas lands following the deaths of his relatives.

The power of the Douglases was severely diminished in 1440. Archibald, 5th Earl of Douglas (c. 1391-1439), had been the count of Longueville in Normandy and fought at Bauge in 1421. After his death in 1439, his sons, William (6th Earl) and David, were summoned to court on November 24, 1440, by Sir William Crichton, the lord chancellor of Scotland. Following a mock trial in the presence of the young king, both sons were beheaded in the courtyard of Edinburgh Castle. This event broke the dangerous power of the Douglases, resulting in the crown acquiring the lordships of Bothwell and Annandale, while Galloway went to the earl's sister, Margaret.
