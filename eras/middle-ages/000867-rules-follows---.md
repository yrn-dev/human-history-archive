## Maritime Law and General Average
The fundamental principle of "general average" dictates that any loss incurred for the benefit of all coadventurers must be made good by all parties in equitable proportion to their stakes in the adventure. Due to the complexity of maritime commerce, this principle required technical rules to be applied consistently.

Efforts to unify these rules began in May 1860 at a congress in Glasgow, presided over by Lord Brougham and assisted by Lord Neaves. Subsequent congresses took place in London (1862) and York (1864), resulting in the "York Rules." These were later refined by the "Association for the Reform and Codification of the Law of Nations" through conferences at the Hague (1875), Bremen (1876), and Antwerp (1877). The resulting "York and Antwerp (or York-Antwerp) Rules" were widely adopted, though a further conference was held in Liverpool in the autumn of 1890 to address remaining insufficiencies.

Specific rules within this framework include:
*   **Deck Cargo:** No jettison of deck cargo is made good as general average. Any structure not built into the vessel's frame is considered part of the deck.
*   **Common Safety:** Damage to a ship or cargo resulting from a sacrifice made for common safety—including water entering through hatches or openings made for jettison—is made good as general average.
*   **Fire Extinguishment:** Damage caused by water, beaching, or scuttling a burning ship to extinguish a fire is made good as general average, except for the specific portions of the ship, bulk cargo, or separate packages that were actually on fire.
*   **Wreck Removal:** Loss or damage caused by cutting away a wreck or remains of spars previously carried away by sea-peril is not made good as general average.

## Judicial and Legal Rules
In the Supreme Court of Judicature, the general rule regarding costs is that they are at the discretion of the court or judge, subject to the Judicature Acts and court rules. The judge has full power to determine who pays these costs and to what extent. However, two provisions apply:
1.  Executors, administrators, trustees, or mortgagees who have not unreasonably resisted proceedings retain the right to costs out of a particular estate or fund, as per rules in the chancery division prior to 1883.
2.  In cases tried with a jury, costs typically follow the event unless the judge or court orders otherwise for good cause.

Additionally, while many enactments were superseded by the Judicature Acts, the Slander of Women Act 1891 re-enacted provisions from an act of 1623 regarding slander against women. In other legal contexts, such as federal interests in specific cases, "Rule 11(c)" refers to a type of plea. In one instance, internal communications between Menchel, Villafana, and Acosta indicate that a Rule 11(c) plea was discussed but ultimately "nixed" or omitted from a final letter, possibly due to the belief that judges were generally averse to them.

## Governance and Legislative Codes
In the history of the Japanese people, the Yedo Bakufu developed legislative works to govern conduct. In 1767, under the shogun Ieharu, laws promulgated since the days of Ieyasu were collated into a prefatory volume for the "Rules for Judicial Procedure," together forming the *Kajo-ruiten*. In 1790, Matsudaira Sadanobu revised this code, reducing it to one hundred articles and renaming it the *Tokugawa Hyakkajd* (One Hundred Laws and Regulations of the Tokugawa). These laws were not disclosed to the general public, as the Tokugawa followed the Confucian maxim: "Make the people obey but do not make them know."

## Religious Rules and Orders
The Dominican order is divided into different orders with specific rules. The "Second Order" consists of nuns (Dominicanesses), with the first nunnery established by St Dominic in 1206 at Prouille. Their rule mirrored that of the friars (the "First Order"), but required the nuns to be purely contemplative and strictly enclosed, although they later engaged in educational work. By 1909, there were nearly 100 such nunneries and 1,500 nuns, including operations in the Transvaal of South Africa.

The "Third Order" follows a rule not written until the 15th century. It is debated whether this order originates with St Dominic or was an imitation of the Franciscan Tertiaries. This rule is followed by both conventual Tertiaries and lay men and women, such as St Catharine of Siena, who lived their family lives in the world.
