# The Picts

The Picts were one of the two great tribes of the northern region of Caledonia, alongside the Scots. While the Scots were characterized as men of the hills, the Picts inhabited the plains. The eastern coast of Caledonia, where the Picts resided, was a level and fertile country capable of producing significant quantities of corn. This agricultural capacity led to the use of the epithet *cruitnich*, meaning "wheat-eaters," a term used by the carnivorous highlanders to express envy or contempt. Despite their sedentary habits and tillage, the Picts were driven by a passion for rapine and arms; Roman observers noted that their warriors would strip naked for battle and paint their bodies with gaudy colors.

## Early History and Roman Contact
The Picts, along with the Scots, were known for destructive inroads and devastating raids against the provinces of Britain. These incursions were so severe that, according to Gildas, the Britons called in the Saxons for protection. During the reign of Constantine, the hostile tribes of the North suspended their internal feuds to oppose Roman power, with the Picts, Scots, and Saxons spreading their fury from the shores of Kent to the wall of Antoninus.

Roman advancement into Pictish lands was largely obstructed by mountains in the south, though a suspected Roman camp remains in Glen Barry. In the district of Pictavia, the Romans briefly overran the land; tradition places the camp of Raedykes in the parish of Fetteresso as the location where the Caledonians, led by Galgacus, stayed before their battle with Agricola.

## English Hegemony and Independence
During the era of Oswic, English influence became predominant in northern Britain. Following the death of the Pictish king Talorgan, Oswic appears to have gained sovereignty over a significant portion of the Pictish nation. Early in the reign of Ecgfrith, the Picts attempted a revolt, but it was unsuccessful. However, the independence of the Picts, Scots, and some Britons was recovered following the disastrous overthrow of Ecgfrith in 685.

Later, in 756, Eadberht enlarged the Northumbrian dominions and, acting in conjunction with Oengus mac Fergus, king of the Picts, reduced the Britons to submission.

## The Rise of Kenneth mac Alpin
A major revolution occurred among the Picts around 844. The throne was acquired by Kenneth mac Alpin, a prince of Scottish family and descendant of Conall Gabhrain of the old Dalriadic Scottish kingdom. Kenneth's claim to the Pictish throne was likely through female descent, and he found primary support in Fife. 

Kenneth's father, Alpin (also called king of the Scots), had been slain by the Picts in 832 or 834 while attempting to assert his own claim to the throne. In the seventh year of his reign (839 or 841), Kenneth took advantage of a Danish invasion of the Pictish kingdom to attack the remaining Picts, whom he finally subdued between 844 and 846. Kenneth is often described as the first king of Scotland (kingdom of Scone). Under his rule, he invaded "Saxonia" six times, burning Melrose and Dunbar.

## Legacy and Archaeological Remains
Over time, the power and memory of the Picts were extinguished by their rivals, the Scots. Today, few remains of the northern Picts exist. These consist primarily of cairns found in districts such as Ballindalloch, Boharm, Glen Livet, and Rothiemay, as well as the continued presence of the word "cairn" in many place names. Other antiquities include standing stones, tumuli, stone circles, and a structure in the parish of Dunnottar identified as a "Picts' kiln."
