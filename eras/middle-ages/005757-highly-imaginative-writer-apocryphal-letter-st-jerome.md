# The Apocryphal Letter of St Jerome to Dardanus

The apocryphal letter of St Jerome to Dardanus is a text characterized by the highly imaginative nature of its writer. It is primarily known for its descriptions of musical instruments, which have served as the basis for various artistic interpretations in medieval manuscripts.

## The Bumbulum
One of the most notable elements described in the letter is a fabulous musical instrument known as the bumbulum (also referred to as the bombulum or bunibulum). This instrument is known from the description in the letter and subsequent illustrations in illuminated manuscripts dating from the 9th to the 11th century. These include the *Psalter of Angers* (9th century), the *Psalter of Emmeran* (9th century), the *Boulogne Psalter* (A.D. 1000), and the Cotton MS. *Tiberius C. VI.* (11th century).

The physical form of the bumbulum varies by source:
* **Cotton MS. Interpretation:** The instrument is depicted as an angular frame with a rectangular metal plate suspended by a chain. This plate features twelve bent arms arranged in two rows of three on each side. These arms end in small plates or rectangular bells. It is believed the frame was meant to be shaken, similar to a sistrum, to make the bells jangle.
* **Virdung's Interpretation:** Sebastian Virdung provided illustrations of the instruments mentioned by Jerome. In his version, which he calls the *Fistula Hieronimi*, the metal arms are replaced by twelve bent pipes with a conical bore.

## Symbolic Meaning
Following the text of the apocryphal letter, Virdung explains the symbolic significance of the bumbulum's components. The stand, which resembles a draughtsman's square, represents the Holy Cross. The rectangular object hanging from the stand signifies Christ on the Cross, and the twelve pipes represent the twelve apostles.

Despite these detailed descriptions and fanciful pictures, there is no evidence that such an instrument actually existed during the middle ages; it is known only through the description in the letter and the resulting illustrations.

## Etymology and the Buccina
The term *bombulum* is likely derived from the same root as the *bombaulios* mentioned by Aristophanes in *Acharnians*. This Greek term was a comic compound combining *bombos* and *aulos*, referring to a bag-pipe and playing on *bombulios*, an insect that buzzes or hums.

The imaginative writer of the letter to Dardanus also discusses the buccina, specifically its use among Semitic races. The author states: "Bucca vocatur tuba apud Hebreos: deinde per diminutionem buccina dicitur."
