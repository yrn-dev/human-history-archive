# Legal Bonds and the Concept of Knowledge

## The English Law Bond
In English law, a bond is defined as an obligation created by deed. Its primary purpose is to ensure that the obligor—the individual providing the bond—either performs a specific act, refrains from a particular act, or pays a designated sum of money. To secure this, the obligor binds themselves to the obligee through a penalty. 

A bond typically includes a condition known as a "defeasance," which serves to undo or defeat the bond if the obligor fulfills the requirement. For example, in a common money bond, the obligor may bind themselves to the obligee for a specific sum (such as L[2000]). The defeasance condition stipulates that if the obligor (or their heirs, executors, or administrators) pays a smaller sum (usually half the penalty, such as L[1000]) plus interest at a specified annual rate by a certain date, the bond becomes void. If these conditions are not met, the bond remains in full force.

## Social and Political Applications of Recognition
The act of knowing or being known has historically carried significant social and political weight. In certain contexts, "nomenclators" were employed—often slaves—whose specific business was to know the names of every man. They accompanied candidates during canvassing to inform the candidate of the names of people they were about to address. This created a pretense of familiarity, satisfying a general human desire to be known. For individuals who felt they possessed no real merit or felt insignificant, this attention from those of higher rank was particularly gratifying, as it provided a false appearance of respect that others might be deceived by.

In the early foundation of Rome, Romulus established social distinctions through naming and organization. He divided able-bodied males into legions of three thousand infantry and three hundred cavalry. He further organized the people into the "Populus," selecting one hundred of the noblest to form a council called the Senate (an assembly of old men). These members were called Patricians. Some sources suggest this name derived from being fathers of legitimate offspring or the ability to account for their own fathers. Others suggest it came from *Patrocinium* (patronage). Romulus intended for the powerful "patrons" (defenders) to show kindness to the "clients" (dependants), fostering a mutual feeling where patrons acted as friends and legal advocates for their clients.

## Knowledge in Sacred Texts
The Qur'an describes the origins of human knowledge through the story of Adam. When God announced to the angels His intention to place a *khalif* on earth, the angels questioned this, citing the potential for mischief and the shedding of blood, while noting their own celebration of God's holiness and praise. God responded, "Surely I know what you do not know."

God then taught Adam the names of all things and presented them to the angels, challenging them to provide the names if they were truthful. The angels acknowledged their limitation, stating they had no knowledge except that which God had taught them, and recognizing God as the Knowing and the Wise. God then commanded Adam to tell the angels the names. Following this, Allah stated that He knows the secrets of heaven and earth, as well as everything revealed or concealed.

## Intellectual and Military Pursuit of Knowledge
Throughout history, individuals have sought specialized knowledge to achieve their goals. A navigator writing to Ferdinand and Isabella described a lifelong devotion to the sea and a desire to know the "secrets of Nature." This pursuit involved studying history, chronicles, philosophy, and other arts, as well as acquiring knowledge of Astronomy, Geometry, Arithmetic, and seamanship. This individual maintained relations with men of learning across various backgrounds, including Latins, Greeks, Moors, and others of many sects.

In the Grecian world, Dionysius of Syracuse is noted as a man of literary ability who composed tragedies and odes. Despite being of low birth and occupation—serving as a scribe or secretary—he distinguished himself through military talents, including quickness of discernment, bravery, and force of will.

## Divine Presence and Human Address
In the account of the temple built by Solomon, the presence of God is described as a thick, temperate cloud that darkened the temple to the point that priests could not discern one another. Solomon addressed God, acknowledging that God possesses an eternal house created from His own works—the heaven, air, earth, and sea—and is not contained within their limits. Solomon stated that the temple was built so that prayers could be sent up and the people could believe God is present and not remote. Solomon emphasized that God does not leave the care of all men and is especially present to those who address Him by day or night.
