# Slavonic Migrations and Settlements in the Balkans

## Patterns of Immigration
The ethnographic character of the Balkan Peninsula was profoundly altered by a series of Slavonic immigrations that began in the 3rd century A.D. and continued at intervals over the following four centuries. While the initial crossing of the Danube occurred at the start of the 3rd century, the most significant waves of immigration took place during the 6th and 7th centuries. These migrations resulted in permanent settlements across the region, as evidenced by the persistence of Slavonic place-names throughout the peninsula.

The Slavs are described as a primitive agricultural and pastoral people. In their early history, they lacked central organization or national leaders, operating instead through democratic local institutions centered on the *pleme*, or tribe.

## Territorial Expansion and Displacement
The Slavonic expansion overran much of the peninsula, displacing or absorbing existing populations. The native Thraco-Illyrian race inhabiting the area between the Danube and the Aegean was largely absorbed or expelled. In Macedonia, the Slavs drove the latinized population into highland districts such as Olympus, Agrapha, and Pindus, while the Greeks were pushed toward the islands, the shores of the Aegean, and fortified towns. Because the Slavs were often unsuccessful in attacking these fortified centers, the towns remained hubs of Hellenism.

In the 6th century, the Slavs penetrated as far as the Morea, where a Slavonic dialect continued to be spoken until the middle of the 15th century. In the 7th century, Serbo-Croats invaded north-western regions, including Bosnia, Herzegovina, Montenegro, northern Albania, Servia, and Croatia. In these areas, they appropriated old Roman colonies on the Adriatic coast and either assimilated or expelled the Illyrian population. In Albania, however, the aboriginal Illyrian element maintained its ancient language in the mountains and eventually forced the immigrant race back.

## Regional Diversification and Assimilation
The Slavonic populations developed distinct characteristics based on their geography and the peoples they encountered:

*   **Western Peninsula:** Occupied by Slovene and Serb tribes. In Illyria, between 600 and 650, the region was split between Croats in the west and Serbs in the east. This division led to different cultural trajectories, with Croats falling under the influence of Roman Catholicism and Italy, and Serbs under the influence of the Greek Church and Byzantium.
*   **Central and Eastern Peninsula:** In central Macedonia, the population presents a peculiar type due to the admixture of Albanian, Hellenic, Turkish, and Vlach blood. The bulk of this population is considered Bulgarian.
*   **Dalmatia:** The "Morlachs" (or Mavrovlachi) represent a group of Dalmatian Slavs who absorbed Latinized Illyrians and Albanians. Originally referring to the remnants of Latinized inhabitants who were nomadic shepherds, the name eventually became identified with the Slavs who adopted their language and manners.

## Political Consolidation and Foreign Influence
While the Slavs were initially scattered, they were eventually welded into a powerful state by a foreign race: the Bulgarians (described as an Ugro-Finnish or Turanian horde). Crossing the Danube at the end of the 7th century, the Bulgarians established a despotic political organization and subjected the Slavonic inhabitants of Thrace and Moesia. However, because the Bulgarians were less numerous than the subjected race, they were eventually absorbed by the Slavs.

Under Tsar Krum (802–815), the Bulgarians invaded central Macedonia and the districts of Adrianople. Later, under Simeon (893–927), the empire extended from the Black Sea to the Adriatic, with the capital fixed at Preslav.

## Language and Culture
The "Old Bulgarian" or archaic Slavonic was an inflexional language of the synthetic type with few foreign elements, though it adopted Greek terminology for Christian concepts. In the 9th century, St. Cyril (who died in 869) and St. Methodius (who died in 885) wrote as Slavonic apostles. Over time, this language evolved into various recensions, including Russian-Slavonic, Croatian-Slavonic, and Servian-Slavonic. By the 12th to 15th centuries, a "Middle Bulgarian" emerged, standing between the archaic ecclesiastical model and modern speech.

Culturally, the Slavs were influenced by Roman civilization. Their early religion was a form of paganism similar to that of the early Greeks and Germans, characterized by gods who acted like "school prefects" of more powerful beings. A steady process of conversion to Christianity occurred throughout the 7th and 8th centuries. Notably, the Slavs who migrated into the Peloponnese and central Greece from the 6th century underwent a process of Hellenizing, a rare instance of complete assimilation by the Greeks.
