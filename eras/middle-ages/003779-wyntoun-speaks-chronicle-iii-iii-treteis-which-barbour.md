# John Barbour and His Works

John Barbour was a writer active around 1375, associated with the early development of the Scottish dialect of Middle English. His most recognized contribution to literature is *The Brus*, a narrative poem consisting of twenty books and over 13,500 four-accent lines written in couplets.

## The Brus
*The Brus* serves a purpose that is both patriotic and historical. The poem begins with a description of Scotland following the death of Alexander III in 1286 and concludes in 1332 with the burial of the Bruce's heart and the death of Douglas. The central event of the narrative is the battle of Bannockburn.

In the poem, the king is depicted as a chivalric hero, a type common to contemporary romance. Barbour presents freedom as a "noble thing" that should be won at any cost, and while the opponents of this freedom are portrayed in dark colors, the work avoids provincial complacency. One of the most frequently quoted sections of the poem begins with the phrase, "A! fredome is a noble thing."

While *The Brus* is considered historically trustworthy when compared to contemporary verse-chronicles—and specifically more so than the *Wallace* of the following century—it contains several factual errors, most notably the confusion of three different Bruces into the single person of the hero.

The authorship of *The Brus* is not doubted, though some have argued that the existing text is an edited copy. This potential editor may have been John Ramsay, a scribe from Perth who wrote the two extant texts preserved in the library of St John's College, Cambridge, and the Advocates' library, Edinburgh. Wyntoun incorporated extensive portions of *The Brus* into his own *Chronicle*. The first printed edition was published by Charteris in Edinburgh in 1571, followed by Hart's edition in 1616.

## The Troy Fragments and the "Treteis"
In *Chronicle* III. iii., Wyntoun mentions a "Treteis" created by Barbour, which served as a genealogy of "Brutus lynagis." Other parts of Wyntoun's poem refer to a work called "Stewartis Oryginale." This "Brut" is currently unknown. 

Some scholars, beginning with Henry Bradshaw, the librarian of Cambridge University, suggested that this reference pointed to a Troy-book based on Guido da Colonna's *Historia Destructionis Troiae*. Two fragments of such a work exist in texts of Lydgate's *Troy-book* (found in MS. Douce 148 in the Bodleian library, Oxford, and MS. Camb. Univ. Lib. Kk. v. 30). However, the current critical consensus opposes this ascription. Critics argue that the scale of these fragments—totaling 3,713 lines (595 + 3118) and focusing entirely on Trojan matters—would be an "undue allowance" for a Scottish genealogy.

## Other Attributed Works
Several other works have been attributed to Barbour, though these are subject to academic debate:

*   **Legends of the Saints:** C. Horstmann upholds Barbour's authorship of the *Legends*, while W. M. Metcalfe disputes it. If Barbour wrote the *Legends*, it is inferred he also wrote a Gospel story regarding the birth of Jesus Christ and the genealogy of the Virgin Mary, as mentioned in the prologue of the *Legends*.
*   **Buik of Alexander:** This work is a translation of the *Roman d'Alexandre* and associated pieces, including the *Voeux du Paon*, known from a unique edition printed circa 1580 by Alexander Arbuthnot in Edinburgh. Attempts to name Barbour as the author rely on parallel passages between *The Brus* and *Alexander*, an argument critics claim is an exaggerated inference that ignores the common vocabulary and epithets used by all writers of that period.
