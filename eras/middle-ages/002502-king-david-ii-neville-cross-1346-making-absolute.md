# David II and the Battle of Neville's Cross

## The Invasion of 1346
In 1346, King David II of Scotland led an invading force into England. During this campaign, the Scottish forces ravaged the town of Corbridge, a small market town in Northumberland located on the north bank of the river Tyne. The Scottish army eventually encamped at Beaurepaire (also known as Bearpark), a site that housed the county residence of the priors of Durham, which had been damaged by previous invaders.

## The Battle of Neville's Cross
The military campaign culminated in the battle of Neville's Cross, fought in 1346. The engagement resulted in a defeat for the invading Scots. The English forces were led by Henry Percy and Lord Neville. Following the battle, several Scots were taken prisoner, including the knight of Liddesdale.

## Aftermath and Captivity
The knight of Liddesdale, who had previously served as the warden of the western marches during the minority of David II and had played a heroic role in removing the English from southern Scotland, remained a prisoner after the battle. He was eventually released only after becoming a liegeman of Edward III in exchange for the castle of the Hermitage and the lands of Liddesdale.

## Ransom and Political Treaties
Following the conflict, a ransom was required for the release of David II. William Douglas, the 1st Earl of Douglas, served as one of the securities for the payment of this royal ransom. However, some of the moneys raised for this purpose were misappropriated by the king, leading Douglas to enter a short period of rebellion in 1363.

By 1364, William Douglas joined David II in pursuing a treaty with England. The proposed terms of this treaty sought to guarantee the independence of Scotland and provide for the restoration of the Douglas family's English estates. A significant clause of the treaty aimed to deprive Robert the Steward of the succession by installing an English prince on the Scottish throne.
