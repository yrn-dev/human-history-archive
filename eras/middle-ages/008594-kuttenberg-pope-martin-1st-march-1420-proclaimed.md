# Kuttenberg and the Hussite Crusades

## Overview of Kuttenberg
Kuttenberg (also known as Kutna Hora) is a town in Bohemia that originated from silver mines dating back to the first part of the 13th century. The city experienced rapid development and became the second most important city in Bohemia after Prague. Due to its significance, it served as a favorite residence for several Bohemian kings. On January 18, 1410, Wenceslaus IV signed the decree of Kuttenberg in the city, which granted the Bohemian nation three votes in the elections to the faculty of Prague University, compared to one vote for the three other "nations."

## Religious Conflict and Atrocities
During the outbreak of the Hussite troubles in the early 14th century, Kuttenberg was characterized by a fierce mining population that was primarily German and fanatically Catholic. This stood in contrast to Prague, which was Czech and utraquist. In the autumn of 1410, the town became the site of severe atrocities. As reprisals for Hussite outrages in Prague, the miners of Kuttenberg captured Hussites and executed them by beheading, burning, or throwing them alive into disused mine shafts. It is reported that 1,600 people perished during these events, including the clergy and magistrates of the town of Kaurim.

## The Crusade of 1420
Following the death of the king, rioting broke out in Prague and other Bohemian cities, leading to the expulsion of many Germans who adhered to the Church of Rome. Sigismund, claiming the Bohemian crown as the successor to his brother, arrived at Kuttenberg early in the following year. On March 1, 1420, Pope Martin V proclaimed a crusade against Bohemia. This bull was issued for the destruction of Hussites, Wycliffites, and all other heretics in Bohemia.

The invitation attracted crusaders from across Europe, including unemployed soldiers of fortune. Sigismund used Kuttenberg as the base for his attack on the Taborites. By June 30, Sigismund led a large army of Bohemians and men from various other countries to occupy the castle of Prague. The crusaders viewed the city as a heretical community because the inhabitants accepted other evangelical truths and used the sacred chalice.

## Military Outcomes and the Role of Zizka
The attempt to conquer Prague failed. The Taborites, led by Zizka, repulsed a crusader attack on the Vitkov (now Zizkov) hill, forcing Sigismund to abandon the siege. Sigismund suffered another defeat on November 1, 1420, at the battle of the Vysehrad while attempting to relieve a besieged garrison.

Zizka eventually took control of Kuttenberg. Although a temporary reconciliation occurred between the warring parties, imperial troops burned the city in 1422 to prevent it from falling back into the hands of the Taborites. Despite this, Zizka took the location, and the city entered a new period of prosperity under Bohemian auspices.

## Subsequent Crusades and Decline
The initial failure of the crusaders was followed by a second crusade in 1421 and two subsequent crusades, all of which failed. The Hussites, who conducted their affairs on extreme democratic lines, eventually fell into internal dissensions. This allowed a fifth crusade to cross the frontier in 1431 under Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg. By 1436, the Hussites came to terms with the church.

The long-term decline of Kuttenberg began in 1541 when its richest mine was flooded. The city later lost all its privileges during the Bohemian insurrection against Ferdinand I. Its ruin was further accelerated by the horrors of the Thirty Years' War and repeated visitations of the plague. A fire devastated the town in 1770, and the mines were abandoned by the end of the 18th century. Although the government reopened one mine in 1874, work there was discontinued in 1903.
