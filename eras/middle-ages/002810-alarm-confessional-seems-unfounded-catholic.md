# The Confessional and the Secret Ballot

## The Secret Ballot and the Confessional
The introduction of the Ballot Act established several new legal offences to protect the integrity of elections. These included the fraudulent defacing, destroying, or forging of papers, as well as the unauthorized supply of papers or the removal of papers from a station. Additionally, it became an offence to interfere with, open, or destroy packets of papers or boxes used for election purposes. Officers and clerks found guilty of these crimes, or attempts to commit them, faced imprisonment for two years, with or without hard labour; for others, the term of imprisonment was six months.

Despite these protections, the secret ballot faced criticism for allegedly encouraging deception and universal hypocrisy. Sydney Smith described a scene of voters arriving at the polls in sedan-chairs with curtains closely drawn and wearing dominos. However, the observed result of the secret ballot was the gradual extermination of undue influence. 

Concerns were raised regarding "the confessional" in relation to the secret ballot, but such alarms are considered unfounded. A Catholic penitent is not required to confess how they voted. Furthermore, if a penitent were to disclose their vote, it would be a crime for the confessor to divulge that information.

## The Role of the Confessional in Catholic Practice
In Catholic tradition, the confessional serves as a space for the admission of sins. Observations at St. Jacques, a church noted for its works of art depicting sacred history, show worshippers using confessional chairs to confess their sins to listening priests. This practice provides priests with insight into the most secret recesses of human life.

By 1436, an agreement was reached between the moderate Hussites and the Council of Basle. This agreement allowed the Bohemian church to maintain certain distinctions from general Catholic practice until the sixteenth century's German Reformation. The split within the Hussites was largely driven by the extreme section's drift toward a primitive communism, which caused alarm among influential and wealthier Czech noblemen. ## Theological Conflicts and the Catholic Faith
Historical records indicate periods of intense theological dispute involving Catholics. In the kingdom of the Burgundians, the Arian monarch Gundobald convened an assembly of bishops at Lyons to address religious and political discontent following the conversion of Clovis. During this conference, Arians accused Catholics of worshipping three Gods, while Catholics used theological distinctions to defend their position. 

The conflict culminated in a challenge from Gundobald, who questioned the sincerity of the Frankish king's conversion, arguing that a covetous and sanguinary mind was not a symptom of sincere faith. Avitus, the bishop of Vienna, responded on behalf of the orthodox bishops, stating that while they were ignorant of the king's motives, Scripture teaches that kingdoms abandoning divine law are frequently subverted and face enemies on every side. Gundobald ultimately dismissed the conference because he would not accept the conditions the Catholics viewed as essential to the treaty.
