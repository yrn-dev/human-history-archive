# Ancient and Historical Weaponry and Burial Customs

## Weapons of Early Warfare
In certain historical periods, the primary weapons utilized were the spear and the sword. Swords were typically two-edged and averaged approximately 3 feet in length. These weapons sometimes held considerable value, featuring hilts that were often elaborately ornamented. Regarding spears, there was no definite distinction between the javelin and the spear proper. Archaeological findings from graves show that spear-heads varied significantly in size and form; these were attached to the shaft via a socket that remained open on one side.

Other forms of weaponry were relatively rare. While bows and arrows were used for sporting activities, there is no evidence to suggest they were used extensively in warfare prior to the Danish invasions, and they are very seldom found in graves.

## Defensive Armour and Equipment
The most common piece of defensive equipment was the shield. These were small, circular, and constructed from relatively thin lime-wood, with the edge likely reinforced by a thin band of iron. To protect the hand of the user, a strong iron boss—approximately 7 inches in diameter and projecting about 3 inches—was placed in the center.

Literary evidence indicates that coats of chain mail (known as *byrne*) and helmets (*helm*) were in common use. However, these items are seldom discovered in graves. This rarity may be attributed to the custom of heriots or the fact that their high value led them to be preserved as heirlooms and passed down through generations. Greaves are not frequently mentioned in the records.

## Social Class and the Heriot
In later times, the heriot of an "ordinary thegn" (*medema þegn*)—referring to a man of the *twelfhynde* class rather than a king's thegn—consisted of his arms and his horse with its saddle, or an equivalent payment of two pounds of silver. The required arms likely included one or two spears, shields, a helmet, a sword, and a coat of mail. 

Indications suggest a similar outfit was fairly common during the time of Ine and held a similar value. However, this equipment was not universal, as the purchasing power of two pounds of silver was considerable, representing approximately 100-120 sheep or 16-20 oxen. It is considered unsafe to assume that men of the *sixhynde* class generally possessed such equipment.

## Burial Practices and Grave Goods
Burial customs varied across different cultures and eras. In some ancient traditions, there was a practice of requiring human beings to follow the dead to the grave. Evidence suggests that the idea of substituting terracotta figures of humans and animals for actual people originated in China and was adopted in connection with Yamato sepulchres.

In other contexts, graves served as sites of commemoration and shared resting places. For example, in Bapaume Cemetery, there is a common grave where Germans and Frenchmen have rested side by side since 1871, alongside more recent graves from subsequent wars.
