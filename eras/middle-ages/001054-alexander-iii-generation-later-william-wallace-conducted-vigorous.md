# Alexander III and the Era of William Wallace

## Alexander III
Alexander III was a King of Scotland who reigned until his death in 1286. During his rule, the Scots crushed the pretensions of Haakon of Norway to the sovereignty of the Isles at the battle of Largs in 1263. In the town of Ayr, the "Auld Brig" is said to date from his reign. Additionally, Alexander III was born in the castle of Roxburgh, a location where Alexander II had previously been married.

## The Campaigns of William Wallace
A generation after the reign of Alexander III, William Wallace conducted a vigorous campaign in Ayrshire. During this period, he burned the barns of Ayr where the forces of Edward I were lodged and surprised the English garrison at Ardrossan. 

Tradition holds that Wallace held a council at a rock called "Wallace's Chair," located on the top of Quothquan Law, prior to the battle of Biggar in 1297. In the same year, Sir William of Douglas joined the rising led by Wallace. Wallace's military efforts also included an act of vengeance against the English sheriff William Heselrig. After Heselrig laid siege to Lamington Tower—resulting in the death of Hugh de Bradfute and his son, and the execution of Marion (who is described as the wife or betrothed of Wallace) in Lanark—Wallace killed the sheriff and burnt out the English garrison.

Wallace's forces included Sir Alexander Scrymgeour of Dundee, who served as his standard-bearer. Following the defeat of Wallace at Falkirk, the castle of Roxburgh fell into English hands.

## Subsequent Conflicts and Figures
Following the era of Wallace, Robert Bruce continued the struggle against the English. Bruce is alleged to have been born at Turnberry Castle. In 1307, he defeated the English at Loudoun Hill. The castle of Roxburgh was eventually delivered from English control in 1314 by Sir James Douglas.

Other figures of the period include Sir William of Douglas ("le hardi"), who joined Wallace's rising in 1297 but died as a prisoner in the Tower of London in 1298. The English presence in the region was further marked by Cromwell, who demolished the castle of Ardrossan and reportedly used its stones to build a fort at Ayr.

## Historical Commemorations and Records
The events of this era are preserved in various forms of record and monument. The narrative poem *The Brus*, written in twenty books, opens with a description of Scotland at the time of Alexander III's death in 1286 and concludes in 1332. While the poem is noted for some factual errors, it is considered more historically trustworthy than the *Wallace* written in the following century.

In the town of Ayr, the legacy of the period is marked by the Gothic Wallace Tower on High Street and a statue of Sir William Wallace sculpted by James Thorn.
