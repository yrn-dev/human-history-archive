# Nomocanon of Patriarch Photius and the Basilica

The *Nomocanon* of Patriarch Photius is a significant legal work within the tradition of Byzantine jurisprudence. It represents the blending of purely ecclesiastical rules of law, known as the *Canones*, with the rules of civil law. This synthesis of religious and secular legislation resulted in the creation of the *Nomocanon*. While various editions exist, the most important edition of the *Nomocanon* was produced by Theodorus Bestes in 1090.

## The Basilica: Context and Origin
The *Nomocanon* is closely linked to the *Basilica* ([Greek: *Ta basilika*]), a grand compilation of law drawn up in the Greek language. The *Basilica* was created to resolve legal uncertainty that prevailed throughout the East Roman empire during the 9th century. This uncertainty had arisen because 6th-century jurists held conflicting opinions on how to interpret the legislation of the emperor Justinian, which had effectively stripped that legislation of its authority and left imperial judges without clear rules for their decisions.

An earlier attempt to remedy these issues was made by the emperor Leo the Isaurian, though his reforms were seen as increasing uncertainty. The task of purifying the administration of justice and reducing the law into an intelligible code was eventually undertaken by Basil the Macedonian (Basil I, r. 867–886). Basil I began the process by having extracts made from existing laws and preparing for a comprehensive codification.

## Development and Structure of the Basilica
The *Basilica* served as a resuscitation of the code of Justinian, specifically acting as a compilation from the *Novels*, the *Codex*, and the *Digests*. In the East, it completely superseded the original Latin *Corpus juris* of Justinian. 

The formation of the code occurred in stages:
* **Basil I:** He formally abrogated ancient laws that had fallen into desuetude and is believed to have revised the laws that remained in force, dividing them into forty books.
* **Leo VI (the Wise):** The son of Basil, Leo VI enlarged and distributed the code into sixty books. He appointed a commission of jurists to oversee the revision, presided over by Sympathius, the captain of the body-guard.
* **Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus:** Some sources, specifically the 12th-century canonist Theodorus Balsamon, state that Constantine VII made a further revision of the code.

## The Role of Theodorus Balsamon
The connection between the *Nomocanon* of Patriarch Photius and the *Basilica* is highlighted through the work of Theodorus Balsamon. In the preface to the *Nomocanon* of Patriarch Photius, Balsamon cites passages from the *Basilica* that differ from the text of the code as it was revised by Emperor Leo. However, the weight of authority generally opposes the claim that any further revision took place after the reign of Leo.

## Preservation and Transmission
No perfect manuscript of the *Basilica* has been preserved. The code's massive bulk acted as an obstacle to the multiplication of copies. By the 12th century, copies were very scarce; a letter from Mark, the patriarch of Alexandria, to Theodorus Balsamon reveals that the patriarch was unable to procure a copy of the work. Because of this scarcity, jurists frequently relied on *encheiridia* (handbooks) and synopses of the *Basilica's* contents.

The *Basilica* remained largely ignored by Western European jurists until 1533, when Viglius Zuichemus mentioned its importance in the preface to his edition of the Greek *Paraphrase of Theophilus*. Later editions include a seven-volume set published by Charles Annibal Fabrot under the patronage of Louis XIII of France, and a more complete six-volume text published at Leipzig between 1833 and 1870, edited by G. E. Heimbach and K. W. E. Heimbach.
