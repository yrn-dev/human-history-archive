# Spain and its Historical Development

The history of Spain is characterized by shifting political dominions, the rise and fall of commercial centers, and a complex intellectual landscape influenced by various religious and cultural forces.

## Commercial and Urban Evolution of Barcelona
The city of Barcelona, known anciently as Barcino, is traditionally linked to the Carthaginian Hamilcar Barca in the 3rd century B.C. Following the Roman conquest, Augustus renamed the city Julia Faventia, and it subsequently became a leading mart of the western Mediterranean by the 2nd century A.D., competing with Massilia and Tarraco. 

The city served as a temporary capital for the Visigoths in 415 and 531. In 713, the Moors captured the city (then called Barcinona or Bardjaluna), but by 801, it passed under the dominion of the Franks. Independent rule by the counts of Barcelona began in 874. The city reached its zenith of wealth and fame following the 1149 union between Aragon and Catalonia. During this era, Barcelona's merchant ships traded as far east as Alexandria and as far west as the Baltic and North Seas, rivaling Venice, Genoa, and Ragusa. 

In 1258, James I of Aragon authorized the *Consulado del Mar*, a maritime law code recognized by many European states. Barcelona was also an early adopter of marine insurance. However, the 1479 union of Aragon and Castile shifted commercial supremacy to western Spanish ports, a transition accelerated by the 1492 discovery of America. This led to local resentment toward the "Castilian" government; in 1640, Barcelona became the center of a rebellion against Philip IV and sought French protection. While it returned to its allegiance in 1652, it was captured by the duke of Vendome in 1697.

## Intellectual Life and the Legacy of Averroes
During the final period of Mahommedan rule in Spain under the Almohades, the philosopher Averroes (1126-1198) emerged as a prominent figure. Born in Cordova, Averroes mastered jurisprudence, theology, medicine, philosophy, and mathematics. He was versed in the Malekite system of law and served as cadi of Seville starting in 1169. He also likely served as a physician to Yusef al-Mansur, who engaged him in discussions regarding philosophy and the faith of Islam.

Intellectual freedom during this time was largely dependent on the favor of the monarch and the wealthy. The general population often viewed speculative studies with distrust, labeling those who did not adhere strictly to the natural science of the Koran as *Zendik* (infidels). This tension culminated around 1195 when Averroes was accused of heretical pursuits, stripped of his honors, and banished to a location near Cordova. This period saw a broader attempt to eliminate liberal culture in Andalusia, sparing only basic astronomy, arithmetic, and medicine. Averroes was eventually recalled to Morocco, where he died shortly before his patron, al-Mansur. The political power of the Moslems and the culture of liberal science ended with al-Mansur in 1199.

## Military Orders and the Reconquest
Spain established several ancient military religious orders to fight the Moors for the true faith, similar to the Hospitallers and Templars. These include the orders of Calatrava, Alcantara, and St James of Compostella (also known as St James of the Sword). 

The Order of Knights of St James of Compostella is traditionally attributed to King Ramiro II of Leon in the 10th century to mark a victory over the Moors, though it was historically confirmed by Pope Alexander III in 1175. The order became wealthy and reputable through its wars against the Moors. In 1493, Ferdinand the Catholic annexed the grand-mastership, and Pope Adrian VI permanently vested it in the Spanish crown in 1522. Today, these exist as orders of merit; the Order of St James of Compostella is divided into three classes, while Alcantara and Calatrava are single-class orders of military merit.

## Arts, Industry, and Literature
Spain's industrial history includes a long tradition of metallurgy. In the Basque Provinces, iron mining has occurred since prehistoric times, with mines around Bilbao being particularly significant. In 1905, the Basque Provinces produced 5,302,344 tons of iron (with over five million coming from Biscay), more than half of which was exported to England. The region is also known for small-arms production in Eibar and the renowned swords of Mondragon in Guipuzcoa, which were famous before those of Toledo. In the 19th century, Senor Zuloaga revived the art of inlaying steel and iron with silver and gold.

In literature, Spain produced the legendary character of Don Juan. While the story of a profligate inviting a dead man to dinner existed before 1630, it was formalized in the play *El Burlador de Sevilla y convidado de piedra*, first printed in Barcelona in 1630 and usually attributed to Tirso de Molina. The character represents arrogant blasphemy and perverse sensuality. The Spanish drama of this era gained significant popularity in Italy, where it was often performed in pantomime.
