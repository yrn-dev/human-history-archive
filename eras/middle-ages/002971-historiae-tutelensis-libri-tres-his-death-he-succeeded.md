# Historiae Tutelensis libri tres

## Overview
*Historiae Tutelensis libri tres* is a historical work published in 1717. It serves as a history of the author's native town and was produced during the final years of his life.

## Authorship and Context
The work was written by Baluze, a scholar who became embroiled in a series of controversies regarding genealogical authenticity. Baluze had been encouraged and financially supported by the cardinal de Bouillon, an intriguing patron. Under this patronage, Baluze produced several works, including a *Table genealogique* in 1705 and a *Histoire genealogique de la maison d'Auvergne* in 1709. 

The *Histoire genealogique de la maison d'Auvergne* included "Proofs" that contained deeds previously pronounced spurious. These documents were part of a larger effort to support certain pretensions through fabricated evidence. A "regular manufactory of forged documents" had been organized by Jean de Bar, an intimate companion of the cardinal. These forgeries were so skillful that they initially duped illustrious scholars, including Baluze, Dom Thierry Ruinart, and Dom Jean Mabillon (the founder of Diplomatics), who all provided a unanimously favourable report on July 23, 1695.

## Legal and Professional Decline
The authenticity of the documents led to a "war of pamphlets" due to the cardinal de Bouillon's many enemies. In March 1698, Baluze wrote a *Letter* in response, though it proved nothing. In 1700, Jean de Bar and his accomplices were arrested and subsequently declared guilty in 1704 after a searching inquiry. Despite these findings, Baluze remained obstinate, convinced that the incriminated documents were genuine.

In the year following the publication of his 1709 work, Baluze was caught in the disgrace of his patron. He was exiled far from Paris and stripped of his pensions, appointments, and benefices.

## Final Years and Death
While in exile, Baluze continued his scholarly pursuits, leading to the 1717 publication of *Historiae Tutelensis libri tres*. Before his death, he succeeded in returning to Paris. He died on July 28, 1718, remaining unconvinced of his errors. Historical analysis of his correspondence with the cardinal suggests that Baluze may have been the victim of clever cheats rather than an accomplice to the forgeries.
