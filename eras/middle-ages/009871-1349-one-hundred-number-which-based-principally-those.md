# 1349: Historical Events and Figures

The year 1349 appears in the archive's records in relation to literary production, urban development in Bohemia, and the history of cartography.

## Ulrich Boner and "Der Edelstein"
Ulrich Boner (also known as Bonerius), a German-Swiss writer of fables born in Bern, is mentioned in records between 1324 and 1349. Descended from an old Bernese family, Boner took clerical orders and became a monk, though he later married. Because he had received only the "tonsure," he was entitled to the benefit of the *clerici uxoriati*, allowing him to return to secular life after divesting himself of clerical garb.

Around 1349, Boner wrote a collection of fables in Middle High German titled *Der Edelstein*. The work consists of one hundred fables, which were based primarily on the *Anonymus* (edited by I. Nevelet in 1610) and the 4th-century works of Avianus. Boner utilized a clear and simple style, incorporating touches of humor to relieve the didactic tone of the collection. He treated his sources with originality and freedom. *Der Edelstein* was dedicated to Johann von Rinkenberg, a poet and Bernese patrician who served as the *advocatus* (*Vogt*) of Brienz and died circa 1350. The work was printed in Bamberg in 1461, and it is claimed to be the first book printed in the German language.

## The City of Krumau
In Bohemia, the town of Krumau (known in Czech as *Krumlov*) is situated on the banks of the Vitava (Moldau). The town was long the stronghold of the Rosenberg family, also referred to as *pani z ruze* (the lords of the rose). While Henry II of Rosenberg (who died in 1310) was the first family member to reside at Krumau, it was his son, Peter I, who raised the location to the rank of a city in 1349. Peter I died in that same year.

## Cartography and Ibn al Wardi
The archive's records on geography note that Arabian travellers and geographers provided significant materials for map compilers. Among these figures was Ibn al Wardi, who produced maps in 1349. However, the archive characterizes the maps of Ibn al Wardi, as well as those of Istakhri (950), as being of a "most rudimentary type." These are contrasted with the earlier maps seen by Masudi in the *Rasm el Ard* (or *Horismos*), a description of the world by Abu Jafar Mahommed ben Musa of Khiva, the librarian to caliph el Mamun in 833, which Masudi declared superior to the maps of Marinus or Ptolemy.
