# Jura

The term Jura appears in the archive's records in two distinct contexts: as a geographical region in France and as a legal classification within Roman law.

## The Jura Region
Jura is a department in France. It is characterized by the Jura mountains, the geology of which was studied in the early years of the Swiss-American geologist Jules Marcou (1824–1898). Marcou, who was born in the department of Jura on April 20, 1824, authored several works regarding this region, including *Lettres sur les roches du Jura et leur distribution géographique dans les deux hémisphères* (1857–1860). His research also focused on the Jura-Cretaceous formations of the area.

## Jura in Roman Law
In the context of Roman legal collections, *jura* refers to juridical treatises. This is distinguished from *leges*, which are imperial constitutions. 

The *Breviarium Alaricanum* (Breviary of Alaric) is a collection of Roman law compiled in A.D. 506 during the twenty-second year of the reign of Alaric II, king of the Visigoths. This compilation was produced with the advice of nobles and bishops. Unlike the code of Justinian, which excluded the writings of jurists, the *Breviarium* includes both *leges* and *jura*.

The *Breviarium* comprises several components:
* Sixteen books of the Theodosian code.
* The Novels of Severus, Majorianus, Marcian, Valentinian III, and Theodosius II.
* The Institutes of Gaius.
* Five books of the *Sententiae Receptae* of Julius Paulus.
* Two titles of the Hermogenian code and thirteen titles of the Gregorian code.
* A fragment from the first book of the *Responsa Papiniani*.

## Administration and Nomenclature of the Breviarium
The *Breviarium* is sometimes called the Breviary of Anianus (*Breviarium Aniani*) because of a royal rescript (*commonitorium*) prefixed to the work. This rescript directed that copies certified by Anianus, the king's referendary, be received exclusively as law throughout the Visigothic kingdom. In the certificate of Anianus, the collection is termed a code (*codex*).

Among the Visigoths, the work was known as "Lex Theodosii" or "Lex Romana." The title "Breviarium" was not introduced until the 16th century; this was done to distinguish the original work from a 9th-century recast of the code used by Romans in Lombardy (northern Italy). This Lombard recast was preserved in the Codex Utinensis, which is now lost but was published in the 18th century by P. Canciani in *Barbarorum Leges Antiquae*. Another manuscript of this recast was found by Hänel in the library of St Gall.

The primary value of the Visigothic code is that it is the only Roman Law collection containing the first five books of the Theodosian code and five books of the *Sententiae Receptae*.

## Related Legal Editions
The most complete edition of the *Breviarium* is found in the collection of Roman law titled *Jus Civile Ante-Justinianum*, published in Berlin in 1815. Additionally, G. Hänel published *Lex Romana Visigothorum* between 1847 and 1849.
