# Kent: History, Governance, and Agriculture

## Political History and Governance
The kingdom of Kent was characterized by a kingly government that predated the invasion of Britain. While kings were typically chosen from a single family—whose ancestry was traced back to a founder and, more remotely, to a god—the royal title was sometimes held by multiple people simultaneously. This included arrangements of a supreme king with under-kings (*subreguli*), or multiple kings (often brothers) reigning on equal terms. During much of the 8th century, Kent appeared to be divided into two kingdoms.

The late 7th and 8th centuries were marked by instability. Following the death of Eadric, Kent was ruled by kings Bede described as "*dubii vel externi*." In 687, a West Saxon prince named Mul was killed during an unsuccessful conquest attempt, though the East Saxon king Sigehere may have successfully invaded that same year. By 688, Oswine of the native dynasty obtained part of the kingdom, while Swefheard (likely a son of the East Saxon king Sebbe) took the other part in 689. Wihtred, son of Ecgbert, succeeded Oswine around 690 and unified the kingdom by 694, leaving behind a code of laws.

Following Wihtred's death in 725, the kingdom was split among his sons: Aethelberht, Eadberht, and Alric. Aethelberht reigned until 762, while Eadberht died in 748 (though some charters suggest 761-762). Subsequent rulers included Eardwulf, Eanmund, and Sigered. From 764 to 779, Ecgbert reigned, initially with a colleague named Heaberht. History becomes obscure around 781-784, during which time Offa may have annexed Kent. After Offa's death in 796, Eadberht Praen claimed the throne but was captured in 798 by Coenwulf, who installed his brother Cuthred as king. After 807, Coenwulf and his successors Ceolwulf and Beornwulf held Kent. In 825, King Baldred was expelled by Ecgbert of Wessex. Under the West Saxon dynasty, Kent was occasionally a dependent kingdom given to royal family members, such as Aethelwulf and later Aethelstan.

Administratively, the kingdom was divided into West and East Kent. It utilized "lathes"—administrative districts under reeves attached to royal villages. By the 9th century or earlier, East Kent had four lathes: Canterbury, Eastry, Wye, and Lymne. By the 11th century, West Kent had two: Sutton and Aylesford.

## Social Structure
The social system in Kent differed from other English kingdoms like Wessex or Mercia. While other regions used classes such as *twelfhynde*, *sixhynde*, and *twihynde*, Kent utilized the terms *gestiðcund* and *ceorlisc*. The *gestiðcund* class had a wergild of 300 shillings. The *ceorlisc* class had a wergild of 100 shillings (each consisting of twenty silver coins called *sceattas*), which was higher than the equivalent class in other kingdoms. Below the *ceorlisc* were three classes of *laetas* (likely freedmen) with wergilds of 80, 60, and 40 shillings. These distinctions affected not only wergilds but also fines, compensations for injuries, and the value of oaths.

## Agriculture and Land Use
Kent is noted for its fertile rural scenery, with approximately three-quarters of its area under cultivation. While oats and wheat are grown in nearly equal amounts, barley is less prominent. Thanet produces mustard, spinach, and canary seed, and beans are grown on a considerable acreage.

The county is especially famed for fruit and hops. Hops are grown in the Weald, the Medway valley in mid Kent, and the area between Canterbury and Faversham. Kent produces about two-thirds of the hop acreage in the British Isles. Hops are classified by district: "East Kents" (grown on Chalk), "Bastard East Kents" (alluvial and mixed soils), "Mid Kents" (Greensand and London Tertiaries), and "Wealds" (Weald Clay, Hastings Sand, and Tunbridge Wells Sand). Since 1894, poor-quality hop gardens have been grubbed due to low prices and importations.

Fruit production is centered in the Medway and Darent valleys and the tertiary soils between Canterbury and Rochester. Kent is particularly famous for cherries—said to have been introduced by Henry VIII from Flanders—and filberts. Filberts and cob-nuts are grown primarily on Ragstone soils near Maidstone. Soft fruits, including strawberries (produced in enormous quantities near Orpington, the Crays, and Sandwich) and raspberries, are grown for jam-making. This industry is supported by jam factories, such as one at Swanley that farms nearly 2,000 acres.

Livestock and other practices include:
* **Sheep:** One of England's chief sheep counties, raising "Kents" on Romney Marsh and Southdowns on the uplands.
* **Cattle:** Grazed on marsh lands, with numerous dairy farms near London.
* **Pigs:** Historically numerous, especially in Kent, and kept in the woods.
* **Bee-keeping:** Extensively practiced.

## Industry and Trade
Early industries included salt-works along the coast (over 100 mentioned in the Domesday Survey) and iron-mining in the Weald, which dates back to Roman times. The iron trade declined after a 1558 statute forbade felling timber for smelting within fourteen miles of the coast, and was eventually ended by the discovery of coal in northern counties.

Other historical industries include:
* **Textiles:** Flemish weavers settled in the Weald during the time of Edward III; silk-weaving existed in Canterbury and thread-making in Maidstone during the 16th century.
* **Manufacturing:** Tiles were made at Wye in the 14th century, and one of England's first paper-mills was established at Dartford before 1590.
* **Trade:** A 1630 statute forbidding wool exportation, combined with the 1665 Plague, caused a depression and spurred a massive smuggling trade; 40,000 packs of wool were smuggled to Calais from Kent and Sussex over two years.
