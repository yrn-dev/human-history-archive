# The Chambered Tumulus in the Saga of Beowulf

The Anglo-Saxon saga of *Beowulf* contains detailed descriptions of sepulchral usages, specifically regarding the construction and contents of the chambered tumulus. These descriptions reflect general features of barrow-burial found across Europe.

## Architecture and Contents of the Tumulus
The saga describes a chambered tumulus characterized by gigantic masonry. This structure is noted for having vaults of stone that are held fast on props. Within the mound, there is a passage that is haunted by a dragon. This creature serves as the guardian of the treasures contained within the barrow, which consist of heathen gold. The looting of such a barrow, already considered ancient within the timeline of the narrative, constitutes one of the main adventures in the poem.

## The Burial of Beowulf
The burial of the hero Beowulf is described with minute detail, bearing a strong resemblance to parallel passages found in the *Odyssey* and the *Iliad*. The process occurs in several distinct stages:

*   **Preparation of the Pyre:** A pile is first prepared and hung around with coats of mail, shields, and helmets.
*   **The Cremation:** The corpse is placed in the center of the pile. The pyre is then kindled, and a roaring flame rises, accompanied by weeping, until everything is consumed.
*   **Construction of the Mound:** Following the cremation, warriors labor for ten long days to rear a mighty mound. This mound is built on a headland and is described as being both broad and high, ensuring it can be seen from a distance by passers-by traveling by sea or land.

## Literary and Historical Context
*Beowulf* is an epic poem of the Old English. While it exists in a later form, the original nucleus of the work is thought to date to continental and heathen times. One source suggests the saga was made long before the English crossed from Germany into England. 

The narrative features several figures associated with Danish and Scandinavian tradition. It mentions a Danish king named Healfdene, who had three sons: Halga, Hrothgar, and Heorogar. The hero Beowulf arrives at the court of Hrothgar from the land of the Götar, where Hygelac is king. Based on chronological data linking Hygelac to a raid against the Franks around 520, the reign of Healfdene can be placed in the last half of the 5th century, and the reign of Hrothwulf (son of Halga and nephew of Hrothgar) around the middle of the 6th century.
