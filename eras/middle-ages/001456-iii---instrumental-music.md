# Instrumental Music

The history of instrumental music is characterized by a diverse array of string, wind, and percussion instruments developed across various civilizations, including the Semitic races, Persians, and Europeans. These instruments have evolved through mechanical innovation and cultural exchange, often reflecting the specific nomenclature and artistic preferences of their regions.

## Stringed Instruments of the East and Antiquity

Among the ancient Semitic races, the *kinnor* (Greek: *kinyra*) was a prominent stringed instrument used before 2000 B.C. While frequently translated as "harp" in the Bible, evidence suggests it is the equivalent of the Greek *cithara*. This instrument was widely utilized in Asia Minor and is depicted in Hittite sculptures and on Hebrew coins.

In the Persian and Arab traditions, nomenclature often changed based on slight deviations in an instrument's construction. The *barbat* was a bass instrument and a variety of the *rebab*, differing primarily in size and the number of strings. The *barbud* (barbiton) is associated with a famous musician who lived during the time of Chosroes II (A.D. 590-628). Persian musical ensembles have included the *barbut*, *chang* (lyre), *rubab* (lute), and *nei* (flute). As late as the 19th century, the *barbut* and *rubab* remained distinct instruments. Early archetypes of the lute and the pear-shaped *rebab* were similar before the introduction of the bow; both featured gut strings plucked by the fingers, vaulted backs, and a combined body and neck.

Another instrument of Eastern origin is the dulcimer. Bas-reliefs from Kuyunjik show musicians playing ten-stringed dulcimers using long sticks curved at the ends while damping the strings with their hands. This instrument is identified as the *pisantir* from the era of Nebuchadrezzar. In the Septuagint, the term *psalterion* was used broadly to describe four different instruments: the *ugab*, *nebel*, *pisantir*, and *toph*, which included string, wind, and percussion types. The modern Persian *santir* is nearly identical to the German *hackbrett*.

## Biblical and Levite Instrumentation

According to historical accounts, David composed hymns to God in various meters, such as trimeters and pentameters, and created musical instruments to teach the Levites to sing during festivals and on the sabbath day. The instruments described include:
*   **The Viol:** A ten-stringed instrument played with a bow.
*   **The Psaltery:** An instrument featuring twelve musical notes, played with the fingers.
*   **Cymbals:** Large, broad instruments constructed from brass.

## Evolution of Wind Instruments

The development of wind instruments often involved adapting natural materials into mechanical forms. The curved cornet originated from animal horns with lateral holes. When these were replicated in wood and metal, the straight cornet was developed to simplify construction. This evolution likely occurred in Asia Minor, where conical bore tubes were common, before the instrument entered Europe. An 11th-century miniature depicts a straight *Zinck* with six visible holes and a grotesque animal's head at the bell-end. Similar grotesque additions appeared on the reed-blown chaunter of the bagpipe. By the time of Emperor Maximilian I (d. 1519), instruments known as *stille Zincken* or *cornetti muti* were utilized.

The bassoon also underwent significant nomenclature and design changes. In 1426, Louis Willay, a maker in Bruges, sold a set of wood-wind instruments to Philippe le Bon, including "4 bombardes, 4 doucaines and 4 flutes." It is suggested that the *doucaine* may have been the origin of the satirical Italian name *fagotto*. Later, Ravilius of Ferrara constructed the first *phagotus* based on a design by Afranio, an inventor known for creating mechanical musical devices that were often "mere freaks" and did not survive their creator. Early forms of the bassoon, such as the *dolcian* and the *double curtail*, have been described and illustrated by Michael Praetorius in *Syntagma Musicum* (1618) and by Mersenne.
