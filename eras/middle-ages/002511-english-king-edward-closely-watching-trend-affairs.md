# Edward I and the Affairs of Scotland

During the reign of King Edward I of England, the English crown maintained a close and active interest in the political trends and succession disputes of Scotland. This involvement ranged from diplomatic marriage arrangements and the arbitration of royal claims to direct military campaigns and the assertion of overlordship.

## Intervention in the Scottish Succession
Edward I's involvement in Scotland intensified following the death of King Alexander III in 1285. During this period, Edward interfered in Scottish affairs, employing Antony Bek, the bishop of Durham, to assist in these matters. In 1284, the estates of Scotland had decided that if Alexander died childless, the crown would pass to his granddaughter, Margaret, known as the "maid of Norway." Margaret became queen in March 1286. In 1289, Edward I arranged a marriage between the infant queen and his own son, the future Edward II. However, Margaret died in the Orkneys around the end of September 1290.

The resulting vacancy of the Scottish throne in 1290 led to a dispute among three candidates. The primary rivalry existed between Robert Bruce, earl of Annandale, and John de Baliol. Baliol claimed the throne through his maternal grandmother, Margaret, the eldest daughter of David, earl of Huntingdon. While Bruce was the nearest of kin, Baliol claimed the inheritance based on the principles of feudal law for an indivisible inheritance.

## Arbitration and Overlordship
Edward I was invited to settle the dispute between the claimants. In May 1291, Edward met with Scottish nobles at Norham, where he demanded a formal recognition of his position as the overlord of Scotland. Although the rights of English kings over Scotland were doubtful, the nobles tacitly admitted this position, and the competitors, including Baliol, agreed to abide by Edward's decision.

To resolve the claim, a court was appointed consisting of twenty-four Englishmen and eighty Scotsmen. Following the court's advice, Edward decided in favor of John de Baliol. After swearing fealty to the English king, Baliol was crowned king of Scotland at Scone on November 30, 1292. Baliol subsequently did homage to Edward at Newcastle and, in January 1293, released the English king from all obligations made while Scotland had been in his hands.

## Conflict and Military Campaigns
The amicable relations between Edward I and King Baliol were short-lived. Tensions rose when a Scottish vassal brought a case to Edward in his capacity as Baliol's overlord, leading to Baliol being summoned to the English court. 

Edward I launched campaigns in Scotland, in which figures such as Bishop Antony Bek played significant roles. Bek led a division of the English army at the battle of Falkirk in 1298 and received the surrender of John de Baliol at Brechin in 1296. Other English nobles, such as Bartholomew Badlesmere, also fought in Scotland during the later years of Edward's reign.

The English influence extended to various Scottish nobles. Patrick Dunbar, the 8th earl of Dunbar and 1st earl of March, was favorable to English interests and did homage to Edward I; he was later appointed as the English king's lieutenant in Scotland in 1298. Similarly, Sir William of Douglas ("le hardi") swore fealty to Edward I in 1291. Although Douglas later commanded Berwick Castle and surrendered it to the English in 1296 after the sack of the town, he was restored to his Scottish estates after renewing his homage to Edward, though his English possessions were forfeited.

## Diplomatic and Administrative Activity
Edward I's administration utilized various agents to manage Scottish and border affairs. Alexander de Baliol, lord of Cavers and chamberlain of Scotland, participated in negotiations between Scottish nobles and Edward I, which resulted in the treaty of Brigham in 1290 and the treaty of Salisbury in 1289. Alexander later fought for Edward in Scotland.

Beyond Scotland, Edward I's influence extended to the Welsh marches. Under his reign, the marcher lordships—granted to powerful nobles for the defense of English counties—included more than half of the area of Wales. This encompassed nearly the entire principality, excluding the counties of Carnarvon, Anglesea, and Merioneth in the north, and Cardigan and Carmarthen in the south, as well as parts of Shropshire, Hereford, and Monmouth.
