# Foreign Intercourse, Literature, Art, Religion, Manners, and Customs

## Japanese Art and Literature
During the Kamakura period, Japanese art diverged into two distinct schools: Kyoto and Kamakura. The Kyoto school, centered in the Imperial Court, originated from Chinese masters of the Tang and Sui dynasties and eventually evolved into the national school of Japan. Its style was characterized by a decorative tendency. In contrast, the Kamakura school was centered in the Court of the Hojo and borrowed from the academies of the Yuan and Sung. This school was favored by the literary classes and maintained its Chinese traditions, focusing primarily on seascape and landscape motives and the melancholy aspects of nature, which appealed to cenobites and students.

These distinctions extended to horticulture, architecture, painting, and calligraphy. While Kyoto penmanship followed the graceful, cursive "domestic fashion" (o-ie-fu) modeled after Ono no Tofu and Kobo Daishi, Kamakura calligraphists adopted the "pure Chinese mode" (karayo), as seen in the work of Ichinei (I Ning) and Sogen (Chu Yuan).

## Religion and Foreign Influence in Japan
In Kamakura, the Bakufu regents—Tokimune, Tokiyori, and Yasutoki—were earnest disciples of Buddhism. They established five great temples in the Bakufu capital, assigning them to Chinese priests from the Yuan and Sung eras who arrived as propagandists or official envoys. Because these Chinese bonzes superintended the construction, the resulting buildings reflected the art-canons of the priests, Japan, and China.

The Hojo regents utilized these foreign priests as channels for gathering political information regarding the neighboring empire. Furthermore, they sought to create a literary and artistic atmosphere in Kamakura that was independent of Kyoto and free from its enervating elements.

In the Muromachi epoch, religious practices intersected with family obligations. Under the Muromachi Bakufu, every son of a sovereign was expected to become a monk, with the exception of the Prince Imperial. The Ashikaga family applied a similar system ruthlessly. This era was noted for the neglect of consanguinity, with instances of brothers, uncles, nephews, fathers, and sons pitted against one another.

## Ancient Customs and Manners
The Nasamonians, a numerous race, practiced specific seasonal and social customs. In summer, they left their flocks by the sea to gather large, fruit-bearing date-palms in the region of Augila. Their diet included wingless locusts, which they dried in the sun, pounded, and drank sprinkled upon milk. 

Nasamonian marriage customs involved polygamy and common intercourse. On the first night of marriage, the bride would have intercourse with all the guests, and each man would give her a gift brought from his house. Their spiritual practices included divination by sleeping upon the sepulchral mounds of ancestors after praying and accepting the resulting dreams. Oaths were sworn by laying hands upon the tombs of men reported to be the most brave and righteous. Pledges were exchanged by drinking from one another's hands or, if no liquid was available, by licking dust from the ground.

In ancient Egypt, the kingdom was regarded by ancients as the source of most sciences and arts and a renowned school for politics and wisdom. This reputation led illustrious Greeks, including Plato, Pythagoras, Homer, and legislators such as Solon and Lycurgus, to travel to Egypt to complete their studies.

## Foreign Intercourse and Global Exchange
The nature of foreign intercourse varied significantly across different eras and regions. In the Byzantine Empire, the language and religion of the polished Arabs acted as an insurmountable bar to social intercourse. Similarly, the successors of Heraclius were rarely connected with the Franks or Latins, whose manners were considered rude and whose speech was unknown, despite a shared Christian faith. This isolation led to a lack of competition and a "languid temper" among Byzantine subjects. Conversely, expeditions to the Holy Land mingled the nations of Asia and Europe, rekindling military virtue and knowledge under the Comnenian dynasty.

In Denmark, foreign intercourse was historically hindered by restrictive commercial legislation. Imported manufactures were required to be delivered to customs for public auction, and inland duties impeded commerce between provinces. These restrictions, along with a lack of roads, slowed industrial and commercial development until the 19th century, when commercial treaties with other European powers made the Danish tariff more liberal and regular.

## Literature and the Representation of Customs
The term "costume" entered the English language in the 18th century, initially referring to the correct representation in art and literature of general surroundings, furniture, dress, and manners. By the early 19th century, the term became restricted to jewelry, head-dresses, and the style of personal apparel.

In the realm of poetry, Byron is noted as a creative artist who used his work to protest customs, faith, or laws of external tyranny. His poems served as a liberal education for his countrymen regarding the history, politics, art, and scenery of Greece and Italy, as well as the manners and customs of "the gorgeous East."
