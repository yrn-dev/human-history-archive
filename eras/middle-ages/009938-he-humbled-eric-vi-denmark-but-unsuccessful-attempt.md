# Boniface VIII and Eric VI of Denmark

## Overview of Boniface VIII
Boniface VIII, born Benedetto Gaetano to a noble family in Anagni, served as pope from 1294 to 1303. His early career was marked by studies in civil and canon law in Italy and potentially Paris. He held various positions, including canonicates at Todi and in France, and served as a notary and advocate at the papal court. In 1267, while aiding King Henry III of England against the baronial party, he was besieged in the Tower of London by the rebellious earl of Gloucester before being rescued by the future Edward I on April 27.

After being created a cardinal deacon in 1281 and a cardinal priest in 1291, Boniface became a highly influential figure in the Sacred College and undertook numerous diplomatic missions. He played a role in the abdication of the ineffective Celestine V, who had ruled in humility and monastic poverty. Boniface was subsequently chosen as pope at Naples on December 24, 1294, though his election was not unanimous, likely due to hostility from certain French cardinals.

## Papal Ambitions and Governance
Unlike his predecessor, Boniface VIII ardently asserted the lordship of the papacy over every kingdom in the world. He was crowned in Rome in January 1295 with great pomp. While he initially planned to pacify the West to recover the Holy Land from the infidel, his nine-year reign was characterized by controversies with leading European powers rather than peace.

His tenure was marked by arrogance, avarice, and lofty claims, which earned him many enemies. His attempts to build large estates for his family alienated the Colonna, and his policy of supporting the house of Anjou in Sicily ended in failure. Additionally, he refused to recognize Albert of Austria as the rightful German king until 1303 and declared that the crown of Hungary should fall to the house of Anjou, assuming the role of overlord of that nation.

## Interactions with European Monarchs
Boniface VIII engaged in several power struggles with the monarchs of Europe. He succeeded in humbling Eric VI of Denmark. However, he was unsuccessful in his attempt to try Edward I of England, the conqueror of Scotland, on charges that the king had interfered with a papal fief. This attempt failed after the English parliament declared in 1301 that Scotland had never been a fief of Rome. His most significant conflict during his papacy was with Philip IV of France.
