# Regional Centers of Belgium and Italy: Historical and Institutional Perspectives

The cities of Ghent, Namur, Venice, and Messina, among others, appear across historical records as centers of political administration, academic study, religious conflict, and international commerce. Their roles have evolved from the medieval era through the early 20th century, reflecting broader shifts in European governance and scholarship.

## Ghent and Namur in Belgian History

Ghent and Namur have frequently served as focal points for Belgian political and religious transitions. During the era of the Burgundian dukes, the territories were an agglomeration of states where the dukes ruled each principality separately; they held titles such as the counts of Flanders and Namur. The wealth of Philip the Good, who died in 1467, was so vast that his annual income nearly equaled that of the republic of Venice.

In later centuries, Ghent became a center of religious and political upheaval. During a period of anarchy and misrule, Calvinists took control of the city, transforming Ghent into a Calvinistic republic characterized by the rule of terror and the persecution of Catholics. This occurred around the time Don Juan of Austria died at Bouges, near Namur, in 1578.

In the 18th century, both cities were central to the Barrier Treaties between Great Britain and the United Netherlands. The treaty of October 29, 1709, sought to secure Holland against French aggression by establishing a barrier of towns, which included Namur and the citadel of Ghent. A subsequent treaty on January 29, 1713, maintained the citadel of Ghent and other strong places as part of this defensive system.

During the German occupation of Belgium, Governor von Bissing attempted to destroy Belgian nationality through "activism." He sought to sow dissension between Walloons and Flemings by creating a Flemish university of Ghent and establishing autonomous administrations ("Verwaltung") for Flanders in Brussels and for Wallony in Namur.

## Educational and Religious Institutions

By the early 20th century, Ghent and Namur held significant institutional roles. Ghent is home to a state university, which recorded 899 students for the 1904-1905 academic year, and a royal conservatoire. Namur hosts a school of cadets for military instruction.

Religiously, the hierarchy of the Roman Church in Belgium includes bishops for both Ghent and Namur. The Belgian constitution provides for absolute liberty of conscience, though the majority of the population is Roman Catholic.

## Venice and Messina in Mediterranean Commerce

Venice and Messina are linked through records of trade and diplomacy. In the 14th century, the Venetian archives record a procuration from the Doge in 1342 in favor of Ser Marco Paulo for a presentation before the king of Sicily. Subsequently, in 1346 or 1347, King Lewis of Sicily issued an order to the Maestri Procuratori of Messina granting Marco Polo of Venice the privilege of free import and export at the port of Messina. This grant, given for services rendered to the king's court, allowed for the movement of goods up to 20 ounces annually without the payment of customs.

Venice also engaged in strategic alliances. In 1306, Pope Clement V stimulated the Venetian government to participate in an enterprise involving Charles of Valois, who claimed the Empire of the East. Envoys including Thibault de Cepoy and Pierre le Riche, an ecclesiastic of Chartres, executed a treaty of alliance with Venice on December 14, 1306.

## Hagiographic Scholarship and Archives

The cities of Chartres, Namur, Ghent, Messina, and Venice are also noted in the records of the Bollandists, Jesuit fathers who sought to align their work with historical methods starting in 1882. Through their quarterly review, *Analecta Bollandiana*, the Bollandists analyzed hagiographic manuscripts in principal libraries. They published numerous library catalogues from these cities, as well as separate volumes dedicated to Latin and Greek manuscripts in the libraries of Rome, Paris, and the Bibliotheque Royale at Brussels. Their efforts included the preparation of inventories such as the *Bibliotheca hagiographica graeca* (1895) and the *Bibliotheca hagiographica latina* (1899).
