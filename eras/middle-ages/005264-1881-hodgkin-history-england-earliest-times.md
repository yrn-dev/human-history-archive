# Historical Authorities and Figures of the 19th and Early 20th Centuries

The study of history, law, and classical scholarship during the late 19th and early 20th centuries was characterized by a reliance on primary authorities and the production of comprehensive thematic histories. This period saw significant academic output regarding the Anglo-Saxon era, the development of English law, and the critical analysis of ecclesiastical documents.

## Authorities on Anglo-Saxon England
The study of early England relies on several primary authorities. These include the *Historia Britonum* by Nennius, *De Excidio Britanniae* by Gildas, the *Saxon Chronicle*, and the *Anglo-Saxon Laws*. Other essential primary sources include the *Hist. Eccl.* by Bede, the *Cartularium Saxonicum* (Charters), and the works of Th. Mommsen in *Mon. Germ. Hist., Auct. Antiquiss.*

Modern authorities who expanded the understanding of this period include J.R. Green (*The Making of England*, 1881), T. Hodgkin (*History of England from the Earliest Times to the Norman Conquest*, 1906), and J.M. Kemble (*The Saxons in England*). Other specialized studies focused on different aspects of the era: F. Seebohm wrote on the English village community and tribal custom in law, while P. Vinogradoff examined the growth of the manor. H.M. Chadwick contributed *Studies on Anglo-Saxon Institutions* (1905) and *The Origin of the English Nation* (1907). Additionally, M. Heyne and R. Henning provided research on early Germanic housing and the construction of the Halle Heorot.

## Legal and Constitutional History
The history of English law is documented through various authorities. Key texts include the works of Sir F. Pollock and F.W. Maitland (*History of English Law*), W.S. Holdsworth, and J. Reeves. Other significant contributors to the field include O.W. Holmes (*The Common Law*, 1881), H. Brunner (*The Sources of the Law of England*, 1888), and A.V. Dicey (*Law and Public Opinion in England*, 1905). The archive also notes the importance of the Year Books in the Rolls Series and the publications of the Selden Society.

## Ecclesiastical History and Critical Analysis
A significant portion of historical scholarship focused on the authenticity of papal documents and the lives of religious figures. T. Hodgkin, in *Italy and Her Invaders*, suggested that the *Constitutum* might have originally been a pious romance. However, this view is noted as being inconsistent with critical analysis of the text. Other scholars, such as L. Weiland, maintained that the *Constitutum* was a forgery created in Rome between 813 and 875 to support papal claims regarding the crowning of emperors. W. Mayer, in his 1881 work *Die romische Frage*, had previously placed the forgery in 805 or 806.

In the realm of religious biography, Thomas Edward Bridgett, a Roman Catholic priest, wrote on the history of the Reformation in England. His works include *History of the Eucharist in Great Britain* (1881), *The Life of Blessed John Fisher, Bishop of Rochester* (1888), and *The Life and Writings of Sir Thomas More* (1890).

## Industrial and Social History
The archive also records the history of specific crafts, such as bell-founding. Originally a monastic art, it became a professional trade. In England, early founders were often peripatetic, setting up temporary foundries. Notable founders include Christopher Hodson, who cast "Great Tom" of Oxford in 1681, and the firm Taylor & Co. of Loughborough, who founded "Great Paul" for St Paul's cathedral in 1881. The Whitechapel Bell Foundry, established by Robert Mot in 1570, eventually incorporated several other businesses, including those of the Rudhalls and Phelps.
