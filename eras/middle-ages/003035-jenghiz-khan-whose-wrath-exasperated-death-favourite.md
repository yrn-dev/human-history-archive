# Jenghiz Khan

Jenghiz Khan (also referred to as Chinghiz) was the supreme leader of the Mongols who initiated a vast series of conquests across Asia during the 13th century. His campaigns extended from the steppes of Central Asia to the borders of China and beyond.

## Military Campaigns and Conquests
Jenghiz Khan's military activities spanned several regions. In the 13th century, he conquered Kulja, and the Mongol Khans subsequently resided in the valley of the Ili. He also partially conquered Northern China, known as Cathay. These operations eventually led to the complete subjugation of the "golden" dynasty of Tatars, the Kin, who reigned at K'ai-feng Fu on the Yellow River, by 1234.

In the region of Tokharistan, Jenghiz Khan besieged the city of Bamian. The city was finally annihilated in 1222. This total destruction was prompted by the Khan's exasperated wrath following the death of a favourite grandson, who had been killed by an arrow fired from the city's walls.

## Administration and Influence
Under the sway of Jenghiz Khan, the people of the "Kirghiz steppes"—stretching from Lake Balkash to the lower Volga and from the river Irtish to the lower Oxus—came under Mongol rule. Following his death, this domain fell to his son Juji, the head of the Golden Horde, although the local khans continued to retain their own authority.

Jenghiz Khan's influence extended to religious and cultural spheres. In Tibet, the religion of Lamaism—a form of late Indian Buddhism—attained temporal power and developed into an ecclesiastical state, largely due to the favour shown to it by Jenghiz Khan and his successors.

## Succession and Family
Jenghiz Khan had four sons by his favourite wife. His third son, Okkodai (or Ogdai), succeeded him in the khanship in 1229. Okkodai was followed by his son Kuyuk in 1246, and then by Mangu in 1252. Mangu was the eldest son of Tule, who was the youngest of Jenghiz's four sons.

Kublai Khan, born in 1216, was the second son of Tule. Despite his youth, Kublai participated in Jenghiz's final campaign from 1226 to 1227, alongside his younger brother Hulagu. According to the Mongol poetical chronicler Sanang Setzen, Jenghiz Khan predicted Kublai's future distinction while on his death-bed.

## Death and Legacy
Traditions regarding the burial of Jenghiz Khan vary. One legend places his burial place in the Ordo country, 200 versts south of lake Dabasun Nor, where his remains are said to be kept in two coffins—one silver and one wood. A prophecy attributed to the Khan suggests that after eight or ten centuries, he would return to life to defeat the Emperor of China and lead the Mongols from the Ordos back to the country of Khalka.

Another tradition identifies the "holiest place" of the Mongols of Ordos as a collection of felt tents called "Edjen-joro," which are reputed to contain his bones. These relics are cared for by a caste of Darhats consisting of some fifty families. Every summer, on the twenty-first day of the sixth moon, people congregate for celebrations called *táilgan* to offer sacrifices in his honour. Additionally, the ruins of Boro-balgasun (Grey town) on the southern border of the Ordos are said to date from the time of Jenghiz Khan.
