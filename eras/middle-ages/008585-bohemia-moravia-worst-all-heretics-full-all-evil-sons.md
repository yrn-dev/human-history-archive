# Bohemia and Moravia

Bohemia is a kingdom and crownland of Austria, situated in the geographical center of the European continent. It is bounded to the northwest by Saxony, to the northeast by Prussian Silesia, to the south by Upper Austria, to the southwest by Bavaria, and to the southeast by Lower Austria and Moravia. Covering an area of approximately 20,060 square miles, it serves as a borderland between the Slavonic and German worlds and is the principal province of the Austrian empire.

## Early History and Christianization
The introduction of Christianity into Bohemia initially came from Germany, though these efforts saw little success due to an innate distrust of the Germans. The faith was more readily adopted when it arrived from Moravia, taught by Cyrillus and Methodius, Greek monks from Thessalonica. In approximately 873, the Bohemian prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius, leading to a rapid adoption of the faith.

Among the early rulers, Wenceslas (surnamed the Holy) is most famous; he was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized by the Church of Rome. Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, expanded the frontiers of the state. Boleslav II established rule over Bohemia, Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and the area of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. However, this empire did not endure; Boleslav III lost the foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland and faced internal civil wars with his brothers, Jaromir and Ulrick. This instability eventually led the Bohemians to dethrone Boleslav III in favor of the Polish prince Vladivoj, the son of Bohemian princess Dubravka.

## Medieval Expansion and Governance
By 1197, Premysl Ottakar became the undisputed ruler and was crowned king in 1198, beginning an uninterrupted line of royal titles. A notable successor, Premysl Ottakar II, expanded the realm significantly, obtaining the archduchies of Austria and the nobility of Styria. Following a victory over Bela, king of Hungary, at the battle of Kressenbrunn in 1260, his power reached its peak, extending to Carinthia, Istria, and parts of northern Italy. Known as "the man of gold" for his wealth and "the man of iron" for his military power, Ottakar encouraged German immigration to create a counterpoise to the power of the Bohemian nobility.

## Religious Conflict and the Hussite Wars
In the later reign of King Wenceslas, religious conflict emerged as the Church of Rome's hold on Bohemia weakened. Pious priests like Waldhauser and Milic attacked clerical immorality, and a church schism further alienated the population. Most of the Bohemian nation supported Huss, who served as the confessor to Queen Sophia. Although Wenceslas's brother, Sigismund, provided Huss with a letter of safe conduct to the council of Constance, Huss was declared a heretic and burnt on July 6, 1415.

This execution triggered a civil war. On September 2, 1415, the nobles of Moravia and Bohemia met in Prague and issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum*. In this document, they described Huss as a "good, just and catholic man" and asserted that those claiming heresy existed in Bohemia and Moravia were "liars, vile traitors and calumniators of Bohemia and Moravia, the worst of all heretics, full of all evil, sons of the devil." This protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church, initiating the Hussite wars.

The Hussites, led by Ziska, operated on extreme democratic lines. Pope Martin V issued a bull in 1420 proclaiming a crusade to destroy the Hussites and Wycliffites. Despite four failed crusades and the beleaguering of Prague, the movement eventually suffered from internal dissensions, allowing a fifth crusade under Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg, to cross the frontier in 1431. Later, George of Podebrad became the only Hussite king of Bohemia. Despite a struggle with the papal see and excommunication by Pope Paul II, George is remembered as one of the rulers most endeared to his countrymen.

## Later Political and National Struggles
Political instability continued into the 17th century. In 1608, a conflict arose between Rudolph and his brother Matthias. While the estates of Moravia renounced allegiance to Rudolph, the Bohemians initially declined the overtures of Matthias. Eventually, Rudolph ceded the government of Moravia, Hungary, and Austria to Matthias while retaining the kingship of Bohemia.

In more recent history, Bohemia became a center for nationalist movements. Over three-fifths of the population were Czech, and leaders such as Rieger and Palacky demanded the restoration of the Bohemian monarchy, including Silesia and Moravia. They insisted the emperor be crowned at Prague. This movement was supported by some German nobles, such as Prince Friedrich von Schwarzenberg and Count Leo Thun, who sought to preserve their power against German Liberals. In 1868, Czech leaders presented a declaration to the diet at Prague demanding full restoration of the kingdom and asserting that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia. This led to serious riots and the decree of exceptional laws by the government, with similar events occurring in Moravia.
