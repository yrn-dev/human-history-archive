# The Battle of Bannockburn and the Campaigns of Robert Bruce

## The Battle of Bannockburn
The Battle of Bannockburn took place on June 24, 1314, fought for the purpose of relieving Stirling Castle. The castle was under siege by Scottish forces led by Robert Bruce. The English governor of the castle had agreed to surrender the fortress if he was not relieved by that date. In response, Edward II quickly assembled an army from the midland and northern counties of England.

Robert Bruce made no attempt to defend the border, choosing instead a defensive position on the Bannock Burn, located 2-1/2 miles south of Stirling. Bruce utilized the geography of the area for protection: the marshy bed of the stream covered his front, a northerly bend toward the Forth protected his left flank, and a group of woods shielded his right. The Scottish army remained concealed behind these woods until the English appeared. To maintain observation, Bruce stationed two corps in the open: one at St Ninian's to monitor the lower course of the burn, and another to guard the crossing of the Falkirk-Stirling road.

## Initial Engagements and Forces
On June 23, the vanguard of Edward's army appeared south of the burn. The English forces numbered approximately 60,000, while the Scottish forces numbered 40,000. Edward dispatched two bodies of men toward Stirling. One group traveled via the direct road and was met by a Scottish outpost; during this encounter, Robert Bruce killed Sir Henry Bohun in a famous single combat, despite Bruce not being fully armed for battle. The second English corps took a route over the lower Bannock Burn near the Forth, where they were defeated by the second Scottish outpost near St Ninian's after a severe struggle.

## Scottish Defensive Strategy
The Scottish army took its assigned positions early on St John's day. The first line consisted of three corps of pikemen arranged in solid masses, kept hidden behind the crest until the English advanced in earnest. For additional frontal protection, a line of military pits, known as "pottes," had been dug across a front extending about one mile from wing to wing. 

Robert Bruce commanded the reserve, which included a corps of pikemen and a squadron of 500 chosen men-at-arms led by Sir Robert Keith, the marischal of Scotland. The defensive line was noted for being unusually dense. On the battlefield, the left wing was commanded by Walter the Steward and Sir James of Douglas, the latter of whom was knighted on the field.

## The Role of Sir James of Douglas
Sir James of Douglas, known as the "Good" and later the "Black Douglas," was a key ally of Robert Bruce, joining him before his 1306 coronation at Scone. After escaping the battle of Methven with Bruce and wandering the Highlands, Douglas operated in the south of Scotland. He is noted for destroying the English garrison at Douglas and for an event on March 19, 1307, known as the "Douglas Larder," characterized by excessive barbarities.

Douglas was entrusted with conducting the war in the south while Bruce focused on the Highlands. He conducted many successful raids on the English border and routed Sir John de Mowbray at Ederford Bridge. In 1314, he secured Teviotdale by capturing Roxburgh Castle through a stratagem in which the assailants were disguised as black oxen. Following Bannockburn, Douglas continued intermittent warfare for thirteen years, including the 1316 killing of Sir Robert de Nevill in single combat and a 1319 invasion of Yorkshire. In September 1319, he defeated an army led by William de Melton, archbishop of York, at Mitton-on-Swale in a fight called "The Chapter of Myton." In 1322, he forced an English retreat by capturing the pass of Byland. For these services, Bruce granted him the "Emerald Charter," providing criminal jurisdiction over family estates and releasing the lords of Douglas from various feudal obligations.

## Regional Context of the Borderlands
The county of Dumfriesshire, a Border county, was a site of significant activity during the era of Robert Bruce, who was the lord of Annandale. The region was marked by constant feuds between the factions of Bruce, John Baliol, and John Comyn, as well as strife among the hot-headed Border clans. Other notable figures associated with the region in later epochs include the Covenanters, who published a declaration against the king at Sanquhar in 1680, as well as Robert Burns and Thomas Carlyle.
