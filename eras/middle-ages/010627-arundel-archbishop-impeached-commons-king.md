# Thomas Arundel

Thomas Arundel (1353–1414) was the Archbishop of Canterbury and a prominent figure in the English church and state during the late 14th and early 15th centuries. The third son of Eleanor, daughter of Henry Plantagenet, earl of Lancaster, and Richard Fitzalan, earl of Arundel and Warenne, he belonged to an old and influential family.

## Ecclesiastical and Political Career
Arundel's rise within the church was rapid. He was appointed archdeacon of Taunton in 1373 and was consecrated as the bishop of Ely in April 1374. During the early years of King Richard II's reign, Arundel aligned himself with a political party that included his brother Richard, earl of Arundel, Henry, earl of Derby (the future King Henry IV), and Thomas, duke of Gloucester.

In 1386, Arundel and Gloucester were sent to Eltham to persuade King Richard II to return to parliament. Following the success of this mission, Arundel was appointed lord chancellor, replacing Michael de la Pole, duke of Suffolk. In this capacity, he helped facilitate peace between the king and those supporting the commission of regency. 

Arundel's career continued to advance with his appointment as archbishop of York in April 1388. When King Richard declared himself of age in 1389, Arundel resigned as chancellor, though he returned to the office in 1391. During his second term as chancellor, he attempted to move the courts of justice from London to York, but they were eventually returned to the metropolis. In September 1396, he was translated from York to Canterbury, resigning the chancellorship once more.

## Tenure as Archbishop of Canterbury
As archbishop of Canterbury, Arundel vigorously asserted his rights. He conducted a thorough visitation of his province, deciding appeals from the judgments of his suffragans, and warned the citizens of London against withholding tithes. In November 1396, he officiated at the marriage of King Richard II and Isabella, the daughter of King Charles VI of France.

Arundel also exercised judicial authority in religious matters. He presided over a court in St Paul, London, which condemned John Badby, an early Lollard martyr and tailor (or blacksmith) from the west Midlands. Badby had been condemned for denying transubstantiation, arguing that Christ did not have his body in his hand to distribute at supper and claiming that if every consecrated host were the Lord's body, there would be 20,000 Gods in England. Badby was subsequently burned at Smithfield.

## Impeachment and Fall from Power
Arundel's downfall occurred as a result of King Richard II's sudden attack on the lords appellant in 1397. This period was marked by a conspiracy organized at Arundel Castle by the archbishop, the duke of Gloucester, and the earl of Arundel to murder the lords of the king's council and dethrone Richard II; however, the plot was discovered before it could be executed.

Following the arrests of Warwick, Gloucester, and Arundel, the archbishop was impeached by the Commons with the consent of the king. Although King Richard initially held out hopes of safety for him, Arundel was charged with assisting in the procurement of the commission of regency in derogation of royal authority. He was sentenced to banishment and given forty days to leave the realm.
