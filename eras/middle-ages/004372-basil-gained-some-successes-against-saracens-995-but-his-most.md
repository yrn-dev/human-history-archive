# The Emperors and Tsars Named Basil

The name Basil is associated with several prominent rulers across the Roman (Byzantine) and Muscovite empires, as well as a significant influence in early Christian monasticism.

## Basil I (The Macedonian)
Basil I, known as the "Macedonian," was a Roman emperor in the East of Armenian descent. Born into a family settled in Macedonia, he spent part of his youth in captivity in Bulgaria after being carried away by Prince Krum in 813. After escaping, he entered the service of Theophilitzes, a relative of Caesar Bardas, as a groom. Basil eventually gained the favor of a wealthy lady named Danielis and later the attention of Emperor Michael III, who appointed him chamberlain (*parakoemenos*) after Basil won a wrestling match.

Basil's rise to power was marked by unscrupulous actions; he divorced his wife to marry Eudocia Ingerina, a mistress of Michael III, to please the emperor. In April 866, with the emperor's consent, Basil murdered Caesar Bardas and was raised to imperial dignity. Shortly thereafter, in September 867, he had Michael III assassinated to reign alone.

As emperor, Basil founded the Macedonian (or Armenian) dynasty, a period characterized by territorial expansion and the revival of Justinianean law. Though not naturally a warrior, he formed a new army through discipline and exercise. He led expeditions to the banks of the Euphrates to curb the Saracens and suppressed a revolt by the Manichaeans. He notably pursued a rebel named Chrysochir, whose head was eventually suspended from a tree and shot three times by the Imperial archer.

## Basil II
Basil II, the son of Romanus, ascended to power after a twelve-year regency during which he and his brother Constantine were pupils of a minister who encouraged them to avoid the labors of government. While Constantine remained weak, Basil developed a desire for action and eventually removed the minister.

Upon taking power, Basil first dealt with domestic enemies in Asia—two veteran generals, Phocas and Sclerus. Phocas was killed in battle, and Sclerus eventually surrendered in peace. Basil's reign was marked by frequent expeditions against the Saracens and the annexation of Armenian principalities, where he established a fortified frontier intended to block Seljuk Turk invasions.

His most significant achievement was the subjugation of Bulgaria. After initial failures in 981 and a period where Tsar Samuel extended Bulgarian rule to Servia and the Adriatic coast, Basil resumed the war in 996. Eastern Bulgaria was recovered in 1000, and in 1014, the Bulgarian army suffered a massive defeat. Basil blinded 15,000 prisoners, leaving one man with one eye for every hundred to lead them back to Tsar Samuel; the Tsar died two days after seeing them. By 1018, Bulgarian resistance ended. Basil died in December 1025 while preparing a naval expedition to recover Sicily from the Saracens.

## Basil III Ivanovich
Basil III (1479–1533) was the Tsar of Muscovy and son of Ivan III and Sophia Palaeologa. He succeeded his father in 1505. A crafty ruler, he incorporated the remaining independent principalities into Muscovy by accusing the princes of Syeversk and Ryazan of conspiracy and annexing their lands between 1517 and 1523. In 1510, he deprived the republic of Pskov of its charter and assembly-bell.

In 1512, with the help of the rebel Lithuanian Prince Michael Glinsky, Basil captured the Polish fortress of Smolensk. He also managed the threat of the Tatars; although he had to buy off the Crimean khan Mahommed Girai in 1519, he later established Russian influence on the Volga and placed the pretender Elanyei on the throne of Kazan in 1530. Basil III was the first grand-duke of Moscow to adopt the title of tsar and the double-headed eagle of the East Roman empire. He was succeeded by his son, Ivan IV.

## Basilian Monasticism
Separate from the political rulers, St. Basil's Rules influenced the development of monasticism. Translated into Latin by Rufinus around 400, these rules informed certain Italian monasteries and served as a primary foundation for the Rule of St. Benedict. Greek monasteries in Rome, south Italy, and Sicily appeared in the 6th and 7th centuries. Between the 9th and 10th centuries, Greek-speaking monks fleeing Saracenic and Arab invasions established numerous Basilian monasteries in Calabria and as far north as Rome. One such survivor is the monastery founded by St. Nilus around 1000 at Grottaferrata.
