# Gloucester and the Berkeleys

The early history of Bristol and its architectural heritage is closely linked to the influence of several prominent baronial families, most notably the earls of Gloucester and the Berkeleys. These families, along with later merchants such as the Framptons, Shipwards, and Canyngs, were primarily responsible for the collection of ecclesiastical architectural antiquities found in the center of the city.

## Land Ownership and the Manor of Bedminster
During the reign of King John, the city of Bristol sought to absorb the neighboring manor of Bedminster. This manor was divided into two halves. The eastern portion had been given to the Templars by Earl Robert of Gloucester. The western portion, known as Redcliffe, was sold by the same earl to Robert FitzHardinge, who later became Lord Berkeley. 

While the Templars did not offer significant resistance to the union with Bristol, the owners of the manor of Redcliffe resisted for nearly a century. This resistance was bolstered by the fact that the owners of Redcliffe possessed their own quay, fair, market, and manorial courts.

## Political Influence and Conflict
The Berkeleys maintained a significant presence in the political struggles of the region. During the great insurrection of 1312, which was caused by the growing exclusiveness of the merchant gild, the Berkeleys provided support to the oligarchical party. This period of rebellion continued until 1313, when royal forces besieged and captured the town.

## Architectural and Funerary Legacy
The influence of these families is preserved in the city's ecclesiastical structures. The cathedral in Bristol—the church of the abbey of St Augustine founded by Fitz-Harding in 1142—contains canopied tombs of the Berkeleys. These tombs feature effigies depicted in chain mail. 

Other notable architectural remnants of the period include the abbot's gateway, the vestibule to the chapter-house, and the chapter-house itself. The latter is described as one of the finest Norman chambers in Europe, characterized by Byzantine exuberance in its decoration. Additionally, the stately entrance gateway of the abbey remains largely uninjured, featuring sculptured mouldings.
