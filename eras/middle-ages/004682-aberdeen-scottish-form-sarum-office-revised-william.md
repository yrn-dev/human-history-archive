# Aberdeen Breviary

The Aberdeen Breviary is a Scottish form of the Sarum Office. It was revised by William Elphinstone, who served as bishop from 1483 to 1514. The text was printed in Edinburgh between 1509 and 1510 by Andrew Myllar and Walter Chapman.

## History and Distribution
Although a royal mandate was issued in 1501 for the Aberdeen Breviary to be used generally throughout the realm of Scotland, it is probable that the work was never widely adopted. From a bibliographical perspective, early printed Breviaries are considered rare literary curiosities because they were often local in nature. Such copies were not distributed far and were frequently worn out through daily use. Consequently, many editions have vanished entirely, while others are known only through unique surviving copies.

The Aberdeen Breviary is the only such work in Scotland to have survived the convulsions of the 16th century. Only four copies of the original printing have been preserved, and of these, only one is complete. In 1854, the duke of Buccleuch provided the munificence to have the work reprinted in facsimile for the Bannatyne Club.

## Content and Significance
The Aberdeen Breviary is regarded as particularly valuable due to the trustworthy notices of early Scottish history embedded within the lives of national saints. 

The work is a variation of the Sarum (or Salisbury) Breviary, a text that saw wide use with editions printed in Venice as early as 1483 and in Paris as late as 1557. While modern Breviaries are typically printed in four volumes—one for each season—Sarum editions never exceeded two parts. The general structure of such Breviaries typically includes introductory matter (such as the calendar, general rubrics, and tables for determining the date of Easter) and is divided into four seasonal parts: winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Each seasonal part comprises the Psalter, the *Proprium de Tempore* (special office of the season), the *Proprium Sanctorum* (special offices of saints), the *Commune Sanctorum* (general offices for saints), and Extra Services.

The influence of the Aberdeen Breviary persisted into the 20th century; the new Scottish *Proprium* sanctioned in 1903 for the Roman Catholic province of St Andrews contains many of the antiphons and collects from the old Aberdeen version.
