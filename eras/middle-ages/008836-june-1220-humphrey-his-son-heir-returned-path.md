# Humphrey V de Bohun

Humphrey V de Bohun was a prominent English nobleman and member of the Bohun family, who held a significant position in English history during the 13th and 14th centuries. The family originated from a village in the Cotentin, located between Coutances and the estuary of the Vire, and arrived in England at or shortly after the Norman Conquest.

## Family Lineage and Inheritance
Humphrey V was the son and heir of Henry de Bohun. Henry had obtained the earldom of Hereford from John in 1199, an acquisition likely aided by his mother Margaret, who was the sister of William the Lion and thus connected to the royal house of Scotland. The Bohun family estates were primarily situated on the Welsh Marches, establishing the family as leading Marcher barons.

Humphrey V's grandfather, Humphrey III, had served as a *dapifer* (steward) in the royal household during the later years of Henry I. Humphrey III had married the daughter of Milo of Gloucester and maintained loyalty to Henry II, fighting against the rebels of East Anglia at Farnham in 1173. Humphrey III outlived his eldest son, Humphrey IV, which led to the family estates passing to his grandson, Henry.

## Political Allegiances and Career
The political trajectory of Humphrey V was marked by shifts in loyalty, mirroring the patterns of his father. Henry de Bohun had been one of the twenty-five barons elected to enforce the terms of the Great Charter. However, during the subsequent civil war, Henry fought for Louis and was captured at the battle of Lincoln in 1217. Following his capture, Henry took the cross and died on his pilgrimage on June 1, 1220.

Upon his father's death, Humphrey V returned to the path of loyalty. As a result, some time before 1239, he was permitted to inherit the earldom of Essex from his maternal uncle, William de Mandeville.

Despite this period of loyalty, Humphrey V later shifted his allegiance again in 1258, moving from the royal cause to the baronial cause. During the Oxford parliament of 1258, he served as a nominee of the opposition on the committee of twenty-four tasked with reforming the administration. Humphrey V eventually returned to his allegiance to the crown, a shift prompted by the alliance between Montfort and Llewelyn of North Wales.
