# The Evolution of Baronial Status and the Statements of Camden

## Transition of Baronial Status
Historically, the position of a baron was linked to the tenure of land. In theoretical terms, barons were individuals who held their lands from the king; in practical application, this status applied to those who held a large quantity of land. A significant change occurred when presence in the council of the realm—which later became the House of Lords—was no longer determined by land tenure. Instead, it became dependent upon the king's will, specifically through the issuance of a writ of summons.

## The Debate on Timing and Attribution
There has been historical disagreement regarding when this shift in status took place. William Camden stated that this change was implemented by Henry III following the "Barons' War." While this assertion was widely accepted for a long period, modern historical attribution, as noted by Stubbs, assigns the change to Edward I. This is supported by the fact that the earliest writs recognized as creating hereditary baronies were issued during the reign of Edward I.

## Development of Titles and Styles
Despite the change in how baronies were created, those receiving summonses were not immediately distinguished from commoners by a specific title or style. During this era, the prefix *Dominus* (lord) was used regularly by simple knights. Furthermore, writs of summons continued to be issued to the lowest order of peers using only the style of knights (*chevaliers*).

The specific style of "baron" was not introduced until 1387 by Richard II. In that year, Richard II used a patent to create John de Beauchamp as "Lord de Beauchamp and baron of Kidderminster," designating him as *unum parium et baronum regni nostri*. This was followed by the creation of Sir John Cornwall as the baron of Fanhope in 1433.

## Peerage Styles under Henry VI and Beyond
Under the reign of Henry VI, such creations became common, marking a transition period for peerage styles. However, the term "Baron" did not replace existing styles such as "Sire," "Chevalier," and "Dominus." While patents of creation utilized the formula "Lord A. (and) Baron of B.," the individuals granted these titles continued to style themselves simply as "Lord." 

This historical preference persists in modern correspondence, where a baron is addressed as "the Lord A." rather than "Baron A." or "Baron B.," the latter of which is described as a recent and unhistorical practice. It is noted that while all peers below the rank of Duke are spoken of as "lords," they are addressed in correspondence by their specific proper styles.

## William Camden
William Camden is identified as an antiquary and historian. In addition to his statements on the baronial changes under Henry III, Camden's work is cited in various contexts, including his *Annales* and his mentions of the famous horse fairs in Biggleswade. Camden also assisted William Cecil, Baron Burghley, in elaborating pedigrees that attempted to trace Cecil's descent from a Sitsyllt of the reign of Rufus and an Owen from the time of King Harold. 

In his personal interactions, Camden is noted to have been the ex-head-master of Westminster. He maintained a distance from the scholar Casaubon, possibly due to silent resentment after Casaubon had corrected Camden's Greek.
