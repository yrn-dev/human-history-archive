# The Folding of Vellum and the Evolution of the Book

The transition from the roll to the bound book involved several distinct methods of arranging and securing writing materials, including papyrus, paper, and vellum. One of the most significant developments in this evolution was the method of folding vellum into pages.

## The Folding of Vellum
The practice of folding vellum into pages appears to have first been followed around the 5th century. In this process, sheets of vellum were folded once. These folded sheets were then organized into gatherings of four or more. To secure the structure, stitches were passed through the fold at the back, holding all the sheets together and allowing each leaf to be turned over conveniently.

To fix several of these gatherings, known as quires, together, a simple method was employed: the threads at the back were fastened around a strong strip of vellum or leather that was held at right angles to the line of the backs. This specific technique of sewing books is referred to as "flexible" work. It is still utilized today for valuable books and is considered a method that has never been improved upon.

## The Roll and the Orihon
Before the prevalence of folded vellum, rolls of paper, vellum, or papyrus were used. These were written upon in three primary ways:
1. Short lines written at right angles to the length of the roll.
2. Long lines spanning the entire length of the roll (an instance of which is found in Buddhist prayer-wheels).
3. Short lines parallel to the length of the roll, where each page or column of writing had a space left on either side.

The third method of arranging manuscripts provided a link between the roll and the book. Because of the vacant spaces between columns, these rolls could be folded backwards and forwards. This created a form known as *orihon* in Japanese and Chinese, where writing appears on one side of the strip and the other side remains blank. 

These *orihon* books were sometimes guarded by two boards or fastened along one side, which became the back. The earliest fastening for this style involved a lacing of fibre or cord run through holes stabbed through the substance of the roll near the edge. This "stabbed" form of binding is the earliest method for keeping book leaves together; it is seen in a Coptic papyrus from approximately the 8th century found at Thebes, though papyrus is often too brittle to retain such threads.

## Technical Dimensions and Folding
In the technical terminology of bookbinding, the "size" of a book refers to the relationship between individual leaves and the original sheet of paper. The number of leaves is determined by the number of folds:
* **Folio**: The paper is folded once, creating two leaves.
* **Quarto**: The paper is folded twice, creating four leaves.
* **Octavo**: An additional fold produces eight leaves.
* **16mo, 32mo, and 64mo**: Further folds continue to increase the number of leaves.

For books in sizes such as twelves or twenty-fours, the paper must be folded in three at some stage. In the early 16th century, a 12mo sheet was folded so the height formed two pages and the width was divided into six. Later habits changed this so the height formed the width of four pages and the width formed the height of three pages.

## Materials and Decoration
Vellum, along with papyrus and parchment, served as a primary material for manuscripts. In the earliest vellum manuscripts, independent decoration appeared in primitive forms, such as scrolls, dotted patterns, cables, and cross-hatchings at the head or end of the text, sometimes featuring simple domestic objects or birds. 

Further decorative enhancements included the use of gold or coloured inks for the initial lines or the entire text, as well as the gilding and staining of vellum with purple. While these practices enhanced the decorative aspect, they are categorized as concerns of the material rather than the artistic element of the manuscript.
