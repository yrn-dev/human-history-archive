# THE KINGDOM OF MAUSUL

## Geography and Demographics
The kingdom of Mausul is located on the south-east frontier of Armenia. It is described as a very great kingdom inhabited by several distinct groups of people. Among these are the Arabi, who worship Mahommet, and a population of Jacobite and Nestorian Christians. 

Additionally, the mountains in this region are inhabited by a race of people known as the Curds. The Curds are divided by faith, with some being Christians and others Saracens; they are characterized as an evil generation that finds delight in plundering merchants.

## Religious Administration
The Nestorian and Jacobite Christians of Mausul are led by a Patriarch referred to as the Jatolic. Similar to the Pope of Rome in Latin countries, the Jatolic creates Prelates, Abbots, and Archbishops, whom he dispatches to various regions, including Cathay, Baudas, and India. While there are many Christians in these distant lands, they are all Nestorians and Jacobites; they are noted as being Christians, though they differ from the fashion enjoined by the Pope of Rome and fall short in several points of the Faith.

## Commerce and Industry
Mausul is the center of production for silk and gold cloths known as Mosolins. The kingdom is also home to the Mosolins, great merchants who trade in large quantities of pearls, spicery, and cloths of gold and silk.

Near this province lie Mus and Merdin. These areas produce immense quantities of cotton, which are used to manufacture other cloth and a great deal of buckram. The inhabitants of Mus and Merdin are traders and craftsmen, and all are subject to the Tartar King.

## Political History and Governance
The political status of the kingdom has been subject to historical transition. Badruddín Lúlú, the last Atabeg of Mosul from the race of Zenghi, maintained a high standing with Hulaku at the age of 96. However, his son, Malik Sálih, revolted and surrendered to the Mongols in 1261. Although he was promised his life, the Mongols tortured him to death, and the kingdom ceased to exist as such following these events.

Coins from the era of Badruddín feature the titles and name of Mangku Kaan on the reverse. Some coins from Badruddín and other atabegs are noted for their curious imitations of Greek art. In later centuries, the region faced further instability; Mosul was pillaged by Timur at the end of the 14th century, fell to the Turkomans during the 15th century, and was taken by Ismail, Shah of Persia, during the 16th century.

## Relations with Baudas
Historical accounts describe a "great marvel" involving the relationship between Mausul and Baudas. A Calif in Baudas held a deep hatred for Christians and sought counsel from his priests and devotees on how to either convert the Christians in his kingdom to his own faith or have them all slain. This animosity is presented as reflective of a broader, malignant disposition of the body of Saracens throughout the world toward the body of Christians.
