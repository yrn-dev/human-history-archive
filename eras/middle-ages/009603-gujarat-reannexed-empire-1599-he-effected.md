# Gujarat

Gujarat is a province in western India characterized by its rich alluvial plains and a complex history of shifting imperial dominations. Geographically, the region is defined by the Tapti and Nerbudda rivers, which water its highly cultivated plains. Its western boundary is formed by the Runn or Lake of Cutch, a feature that varies by season between a salt marsh, an inland lake, or an arm of the sea; during rains, it can unite the Gulfs of Cutch and Cambay, turning Cutch into an island. The geology of the region includes alluvium and wind-blown sand in eastern Gujarat, while the Kathiawar peninsula and Cutch are characterized by horizontal lava flows of the Deccan Trap series, underlaid by Neocomian and Jurassic beds.

## Early History and Ancient Dynasties
In the earliest recorded times, the west coast of India was occupied by Dravidian tribes. By 1000 B.C., export trade existed with the Red Sea via East Africa, and by 750 B.C., trade with Babylon via the Persian Gulf introduced the legend of the Flood, the art of brick-making, and the Brahmi alphabet. The region was later Aryanized in language and religion by the settlement of Brahmans.

Under the Maurya empire, Gujarat, Malwa, and Kathiawar were ruled by a viceroy based in Ujjain. Following the collapse of the Mauryas after 231 B.C., the area fell to the Andhra dynasty of the Satavahanas of Paithan. By approximately A.D. 210, the Kshaharatas (Saka satraps of Surashtra) took control, mastering Gujarat and Ujjain. Trade with the Roman empire enriched coastal cities from A.D. 40 until the extinction of the main Kshaharata dynasty around 300.

Between A.D. 500 and 770, a dynasty founded by a chief named Bhatarka existed at Valabhi in Kathiawar. Around A.D. 600, the Gurjaras—believed to be ancestors of the Gujar caste—established power over Gujarat and north-eastern Kathiawar, making the raja of Valabhi a tributary and establishing a branch at Broach. Gujarat also fell under the sway of Harsha until his death in 647 or 648.

## Medieval Transitions and Conflict
The 8th century saw the arrival of Arab invaders from Sind, who destroyed the city of Valabhi in 770, ending Bhatarka's dynasty. While the Arabs conducted raids, the region remained largely under the influence of the Gurjaras and the Rashtrakutas. For two centuries (c. 750–950), a balance of power existed between these two kingdoms, with the Rashtrakutas establishing a branch line in Gujarat.

Mussulman domination began with the 1023 invasion by Mahmud of Ghazni, who destroyed the national Hindu idol of Somnath and took immense booty. Mahommed Ghori also invaded the region, leaving a garrison in the capital. In 1297, the emperor Ala-ud-din conquered Gujarat. By the middle of the 14th century, the weakness of the Delhi sovereigns led provincial governors to revolt, resulting in the establishment of an independent kingdom in Gujarat.

## Imperial Reannexation and European Influence
The Mughal emperor Akbar conquered Gujarat in 1573, reannexing it to the empire. During the latter part of the 17th century, the Mahrattas rose to power, eventually bringing almost every part of the Bombay presidency under their sway.

Simultaneously, European powers began establishing coastal footholds. The Portuguese, having captured Goa in 1510, took advantage of the decay of the Gujarat kingdom to occupy several sites: Chaul (1531), Bassein and Bombay (1534), Diu (1535), and Daman (1559). Notably, the islands of Bombay had been part of the province or kingdom of Gujarat until 1534, when Sultan Bahadur ceded them to the Portuguese.

## Demographics, Language, and Culture
Hinduism is the predominant religion in Gujarat, although the region retains the influence of previous Mahommedan kingdoms. The primary languages spoken in the province are Gujarati and Hindustani. 

Linguistically, Gujarati is closely related to languages to its north, such as those spoken in Delhi, Jaipur, and Marwar. However, it possesses a sharp linguistic frontier with Marathi to the south. Native accounts indicate that while Gujarati speakers easily understand those from the north, they do not comprehend the Marathi spoken by people from Maharashtra or the Konkan.

Within the Gujarat province of Bombay lies the native state of Baroda. This state consists of four isolated divisions: Kadi, Baroda, and Nausari in Gujarat proper, and Amreli with Okhamandal in the Kathiawar peninsula. In 1901, Baroda had a population of 1,952,692, though this reflected a 19% decrease over the previous decade due to epidemics of fever and cholera, the plague, and famines occurring between 1896 and 1901.
