# Vienna

Vienna serves as the capital of Lower Austria and is the central hub for the province's extensive commerce and industrial activity. Situated within the watershed of the Danube, the city is a primary center for various industries, including the production of musical and physical instruments, textiles, chemicals, milling, brewing, and the manufacture of sugar, tobacco, and foodstuffs. Other industrial outputs include leather, paper, silk, cloth, and *objets de luxe*.

## Historical Development
The origins of the region are linked to Otto the Great, who is regarded as the real founder of Austria following his victory over the Magyars at the Lechfeld in August 955. In 976, Emperor Otto II entrusted the government of the East Mark to Leopold of the Babenberg family. Under the rule of Leopold and his descendants, the mark was developed through the colonization of lands along the Danube valley. This expansion led to the relocation of the capital from Poechlarn to Melk, then to Tulln, and finally to Vienna around 1140.

The Babenbergs continued to expand the territory, incorporating lands as far as the Leitha. In 1156, the district between the Inn and the Enns was added, and King Frederick I raised Austria to the rank of a duchy. This status brought exceptional privileges, including the right for the duchy to descend to females if male heirs failed, or for the duke to nominate a successor. The Babenberg family ruled until 1246.

## Urban Infrastructure and Geography
Vienna is located near the Danube, and significant engineering projects were undertaken in the 19th century to improve the river's utility and protect the city. Beginning in 1866, an artificial channel 10 miles long and 330 yards wide was constructed at a cost of over three million sterling. This project involved the removal of 12,000,000 cubic meters of sand and gravel, which successfully protected Vienna from disastrous inundations and improved navigability.

The city is also connected to other regional centers; for example, Budapest is located 163 miles southeast of Vienna by rail. Additionally, the town of Baden, situated 17 miles south of Vienna by rail, serves as the principal summer resort for Vienna's inhabitants. The Vienna waterworks are supported by a magnificent aqueduct that crosses the valley near Baden.

## Political and Social History
In the 19th century, Vienna was the seat of government for the house of Habsburg. During the mid-century, the city was a focal point for national aspirations and political tension. In 1848, the estates of Bohemia opposed the government in Vienna, demanding that the Bohemian language be granted equal rights with German in government offices and requesting a general diet elected on extensive suffrage. While a deputation in Vienna received an equivocal answer, Emperor Ferdinand declared on April 8 that equality of rights for both nationalities in Bohemia would be secured.

During the revolutionary periods, Vienna also saw internal ideological conflicts. German revolutionists in the city viewed the military successes of Radetzky in Italy as victories for "Germanism." Conversely, the radicals in Vienna were seen as enemies by various Slav nationalities, who consequently aligned themselves with the imperial government.

In a social context, Vienna has been characterized as the "Mecca of the Jew-baiter," contrasting with the atmosphere in Budapest, where the Magyar upper classes and the Hebrew element collaborated more closely in the city's commercial and industrial development.
