# Resurrection

The concept of resurrection and the events associated with the Day of Resurrection are detailed in sacred texts and historical accounts of various religious sects, ranging from early Christian movements and medieval dissident groups to the teachings of the Qur'an.

## The Day of Resurrection in the Qur'an

According to the Qur'an, the Day of Resurrection (also referred to as the Day of Judgment) is the time when individuals are held accountable for their earthly actions and falsehoods. It is described as the day when the Trumpet is blown, and the guilty are gathered, appearing "blue-eyed" or "white-eyed" with terror. Upon this day, those who turn away from the Message or dispute concerning Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating book will bear a burden.

The texts teach that on the Day of Resurrection:
*   **Accountability:** Individuals will be questioned regarding what they forged or invented. Those who believed they could have others bear their wrongs are described as liars; instead, they will bear their own burdens and additional burdens along with them.
*   **Judgment of the Unjust:** Those who served as leaders inviting others to the Fire will find no help or assistance. Those who were cursed in this world will be among the hateful or those made to appear hideous.
*   **Punishment:** Those who haughtily led others astray from the way of Allah will be made to taste the punishment of burning fire.
*   **Social Dissolution:** In the case of those who worshipped idols due to mutual love and friendship in the worldly life, the Qur'an teaches that on the resurrection day, they will disown and curse one another, with the Fire as their abode.

## The "Sons and Daughters of the Resurrection"

In the history of the early church, the term "Sons and daughters of the Resurrection" was used in the Syriac church as late as 340 to describe specific relations between individuals. This was linked to a "rage for virginity" and the institution of *virgines subintroductae*, or spiritual wives. 

The underlying assumption of this practice was that the grace of baptism could restore the original purity of life that Adam and Eve shared in common before the Fall. Such rigors were encouraged in the *Shepherd of Hermas* and were present in the African churches, the Celtic church of St Patrick, and among Celtic elders in northern France into the 7th century. These customs were generally condemned by early church councils, alongside the practice of self-emasculation. In the Catholic church, those seeking the Encratite ideal were directed toward monasteries.

## The Albigenses and Materiality

The Albigenses, also known as Cathars, were a dissident sect in the south of France and Bulgaria whose tenets were an extreme form of Manichaeism. They believed in two gods: a good god (whose son was Christ) and an evil god (whose son was Satan). Because they believed matter was the creation of the evil principle, they viewed it as essentially evil.

This abhorrence of the material world led to several strict prohibitions:
*   **Sexual Intercourse:** Regarded as the greatest of all sins, even within marriage, which they termed the "greater adultery" (*maius adulterium*).
*   **Material Goods:** The possession of material goods and the eating of flesh meat were considered sinful.
*   **Extreme Acts:** Some adherents believed it was an act of religion to commit suicide via voluntary starvation or to starve children to death.

The Albigenses continued the practice of spiritual wives. This served a dual purpose: it allowed elders to demonstrate their chastity and helped them elude inquisitors, as a man cohabiting with a woman appeared less likely to be a follower of a sect that viewed marriage as adultery.

## Suppression of the Albigenses

The Albigenses viewed themselves as devout Christians and lived lives of conspicuous virtue and purity, though they questioned the doctrinal soundness of Rome and the interpretation of the Bible. They believed Jesus was a rebel against the cruelty of the God of the Old Testament rather than his harmonious son.

Due to the destructive nature of their tenets toward Catholicism and civil society, efforts were made to suppress them. Emperor Frederick II attempted to suppress kindred sects in Italy. In 1208, following the murder of a papal legate, Pope Innocent III called upon Christian princes to suppress the Albigensian heresy by force. This resulted in a seven-year war in the south of France characterized by the slaughter of thousands of Albigenses and the wholesale confiscation of their property.
