# France, Spain and the Levant: The Records of the Society of Merchant Venturers

## The Society of Merchant Venturers and Bristol Trade
The Society of Merchant Venturers was founded during a period of significant prosperity for the city of Bristol. The records of the society began in 1467, and the organization experienced a rapid increase in influence. By the year 1500, the society directed all of the city's foreign trade and held a lease of the port dues. The society was later incorporated in 1552, with subsequent charters granted in 1638 and 1662.

During this era, Bristol merchants exported cloth to several key regions, specifically France, Spain, and the Levant. This commercial activity occurred alongside the huge cloth trade with the Baltic conducted by William Canyng (1399-1474), who served as mayor five times and represented Bristol in parliament twice. The further development of trade was aided by voyages of discovery, including those of Sebastian and John Cabot. By the 16th century, Bristol's trade expanded to include the Canaries, Spain, and Spanish colonies in America. Additionally, Bristol participated in attempts to colonize Newfoundland and initiated a trade in African slaves that flourished throughout the 17th century.

## Economic and Political Dynamics in Spain
Spain's rise to global prominence was sudden and memorable. From the 11th century, the peninsula had been divided, with its Christian population in perpetual conflict with the Moors. Spain achieved unity in time to benefit from the discovery of America, which allowed it to dominate the world for a century through a monopoly on American silver and gold. Despite this period of dominance, Spain has historically been a poor country, with its primary wealth residing in its mines.

The administration of Spanish trade with America was managed by the Casa de Contratacion, or governing board of the American trade. Pedro Menendez de Aviles (1519-1574), a Spanish seaman, served as the captain-general of the "flota" (the convoy carrying trade between Spain and America) starting in 1554. While Aviles was approved by Emperor Charles V for his success in bringing back treasure, he became unpopular with merchants and the Casa de Contratacion due to his high-handed nature and his refusal to accept bribes to permit breaches of regulations.

## European Trade and the Shift from the Levant
Historically, the Levant served as a center of interest for European history. However, the discovery of America—which was itself a consequence of Turkish conquests—shifted the center of European interest from the Alps and the Mediterranean Sea toward the Atlantic. This shift was influenced by Mongolian inventions such as paper and the compass, as well as a growing knowledge of Asiatic civilization and wealth. Following Portugal and Spain, France, England, and later Holland took up the role of overseas empire and expansion. Consequently, for several centuries, Central Asia, China, and the Turkish Empire were relatively neglected by European historians.

## Commercial Regulations in France and Spain
The conduct of business in France and Spain was influenced by specific legislative requirements regarding book-keeping. In France, the Code of Commerce required traders to maintain three specific books: a letter book, an inventory, and a journal. The compulsory nature of the journal was noted to make the use of numerous first-entry books impossible, which contributed to a comparatively backward state of book-keeping methods.

In Spain, the Commercial Code required the maintenance of five different records: a ledger, a journal, an invoice book, a letter book, and an inventory.

## Cultural and Material Exchanges
Trade and conflict between these regions led to various cultural exchanges. In the 13th and 14th centuries, Venice served as a primary center for distributing Oriental goods, including carpets, throughout western Europe. In Spain, carpets were manufactured by Moors or Saracens in the southern part of the country; these were brought to England by Queen Eleanor of Castile and her suite in the latter part of the 13th century. In France, the *Livre des metiers* (1258-1268) recorded "tapicers" or makers of *tapis sarrasinois*, who produced work exclusively for nobles, kings, or churches.
