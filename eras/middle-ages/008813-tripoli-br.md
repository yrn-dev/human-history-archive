# Tripoli

The name Tripoli refers to multiple historical and geographical entities, including a county in Syria and a city in North Africa.

## The County of Tripoli
The County of Tripoli was a territory in Syria whose history was closely linked with the Principality of Antioch. From the reign of Bohemund IV, the history of the Bohemunds encompassed both the principality and the county. Bohemund IV became count of Tripoli in 1187 and later succeeded his father in Antioch in 1201. During his rule, Bohemund IV was supported by the Templars, who, along with the Knights of St John, held estates in Tripoli. He was a determined enemy of the Hospitallers, which led to his excommunication in 1230, though he was released from this by Gregory IX before his death in 1233.

Bohemund V succeeded his father in 1233 as both prince of Antioch and count of Tripoli. He resided primarily at Tripoli, leaving the governance of Antioch to a commune and its bailiff. Bohemund V maintained an alliance with the Templars and faced hostility from Armenia until 1251, when peace was achieved through the mediation of St Louis and the marriage of Bohemund VI to the sister of the Armenian king.

Bohemund VI succeeded his father in 1251. After being knighted by St Louis in 1252, he established himself in Antioch and left Tripoli to its own administration. Following the loss of Antioch to Bibars in 1268, Bohemund VI held only the title of count of Tripoli until his death in 1275. He was succeeded by his son, Bohemund VII, with his mother, Sibylla, serving as regent. Bohemund VII's reign was troubled by conflicts with the Templars established in Tripoli. In 1287, the year of his death, he lost Laodicea to the sultan of Egypt. Because Tripoli was captured within two years of his death, and he died without issue, the county of Tripoli is considered to have become extinct with him.

## Political and Literary Connections
Tripoli's leadership occasionally intersected with the government of Jerusalem. Raymund of Tripoli served as regent for Baldwin V, the nominal king of Jerusalem from 1183 to 1186. This period was characterized by the advance of Saladin and internal dissensions between the government and Guy of Lusignan.

In the realm of literature, Friar William of Tripoli is noted as an author. Born circa 1220 in Tripoli, Syria, he served at the Dominican convent at Acre as early as 1250. He is credited with writing *De Statu Saracenorum post Ludovici Regis de Syriâ reditum* (also referred to as *de Egressu Machometi et Saracenorum, atque progressu eorumdem, de Statu Saracenorum*), dedicated to Theoldus, the Archdeacon of Liège. He is also ascribed a tract titled *Clades Damiatae*.

## Tripoli in North Africa
In North Africa, the city of Oea flourished under the provincial name of Tripoli. 

In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, Tripoli served as a point of departure and return for European explorers. In 1799, the German traveller Frederick Hornemann visited the city of Tripoli from Murzuk, using it as a location to dispatch his journals to London in August of that year. 

In 1821, the British government sent an expedition via Tripoli to the central Sudan, including Dr Oudney and Hugh Clapperton. Dixon Denham joined this expedition at Murzuk. Denham later traveled to Marseilles to complain about the "duplicity" of the pasha of Tripoli regarding the failure to provide a promised escort. After the pasha sent messengers with promises to meet his demands, Denham returned to Murzuk in November 1822. Denham eventually left Bornu on a return journey to Tripoli and England in August 1824.

## Regional Geography and Disputes
The town of Tripoli is located 800 meters due north of the oasis of Bilma (or Kawar) in the Sahara desert. In the early 20th century, the oasis of Bilma became a point of contention between Turkey and France. Turkey claimed Bilma as part of the hinterland of Tripoli and garrisoned the oasis in 1902. However, in 1906, a French force from Zinder occupied the town without opposition from Ottoman authorities.
