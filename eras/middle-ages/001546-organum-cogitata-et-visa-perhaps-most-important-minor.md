# Organum

The *Organum* (specifically the *Novum Organum*, published in 1620) is the most celebrated work of Francis Bacon. It serves as a central component of his project to reorganize natural science, providing a new method of inductive investigation designed to achieve mechanical simplicity and absolute certainty.

## The Method of Induction
The core of the *Organum* is the inductive method, which Bacon used to distinguish his philosophy from all previous systems. The process begins with the collection of instances to determine the "form" of a nature. Because a form is always present when the nature is present, absent when it is absent, and varies in degree as the nature increases or decreases, Bacon established three specific tables for investigation:

*   **Table of Essence and Presence:** A list of instances in which the nature is present.
*   **Table of Absence in Proximity:** A list of instances where the nature is absent, specifically focusing on cases most akin to those of presence.
*   **Table of Degrees (or Comparison):** A list of instances where the nature is present in varying degrees, either across different subjects or within the same subject.

Following the creation of these tables, the most critical part of the method is the process of exclusion or rejection. This elimination of non-essential elements is intended to lead infallibly to the detection of the cause or form, provided the tables are complete and the notions of the phenomena are clear. To assist in this process, Bacon devised "adminicula" (helps), which include the Rectification of Induction, Supports of Induction, Prerogative Instances, Prerogative Natures, Limits of Investigation, Application to Practice, Preparations, and Varying the Investigation according to the Nature of the Subject.

## Obstacles to Progress and the Doctrine of Idols
Before unfolding his method, Bacon devoted nearly the entire first book of the *Organum* to examining the obstacles that hinder human progress. This discussion centers on the doctrine of Idols (*Idola*), a concept so closely associated with Bacon's name as the theory of induction. One specific subset of these obstacles is the *Idola Theatri* (Idols of the Theatre), which Bacon explored in detail in his earlier work, *Redargutio Philosophiarum*.

## Relationship with Natural History
Bacon viewed the *Organum* and "Natural History" as correlative and equally necessary components of a true philosophy. He asserted that the *Organum*, even if completed, would not significantly advance the "Instauration of the Sciences" without a corresponding Natural History. Conversely, he believed a Natural History without the *Organum* would still provide some advancement. 

For Bacon, a true natural history consists exclusively of observations, connections, canons, and instances. He maintained that a store of instances must be procured for the business of science to proceed, regardless of how many volumes such a description might fill.

## Minor Philosophical Writings and Cogitata et Visa
The development of the *Organum* is reflected in several of Bacon's minor philosophical tracts. Among these, *Cogitata et Visa* is considered perhaps the most important. Dating from 1607, it was sent to friends as a small tract and is regarded as a first draft that contains the substance of the first book of the *Organum* written in sonorous and weighty Latin. 

Other related minor works include:
*   ***Valerius Terminus*** (c. 1603): An obscure outline of the first two parts of the *Instauratio*.
*   ***Redargutio Philosophiarum*** (c. 1608 or 1609): An oration detailing the *Idola Theatri*.
*   ***Temporis Partus Masculus***: A fragment characterized by an arrogant style, where the term *masculus* suggests the fruit-bearing power of the new philosophy.
*   ***The Advancement of Learning***: A survey of the ground that was later expanded into the *De Augmentis*.

## Influence
Bacon's work in the *Novum Organum* is seen by many as the foundation of English psychological speculation. Specifically, the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum* briefly states the principle that all ideas are products of reflection and sensation, a concept later central to Locke's *Essay*. In ethics, Bacon is viewed as a forerunner of the empirical method; the inductive method of the *Novum Organum* and the spirit of *De Augmentis* are considered the roots of theories that construct moral codes through the inductive examination of actions and human consciousness, most notably utilitarianism.
