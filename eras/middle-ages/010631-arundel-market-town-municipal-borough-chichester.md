# ARUNDEL

Arundel is a municipal borough and market town located in the Chichester parliamentary division of Sussex, England. It is situated on the slope of a hill above the river Arun, which is navigable for small vessels to Littlehampton at the mouth, located 6 miles to the south. The town is 58 miles south-southwest of London and is served by the London, Brighton & South Coast railway. As of 1901, the population of Arundel was 2,739.

## History and Governance
The first mention of Arundel (recorded as Harundell) occurs as early as 877, appearing in the will of King Alfred, who left it to his nephew Aethelm. During the era of Edward the Confessor, the town likely consisted of a fortification or earthwork—probably established by Alfred to defend against the Danes—and a mill. By the time of the Conquest, it had grown into a thriving port and borough, as noted in Domesday.

The borough's parliamentary representation began in 1302 with two members; however, the Reform Act of 1832 reduced this to one member, and the borough was entirely disfranchised in 1868. While no early charters exist, Queen Elizabeth acknowledged in 1586 the rights of the burgesses and mayor to hold a court for pleas under forty shillings, two weekly markets, and four annual fairs. In 1688, James II confirmed a charter from two years prior, incorporating the borough with a government consisting of a mayor, 12 burgesses, and 4 aldermen.

## Arundel Castle
Arundel Castle rises from the summit of the hill, a position that allowed it to guard the passage through the hills along the river. A castle existed during the time of King Alfred, and Roger de Montgomerie rebuilt it following the Conquest. The castle was later taken from Roger's son after he rebelled against King Henry I.

The stronghold was the site of a conspiracy in 1397 involving the duke of Gloucester, the archbishop of Canterbury, and the earl of Arundel. The plot aimed to murder the lords of the council and dethrone Richard II, though it was discovered before it could be executed. During the 17th-century civil wars, the castle suffered great damage due to frequent assaults by opposing parties. It was later restored by Charles, 11th duke of Norfolk (who died in 1815), transforming it into one of England's most splendid baronial mansions. Toward the end of the 19th century, the castle underwent extensive reconstruction in the style of the 13th century.

## Town Landmarks and Architecture
The town's name is derived from *hirondelle* (a swallow), according to the whimsical etymology found on the corporation seal. The town hall is a castellated building provided to the corporation by the duke of Norfolk.

The church of St Nicholas was founded around 1375 and is built in the Perpendicular style with a low central tower. It is noted for its iron work and reredos, and the north aisle of the chancel contains several ancient monuments of the earls of Arundel. The chancel is owned by the duke of Norfolk and is screened from the rest of the church; this exercise of ownership was the subject of a legal action and appeal in 1880. Additionally, the duke of Norfolk built the Roman Catholic church of St Philip Neri in 1873.

## Commerce and Infrastructure
Arundel was formerly a thriving seaport and was connected to London by canal in 1813. Markets have been held within the borough on Saturdays and Thursdays from very early times. In 1285, Richard Fitzalan, earl of Arundel, obtained a grant for two annual fairs, held on December 17 and May 14. Although the town was half destroyed by fire in 1338, it was rebuilt shortly thereafter.
