# Deputations

In historical and political contexts, deputations refer to groups of appointed representatives sent to act, negotiate, or speak on behalf of a larger body. These delegations have appeared in various capacities, ranging from the administrative organization of ecclesiastical councils to the diplomatic representation of city-states and the political maneuvering of estates.

## Ecclesiastical Organization at the Assembly of Basel
At the assembly of Basel, the organization of the council was designed to reflect a democratic character. This was achieved through both the composition of the assembly—where the number of prelates was small compared to the number of monks, clerks of inferior orders, masters, doctors, and representatives of chapters—and its specific organizational structure. 

Rather than being separated into "nations," as had occurred at Constance, the fathers of the assembly divided themselves into four large committees known as "deputations" (_deputationes_). These were organized based on the aptitudes or tastes of the members and were categorized by their specific focus:
*   **Fidei**: Concerned with questions of faith.
*   **Pacis**: Concerned with negotiations for peace.
*   **Reformatorii**: Concerned with reform.
*   **Pro communibus**: Concerned with "common concerns."

The lower clergy formed the majority within each of these deputations. Decisions made by three of these committees were ratified in general congregation for the sake of form, which could then lead to the promulgation of decrees in session. Because of this structure, the council was sometimes described, though perhaps with exaggeration, as a "set of grooms and scullions" or "an assembly of copyists."

## Political Deputations in Denmark
In the political history of Denmark, deputations played a role in the transition of the realm to a hereditary monarchy. Following arrangements made by Frederick III and communicated via Svane and Nansen, the "Instrument" was signed by the Lower Estates. This document offered the realm to the king and his house as a hereditary monarchy, primarily as a thank-offering for the king's courageous deliverance of the kingdom during the war.

On October 10, a deputation consisting of burgesses and clergy proceeded to the Council House to demand an answer to their propositions from the _Rigsraad_ (the aristocratic council). After the _Rigsraad_ rejected the "Instrument" during a tumultuous scene, the deputies of the commons went to the palace, where they were received by the king.

## Ceremonial and Diplomatic Deputations
Deputations have also served ceremonial functions and as a means of formal representation in legal or diplomatic disputes.

### The Barons of the Cinque Ports
The "Barons of the Cinque Ports"—originally comprising the freemen of Sandwich, Romney, Hythe, Dover, and Hastings—eventually saw this title restricted to their jurats, mayors, and members of the House of Commons (two for each port). Despite a 1606 protest from the peers regarding the title of "baron of parliament" for those in the Lower House, these individuals maintained the ancient privilege of retaining a canopy over the sovereign during coronations. When they petitioned to attend the coronation of Edward VII, a deputation was permitted to do so.

### The Plataeans and Lacedaemonians
In the conflict between the Plataeans and the Peloponnesians, a deputation was used to facilitate a voluntary surrender. After the Plataeans were unable to repel an assault on their wall and were without provisions, a Lacedaemonian herald offered them the chance to surrender voluntarily to avoid being taken by storm. This was intended to ensure that if peace were later made with Athens, Plataea would not be included in a list of restored places. 

Once the town surrendered and five judges from Lacedaemon arrived, the Plataeans were asked if they had provided any service to the Lacedaemonians and their allies during the war. To answer this, the Plataeans deputed two representatives to speak on their behalf: Lacon, son of Aeimnestus (a proxenus of the Lacedaemonians), and Astymachus, son of Asopolaus.
