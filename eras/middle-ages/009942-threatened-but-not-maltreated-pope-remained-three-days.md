# Papal Authority and Conflict in the Middle Ages

The history of the papacy during the Middle Ages is characterized by periods of extreme vulnerability, internal corruption, and eventual assertions of supreme authority over European monarchs. This trajectory moved from the instability of the tenth century to a peak of political power where popes claimed lordship over the kingdoms of the world.

## The Crisis of the Tenth Century
During the tenth century, the state of Rome was described as almost indescribable. Following the decay of the Empire of Charlemagne, the Pope was left without a protector. This left the papacy threatened by the Saracens, who had captured Sicily, as well as by Byzantium and the unruly nobility of Rome.

Temporal power in Rome was largely seized by the patrician Theophylact and held within the Castle of St. Angelo. This power was subsequently wielded by his wife, Theodora, and their daughter, Marozia. These women are described by historians as bold, unscrupulous, and dissolute. In 928, Marozia seized and imprisoned Pope John X, who died shortly thereafter under her care. Marozia later installed her illegitimate son as Pope John XI, followed by her grandson, Pope John XII. The lives of John XI and John XII are characterized as abominable and discreditable. John XII was eventually degraded by the German Emperor Otto I, who entered Italy to be crowned Emperor in 962.

## The Rise of Papal Supremacy
A period of papal greatness began toward the close of the eleventh century, founded by the clerical statesman Hildebrand, who became Pope Gregory VII (1073–1085), and his successor Urban II (1087–1099). During this era, the popes "lorded it over the Emperors," exercising supremacy from Norway to Sicily, and from Ireland to Bulgaria and Jerusalem.

The extent of this power was demonstrated when Pope Gregory VII compelled Emperor Henry IV to travel to Canossa in penitence. Henry IV was forced to wait in the courtyard of the castle for three days and nights, barefooted in the snow and clad in sackcloth, to await forgiveness. Later, in 1176 at Venice, Emperor Frederick Barbarossa knelt before Pope Alexander III and swore fealty to him, acknowledging the Pope's supremacy.

## The Reign of Boniface VIII
The assertion of papal lordship reached a peak under Boniface VIII (Benedetto Gaetano), who served as pope from 1294 to 1303. Born into a noble family in Anagni and educated in civil and canon law, Boniface had a distinguished career as a notary at the papal court and a cardinal before his election. He had previously been besieged in the Tower of London in 1267 while aiding King Henry III against the baronial party, from which he was rescued by the future Edward I.

Unlike his predecessor Celestine V, who ruled in monastic humility, Boniface VIII ardently asserted the lordship of the papacy over all the world's kingdoms. His reign was marked by great pomp and an ambition to recover the Holy Land from the infidel. However, his tenure was defined by controversy, arrogance, and avarice. He faced numerous conflicts:
* **France:** He engaged in a noted conflict with Philip IV of France.
* **England:** He attempted to try Edward I on charges of interfering with a papal fief, but the English parliament declared in 1301 that Scotland had never been a fief of Rome.
* **Germany and Hungary:** He refused to recognize Albert of Austria as the rightful German king until 1303 and declared that the crown of Hungary should fall to the house of Anjou.
* **Denmark:** He humbled Eric VI.

Additionally, Boniface VIII's attempts to build large estates for his family created enmity with the Colonna family, and his support for the house of Anjou in Sicily ended in failure.
