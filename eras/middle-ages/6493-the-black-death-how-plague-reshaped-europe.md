# The Black Death: How Plague Reshaped Europe

## Overview
The Black Death, which ravaged Europe between 1347 and 1351, was one of the most transformative catastrophes in human history. Caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*, the pandemic is estimated to have killed between 30% and 60% of Europe's population. While the immediate result was unimaginable horror and social collapse, the long-term aftermath triggered a fundamental restructuring of European society.

The most significant shift occurred in the economic landscape. The sudden, massive loss of life created an acute labor shortage. For the first time in the Middle Ages, peasants and artisans possessed bargaining power. As landlords competed for a dwindling number of workers, wages rose and the rigid bonds of serfdom began to dissolve. This shift laid the groundwork for the transition from a feudal economy to a wage-based economy, effectively signaling the beginning of the end for the manorial system.

Psychologically and spiritually, the plague shook the foundations of the Catholic Church. The inability of the clergy to stop the pestilence—and the high mortality rate among priests—led to a crisis of faith and the rise of fringe movements, such as the flagellants. However, historians debate the extent to which the plague "caused" the Renaissance; while some argue the resulting wealth concentration and skepticism fueled humanism, others suggest the recovery was a slower, more complex process of institutional adaptation.

## Key Facts
*   **Transmission:** The plague was primarily spread by fleas living on black rats (*Rattus rattus*), though pneumonic transmission (person-to-person via droplets) occurred in colder climates.
*   **Social Unrest:** The economic tension following the plague contributed to major uprisings, most notably the English Peasants' Revolt of 1381.
*   **Medical Response:** Medieval medicine, based on the theory of "miasma" (bad air), was powerless. Doctors wore rudimentary masks or used aromatic herbs to ward off the scent of decay.
*   **Demographic Impact:** Some regions, such as Florence or parts of Germany, saw mortality rates exceeding 50%, leading to the total abandonment of "lost villages."

## Did You Know?
1. **Quarantine Origins:** The word "quarantine" comes from the Venetian *quaranta giorni* (forty days), the period ships were required to anchor offshore to ensure they were plague-free.
2. **The Caffa Incident:** Some historians cite the Siege of Caffa (1346) as an early instance of biological warfare, where Mongol forces allegedly catapulted plague-infected corpses over city walls.
3. **Dietary Shift:** Because there were fewer people to farm grain, more land was converted to pasture, leading to an increase in meat and dairy consumption for the surviving peasantry.
