# Cordova

Cordova (Spanish: *Cordoba*; Latin: *Corduba*) is a city and the capital of the Spanish province of the same name. It is situated on the right bank of the river Guadalquivir, located on the southern slopes of the Sierra de Cordova.

## Early History and Roman Period
The city is believed to be of Carthaginian origin. It was occupied by the Romans in 152 B.C. under Marcus Marcellus, subsequently becoming the first Roman *colonia* in Spain. Due to the high number of noble colonists, the city was granted the title of *Patricia*. During the 1st century B.C., Cordova supported the sons of Pompey in their conflict against Caesar. Following the battle of Munda in 45 B.C., the city fell to Caesar, who massacred 20,000 inhabitants as punishment for their resistance.

Under Augustus, Cordova became a municipality and served as the capital of the Romanized province of Baetica. During the lifetime of Strabo (c. 63 B.C.–A.D. 21), it was ranked as the largest city in Spain. This prosperity was attributed to its location on the Via Augusta—the great commercial road from northern Spain—and its position on the Baetis, as well as its proximity to grain-producing districts, rich grazing lands, and mines. In 345, the city's bishop, Hosius, presided over the first council of Nicaea. The city's importance continued under the rule of the Visigothic kings from the 5th century until the beginning of the 8th century.

## Moorish Rule and Zenith
Under the Moors, Cordova initially served as an appanage of the caliphate of Damascus. In 756, Abd-ar-Rahman I established it as the capital of Moorish Spain and the center of an independent caliphate. The city reached its peak of prosperity in the middle of the 10th century under Abd-ar-Rahman III (912–961), who built the Alcazar, or royal palace, amid landscaped gardens.

Native chroniclers record that at the time of Abd-ar-Rahman III's death, the city contained 900 baths, 600 mosques, 200,000 houses, a university, and numerous public libraries within its walls. Under the monarch's power along the Guadalquivir, there were further 12,000 populous villages, 300 towns, and eight cities. During this era, Cordova divided the world's supremacy in art, science, and literature with Bagdad, though Bagdad far surpassed Cordova in wealth and commerce.

A period of decadence began in 1016 due to rival dynasties competing to succeed the line of Abd-ar-Rahman. The caliphate never recovered its former position, and in 1236, Ferdinand III of Castile captured the city.

## Commerce and Industry
During the Moorish period, Cordova was celebrated for its silversmiths, who were said to have originated from Damascus. The city also exported a specific type of leather that gave rise to the word *cordwainer*. While Moorish leatherwork was often imitated, the handicraft nearly disappeared by the 15th century. Fine silver filigree ornaments continue to be produced.

In the modern era, the chief industries of the city include the manufacture of silken, linen, and woollen goods, as well as the distillation of spirits. The surrounding outskirts feature modern factories, which contrast with the pastures of the Cordovan school of bull-fighting and plantations of olive, lemon, and orange.

## Geography and Infrastructure
The province of Cordova is an inland region of southern Spain. It is bounded by Badajoz to the northwest, Ciudad Real to the northeast, Jaen to the east, Granada to the southeast, Malaga to the south, and Seville to the southwest and west. The Guadalquivir river divides the province into two distinct areas: the mountainous Sierra Morena on the right bank and the more fertile plains (*La Campina*) and undulating country on the left bank.

The city's layout features narrow, crooked streets with buildings largely covered in whitewash. Its Moorish walls, built on Roman foundations, once enclosed a wide area that is now mostly garden-ground. A bridge of sixteen arches, combining Roman and Moorish masonry, connects the town to the southern suburb.

Modern transportation is facilitated by the Madrid-Seville railway, which meets a branch line from Almorchon to Malaga at Cordova.

## Public Buildings and Institutions
The city is an episcopal see. Its most significant public buildings include:
* The cathedral and the bishop's palace.
* The city hall, hospitals, and various churches and monastic establishments.
* Schools and colleges, including an academy for girls founded in 1590 by Bishop Pacheco of Cordova, which is authorized to grant degrees.
* The Alcazar; while the older sections are in ruins, the "New Alcazar" (erected by Alphonso XI of Castile in 1328) was used by the Holy Inquisition and currently has one wing serving as a prison.
