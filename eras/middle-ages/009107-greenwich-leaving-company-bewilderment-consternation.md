# Greenwich

Greenwich refers to several distinct locations and historical contexts, most notably a metropolitan borough of London, England, and a township in Connecticut, U.S.A.

## Greenwich, London
Greenwich is a south-eastern metropolitan borough of London, bounded by the river Thames to the north, Woolwich to the east, Lewisham to the south, and Deptford to the west. As of 1901, it had a population of 95,770 and covered an area of 3,851.7 acres. The borough is connected to Poplar via the Greenwich tunnel (1902) for pedestrians and the Blackwall Tunnel (1897) for street traffic. Its main east-west thoroughfares are Shooter's Hill Road—the Roman Watling Street—and Woolwich Road.

### History and Landmarks
The area was first noted during the reign of Ethelred as a station for the Danish fleet between 1011 and 1014. The parish church in Church Street is dedicated to St Alphege, an archbishop martyred there by the Danes in 1012. Other notable figures buried in the church include the musician Tallis and Wolfe, who died at Quebec in 1759.

The most prominent buildings are the observatory and Greenwich Hospital. The hospital, which became a Royal Naval College in 1873, stands on the site of the ancient royal palace known as Greenwich House. This palace was a favorite royal residence as early as 1300. It served as the birthplace of Queen Elizabeth, Queen Mary, and Henry VIII, and was the place where Edward VI died. The site was later improved by Humphrey, duke of Gloucester, who named it *Placentia*. Following the Revolution, the palace was appropriated by the Protector but reverted to the crown under Charles II, who demolished it to begin the hospital's construction. The current hospital features designs by Inigo Jones and Sir Christopher Wren.

### Civic and Cultural Life
Greenwich formerly hosted the "whitebait dinner," an annual banquet for cabinet ministers. Originally held at Dagenham, the event moved to Greenwich in the early 19th century and was regularly celebrated at the "Ship" hotel until the hotel closed in 1908; the banquet itself ended in 1994. Politically, the borough returns one member to parliament. Its council is composed of a mayor, thirty councillors, and five aldermen.

## Greenwich, Connecticut
Located in Fairfield County, Connecticut, on Long Island Sound, this township is approximately 28 miles northeast of New York City. As of 1910, the township's population was 16,463, with 3,886 residing in the borough of Greenwich. The township includes the villages of Sound Beach, Riverside, and Cos Cob.

### Settlement and Development
The first settlers arrived from the New Haven Colony in 1640. However, the Dutch claimed the area following Adrian Blok's 1614 exploration of Long Island Sound. Due to a lack of assistance from New Haven, the settlers joined New Netherland in 1642, and Greenwich became a Dutch manor. A 1650 treaty saw the Dutch relinquish their claim, though inhabitants did not submit to the New Haven Colony until October 1656. By 1662, Greenwich was among the first New Haven towns to submit to Connecticut. During the War of Independence, the township suffered from the invasion by the British and the quartering of American troops.

### Local Economy and Institutions
Greenwich serves as a summer resort primarily for New Yorkers. Its principal manufactures include iron and gasolene motors, woollens, belting, and tinners' hardware; the area also ships oysters. Educational institutions include the Brunswick School for boys (1901) and Greenwich Academy (1827). Notable residents have included the landscape painter John Henry Twachtman, Edwin Thomas Booth, and Henry Osborne Havemeyer, the founder of the American Sugar Company.

## Other References
### Geodetic and Scientific Use
Greenwich serves as a primary reference point for determining longitude. Geodetic triangulation in India and Russia is measured in degrees east of Greenwich. Since 1890, the telegraphic method has been used to determine longitudes differentially with Greenwich, including a route through Potsdam, Teheran, Bushire, and Karachi.

### Historical Events at Greenwich
In the context of the English monarchy, a tournament at Greenwich was suddenly broken up by the king on May 1, following reports of the queen's ill-conduct. This led to the arrest of several of Anne's reputed lovers and her own commitment to the Tower on May 2 on charges of adultery, including with her brother, Lord Rochford. Following a trial by twenty-six peers, Anne and Lord Rochford were condemned on May 15. Anne was executed on Tower Green on May 19, her head struck off by a sword.

In the arts, the actor and dancer William Kemp was summoned in 1594, along with Shakespeare and Burbage, to perform before the queen at Greenwich.
