# The Institution of Virgines and the Ideal of Virginity

The pursuit of virginity and the subsequent establishment of specific institutions regarding "virgines" manifested in various forms across different historical, religious, and social contexts, ranging from early Christian spiritual practices to royal selections and liturgical classifications.

## Spiritual Wives and the Virgines Subintroductae
A specific institution known as *virgines subintroductae*, or spiritual wives, emerged alongside a strong emphasis on virginity. This practice was based on the assumption that the grace of baptism could restore the original purity of life that Adam and Eve shared before the Fall. These rigors were encouraged in the *Shepherd of Hermas*, a text that originated in Rome and was read in churches until the 4th century.

The practice of spiritual wives was prevalent in several regions and sects:
* **African and Syrian Churches:** These relations were common in African churches, where they led to abuses that required the energy of Cyprian to address. They were also present in Antioch in 260 and in the Syriac church as late as 340, where they were practiced between the "Sons and daughters of the Resurrection."
* **Celtic Church:** Evidence of these practices is found in the church of St Patrick and among Celtic elders in northern France as late as the 7th century.
* **Dissident Sects:** The Albigenses and other middle-age dissident sects continued the practice. For these groups, it served a dual purpose: elders could prove their own chastity while avoiding suspicion from inquisitors. Because the Albigenses viewed marriage as the "greater adultery" (*maius adulterium*), cohabiting with a woman in appearance allowed them to elude detection.

Most early councils, regardless of size, condemned the custom of spiritual wives as well as the practice of self-emasculation. Within the Catholic church, those seeking the Encratite ideal eventually moved to monasteries.

## Virginity in Royal and Legal Contexts
In other historical contexts, virginity was a criterion for royal selection or a matter of legal evidence. In the account of the king who sought a new wife after the separation from Vashti, the king ordered the search for "comely virgins" throughout the habitable earth. Four hundred virgins were gathered, including a damsel from Babylon named Esther, who was brought up by her uncle Mordecai of the tribe of Benjamin. These virgins underwent a six-month purification process involving costly ointments and sweet odors before being presented to the king.

Regarding legal evidence, "tokens of virginity" are mentioned in Deuteronomy 22:15, 17, and 20. These are interpreted not as physical markers, but as close linen garments that virgins wore after a certain age and did not remove until they were married before witnesses. The integrity of these garments served as evidence of virginity.

## Liturgical and Theological Classifications
In the Catholic church's *Proprium Sanctorum* and *Commune Sanctorum*, "virgins" are listed as one of the twelve groups or classes of saints (alongside apostles, martyrs, confessors, and the Blessed Virgin Mary) for whom specific psalms, antiphons, and lessons are designated. 

The Blessed Virgin Mary holds a distinct place in these traditions. The *Breviary* includes the Little Office of the Blessed Virgin Mary, and the *Commune Sanctorum* provides offices for her feasts. Theological discussions regarding her have varied; for instance, the Docetes proposed the hypothesis that Christ did not issue from the womb of the Virgin but descended on the banks of the Jordan in perfect manhood. Conversely, other traditions emphasize that Christ was born "after the flesh from the holy virgin" and subsequently "born" through baptism.

## Other Historical References
The term "virgins" also appears in the context of the Vestal Virgins in Rome, who held deposits made by citizens and strangers. These deposits were seized by Antonius and Cæsar. Additionally, in the context of personal devotion, Ignatius of Loyola performed an all-night vigil before the Altar of the Blessed Virgin at the Benedictine Abbey of Manresa, an act followed by austerities, fasting, and a pilgrimage to the Holy Land.
