# Edward the Confessor and the Possessions of Earl Morcar

## Overview of Edward the Confessor
Edward the Confessor served as the king over England for twenty-three years. He died in London on January 5th while on a bed of sickness and was buried in Paul's church. Among the English people, he is referred to as a saint. Following his death, Harald Godwinson was chosen, consecrated, and crowned as king. This succession was contested by William the Bastard, the Earl of Rouen, who claimed a better right to the kingdom based on his relationship to King Edward.

## Bridlington and Earl Morcar
During the reign of Edward the Confessor, the settlement of Bridlington (also referred to as Brellington or Burlington) was a borough. At this time, Bridlington formed part of the possessions of Earl Morcar. The value of the area during Edward's reign was recorded as £32.

Following the Norman Conquest, Bridlington suffered from Norman ravages, and its value decreased significantly to 8s. by the time of the Domesday survey. By that period, the land had passed into the hands of the king due to the forfeiture of Earl Morcar. Subsequently, William II granted the area to Gilbert de Gaunt.

## Other Contemporary Manors and Boroughs
The era of Edward the Confessor is noted in the records of several other English locations:

*   **Lydford:** In Devon, Lydford was one of four Saxon boroughs. During the reign of Edward the Confessor, it was the most populous center in Devonshire, second only to Exeter.
*   **Dunwich:** Located in Suffolk, Dunwich (known in the Saxon era as Dunwyk or Dommocceaster) had previously served as the capital of Sigebert, king of East Anglia, and a center for Christianity. However, by the middle of the 11th century, during the time of Edward the Confessor, the town was in decline due to the inroads of the sea on the unstable coast.
*   **Biggleswade:** In Bedfordshire, the manor of Biggleswade was owned by Archbishop Stigand during the time of Edward the Confessor.
*   **Kensington:** The history of the manor of Kensington is traceable from the time of Edward the Confessor. The name may be connected to a Saxon royal residence, a family named Chenesi, the word *caen* (meaning wood), or the Saxon family or tribe of Kensings.
