# Saint Bonaventura

Saint Bonaventura, also known as John of Fidanza, was a prominent Franciscan theologian born in 1221 at Bagnarea in Tuscany. Destined for the church by his mother, he is said to have received the name Bonaventura from St Francis of Assisi, who performed a miraculous cure upon him. He entered the Franciscan order in 1243 and pursued studies in Paris, where he studied under John of Rochelle and possibly Alexander of Hales. Bonaventura succeeded John of Rochelle in his chair in 1253, having already been granted permission to read upon the *Sentences* three years prior. He received the degree of doctor in 1255 and was elected general of the Franciscan order the following year.

## Career and Ecclesiastical Influence
As general of his order, Bonaventura exercised significant authority, including the interdiction of Roger Bacon from lecturing at Oxford, which compelled Bacon to remain under the order's surveillance in Paris. Bonaventura was also instrumental in the election of Pope Gregory X. In recognition of his influence, Gregory X granted him the titles of bishop of Albano and cardinal. Bonaventura's influence extended to diplomatic and ecclesiastical affairs; he is credited with persuading the Greeks to consent to a temporary union with Rome during the great council of Lyons in 1274. He died during this meeting.

## Philosophical Contrast and Influences
In the realm of philosophy, Bonaventura stands in marked contrast to his contemporaries, Roger Bacon and Thomas Aquinas. While Bacon is associated with early physical science and Aquinas with the most perfect form of Aristotelian scholasticism, Bonaventura represents a Platonizing and mystical mode of speculation. This approach had previous expression in Bernard of Clairvaux and in Richard and Hugo of St Victor.

Bonaventura rejected the authority of Aristotle, attributing much of the era's heretical tendencies to Aristotelian influence. He vigorously combated specific cardinal doctrines of Aristotle, such as the eternity of the world. Instead, his philosophy was rooted in a form of Platonism as understood by St Augustine, the Alexandrian school, and the author of mystical works attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite.

## Theological and Metaphysical Views
Bonaventura was a high-ranking dogmatic theologian who provided reasoned decisions on scholastic disputes regarding the *intellectus agens*, matter, the principle of individualism, and universals. He maintained that the *intellectus agens* has no separate existence. He viewed matter as pure potentiality that receives determinateness and individual being from the formative power of God. Regarding universals, he held that they are ideal forms pre-existing in the divine mind, serving as the patterns according to which actual things were formed. He accepted the Platonic theory that ideas exist as thoughts of the divine mind rather than *in rerum natura*.

Agreeing with Albertus Magnus, Bonaventura regarded theology as a practical science whose truths are specifically adapted to influence the affections. He believed that while the purely intellectual element is present, it is of inferior interest compared to the heart or the living power of the affections.

## Reason, Faith, and the Divine Union
Bonaventura addressed the relationship between faith and reason, asserting that all sciences serve as handmaids to theology. He argued that while reason can discover some moral truths that ground the Christian system, other truths can only be apprehended through divine illumination. 

To achieve this illumination, the soul must utilize specific means:
* **Prayer** and the **exercise of virtues**, which make the soul fit to receive divine light.
* **Meditation**, which can lead to an ecstatic union with God.

The supreme end of human life is this union—an intense absorbing love and contemplation in intellect. Bonaventura taught that this union cannot be fully realized in the present life and remains a hope for the futurity. In the process of contemplating God, the mind moves through distinct stages or grades, beginning with the senses, which provide empirical knowledge of the external world and discern the traces (*vestigia*) of the divine.
