# Transylvania, South Hungary, and Bessarabia

## Geography and the Carpathian System
The region is defined largely by the Carpathian Mountains, the eastern wing of Europe's great central mountain system. Extending over 800 miles with a total area of 72,600 square miles, the Carpathians surround Transylvania and Hungary in a large semicircle. The system is divided into the mountains of Transylvania and the Carpathians proper; the latter consists of an inner wall filling Upper Hungary and an outer wall forming the frontier between Hungary and provinces such as Austria, Galicia, and Bukovina.

The Carpathians reach their greatest breadth in the Transylvanian plateau. While they lack the glaciers and perpetual snow of the Alps, they provide a significant geographical barrier, separating Hungary and Transylvania from Lower Austria, Moravia, and Silesia. The range ends on the Danube near Orsova, where the river separates the Carpathians from the Balkan Mountains.

## The Banat Region
The Banat is a fertile district in south-east Hungary comprising the counties of Temes, Torontal, and Krasso-Szoreny. Covering an area of 11,260 square miles, it is bounded by the Maros to the north, the Theiss to the west, the Danube to the south, and the Transylvanian Alps to the east. The terrain is flat or marshy in the north, west, and south-west, while the south and south-east are mountainous.

The region is rich in minerals, including iron, lead, zinc, tin, copper, and especially coal. It is also known for mineral springs, such as the sulphurous waters of Mehadia. Agriculture is highly productive, with large quantities of maize, tobacco, flax, hemp, rye, oats, barley, and wheat grown alongside quality vineyard products. In 1900, the population was 1,431,329, consisting of 578,789 Rumanians, 362,487 Germans, 251,938 Servians, and 170,124 Magyars. Temesvar serves as the chief town.

## Transylvania and Kolozsvár
Transylvania has historically functioned as a distinct entity, recognized as an independent principality following the peace of Vasvar (August 10, 1664) after Ottoman aggression. Later, under the reorganization of the Austrian empire following the war of 1866, Transylvania was included as part of Hungary.

Kolozsvár (also known as Klausenburg or Cluj) served as the former capital of Transylvania and remains its literary and scientific center. Located on the Little Szamos river, the town features a citadel erected in 1715 and the Gothic church of St Michael (1396-1432). It is a center of education, housing a university founded in 1872 with faculties in medicine, law, philosophy, and theology. The town is also the seat of a Unitarian bishop and the Calvinist superintendent for the Transylvanian circle. Its industry includes the production of sugar, paper, soap, candles, earthenwares, and textiles.

## The Bassarab Dynasty and Walachia
The Bassarabs were a dynasty that ruled Walachia from its earliest history until 1658. The name is closely connected to the province of Bessarabia, a term oriental chroniclers once used to describe the entirety of Walachia. The dynasty's first appearances as rulers—holding titles such as *voivod*, *ban*, or *knyaz*—occurred in the southern parts of Transylvania (specifically the dukedoms of Almash and Fogarash) and the western part of Rumania, originally known as Little Walachia.

Tradition states that a member of the Bassarab family came from Transylvania to settle in Tirgovishtea and Campulung, founding the Walachian principality. The princes of this line frequently bore Slavonic surnames, likely due to the influence of the Slavonic Church. In the 13th century, the dynasty split into two rival factions: the descendants of Dan and Dragul. The latter line became known as "Drakul" (meaning devil), a nickname associated with the extreme cruelties of Vlad Drakul (1433-1446) and Vlad Tsepesh (1456-1476).

## Bessarabia and Surrounding Territories
Bessarabia is identified as a government of south Russia, bordering the government of Kherson to the east. The region is part of a broader area where various ethnicities reside; for instance, the neighboring government of Kherson includes populations of Rumanians, Greeks, Germans, and Bulgarians.

In the broader regional context, the area has seen significant ethnic shifts. While the Slavonic population (including Bulgars and Serbo-Croats) is the most numerous in the Balkan Peninsula, the Turkish population, descended from Ottoman invaders and colonists, declined considerably during the 19th century.
