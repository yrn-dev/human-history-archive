# Erfurt, Würzburg, Buraburg, and Eichstätt

The ecclesiastical and civic development of these German regions was significantly shaped by the missionary activities of the 8th century and the subsequent establishment of bishoprics and academic centers.

## Early Ecclesiastical Organization
The organization of the east Frankish church was largely the work of Boniface, who began a systematic crusade in heathen Germany. His efforts included the founding of monasteries and churches, the overturning of idols, and the baptism of the population. Boniface was supported by a group of missionary helpers from England, including monks and nuns such as St. Lull, St. Walburge, St. Thecla, and St. Lioba.

In 732, Boniface was created archbishop, and by 741, Pope Zacharias appointed him legate, charging him with the reformation of the entire Frankish church. With the support of Pippin and Carloman, the mayors of the palace who succeeded Charles Martel, Boniface organized the east Frankish church into four specific bishoprics: Erfurt, Würzburg, Buraburg, and Eichstätt. He placed his own monks in charge of these sees. During this period, Boniface also founded the abbey of Fulda to serve as a center for German monastic culture, appointing the Bavarian Sturm to lead it under a rule stricter than that of the Benedictines.

## Würzburg and Eastern Franconia
Würzburg served as a primary center for the apostle of eastern Franconia, St. Kilian. A British missionary bishop, Kilian began his work in the region toward the end of the 7th century. According to the writings of Hrabanus Maurus, Kilian was a native of Ireland. After preaching the gospel in Würzburg, Kilian and his companions—a priest named Coloman and a deacon named Totman—were put to death by the orders of an unjust judge or duke named Gozbert. Their relics were later elevated by Burchard, the first bishop of Würzburg, and are venerated in the town's cathedral.

The bishopric of Würzburg held significant territorial influence over time. The town of Kitzingen, which possessed a Benedictine abbey in the 8th century, later belonged to the bishopric. Similarly, the town of Kissingen belonged to the counts of Henneberg until 1394, at which point it was sold to the bishop of Würzburg, eventually passing to Bavaria.

In later centuries, Würzburg became a center for academic achievement. In 1847, the university of Würzburg appointed the Swiss anatomist and physiologist Rudolph Albert von Kölliker as professor of physiology and of microscopical and comparative anatomy. Kölliker remained in the Bavarian town for the rest of his life, dying there on November 2, 1905.

## Erfurt and its Cultural Influence
Erfurt is noted for its academic, theological, and musical history. The town was home to an academy where the German orientalist Hiob Ludolf studied philology before traveling to Italy to learn the Ethiopian language from an Abyssinian scholar. Erfurt also served as a center for medical study; Ernst Gottfried Baldinger studied medicine there before becoming a professor at Jena, Goettingen, and Marburg.

The town's musical heritage is closely tied to the Bach family of Thuringia. The family produced musicians of such eminence that, in Erfurt, musicians were referred to as "Bachs" even after members of the family had left the town.

Theologically, Erfurt was a site of significant conflict during the Reformation. Martin Luther's early theological views were not in variance with the theology taught at Erfurt. However, as Luther's "experimental" teaching—which focused on guiding men to the grace of God and joyous obedience—developed, a breach formed between his views and the scholastic theology taught at the Erfurt convent. By September of the period in question, Luther began writing against this scholastic theology, characterizing it as Pelagian and arguing that it neglected the Augustinian doctrines of grace.

## Historical Events in Erfurt
Erfurt also appears in military history. During the Thirty Years' War, the town was visited by Gustavus Adolphus, who spent the night of October 28–29, 1632, at "The Tall Lily" shortly before the battle of Lützen. The town is also associated with the memory of Field-Marshal Johan Banér.
