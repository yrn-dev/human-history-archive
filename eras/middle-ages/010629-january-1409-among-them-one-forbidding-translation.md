# January 1409

The month of January 1409 is associated with significant ecclesiastical legislation in England and specific cultural and visionary records from the early 15th century.

## Ecclesiastical Legislation and the English Bible
In January 1409, constitutions were published following a council at Oxford summoned in 1408. These constitutions were designed to suppress heresy and specifically targeted the Lollards. Among the published decrees was a mandate forbidding the translation of the Bible into the English language. Such translations were prohibited unless they received the consent of a provincial synod or the bishop of the diocese.

These actions were part of a broader effort by the church leadership of the time to defend the church and eliminate heresy. This period of suppression included the use of the statute *de haeretico comburendo* and the passing of a sentence of degradation upon William Sawtrey.

## Cultural and Material Records of 1409
Records from 1409 provide insight into the musical instruments and fashion of the era. A Highland bag-pipe dated MCCCCIX (1409), which was exhibited at the Royal Military Exhibition in London in 1890, demonstrates the instrument's design in the 15th century. This specific pipe featured a chaunter with a thumb-hole at the back and seven finger-holes in front, along with two drones tuned to A in unison with the chaunter's lowest note. These drones were inserted into a single stock shaped like a wide-spread fork.

Regarding dress, a brass dating from 1409 depicts an English Squire and his wife. During this period, fashions became more various. Some men of rank continued to wear rich modes from the previous age, characterized by long sleeves with slittered edges and trailing skirts. A particular fashion of the time involved attaching silver bells, or "gingles," to garments.

## Visionary Accounts
The year 1409 is also noted for a vision experienced by William Staunton at St Patrick's Purgatory. In this vision, Staunton observed the fate of "earth's proud ones" in a place of torment. He recorded that the fashions of the living were transformed into instruments of punishment: the jags in men's clothing became adders, and the trailing skirts of women were burnt over their heads. Furthermore, those who wore garments adorned with silver bells had burning nails of fire driven through each bell, while chaplets made of pearls, gold, and precious stones were turned into iron nails used by fiends for hammering.
