# Neustria

## Overview and Origins
Neustria was one of the two primary divisions of the Frankish kingdom, serving as the nucleus of what would eventually become France. The Frankish kingdom originally arose in the region of present-day Belgium and expanded southward toward the Loire. Following the reign of its founder, Clovis (481-511), the kingdom was divided among his four sons. While a tradition of unity persisted through fraternal wars for control, a more significant split eventually occurred due to the Latinization of the Western Franks.

These Western Franks occupied Romanized Gaul and adopted a "Latinish" speech—a corrupt form of Latin spoken by the subject population—which evolved into the French language. This distinguished them from the Franks of the Rhineland, known as Austrasia, who retained their Low German speech. This linguistic and cultural divide resulted in a political split between Neustria and Austrasia that lasted for one hundred and fifty years.

## Political Conflict and Unification
The relationship between Neustria and Austrasia was characterized by power struggles and shifting control. In 613, Arnulf, a bishop of Metz, and Pippin (also known as Pippin the Old or Pippin I) were Austrasian nobles who appealed to Clotaire II, the king of Neustria, against Brunhilda. In recognition of their services, Clotaire II appointed them as his counsellors and granted Arnulf the bishopric of Metz. In 623, when Clotaire II conferred the kingdom of Austrasia upon his son Dagobert, Pippin and Arnulf were appointed as mentors to the king, with Pippin holding the title of mayor of the palace.

By the end of the 7th century, Pippin II (incorrectly referred to as Pippin of Heristal) had secured dominant authority in Austrasia. In 687, he led the Austrasians in a campaign against Neustria, achieving a decisive victory at Tertry, near St Quentin. This victory allowed Pippin II to become the sole master of the Frankish kingdom until his death in 714. Following this unification, Pippin appointed his son, Grimoald, as the mayor of the palace in Neustria, a position later held by Grimoald's son, Theodebald.

## The Carolingian Transition
The mayoralty of the palace eventually became a hereditary position. Charles Martel, a son of Pippin by a concubine named Chalpaida, seized the mayoralty in both Austrasia and Neustria, continuing the Carolingian dynasty. Charles Martel governed from 714 to 741.

The transition from the Merovingian dynasty to the Carolingians was finalized at the abbey of Saint Médard in Soissons. It was at this site that the last Merovingian king was deposed and Pepin the Short was crowned in his place. The Carolingian family officially gained the throne of France in 751.

## Religious and Social Developments
During the 8th century, Neustria experienced religious disturbances involving a Frankish bishop named Adalbert (or Aldebert). Adalbert gained a significant following throughout Neustria by claiming to have received relics directly from angels rather than from Rome. He performed miracles and planted crosses in open fields, which encouraged people to desert the established churches. These activities were countered by the efforts of Boniface, who was more successful in his missions in France than in other regions.

## Historical Legacy
The ancient division between Neustria and Austrasia is noted for having long-term historical consequences. The distinction between the Latin-speaking West (Neustria) and the German-speaking East (Austrasia) created a divide that persisted into modern history. Additionally, the region produced notable figures such as Robert the Strong, a noble Frank of Neustria who served as the count of Anjou between 863 and 873 and was killed while defending his country against the Normans.
