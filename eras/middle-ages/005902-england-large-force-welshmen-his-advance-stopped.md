# England: Military and Administrative History

## Impressment and Military Recruitment
In England, the state exercised the authority to "press" or compel subjects into service for the defense of the realm, a practice known as impressment. This was viewed as an erratic and frequently oppressive method of enforcing the common obligation to serve in the *posse comitatus* (power of the county) or "the host." 

During the reign of the Plantagenet dynasty, kings utilized this power to press Welshmen via the Lords Marchers and Irish kerns via the Lords Deputy for wars conducted in France. Although parliament raised complaints regarding the oppressive nature of this power as early as the reign of Edward III, the practice continued. Throughout later history, impressment was used by Queen Elizabeth, King Charles I, and the parliament during the Civil War. Notably, Cromwell's New Model Army was largely raised through this method, with county committees ordered to select recruits who were well clothed and of "years meet for their employment." Following the Revolution of 1688, parliament continued to use this resource occasionally, including a general press of vagabonds and rogues in London in 1779. Men who were pressed were given a small sum of "coat and conduct money," also called imprest-money, to reach their rendezvous.

## The Duke of Buckingham and the House of York
Henry Stafford, the 2nd Duke of Buckingham (1454-1483), was a figure of importance to Edward IV due to his vast inheritance and royal lineage. He was the son of Humphrey Stafford and Margaret, daughter of Edmund Beaufort. He married Catherine Woodville, the sister of the queen, in 1466 and was made a knight of the Garter in 1474.

Following the death of Edward IV, Buckingham became an ally of Richard, duke of Gloucester. He played a prominent role in the deposition of Edward V and, on June 24, 1483, delivered an eloquent speech at the Guildhall urging citizens to accept Richard as king. For his support, Richard rewarded him with the offices of chamberlain and justiciar of North and South Wales, as well as the constableship of all royal castles in the Welsh Marches and the principality. After Richard's coronation, Buckingham served as chamberlain and was confirmed as constable of England and in his powers in Wales. However, in early August, Buckingham withdrew from court to Brecon, possibly due to dreams of the crown or a desire for greater rewards.

## Foreign Invasions and Naval Conflicts
England was the target of an invasion planned by Philip II, the "lonely autocrat of the Escurial." Between 1587 and 1592, Philip II sacrificed Spanish resources to pursue the conquest of the French throne and the invasion of England. To facilitate this, he forced Alexander Farnese to suspend his campaign in Flanders to assist in transporting troops for the English invasion. This enterprise failed utterly with the defeat of the Spanish Armada.

In earlier history, King Harald of Norway sailed westward, leaving England and Scotland behind him to plunder Klifland. After subduing that region and defeating a force at Hellornes, the Northmen's actions left the Englishmen with no choice but to submit to King Harald to preserve their lives.

## Imperial Concentration and Global Operations
During the "Imperial concentration" of the early 20th century, England managed a massive transportation of troops to support the war effort. This included moving the equivalent of five divisions from India to Europe, replaced by three divisions of Territorials from England. Additionally, approximately two divisions from Canada were transported to England. By the end of November, these movements, along with the collection of the 7th and 8th divisions from various British Empire garrisons, raised the Army in France to approximately 13 divisions of long-service troops. This sea transportation was completed without the loss of a single life or ship, despite the presence of enemy cruisers.

## Financial Stability and the Bank of England
In October 1847, the Bank of England faced a crisis characterized by a general hoarding of bank notes and coin. The Reserve of Specie dropped from £3,070,000 on October 16 to £1,600,000 by October 30. Despite having more than £6,000,000 of specie in the issue department, the banking department faced the possibility of being compelled to stop payment. While the chancellor of the exchequer, Sir C. Wood, initially declined to relax the Bank Act, a meeting between leading city bankers and Prime Minister Lord John Russell led to a relaxation. An official letter on October 25 recommended that the directors enlarge discounts and advances upon approved security, charging a high rate of 8% to limit operations. This government intervention successfully allayed the panic.
