# BAZOUGES

The term Bazouges, along with analogous forms such as Basoche, Basoque, and Basogue, refers to a French gild of clerks. The name is derived from the Latin *basilica*, used in the sense of law courts.

## Origins and Evolution
The Basoche was a very ancient organization, predating the gild of the *procureurs* (legal representatives), with which it frequently experienced variance. It originated during a period when the profession of the *procureur*—which encompasses the roles of advocate, legal representative, or procurator—was "free." During this era, individuals providing these legal services were not yet ministerial or public officers, provided the law permitted such services.

To support this system, groups of clerks—men who were either skilled in law or reputed to be so—were established near every important juridical center. These clerks initially filled the roles of advocate or representative indifferently. The Basoche of the parlement of Paris formed itself into a gild following the pattern of other trades and professions during the Middle Ages.

## Professional Specialization
Over time, the original organization disintegrated and split into more specialized bodies. One branch became the advocates, while the profession of legal representatives was regularized in 1344, eventually becoming a saleable charge. 

The remaining clerks formed the new Basoche. This later iteration consisted exclusively of those working as clerks for the *procureurs*. While some of the wealthier members of the Basoche aspired to become *procureurs* themselves, the group retained traces of its original status. An 18th-century author noted that these clerks were admitted to plead before M. le juge auditeur and M. le lieutenant civil *sur les referes*. Consequently, the *procureurs* of that era were essentially former clerks of the Basoche who had been granted the preference to officiate in important cases to the exclusion of other clerks.

## Traditions and Governance
The Basoche maintained several picturesque names and forms inherited from its ancient past. The organization was referred to as the "kingdom of the Basoche." For a significant period, its chief was elected annually by a general assembly and held the title of "king." However, by order, this title had to be relinquished toward the end of the 16th century.

## Etymological Context of the Basilica
The root of the term, *basilica*, is a word of Greek origin (*basilikê*). In Latin literature and inscriptions, it generally denoted a large covered building capable of accommodating many people. Strictly, a basilica was located near a city's business center for the convenience of litigants, merchants, and those engaged in public service. 

In Roman society, basilicas served as places of business for merchants and as courts of law. Examples include the Basilica Julia and the Basilica Ulpia, the latter of which featured a semicircular projection distinct from the basilica itself. The Basilica Julia was regularly used for the centumviral court. Architectural variations existed; some featured central spaces surrounded by galleries on columns or piers, while others, such as the Basilica of Maxentius, were entirely vaulted structures resembling Roman Thermae. Some basilicas contained fixed tribunals, while others did not.

In a broader sense, the term *basilica* was applied to various large structures, including:
* Halls of audience or covered promenades in private palaces.
* Riding schools (*basilica equestris exercitatoria*).
* Markets or stores for goods, such as flowers (*basilica floscellaria*) or clothing (*basilica vestiaria*).
* Meeting halls for religious bodies, a usage that eventually extended to the extensive buildings used for Christian worship during the age of Constantine and his successors.
