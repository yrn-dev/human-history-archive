# Bona and the Rulers of Tunis

## Overview of Bona
Bona, also known as Bone or by the Arabic name *annaba* (meaning "the city of jujube trees"), is a seaport located in Algeria. It is situated on a bay of the Mediterranean at 36 deg. 53' N., 7 deg. 46' E., and serves as the chief town of an arrondissement in the department of Constantine. Geographically, the town lies at the foot of the wooded heights of Edugh, approximately 136 miles northeast of Constantine and 220 miles west of Tunis by rail.

## Ancient Origins and Hippo Regius
Bona is identified with the ancient *Aphrodisium* and the seaport of *Ubbo* or *Hippo Regius*. The town derives its name from Hippo Regius, the ruins of which—including fragments of walls and large restored cisterns—are located about a mile south of the town. During the first three centuries of the Christian era, Hippo was among the richest cities in Roman Africa. It was named "Royal" (*Regius*) by the Romans because it served as a favorite residence for the Numidian kings.

The city is most noted for its connection to St. Augustine, who served as its bishop and priest for thirty-five years. In 431, the Vandals under Genseric captured Hippo after a fourteen-month siege, during which Augustine died. While general destruction occurred, Augustine's manuscripts, library, and the cathedral were spared. In the 7th century, the town was sacked by Arabs after having been partially restored by Belisarius. In 1900, a large basilica dedicated to St. Augustine, featuring a chancel facing west and an altar with a bronze statue of the saint, was opened on the hill where Hippo once stood.

## The Kasbah and the Rulers of Tunis
The town of Bona was built by the Arabs and remained a possession of the rulers of Tunis for several centuries. In 1300, these rulers constructed the Kasbah, a citadel that stands on a hill to the northeast of the town. 

From the beginning of the 14th century until the middle of the 15th century, the area was frequented by Spaniards and Italians. In the 16th century, Charles V held the town for a period and strengthened the citadel. Following this, the town was held in succession by Tunisians, Genoese, and Algerines.

## French Influence and Modern Development
From the era of Louis XIV until the Revolution, the French *Compagnie d'Afrique* maintained active trade with the port. The French occupied the town for a few months in 1830 and reoccupied it in 1832. During this latter event, Captains Yusuf and Armandy, leading a small force of marines, seized the Kasbah and held it for several months until reinforcements arrived.

Since that period, Bona has experienced significant industrial development, which was further stimulated in 1883 following the discovery of phosphate beds at Tebessa. The town has evolved into a flourishing French city with wide, well-laid-out streets, though some remain steep. A modern rampart was erected outside the old Arab wall, which had become too small for the town's growth.

## Urban Infrastructure and Commerce
The center of the town features the Cours Jerome-Bertagna (formerly the Cours National), a broad, tree-lined promenade housing hotels, banks, and a theatre. At the northern end of this promenade stands the cathedral of St. Augustine, a large church built in a quasi-Byzantine style; it houses a relic believed to be the right arm of St. Augustine, brought from Pavia in 1842. The Place d'Armes, the chief square, features the Grand Mosque, which was constructed using ruins from ancient Hippo. Other facilities include civil and military hospitals and barracks capable of accommodating 3,000 men.

Bona is a major center of commerce in Algeria, ranking only behind Oran and Algiers. Its exports include wool, iron, zinc, barley, cork, esparto, sheep, and phosphates. The town also hosts manufactories for leather, tapestry, and native garments. 

The port consists of an inner harbour covering 25 acres with fine quays for vessels drawing 22 ft, and a spacious outer harbour built between 1857 and 1868 and enlarged between 1905 and 1907. The town maintains direct steamship communication with Marseilles. To improve sanitary conditions and provide an abundant water supply from the Edugh hills, a system of canals was used to drain the marshes at the mouths of the Bujema and Seybuse rivers.
