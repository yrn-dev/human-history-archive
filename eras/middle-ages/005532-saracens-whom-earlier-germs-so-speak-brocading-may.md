# Saracens and the Development of Brocade

The Saracens played a foundational role in the history of textile arts, specifically in the development of brocading. Early "germs" of brocading are traced to oriental weavers under Saracen domination, particularly those operating in Syria, Persia, northern Africa, and parts of southern Europe.

## Technical Characteristics of Brocade
Brocade is a class of richly decorative shuttle-woven fabrics, typically produced from colored silks and occasionally incorporating silver and gold threads. The defining feature of brocade is that its ornamental elements are wrought as additions to the main fabric. These additions can stiffen the material or create a low-relief effect on the face of the cloth. On the reverse side, the brocaded or "broached" sections are characterized by floating weft threads that either hang in loose groups or have been clipped away.

The term itself is related to the Latin *broccus* and the Italian *brocato*, implying a form of stitching or broaching. This distinguishes true brocades from other enriched fabrics; for example, while some Indian kincobs or Persian silk-weavings are called brocades, they may lack the floating threads that define the actual brocading process.

## Siculo-Saracenic Production
In the 11th and 12th centuries, Siculo-Saracenic weavers produced significant examples of this craft. One specimen from this period features a cream-colored ground with red and olive green silks and gold thread. This fabric utilizes a trellis framework of lozenge-shaped compartments containing pairs of seated animals (*addorsed regardant*) and geese (*vis-à-vis*), separated by conventional trees. The piece also includes Kufic inscriptions, specifically the word "Arrahm[=a]n" (The Merciful) repeated in olive green.

Another notable example of Siculo-Saracenic weaving was produced at the Hotel des Tiraz in Palermo. This sumptuous material, woven with gold threads and colored silks, was used for an official robe for Henry IV (1165-1197) during his tenure as emperor of the Holy Roman Empire.

## Production in Baudas and the East
The city of Baudas (Baghdad), which served as the seat of the Calif of all the Saracens, was a major center for the weaving of gold brocades and silk stuffs. These included tissues such as *nasich*, *nac*, and *cramoisy*, often richly wrought with figures of birds and beasts. 

Certain rich gold and silk brocades from Baudas were known as *Baldachini* (or *Baudekins* in English). These were used for umbrellas and state canopies for Italian dignitaries, eventually leading to the architectural use of the term *Baldacchino*. These fabrics were sometimes made entirely of silk or as *sericum mixtum* (silk mixed with cotton or thread). Beyond Baudas, these materials were manufactured in Cyprus, Damas, and the province of Ahwaz, and were exported as far as England and France.

## Animal Motifs and Related Textiles
Medieval Saracens utilized a specific style of brocade wrought with figures of animals in gold, known as *Thard-wahsh* ("beast-hunts"). This tradition of depicting animals—including lions, panthers, and bears—is mirrored in other Eastern textiles. For instance, Persian carpets and curtains embroidered with animals were noted in antiquity, and the produce of Alexandria included carpets featuring animals and shells.

Beyond brocade, Saracens are associated with the production of fustian (a cut short pile textile). The English name "fustian" is derived from Fostat (old Cairo), where the material was likely produced, particularly under the Fatimite Khalifs prior to the 13th century, who encouraged various arts and manufactures.

## Religious and Cultural Context
In the sacred texts of the Qur'an, silk brocade is mentioned as a feature of the afterlife. The text describes those who will recline on beds or couches whose inner linings or coverings are made of silk brocade, with the fruits of the gardens nearby.

Historically, the Saracens existed as one of the three great empires or nations—alongside the Greeks and the Franks—that disputed the world from the age of Charlemagne until the Crusades. In terms of warlike qualifications, they were considered equal to the Greeks in their arts, riches, and obedience to a supreme head.
