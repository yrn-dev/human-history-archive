# Summa de legibus Anglie que vocatur Bretone

The *Summa de legibus Anglie que vocatur Bretone* (also referred to as *Britton*) is the earliest summary of English law written in the French tongue. The work purports to have been composed by command of King Edward I.

## Authorship and Origins
The authorship and origin of the *Britton* have been the subject of significant dispute. One theory attributes the work to John le Breton, the bishop of Hereford, based on a passage found in some manuscripts of the history of Matthew of Westminster. However, this theory faces chronological difficulties: the bishop of Hereford died in 1275, but the *Britton* contains allusions to statutes passed after that date, most notably the statute *Quia emptores terrarum* of 1290.

An alternative opinion held by Selden suggests that the work is a royal abridgment of the great work on the laws and customs of England written by Henry de Bracton, the last of the chief justiciaries. Selden noted that Bracton's name was sometimes spelled "Bretton" or "Bratton" in the fine Rolls. Despite this theory, the *Britton* and Bracton's work differ in their arrangement, and only a small proportion of Bracton's original work is incorporated into the *Britton*.

## Manuscripts and Provenance
An early 14th-century manuscript of the work is currently held in the Cambridge university library. This specific manuscript was once in the possession of Selden and bears the title *Summa de legibus Anglie que vocatur Bretone*. 

The work was also known in the 14th century as "a book called Bretoun," as evidenced by the will of Andrew Horn. Horn, a learned chamberlain of the city of London, bequeathed the book to the chamber of the Guildhall in 1329, along with another volume titled *Mirroir des Justices*.

## The Cambridge University Library
The manuscript of the *Britton* is part of the broader collections of the University library at Cambridge. This library is noted for its important collections of incunabula and has been the recipient of numerous significant bequests. These include:

*   **Books of Bishop Moore:** A collection including unique Caxton quartos, purchased by George I and presented to the library.
*   **Manuscripts of Edmund Castell:** The English orientalist and professor of Arabic bequeathed his manuscripts to the university.
*   **Collections of Thomas Baker:** An antiquary and fellow of St John's College who bequeathed his valuable books and manuscripts to the university.
*   **Lennox Documents:** A series of documents, notes, and indictments concerning Mary Stuart, written for or by the earl of Lennox, are held in the library (specifically Cambridge University MSS. Oo. 7. 47. fol. 17 b.). These were transcribed by Father Stevenson.

Other Cambridge college libraries also hold notable collections, such as the Pepysian library at Magdalene College (which contains the Maitland Folio MS of Dunbar's poems), the library of Archbishop Williams at St John's College, and the books of Sir Thomas Smith at Queens' College.
