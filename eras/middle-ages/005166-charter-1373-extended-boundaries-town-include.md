# The Charter of 1373 and the Development of Bristol

## Territorial Expansion and Legal Status
The charter of 1373 significantly altered the geography and legal standing of the town of Bristol. Through this charter, the town's boundaries were extended to include Redcliffe, an action that resolved a long-standing dispute. Additionally, the expansion incorporated the waters of the Severn and the Avon up to the Flat and Steep Holmes.

Beyond territorial growth, the 1373 charter established Bristol as a county in its own right. This designation granted the town independence from the county courts. The charter also instituted a new administrative structure, providing for an elected sheriff and a council consisting of forty members, who were to be chosen by the sheriff and the mayor.

## Governance and Administration
Under the provisions of the 1373 charter, the town was organized into five wards. Each of these wards was represented by an alderman. The mayoralty was restricted to these aldermen, as they were the only individuals eligible for the office. This specific charter was later confirmed in 1488 and 1377.

The governance of the corporation underwent changes in later years. In 1499, Henry VII granted a charter (confirmed in 1510) that altered the popular basis of the corporation by requiring that the mayor and council elect the aldermen. Much later, in 1664, Charles II granted a formal charter of incorporation. This established a governing body composed of a town clerk, a clerk of the peace, two coroners, two sheriffs, 30 common councilmen, 12 aldermen, and a mayor, along with 39 minor officers.

## Economic Prosperity and Trade
The period following the 1373 charter was marked by Bristol's greatest era of prosperity. This era saw the foundation of the Society of Merchant Venturers and the voyages of Sebastian and John Cabot. A prominent figure of this time was William Canyng (1399-1474), who served as mayor five times and represented Bristol in parliament twice. Canyng rebuilt St Mary Redcliffe and conducted an extensive cloth trade with the Baltic. During this period, Bristol merchants also exported cloth to the Levant, Spain, and France.

The Society of Merchant Venturers, whose records began in 1467, grew rapidly in influence. By 1500, the society held a lease of the port dues and directed all of the city's foreign trade. The society was incorporated in 1552 and received further charters in 1662 and 1638.

## Later Developments and Global Trade
The voyages of discovery, in which Bristol played a conspicuous role, further developed the city's trade. By the 16th century, Bristol's trade networks expanded to include the Canaries, Spain, and Spanish colonies in America. The city also participated in attempts to colonize Newfoundland. Additionally, Bristol began a trade in African slaves that flourished throughout the 17th century.

In other developments, the dissolution of the monasteries led to the foundation of the diocese of Bristol, which encompassed the counties of Dorset and Bristol. During the Civil War, Bristol was besieged three times and played a significant part in the conflict.
