# Bokhara

## Overview and Early History
Bokhara, known to the ancients as Sogdiana, is a city in Central Asia that has been deeply involved in the various bloody revolutions of the region. The foundation of its capital is ascribed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana became part of the Seleucidae empire and shared the fortunes of Bactria. 

Later, the nomad Yue-chi moved into the valley of the Oxus from the east, eventually establishing themselves as a settled territorial power. Under their king, Kadphises I, who is believed to have ascended the throne around A.D. 45, the dominions extended from Bokhara to the Indus. This district was subsequently reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand.

## Islamic Period and Regional Significance
Islam was introduced shortly after the Arab conquest of Persia (640-642) and rapidly became the dominant faith. During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, Sogdiana—also called Sughd—was one of the most celebrated and flourishing districts of central Asia. It contained two great cities: Samarkand and Bokhara. While Samarkand generally served as the seat of government, Bokhara earned a high reputation as a seat of learning and religion. During the early middle ages, the region was known as Ma wara 'l Nahr (or Ma-vera-un-nahr), a term translated as Transoxiana.

## Medieval Conquests and the Mongol Invasion
At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah, the third of the Persian Seljuk dynasty, crossed the Oxus and subdued the entire country watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus rivers. In 1216, Bokhara was subdued by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm. However, this conquest was wrested from him in 1220 by Jenghiz Khan.

The Mongol invasion brought severe devastation, as the country was wasted by the fury of Jenghiz Khan. Reports brought by the spies of the Sultan of Khwarizm described the army of Jenghiz as countless, comparing them to a swarm of locusts or ants. These warriors were noted for their obedience, endurance, and "lion-like valour." According to these reports, the Mongols took no rest, ate the flesh of all animals (including bears, swine, and dogs), and drank blood from a horse's vein. In victory, they were described as leaving neither small nor great alive, including the killing of pregnant women.

Following this period of destruction, the region recovered some of its former prosperity under Ogdai Khan, the son of Jenghiz, who is described as having a benevolent and humane disposition. His descendants maintained possession of the area until 1369, when Timur (also known as Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand and bore down everything before him.

## Geography and Travel
Bokhara is situated in a region characterized by deserts. Ibn Batuta recorded that the desert extending between Khwarizm and Bokhara is an 18 days' journey. The total journey from Ukek to Bokhara would take at least 60 days.

In the 19th century, the city remained a point of interest for explorers. Sir Alexander Burnes, a British traveller and explorer, visited Bokhara during his travels across the Hindu Kush and through Persia, publishing a narrative of his visits in 1834. Additionally, the city of Balkh was subject to the khan of Bokhara for a period before being captured by Mahommed Akram Khan, Barakzai, in 1850.
