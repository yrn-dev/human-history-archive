# Marco Polo

Marco Polo was a Venetian gentleman and renowned traveler of the thirteenth century. He is primarily known for his narrative of travels, which became enormously popular during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and is regarded as one of the great books of history.

## Life and Family
Marco Polo was the son of Nicolo and the grandson of Andrea Polo of S. Felice. The Polo family was divided into two recognized branches: Polo of S. Felice and Polo of S. Geremia. While some early sources, such as Ramusio and Rusticiano, referred to him as "noble" or "magnifico," his technical nobility was later established through official resolutions of the Great Council and judicial decisions where he was designated *Nobilis Vir*.

Polo's life included a mission to Kublai Khan, continuing his journey after two Dominicans returned. In 1298, following a naval battle in which the Venetians were defeated by the Genoese, Polo was among 7,000 prisoners taken. During his captivity in Genoa, he recounted his travels to a writer named Rusticiano, who recorded the narrative. Other biographical details noted in the archives include his marriage, his daughters, his career as a merchant, and the death of his father before 1300.

## The Travels of Marco Polo
The narrative of Polo's travels provides a window into the thirteenth century. The work exists in several types of text:
*   **The Geographic or Oldest French:** Considered the source of all other versions.
*   **The Remodelled French Text:** Followed by Pauthier.
*   **Friar Pipino’s Latin:** A translation, with a further fifth-hand translation by Grynæus.
*   **Ramusio’s Italian:** A version containing both genuine statements and "injudicious tamperings."

An Irish version of the text also exists. The work describes various regions and structures, such as the "Lord's Great Palace" (identified as the *Ta-ming tien*), which Polo described as having no upper story, a fine pillared balustrade, and a basement raised ten palms above the soil. He also described the city of Balkh as "a noble city and a great," despite the city having been sacked and leveled by Jenghiz Khan in 1220 and again by Timur in the fourteenth century.

## Influence and Historical Reception
Polo's work had a significant impact on later exploration and geography, leading directly to the discovery of America. However, some of his descriptions led to cartographic errors. For example, mapmakers like Zalterius (1566) and Mercator (1569) identified a narrow strait called "Streto de Anian" based on Polo's "Anica" or "Anin" (modern Annam), mistakenly placing it on the American coast rather than in Asia.

The reception of Polo's work among his contemporaries was varied. He was often believed by his neighbors to be given to exaggeration, earning the sobriquet *Milione*. There is little evidence that his work influenced other prominent contemporaries; for instance, Dante never alludes to Polo or Cathay in the *Divina Commedia*. While Francesco da Barberino mentions Cathay, there is no evidence this suggests knowledge of Polo's book. Similarly, no trace of Polo's work appears in the writings of Marino Sanudo the Elder.

Regarding the English poet Chaucer, Professor John Matthews Manly argued against the theory that Chaucer was indebted to Polo. Manly stated that any resemblance between Polo's narrative and the *Squire's Tale* would require "twisting everything" and that Chaucer had access to at least half a dozen other accounts of Tartary.

## Genealogy and Legacy
The genealogy of the Polo family has been a subject of confusion. Some records, such as those by Marco Barbaro, erroneously claim the Castellano of Verona was Marco's grandson via a son named Maffeo—a claim considered impossible given the dates of birth and marriage. Archive research suggests that the families of Polo of S. Geremia and Polo of S. Giov. Grisostomo were often confounded in these records. It is probable that Marco the Castellano of Verona and Maria Trevisan belonged to the S. Geremia branch.
