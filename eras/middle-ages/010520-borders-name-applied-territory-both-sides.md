# THE BORDERS

The term "The Borders" refers to the territory situated on both sides of the boundary line separating England and Scotland. While the name is applied to both sides, it is most frequently used to describe the Scottish side. The term carries geographical, historical, and literary significance.

## Geography and Boundary Line
The boundary line begins on the coast of Berwickshire, at a point 3 miles North by West of Berwick. From there, it runs a short distance West and South to reach the Tweed near the village of Paxton. The line follows the river to a point just beyond Carham, where it strikes South-South-East toward the Cheviot Hills. For 35 miles, the watershed of the Cheviot Hills constitutes the boundary. 

Following this, the border is formed by a series of streams: the Kershope, Bells Burn, Liddel, and Esk. After following the Esk for 1 mile, the line cuts due west across the country to the Sark, which it follows to the river's mouth at the head of the Solway Firth. The total length of this boundary is 108 miles, although the direct distance from the North Sea to the Solway is only 70 miles.

## Regional Characteristics and Administration
The region is characterized by distinct physical differences between the two sides. The southern side consists primarily of rugged glens and bleak, lofty moorland used for the subsistence of cattle and sheep.

The area is watered by various rivers. On the English side, these include the North Tyne, Rede, Coquet, Bowmont, and Till. On the Scottish side, the region is watered by the Sark, Esk, Liddel, Kershope, Jed, Kale, Leet, Whiteadder, and Tweed.

Administratively, the English Border counties are Cumberland and Northumberland. The Scottish Border counties include Dumfries, Roxburgh, and Berwick; additionally, the shires of Peebles and Selkirk have historically been classed as Border shires by usage.

## Special Territories
Two specific areas of the border region are noted for their unique status:

*   **The Liberties of Berwick:** At the extreme eastern end, there is a small district of 8 square miles. This tract, located north of the Tweed but not included in Scotland, forms the "bounds" or "liberties" of Berwick, serving as the country of the town and borough of Berwick-on-Tweed.
*   **The Debatable Land:** At the extreme west, between the Esk and the Sark (up to the Esk's junction with the Liddel), there existed a strip of country known as "No man's land." For generations, this area was a haunt for brigands and outlaws. It was termed the Debatable Land because its possession was a constant source of contention between Scotland and England until the boundaries were finally adjusted in 1552.
