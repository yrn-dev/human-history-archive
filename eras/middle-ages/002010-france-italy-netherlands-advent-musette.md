# Bag-pipes in France, Italy, and the Netherlands Before the Musette

Prior to the advent of the musette, the primary bag-pipes utilized in the Netherlands, Italy, and France were the *cornemuse* and the *chalemie*. These instruments served as the precursors to the musette, maintaining a relationship similar to that between the old Irish bag-pipe and the union-pipes, or the *piva* and *cornemusa* in relation to the *surdelina* or *sampogna* in Italy.

## The French Cornemuse
During the 16th and 17th centuries, two distinct varieties of the cornemuse were known in France. These versions differed primarily in a structural detail that altered the timbre of the instrument. Detailed descriptions and illustrations of these varieties were provided by Pere Marin Mersenne.

### The Shepherd's Cornemuse
One variety of the cornemuse (or chalemie) was used as a solo instrument and by shepherds. This version was similar to the Highland bag-pipe and consisted of the following components:
*   **The Bag:** A leather bag inflated via a valved blow-pipe.
*   **The Drones:** It featured two drones tuned to C. The *gros bourdon* (large drone) measured 2-1/2 feet in length and contained a 2-1/2 inch beating-reed fixed in the stock. The *petit bourdon* (small drone) measured 1 foot in length and contained a 2 inch beating-reed, which was fixed in the same stock as the chaunter.
*   **The Chaunter:** The chaunter possessed a conical bore and a double reed, similar to an oboe, which was hidden within the stock. It featured eight holes (a thumb-hole and seven in front), providing a compass from C to C'. When removed from the stock and played separately by placing the reed directly in the mouth, the compass could be increased by a third to E by overblowing the D and E an octave through the pressure of the lips and breath.

### The Concert Cornemuse
The second variety of cornemuse was used exclusively in concert with a family of instruments called the *Hautbois de Poitou*. In this version, the hautbois featured a reed enclosed in an air-chamber, mirroring the reed arrangement of the bag-pipe. This specific cornemuse differed from the shepherd's version in its drone; it possessed only one drone, which utilized a double reed like that of the chaunter rather than a beating-reed. This drone could be tuned by drawing out the joint to change its length. The chaunter of this instrument had eight holes, with the lowest hole covered by a key enclosed in a perforated box.

## Evolution Toward the Musette
The transition from the cornemuse to the musette was driven by the tastes of the French "elegant world" and courtiers. While the cornemuse of rustic swains and shepherds became a fashionable instrument, the act of inflating the bag with the breath was found to distort the performer's face. To remedy this, bellows were substituted for the blow-pipe. 

The instrument was further refined in tone-quality and appearance to suit its new, more exalted position in court life. This evolution resulted in the musette, which is said to have evolved during the 15th century. By the end of that century, musette players were present at the French court. The instrument reached a fully developed state by the beginning of the 17th century.

## Cultural and Historical Context
The bag-pipe held a significant place in medieval and early modern European culture. By the 13th century, it had become a court instrument played by troubadours and minnesingers. Around 1250, bells for the chaunters and stocks were fashioned using animal or human heads.

In the broader cultural landscape of the regions involved:
*   **Drama:** The Griselda story had been dramatized in the Netherlands, Italy, and France before the year 1500.
*   **Religion:** Before the accession of Henry VIII, Italy, France, and Holland (the Netherlands) possessed the Bible in the vernacular.
