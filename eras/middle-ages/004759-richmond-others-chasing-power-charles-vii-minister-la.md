# The Removal of La Trémoille from Power

## Political Conflict under Charles VII
During the reign of King Charles VII of France, the royal court was characterized by shifting alliances and the rise and fall of influential ministers. One such period of instability involved the minister La Trémoille, who was eventually chased from power in 1433. This removal was the result of a coordinated effort by several high-ranking figures, including the constable Richmond, Yolande (the queen of Sicily), and Pierre de Brézé.

## The Role of Pierre de Brézé
Pierre de Brézé (c. 1410-1465) was a member of a noble Angevin family and served as a trusted statesman and soldier for Charles VII. He first established his reputation as a soldier during the English wars. His involvement in the 1433 campaign to remove La Trémoille from power marked a significant early step in his political ascent. Following this event, Brézé was knighted by Charles of Anjou in 1434 and subsequently entered the royal council. 

Brézé's career continued to advance through various administrative appointments; he became the seneschal of Anjou in 1437 and the seneschal of Poitou in 1440. By 1443, he had become the chamberlain to Charles VII. Through the influence of Agnes Sorel, Brézé attained the chief power in the state, effectively superseding his former allies, including Charles of Anjou and the constable Richmond.

## The Influence of the Constable Richmond
The constable Arthur, earl of Richmond (also referred to as de Richmond), was a figure of significant military and political weight during this era. In addition to his role in the removal of La Trémoille, Richmond exercised a "preponderating influence" over Charles VII. This influence was sufficient to displace other powerful advisers; for example, Jean Louvet, the president of Provence and a principal agent of the Armagnac party, was forced to retire to his captaincy of Avignon when Richmond's power grew. 

Richmond also played a key role in the liberation of the capital. In 1436, he was assisted by the French commander Ambroise de Loré in the effort to expel the English from Paris.

## Later Developments and Successions
The period of ascendancy for Pierre de Brézé between 1444 and 1450 is noted as the most prosperous era of Charles VII's reign. However, Brézé faced opposition from the dauphin Louis, who brought accusations against him in 1448, though Brézé was ultimately exonerated. 

Following the death of Agnes Sorel, Brézé's influence at court declined, and he served as the seneschal of Normandy. His later years were marked by a failed descent on the English coast at Sandwich in 1457 and a subsequent period of disgrace and short imprisonment following the accession of Louis XI. He eventually returned to favor, accompanying Margaret of Anjou to Scotland with 2,000 men in 1462. Pierre de Brézé died in the battle of Montlhéry on July 16, 1465, and was succeeded as seneschal of Normandy by his eldest son, Jacques de Brézé.
