# Strength and Composition of Brickwork

The strength of brickwork is subject to significant variation based on several critical factors: the specific type of brick employed, the position of the brickwork, the quality and kind of cement or lime mortar used, and the quality of the workmanship.

## Crushing Loads of Various Brickwork
Experiments conducted by the Royal Institute of British Architects between 1896 and 1897 utilized short walls to determine the average loads per square foot at which crushing occurs. The results demonstrated a clear difference in strength between brickwork set in lime mortar versus cement mortar:

*   **Staffordshire blue brickwork:** The strongest of the tested materials, crushing at 114.34 tons per sq. ft. in lime mortar and 135.43 tons in cement mortar.
*   **Leicester red brickwork:** Crushed at 45.36 tons per sq. ft. in lime mortar and 83.36 tons in cement mortar.
*   **Gault brickwork:** Crushed at 31.14 tons per sq. ft. in lime mortar and 51.34 tons in cement mortar.
*   **Fletton brickwork:** Crushed at 30.68 tons in lime and 56.25 tons in cement.
*   **Stock brickwork:** The weakest of the group, crushing at 18.63 tons per sq. ft. in lime mortar and 39.29 tons per sq. ft. in cement mortar.

Separate data regarding the crushing load of individual bricks (measured in tons per sq. in.) indicates that sand-lime bricks (2.9 to 3.4) and Staffordshire blue bricks (2.8 to 3.3) are the strongest, while London stock (0.7 to 1.3) and common hand-made bricks (0.4 to 0.9) are weaker.

## Mortar Composition and Materials
Mortar is composed of a binder (lime or cement) and sand. The quality of the sand is paramount; pit sand is considered the best because it is sharp and angular, though it must be washed to remove clay or earth and screened to remove gravel. River sand is less effective as attrition rubs the particles smooth, and sea sand is objectionable due to its saline taint, which attracts moisture and keeps brickwork damp.

Common mortar proportions include:
*   **Grey lime mortar:** One part lime to two or three parts sand.
*   **Portland cement mortar:** One part cement to three or five parts sand.
*   **Roman cement mortar:** One or one and a half parts cement to one part sand.
*   **Lias lime mortar:** Similar to grey lime, though equal parts lime and sand are used for work below ground.

Specialized mortars include blue or black mortar—made with foundry sand or smith's ashes for greater durability in external pointing—and pargetting for chimney flues, which consists of one part lime and three parts cow dung.

## Sand-Lime Bricks
Patented in the early 1880s by Dr. Michaelis of Berlin, sand-lime bricks are produced by mixing dry sand with approximately one-tenth of its weight in powdered slaked lime. This mixture is moistened and molded under pressures of about 60 tons per sq. in. The bricks are then placed in steel cylinders (60 to 80 ft. long) and subjected to high-pressure steam (120 to 150 lb per sq. in.) for ten to fifteen hours. This process creates a bond of hydrated calcium silicate, making the sand-lime brick significantly stronger than ordinary mortar-bricks. A typical German sand-lime brick consists of 84% silica, 7% lime, 2% alumina and oxide of iron, and 7% water, magnesia, and alkalis.

## Construction Standards and Measurement
In London and surrounding counties, brickwork is measured by the "rod," a square of 16½ ft. with a thickness of 1½ bricks. A rod gauged four courses to a foot using bricks 8¾ in. long, 4¼ in. wide, and 2¾ in. thick (with ¼ in. joints) requires 4,356 bricks. Such a rod weighs between 13½ and 15 tons, with cement mortar resulting in a heavier weight than lime.

Structural guidelines dictate that the height of a brick pier should not exceed twelve times its least width. According to the London Building Act, external or party walls in non-public or non-warehouse buildings must not exceed a height sixteen times their thickness; for warehouse-class buildings, this limit is fourteen times the thickness.

## Workmanship and Finishing
The use of "grout" (thin liquid mortar) to fill spaces between courses is viewed as a sign of inefficient workmanship and is discouraged in high-quality work as it can ooze and stain the brickwork. 

Pointing is the final finishing of the joints. Various styles exist:
*   **Flush joints:** Pressed back flush with the brick face, often used for walls to be limewhited.
*   **Jointed flat joints:** Feature a semicircular groove to increase density and durability.
*   **Struck, bevelled, or weathered joints:** Formed with a sloping surface to throw off water.
*   **Recessed jointing:** Creates a deep shadow but is discouraged as it encourages rain to soak into the brick.
