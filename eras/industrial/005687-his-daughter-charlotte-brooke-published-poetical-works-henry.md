# The Poetical Works of Henry Brooke

## Publication and Biographical Sources
In 1792, Charlotte Brooke published *The Poetical Works of Henry Brooke*. Despite this publication, she was able to provide very little material regarding her father's biography. Other sources for the biography of Henry Brooke include *Brookiana* (published in two volumes in 1804) by C. H. Wilson, as well as a biographical preface written by E. A. Baker, which was included in a 1906 new edition of *The Fool of Quality*.

## Early Life and Education
Henry Brooke was an Irish author born around 1703 at Rantavan in County Cavan. He was the son of William Brooke, who served as the rector of Killinkere in the same county, and his mother was a daughter of Simon Digby, the bishop of Elphin. His education included instruction from Dr. Thomas Sheridan. In 1720, Brooke entered Trinity College, Dublin, and was subsequently sent to London in 1724 to study law.

## Literary Career and Political Involvement
Brooke's early literary output included a philosophical poem in six books titled *Universal Beauty*, published in 1735. During his time in London, he lived in a small house at Twickenham near Alexander Pope and aligned himself with the party of the prince of Wales. 

His work often intersected with political tensions. In 1738, he translated the first two books of Tasso's *Gerusalemme liberata*. The following year, he produced a tragedy titled *Gustavas Vasa, the Deliverer of his Country*. Although the play was rehearsed at Drury Lane for five weeks, the performance was forbidden. This prohibition was attributed to a supposed portrait of Sir Robert Walpole in the role of Trollio, as well as a general government dislike for the play's spirit of fervent patriotism. While the play was not staged in London, it was printed, sold extensively, and later performed on the Irish stage under the title *The Patriot*. This event led Samuel Johnson to write a satirical pamphlet in 1739 titled "A Complete Vindication of the Licensers of the Stage from the malicious and scandalous Aspersions of Mr Brooke."

## Later Life and Irish Works
Due to concerns from his wife, Catherine Meares (his cousin and ward, whom he married before she was fourteen), regarding the imprudence of his connection to the opposition, Brooke returned to Ireland. He developed an interest in Irish literature and history, though he abandoned projects for a history of Ireland from the earliest times and a collection of Irish stories because of disputes over the ownership of materials.

During the Jacobite rebellion of 1745, Brooke wrote *Farmer's Six Letters to the Protestants of Ireland* (collected in 1746), a work inspired by Swift's *Drapier's Letters*. As a result of this service, the government appointed him barrack-master at Mullingar, a post he held until his death in 1783. He also wrote other pamphlets supporting the Protestant side and served as secretary to an association.

## Other Literary Works
Beyond his political and philosophical writings, Henry Brooke authored several other works:
* **Satire and Fables:** He wrote *Jack the Giant Queller* (1748), an operatic satire that was forbidden from being repeated due to political allusions. He also contributed fables to Edward Moore's *Fables for the Female Sex* (1744).
* **Novels and Tales:** In 1773, he published a novel titled *Juliet Grenville; or the History of the Human Heart*. He also contributed "Constantia, or the Man of Lawe's Tale" (1741) to George Ogle's *Canterbury Tales modernized*.
* **Drama:** Brooke wrote several tragedies, though only some were staged.
